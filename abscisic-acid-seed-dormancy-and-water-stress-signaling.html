1. The physiological problem of waiting and conserving water

A seed lying in moist soil faces a decision that cannot be reversed easily. Once the radicle emerges and the young root begins to grow, the organism becomes dependent on an adequate supply of water and other resources. A mature plant faces a related decision every time it opens a stomatal pore: carbon dioxide enters through the pore, but water vapour escapes. Both situations involve the risk of committing resources at an unsuitable time. Abscisic acid (ABA) is one of the principal hormonal signals through which flowering plants coordinate these decisions. In developing seeds, it helps establish maturation programmes and prevents premature germination. In vegetative organs, it adjusts the exchange of gases and water and regulates the expression of genes that help tissues withstand dehydration.

A hormone is not the physical energy that accomplishes a biological task. ABA does not itself pull water out of a guard cell, build the seed coat or provide ATP for metabolic adaptation. Instead, it changes the activities of proteins that carry out those functions. Because cells differ in their proteins, a common chemical signal can generate different outcomes in different organs. An embryo that expresses seed-specific transcriptional regulators responds differently from a guard cell equipped with specialized membrane channels. Thus, the identity of a hormone is only the first part of explaining a physiological response. The amount of active hormone, its cellular location, the competence of the responding tissue and the duration of exposure are all important.

ABA was identified during the 1960s in studies of substances associated with dormancy and organ shedding. Its name reflects early observations, not a universal assignment of function. Ethylene is a major direct regulator of many abscission responses, and the contribution of ABA depends on the organ and species. Similarly, the older description of ABA as a growth inhibitor captures some familiar outcomes but conceals important differences between organs. When water availability declines, restriction of leaf expansion can coexist with maintenance of root elongation at moderate levels of stress. The adaptive value of a response must be considered in the environment in which it occurs.

Three distinctions will organize the rest of the chapter. First, hormone biosynthesis is not hormone perception: a plant may contain normal ABA yet be unable to respond. Second, dormancy is not simply a lack of water; a dormant viable seed fails to germinate under conditions that otherwise permit germination. Third, closure of a stomatal pore is not a guarantee of drought survival; it reduces one route of water loss but can also decrease photosynthetic carbon gain. Each distinction can be tested by designing an experiment in which one variable changes while the relevant alternatives are controlled.

Distinguishing a developmental programme from a stress response

Consider a seed developing on its mother plant. Water and carbohydrates arrive from maternal tissues, but the embryo does not immediately elongate its radicle. During maturation, cells accumulate reserves, develop protection against drying and establish a threshold for subsequent germination. In a leaf exposed to dry air, the plant faces an entirely different problem: a continuously operating exchange system must be adjusted without destroying the tissue. These two ABA responses differ in time, cell type and output. They illustrate why a hormone should be classified by its molecular origin and signaling mechanism, rather than by one visible effect. A short pulse may change channel activity within minutes, whereas repeated or sustained exposure can modify gene expression over hours. The signal can therefore connect rapid regulation with developmental history.

Dormancy, quiescence and survival

A viable seed that fails to germinate in favorable conditions may possess physiological dormancy. A non-dormant seed kept dry is quiescent because the external requirements for germination are not met. A dead seed will not germinate even when dormancy-breaking treatment is applied. These three populations can look identical in a single germination count. To separate them, provide water, suitable temperature and oxygen, use an independent viability test, and compare an appropriate dormancy-breaking treatment. ABA sensitivity may help explain the first group, but cannot restore a dead embryo. A useful question asks which state is actually measured, not merely whether a radicle was seen.

Why measurements must be made at the correct scale

A whole-leaf ABA measurement averages cells with different capacities for production, transport and response. The same mean concentration can arise when every cell contains a little hormone or when a small population contains much more. A stomatal aperture measurement, in contrast, reflects the state of a particular pair of guard cells. To connect the two measurements, combine a tissue-level chemical assay with spatial information or cell-specific genetic perturbation. If restoring an ABA biosynthetic enzyme specifically in one cell type improves closure, that shows that ABA from that cell type can support the response under the tested conditions; it does not prove that no other tissue normally contributes.

2. From plastid carotenoids to active ABA

The naturally abundant active form of ABA in many higher plants is a fifteen-carbon oxygenated terpenoid derived from a larger carotenoid. A carotenoid is a lipid-soluble pigment constructed from isoprenoid units. Carotenoids have important roles in photosynthetic antennae and photoprotection, but selected oxygenated carotenoids, termed xanthophylls, also provide substrates for hormone synthesis. The plant route to ABA is therefore called an indirect or carotenoid-cleavage pathway. It is different from assembling a fifteen-carbon molecule directly from a cytosolic sesquiterpene precursor, even though the final product contains fifteen carbon atoms.

In plastids, zeaxanthin epoxidase (encoded by ABA1 in Arabidopsis) participates in the formation of epoxidized xanthophylls, including violaxanthin. The relevant nine-cis forms of violaxanthin and neoxanthin become substrates for a family of enzymes known as nine-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenases, abbreviated NCED. The geometric configuration of the carotenoid matters: the enzyme recognizes a suitable cis substrate rather than indiscriminately cleaving every photosynthetic pigment. Oxidative cleavage of the forty-carbon substrate produces the fifteen-carbon intermediate xanthoxin and a complementary twenty-five-carbon product. Xanthoxin represents the portion of the original molecule that proceeds towards ABA. Counting the carbon atoms before and after cleavage provides a simple check on the pathway.

The remaining conversion steps occur principally in the cytosol. The enzyme encoded by ABA2, a short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase, converts xanthoxin into abscisic aldehyde. Abscisic aldehyde oxidase, represented by AAO3 in Arabidopsis, oxidizes the aldehyde into the corresponding carboxylic acid, ABA. The aldehyde oxidase requires an appropriately sulfurated molybdenum cofactor; the ABA3 gene contributes to this cofactor maturation. A lesion affecting ABA3 can therefore impair aldehyde oxidation without changing the AAO3 coding sequence. This provides a useful distinction between loss of an enzyme and loss of a factor necessary for its activity.

NCED is frequently a major regulatory point because it directs the carotenoid pool towards ABA synthesis. Increasing its activity can increase hormone production, provided the relevant substrate and downstream enzymatic capacity are available. It is not correct to infer that all plants or tissues are limited by precisely the same step under every condition. In one tissue substrate delivery may be restrictive; in another, the rate of aldehyde conversion or hormone removal can determine the concentration of free ABA. A transcriptional increase in NCED must consequently be interpreted as evidence of potential biosynthetic regulation rather than a direct measurement of ABA production.

Different NCED family members can have contrasting developmental expression patterns. Arabidopsis NCED6 and NCED9, for example, are important in developing seeds, whereas NCED3 is associated with dehydration-induced ABA accumulation in vegetative tissue. This division of labour illustrates why a single mutant may have a stronger phenotype in one organ than another. It also explains why genetic experiments involving combinations of mutations can reveal functions masked by partially redundant enzymes. The terminology of Arabidopsis genes should not be applied without qualification to every crop species.

Carbon skeleton and upstream context

The plastidial methylerythritol phosphate pathway supplies isoprenoid precursors for carotenoid synthesis. Geranylgeranyl diphosphate provides the building blocks from which the forty-carbon carotenoid skeleton is assembled. Phytoene synthesis and subsequent desaturation and cyclization lead to carotenes; hydroxylation and epoxidation produce xanthophylls. The ABA branch is distinguished by selective oxidative cleavage rather than by the production of the upstream pigment. A defect very early in carotenoid biosynthesis can therefore cause both ABA deficiency and a photosynthetic or pigment phenotype. A defect at a later NCED step may affect the ABA pool more selectively. This contrast is important when choosing mutants for causal studies: a pale, severely damaged seedling cannot be interpreted as though the only variable were hormone concentration.

Why the cis configuration matters

A double bond restricts rotation, so a cis carotenoid has a different spatial arrangement from a trans carotenoid of the same molecular formula. An enzyme recognizes a three-dimensional substrate, not a molecular formula alone. This helps explain why a large pool of photoprotective carotenoids need not translate into a proportionally large rate of ABA production. The availability of the appropriate substrate, the presence of active NCED and the physiological conditions of the plastid jointly determine cleavage flux. NCED is frequently a major regulatory point, particularly during dehydration, but a single universal rate-limiting enzyme cannot be assumed for every organ and developmental stage.

Cofactor requirements and genetic prediction

ABA2 catalyzes conversion of xanthoxin toward abscisic aldehyde; AAO3 catalyzes oxidation of the aldehyde to ABA. The aldehyde oxidase requires a functional molybdenum cofactor in its sulfurated form, which depends on ABA3. Thus an aba3 mutation can affect more than one molybdenum-cofactor enzyme, while an aao3 lesion more directly affects the terminal oxidation relevant to ABA synthesis. A precursor-feeding experiment is interpretable only when downstream enzymes and transport remain functional. If supplying xanthoxin rescues a cleavage defect but not an aba2 defect, it supports the order of the reactions. If applied ABA rescues both, the result supports a biosynthetic explanation but does not identify the exact lesion by itself.

Tissue and developmental specificity of NCED

Arabidopsis contains several NCED genes whose contributions are not identical. NCED3 is particularly associated with vegetative dehydration-induced ABA accumulation; NCED6 and NCED9 contribute to seed ABA synthesis and dormancy. A single mutant may have a weaker phenotype than a multiple mutant because paralogs overlap in function, or because different tissues contribute hormone. Gene names from Arabidopsis and maize must not be interchanged without considering orthology. VP14 is the classic maize cleavage enzyme; it is not the designation of every NCED enzyme in every plant. An expression plot showing NCED induction is evidence of regulation, but enzyme activity and ABA measurement are needed to establish flux.

Worked carbon-accounting example

Suppose a hypothetical plastid preparation converts 18 nmol of the appropriate C40 carotenoid to cleavage products and all released xanthoxin molecules reach the cytosol. Under a one-to-one cleavage assumption, 18 nmol of C15 xanthoxin and 18 nmol of complementary C25 product are formed. If downstream conversion yields ABA with 75 percent efficiency, 13.5 nmol ABA is produced. The number 40 does not imply forty ABA molecules: carbon number describes atoms per molecule, and stoichiometric yield depends on the reaction. Nor does the example say that endogenous plants convert 75 percent; it is a bookkeeping exercise. Loss to alternative pathways, transport and further metabolism would alter the measured yield.

3. How a maize mutant identified the cleavage reaction

Vivipary in maize means that developing kernels germinate prematurely on the parent plant. Such a phenotype provides an opportunity to investigate the hormonal controls of seed development without beginning from a predetermined biochemical explanation. A viviparous embryo might fail to manufacture a germination inhibitor, might be insensitive to it, or might contain an altered developmental regulator that bypasses inhibition. The appearance of a germinated kernel alone does not distinguish among these possibilities. Comparing the hormone content and biochemical capabilities of wild-type and mutant embryos provides a way to locate the affected process.

The classical vp14 studies by Tan and colleagues and Schwartz and colleagues in 1997 illustrate complementary genetic and biochemical strategies. The mutant retained carotenoid substrates and could convert supplied xanthoxin to ABA, yet showed reduced ABA accumulation in developing embryos. The downstream machinery was therefore not completely disabled. The results focused attention on the reaction between the available carotenoid substrate and xanthoxin. Tests with recombinant VP14 protein demonstrated cleavage of suitable epoxycarotenoids and identified the products, connecting the gene directly to the predicted catalytic activity. The conclusion rests on several kinds of evidence rather than on a simple correlation between a gene and a phenotype.

Imagine three other mutants in this pathway. Mutant X cannot generate the appropriate epoxycarotenoid; mutant Y lacks NCED activity; mutant Z cannot oxidize abscisic aldehyde. All may be deficient in ABA, but their precursor profiles differ. Supplying xanthoxin to tissue extracts should bypass the early blocks in X and Y if the extract retains functional ABA2 and aldehyde oxidase. It cannot bypass a complete block of the final oxidation in Z. Providing ABA itself should bypass a purely biosynthetic defect if the responding tissue can receive and perceive the hormone. The logic is analogous to metabolic complementation: a product supplied downstream can overcome an upstream deficiency.

There are important qualifications when interpreting hormone rescue in intact plants. An externally supplied compound may fail to reach the target cells, may be rapidly metabolized or may be used at an unsuitable dose. A tissue extract removes some delivery barriers but changes the cellular environment and may lose an essential cofactor. Consequently, a convincing interpretation combines direct metabolite measurement, genetic complementation, appropriate vehicle controls and a test of downstream responsiveness. The objective is not merely to observe rescue; it is to identify why rescue is possible in one genotype and not another.

From a mutant phenotype to a biochemical mechanism

A viviparous maize mutant provided a useful starting point because kernels germinated precociously while still on the ear. A physiological response to applied hormone suggested that the defect concerned ABA production rather than complete inability to respond. Cloning a gene associated with the phenotype and showing that its protein cleaves suitable carotenoids gave a substantially stronger argument than phenotype alone. Genetic complementation helps establish that a particular gene lesion is responsible; biochemical assays establish what the protein can do under defined conditions. Together, they connect organism, gene, enzyme and product. Neither a low hormone concentration nor sequence similarity alone is sufficient to establish the catalytic reaction.

Reconstituting the core signal

In 2009 two complementary research approaches identified PYR/PYL/RCAR proteins as ABA receptors. Genetic sensitivity to a chemical ABA agonist and biochemical searches for PP2C regulators converged on a soluble receptor family. In a purified system, adding ABA and receptor reduces the activity of a compatible PP2C; the receptor alone is not simply a protein kinase. Structural work subsequently explained how binding of ABA changes the receptor surface. In a separate reconstitution experiment, receptor, PP2C, SnRK2 and a channel can be combined to test whether the kinase-to-channel relay is sufficient to alter channel activity. These approaches establish different levels of evidence and together explain why a hormone-binding protein can affect a distant membrane target.

Controls that make rescue experiments meaningful

Compare wild type with untreated mutant, solvent-treated mutant and mutant receiving several hormone concentrations. Use a relevant physiological endpoint, and verify that the wild type responds in the same experimental conditions. A high hormone dose can produce nonspecific growth inhibition or overcome a partial transport defect; an apparent rescue at only one extreme dose is therefore weaker evidence than a normal dose-response curve. Genetic complementation with a functional copy under an appropriate promoter is an independent test. To establish that a kinase lies downstream of a receptor, test kinase activation after receptor perturbation and use a downstream activated allele where experimentally appropriate. These tests are conceptually distinct from measuring transcript abundance.

4. Storage, breakdown and free hormone homeostasis

The free concentration of ABA at any moment is the net result of processes that supply and remove it. A tissue can contain abundant ABA-related molecules while only a portion of them is immediately available to bind ABA receptors. One reversible mechanism is the formation of ABA glucose ester, often abbreviated ABA-GE. UDP-dependent glucosyltransferases catalyse conjugation of glucose to ABA, reducing the pool of free active hormone. ABA-GE can accumulate in cellular compartments and, under suitable conditions, beta-glucosidases can hydrolyse the conjugate to release free ABA. In Arabidopsis, examples include BG1 associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and BG2 associated with the vacuole.

A conjugate reservoir allows a tissue to mobilize ABA without waiting for every reaction in the carotenoid-cleavage pathway. This is advantageous when a rapid adjustment of water balance is required. Nevertheless, conjugate hydrolysis does not imply that biosynthesis becomes unnecessary. A reservoir can be depleted and must be replenished, and the relative contributions of hydrolysis and de novo synthesis vary between tissues and conditions. Measuring only the free hormone does not reveal whether it was newly made or recovered from a conjugate. Measuring both free ABA and ABA-GE over time offers more information, especially when combined with enzyme mutants or tracer experiments.

Oxidative breakdown is a distinct process. CYP707A enzymes are cytochrome P450 ABA eight-prime hydroxylases. Their product, eight-prime hydroxy-ABA, can rearrange to phaseic acid, which can subsequently be reduced to dihydrophaseic acid. These conversions generally lower the canonical ABA response, although phaseic acid can have signaling activity through some receptors. This qualification matters because catabolism is often taught as a switch from an active to a completely inert substance. It is more precise to describe oxidative turnover as a change in the abundance and signaling properties of the original hormone. Reversible ester hydrolysis and oxidative turnover should not be confused: the latter does not serve as the ordinary ABA-GE storage cycle.

A simple mass balance clarifies how apparently contradictory changes can arise. Let A represent the amount of free ABA in a specified tissue, in nanomoles. Let S be synthesis, H hydrolytic release, I import, C conjugation, D oxidative removal and E export, all measured in nanomoles per hour. The rate of accumulation is dA/dt = S + H + I − C − D − E. If the sum of the supply terms equals the sum of the removal terms, the measured amount is constant even though ABA molecules continually enter and leave the pool. A constant concentration therefore cannot establish that ABA metabolism is inactive. Concentration additionally depends on tissue volume, which can change during dehydration.

Consider an illustrative sample with ten nanomoles of free ABA. During a short interval the tissue produces six nanomoles, releases two from conjugate and imports one, while conjugating two, oxidizing three and exporting one. Assuming the amounts apply to the same tissue and interval, the free pool increases by three nanomoles to thirteen. An alternative sample that produces only three nanomoles but has less oxidative removal could finish with the same amount. The endpoint measurement alone cannot distinguish the two histories. This is why time courses and independent estimates of production and degradation are useful in interpreting hormonal regulation.

Reversible storage versus oxidative removal

ABA-glucose ester (ABA-GE) has lower direct signaling activity than free ABA and can be a reservoir for rapid regeneration of the active compound. UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases form conjugates, and beta-glucosidases including Arabidopsis BG1 and BG2 release ABA from ABA-GE in different compartments, commonly associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and vacuole respectively. A tissue can increase free ABA by activating hydrolysis before a large increase in de novo synthesis occurs. Conversely, a rise in ABA-GE does not necessarily mean that active ABA has increased. Measure free and conjugated fractions separately rather than interpreting an assay of total hydrolysable ABA as though it were an assay of receptor ligand.

Oxidation and the shape of an ABA pulse

CYP707A enzymes catalyze ABA 8-prime hydroxylation. The hydroxylated product can rearrange to phaseic acid, which can be further reduced to dihydrophaseic acid. Phaseic acid is not absolutely inert for all receptors, so a simplified pathway diagram represents the dominant reduction in active ABA rather than a rule that every product is biologically silent. During recovery from water deficit, renewed catabolism can shorten an ABA pulse and permit stomata to reopen. In germinating seeds, increased CYP707A-dependent turnover can contribute to dormancy release. A phenotype of high ABA and delayed germination in a catabolism-defective line is consistent with prolonged inhibition, but other developmental differences should also be controlled.

A simple kinetic model

If free ABA is produced at constant rate S and removed by a first-order process with rate constant k, the pool A obeys dA/dt = S − kA. At steady state A = S/k. For S = 6 nmol per hour and k = 0.3 per hour, the steady pool is 20 nmol. Doubling synthesis with unchanged k produces a predicted 40 nmol steady pool; halving k with unchanged synthesis also gives 40 nmol. Thus a single concentration cannot distinguish the two mechanisms. When synthesis is switched off, the idealized half-life is ln(2)/k, approximately 2.31 hours for k = 0.3 per hour. Real ABA systems have conjugation, compartmentation and feedback, so this model is useful for comparisons, not an exact description of all tissues.

Pulse–chase reasoning

In a hypothetical tracer experiment, label newly synthesized ABA during a short pulse and then follow labeled free ABA, conjugate and oxidative products while unlabeled precursor is supplied. A rapid appearance of label in ABA-GE supports conjugation; a later decline in labeled ABA-GE accompanied by labeled free ABA supports regeneration if other labeled sources are excluded. Appearance of phaseic acid supports oxidative metabolism. Comparing total pools without tracking label cannot distinguish pre-existing hormone from recently synthesized hormone. Recoveries and isotope dilution must be considered before comparing fluxes, and sample handling must preserve the chemical forms being measured.

5. Transport links production sites to responding cells

A hormone can influence a neighbouring cell, the same cell that produces it or a remote tissue. ABA moves between cells and can travel in vascular tissues, including xylem and phloem. Roots may contribute ABA to transpiration-associated xylem transport, but ABA synthesized in leaves is also important in many drought responses. Neither the observation of ABA in xylem sap nor the expression of an ABA biosynthetic enzyme in roots establishes that all drought-responsive ABA in a leaf originated in the root. Vascular cells, mesophyll and guard cells can contribute to local regulation according to the experimental conditions.

ABA is a weak acid. The uncharged protonated form is more able to partition into membranes than the charged anion. As pH changes, the proportions of these forms change. The Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship gives the ratio of anionic to protonated ABA as 10 raised to the power of pH minus pKa. With an illustrative pKa of 4.7, approximately one seventh of free ABA is protonated at pH 5.5, but less than one hundredth is protonated at pH 7.0. These estimates assume aqueous acid-base equilibrium and do not directly measure transmembrane permeability. A membrane transport protein can facilitate movement even when most hormone is ionized.

The weak-acid behavior of ABA helps explain why the pH of the extracellular space can affect hormone distribution, but passive partitioning is not the only mechanism. In Arabidopsis, ABCG25 is an example of an ABA exporter and ABCG40 an example of an ABA importer. NPF4.6, formerly called AIT1, is another reported ABA uptake transporter. An exporter may help release ABA from a producing cell, and an importer may enhance access to cytosolic receptors in a responding cell. The families differ in their molecular mechanisms; the mere fact that a transporter belongs to an ABC or NPF family does not prove that every member transports ABA.

The direction of an individual transporter does not establish the direction of whole-organ flow. A tissue contains many cell types and possible routes, and the overall distribution depends on vascular movement, local synthesis, membrane transport and metabolic turnover. In a transport-defective mutant, a high ABA concentration in a producing tissue can coexist with a low concentration in a responding tissue. Treating the entire plant as a single homogeneous compartment would conceal this distinction. When designing an experiment, hormone measurements at the scale of whole leaves should therefore be complemented, where appropriate, by tissue-specific expression information or an assay of the target cell response.

Hydraulic communication is not identical to chemical transport

Roots in drying soil can alter both the hydraulic state of the shoot and the concentration of transported signals. ABA can be produced in roots, leaves and specialized cells. Xylem delivery from roots is one route of long-distance communication, but leaf synthesis can be a major source of ABA during drought. A root-to-shoot graft experiment may reveal whether root genotype influences closure, yet the result also depends on water flow, shoot genotype and the environment. A signal that arrives rapidly following a fall in water potential need not be a transported ABA molecule; hydraulic changes can trigger local ABA synthesis or modify stomatal behavior. This distinction prevents the incorrect claim that all drought-induced leaf ABA originates in roots.

Concentration and flux are different quantities

Xylem hormone delivery depends on both concentration and volumetric flow. If the sap contains 80 nmol per liter and flow is 0.2 milliliters per hour, delivery is 0.016 nmol per hour. If drought doubles concentration to 160 nmol per liter but decreases flow to 0.05 milliliters per hour, delivery is 0.008 nmol per hour. The concentration has doubled while the delivered amount per hour has halved. Such an experiment requires representative sap sampling; collection under pressure may differ from natural transpiration. The example illustrates why high sap ABA concentration alone cannot establish a higher flux of ABA to guard cells.

Weak acid partitioning

For a monoprotic weak acid with pKa near 4.7, the approximate fraction in the protonated form is 1/(1+10^(pH−pKa)). At apoplastic pH 5.7 the fraction is about 9.1 percent, whereas at pH 6.7 it is about 1 percent. Protonated ABA can cross a lipid membrane more readily than the charged form under simplified passive diffusion assumptions. Alkalinization may therefore alter distribution among cells and increase the importance of transporter-mediated entry. It is not a universal prediction that alkaline sap always reduces the whole-leaf response: retention in the apoplast, transporters and different target cells complicate the tissue-level result.

Embryo and endosperm as communicating tissues

A seed contains multiple genetically and physiologically distinct compartments. ABA made or released in the endosperm can influence the embryo, and transporter-dependent movement contributes to the distribution of inhibitory signal. In Arabidopsis, ABCG31 and ABCG25 have been implicated in endosperm ABA export, while ABCG30 and ABCG40 contribute to embryo uptake. A genotype affecting endosperm export may show reduced delivery to a competent embryo even if seed ABA production is normal. Tissue-specific expression, isolated embryo assays and measurements of ABA in separated compartments help distinguish these possibilities. The specific transporter combinations should be learned as examples rather than imposed on every seed species.

6. The receptor–phosphatase–kinase switch

The core ABA signaling module contains three groups of proteins. PYR1, PYL and RCAR are related soluble ABA receptors. Clade A PP2Cs, including ABI1 and ABI2, are protein phosphatases that normally oppose signaling. Subclass III SnRK2 proteins are kinases that promote many ABA responses. A kinase transfers a phosphate group to a target protein, commonly using ATP, while a phosphatase removes phosphate groups. Phosphorylation can change enzyme activity, binding or cellular behavior. The particular targets of each kinase determine the final response.

At low ABA, PP2C-mediated inhibition prevents substantial activation of the ABA-responsive SnRK2 pathway. PP2C can dephosphorylate the kinase activation loop and directly restrain kinase activity. When ABA enters the receptor binding pocket, conformational changes allow the hormone-bound receptor to inhibit a compatible PP2C. The phosphatase is thereby less able to restrain SnRK2. Kinase activity increases through appropriate phosphorylation and regulatory events, and the activated SnRK2 phosphorylates downstream targets. The apparent paradox that inhibiting a protein activates a pathway is resolved by recognizing that the inhibited protein is itself an inhibitor. Two negative relationships yield a positive overall effect.

A receptor is not synonymous with a kinase. PYR/PYL proteins do not act as membrane receptor kinases like BRI1 in brassinosteroid signaling. Instead, ABA perception alters the interaction between soluble receptor and phosphatase. The resulting kinase activity belongs to SnRK2. Although some ABA receptor family members can interact with selected PP2Cs at low ABA in particular assays, the ligand-dependent inhibition of negative regulators is the central teaching model. It should not be interpreted as an identical quantitative response for every receptor–phosphatase combination.

The pathway predicts a series of genetic outcomes. Loss of one receptor can be partially masked by other family members, while loss of several relevant receptors may produce clear insensitivity. A PP2C allele that retains inhibitory activity but resists receptor inhibition can keep SnRK2 repressed even when ABA increases. The classical dominant abi1-1 allele exemplifies this logic; it must be distinguished from an ordinary loss-of-function allele in a negative regulator. Conversely, reducing relevant PP2C activity can enhance ABA responsiveness. Loss of downstream SnRK2 activity weakens the response even when receptors bind hormone normally. These predictions are valuable because they locate defects according to pathway position, not simply the direction of the visible phenotype.

Two complementary studies by Park and colleagues and Ma and colleagues in 2009 identified the receptor families through chemical genetic and protein interaction approaches. A chemical genetics experiment uses a compound whose physiological activity allows the investigator to find mutants altered in response to it. Biochemical tests then ask whether the candidate receptor recognizes the ligand and whether its interaction with a phosphatase changes enzymatic activity. Reconstitution with defined components can test whether the proposed proteins are sufficient for a particular biochemical step. A molecular interaction by itself, however, is not evidence that the same interaction accounts for every whole-plant response. Genetic and physiological assays connect the molecular mechanism to biological function.

A molecular double negative

The central ABA switch has two inhibitory relationships. In the low-ABA state, a clade A PP2C interacts with an ABA-responsive SnRK2 kinase and keeps it relatively inactive by dephosphorylation and related inhibitory interactions. When ABA binds a compatible PYR/PYL receptor, the receptor inhibits the phosphatase. The kinase can then accumulate activating phosphorylation and act on substrates. Because a negative regulator is itself inhibited, the net response to ABA is positive activation of kinase-dependent signaling. This is different from a receptor that directly phosphorylates its own tail. ATP is used by the kinase to transfer phosphate to its substrates; ABA binding supplies regulatory information, not the phosphate group or the chemical energy.

The structural basis of receptor specificity

PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors belong to a family of soluble START-domain-related proteins. Hormone binding changes loops surrounding the ligand pocket, frequently described as gate and latch regions. The compatible PP2C docks at this altered surface and its catalytic activity toward substrates is blocked. Different receptors differ in oligomeric state, basal activity and interaction preferences. It is therefore safer to present the classic ligand-dependent module as a core framework and recognize exceptions than to say every receptor behaves identically in the absence of ABA. A mutation in the hormone-binding pocket and a mutation at the receptor–PP2C interface could both weaken signaling, but binding and enzyme assays could distinguish their mechanisms.

Genetic interpretation of ABI1 and ABI2

The classic dominant abi1-1 and abi2-1 ABA-insensitive alleles illustrate how a negative regulator can produce hormone resistance. Certain mutant phosphatase proteins fail to be appropriately inhibited by ABA-bound receptors. The resulting inhibition of downstream kinase signaling can persist even in the presence of high ABA. This should not be confused with a null mutation deleting a negative regulator. Loss of a PP2C may instead increase sensitivity, especially when redundant phosphatases are also impaired. Predicting the phenotype requires knowing whether the mutation removes function, increases effective inhibition or changes the ability to respond to upstream regulation.

Two major downstream output classes

In guard cells OST1, also called SnRK2.6, is a prominent component of rapid stomatal control. In vegetative transcriptional responses, ABA-responsive SnRK2s phosphorylate AREB/ABF family bZIP factors. These factors bind ABA-responsive elements in promoters and influence transcription. ABI5 is another bZIP regulator especially important in seed and early seedling contexts. ABI3, in contrast, belongs to the B3-domain transcription factor family and participates in seed maturation. The similar ABI prefixes arose from genetic phenotype nomenclature, not identical molecular structures. A question listing ABI1, ABI3 and ABI5 is therefore testing whether the student distinguishes phosphatase, developmental transcription factor and ABA-responsive bZIP factor.

Feedback and response timing

ABA can induce expression of some PP2C genes as well as stress-response genes. Increasing the amount of a negative regulator during stimulation may seem counterintuitive, but it creates a potential brake that limits the magnitude or duration of a response and supports reset when hormone concentration falls. The immediate response depends on existing receptors, phosphatases and kinases; a later response also reflects altered protein abundance. A time course that measures kinase phosphorylation at five minutes and transcript levels at two hours is more informative than either measurement alone. Feedback is a plausible explanation for adaptation in an ABA response, but direct perturbation of the proposed feedback component is required to establish causality.

7. From kinase activation to stomatal closure

A stoma is a pore in the epidermis bordered by two specialized guard cells. In many dicots the cells have a kidney-like outline; grasses possess a different guard-cell geometry and associated subsidiary cells. Changes in turgor produce differential deformation of cell walls and alter the aperture. When guard cells accumulate osmotically active solutes, water tends to enter and turgor rises. When sufficient solutes are released, water tends to leave and turgor falls. These effects depend on the surrounding water potential and mechanical properties of the stomatal complex, not on osmotic concentration alone.

ABA signaling in guard cells employs SnRK2.6, also known as OST1, as a major kinase. Activation of anion channels such as SLAC1 increases the release of negatively charged solutes. The exit of negative charge tends to depolarize the membrane, making the interior less negative relative to the extracellular solution. This favors opening of outward-rectifying potassium channels, including GORK under appropriate conditions. At the same time, ABA suppresses pathways that normally support solute accumulation. Loss of potassium and counterions reduces the osmotic driving force for water retention. The resulting water efflux lowers turgor and promotes closure.

Ion movements are coordinated rather than independent. Electrical potential changes influence channel opening, while concentration gradients and membrane potential jointly determine the electrochemical driving force. It would be incorrect to state that depolarization pushes all positively charged ions out irrespective of the gradients. Potassium efflux is favored when the membrane voltage is sufficiently positive relative to the equilibrium potential for potassium and when an appropriate outward pathway is available. Likewise, anion channel activation does not create anion molecules; it allows existing cellular anions to redistribute. Maintenance of these gradients before the closure response depends ultimately on cellular metabolism and membrane transport.

Calcium and reactive oxygen species participate in ABA signaling in guard cells. Activation of NADPH oxidases can contribute to reactive oxygen production, which can interact with calcium-permeable pathways and calcium-dependent kinases. Calcium-dependent signaling can reinforce channel regulation. These interconnected routes do not replace the central receptor–phosphatase–SnRK2 mechanism, and not every response requires an identical calcium transient. Experimental measurements of ion fluxes, calcium and aperture should be related in time rather than assumed to be interchangeable measures of ABA signaling.

Consider the effects of loss of SLAC1 compared with loss of OST1. The channel mutant may have impaired closure because one important anion efflux pathway is unavailable, despite retaining kinase activation and other downstream responses. The kinase mutant disrupts an upstream regulatory component and can therefore affect several outputs. Providing extra ABA cannot restore the function of a completely absent channel or kinase. However, partial closure can persist through other channels and regulatory pathways, so neither genotype should be described as necessarily eliminating every stomatal response. The appropriate inference is that the affected protein is necessary for the normal magnitude or timing of the response under the tested conditions.

Starting from the opening mechanism

In an illuminated, sufficiently hydrated leaf, guard-cell plasma-membrane proton pumps can promote membrane hyperpolarization and potassium uptake. Accumulation of osmolytes, including inorganic ions and metabolites, makes guard-cell solute potential more negative and favors water entry. Turgor and specialized cell wall mechanics allow the pore to open. ABA-induced closure reverses or opposes several of these processes rather than merely turning off photosynthesis. It stimulates net anion release and restricts ion uptake, changes voltage-dependent potassium flux and promotes loss of osmotic water retention. The mechanical relationship between turgor and aperture also depends on surrounding epidermal cells and stomatal type; a simplified pair of kidney-shaped guard cells represents a common model, not every species.

Anion channels and membrane voltage

SLAC1 is a slow anion channel, while ALMT12/QUAC1 contributes a rapid anion current. Activation of anion efflux removes negative charge from the cell, making the membrane potential less negative in the usual guard-cell context. Depolarization favors opening of outward-rectifying potassium channels such as GORK. Net loss of K+ and counterions reduces the intracellular solute pool. In parallel, ABA signaling can inhibit inward potassium channels and oppose proton-pump-driven opening. The coordinated change in conductances is essential: a single ion channel does not sustain large uncompensated charge loss indefinitely. Electrophysiological current direction and physical ion movement are different conventions; always identify the ion and its movement before inferring a voltage change.

Calcium, ROS and convergent kinase pathways

ABA can promote production of reactive oxygen species through NADPH oxidases, including RBOH proteins. ROS and changes in cytosolic calcium contribute to signaling networks that recruit calcium-dependent protein kinases. Several kinases can converge on anion channels, providing overlapping regulation and context-dependent responses. This does not mean that every ABA response requires an identical calcium transient. OST1-dependent and calcium-dependent mechanisms can interact; the relative contribution depends on conditions and cell state. Reactive oxygen species act as signaling molecules in this context, but excessive accumulation can damage cells. A fluorescent ROS probe and an ion-channel current assay measure different steps, so a correlation between them does not alone demonstrate direct activation.

A water-potential calculation

Consider a simplified guard cell whose solute potential is −1.2 MPa and pressure potential is +0.8 MPa; neglect gravity and matric terms. Its water potential is −0.4 MPa. Suppose solute loss makes solute potential −0.7 MPa before pressure adjusts; the initial water potential becomes +0.1 MPa. If the surroundings remain at −0.4 MPa, water initially moves out because it moves down a water-potential gradient. Pressure then decreases until a new mechanical and water-potential equilibrium is reached. The calculated initial +0.1 MPa is a transient idealized state, not a prediction of a stable positive water potential in a real leaf. The example shows how solute loss can lead to water loss and lower turgor.

Separating stomatal conductance from aperture

A microscopy experiment may quantify aperture width for individual stomata. Gas exchange instruments estimate conductance of an entire leaf, which also depends on stomatal density, geometry, distribution and measurement conditions. An ABA-treated leaf with narrower pores generally has lower stomatal conductance, but different leaves with the same average aperture can have different conductance if their stomatal densities differ. Stomatal conductance to water vapor is also distinct from net photosynthetic carbon assimilation. A useful drought study measures both gas exchange variables, leaf water status and growth or survival rather than claiming that a single aperture measurement predicts performance.

8. Seed maturation, dormancy and gibberellin antagonism

Seed maturation prepares an embryo for survival between generations. Developing seeds accumulate reserves, acquire tolerance of drying in orthodox species and establish physiological conditions that restrain germination. ABA participates in coordinating these events. Seed-specific transcriptional regulators such as ABI3, ABI4 and ABI5 contribute to the response, with different roles and regulatory connections. ABA-responsive expression of protective proteins, including many late embryogenesis abundant proteins, helps prepare cells for water loss. Seed maturation and dormancy are related but not identical outcomes: a mature seed may possess desiccation tolerance and still germinate promptly under suitable conditions if its dormancy is weak.

Primary dormancy develops before dispersal, while secondary dormancy may arise in a previously nondormant seed under unsuitable environmental conditions. A viable dormant seed differs from a dead seed; lack of radicle emergence alone cannot distinguish them. It also differs from a nondormant seed that lacks sufficient water, oxygen or a suitable temperature. A dormancy assay must establish conditions that support germination in an appropriate nondormant control. Testing germination over time rather than at a single endpoint can separate a delayed response from a stable failure to germinate.

Gibberellins generally promote germination in many model systems by facilitating embryo growth and weakening tissues that oppose radicle emergence. ABA can restrain these events and maintain protective developmental programs. The balance between the pathways is useful, but a simple numerical ABA-to-GA ratio cannot explain all seeds because sensitivity, spatial distribution and developmental state also vary. A seed with a defective ABA receptor can germinate at a high hormone concentration; a seed unable to perceive GA may remain poorly germinating even after ABA declines. A complete interpretation therefore combines hormone metabolism with downstream signaling.

During imbibition of many nondormant seeds, oxidative ABA removal contributes to decreasing the inhibitory signal. CYP707A2 is an important example of a catabolic enzyme associated with early imbibition in Arabidopsis. After-ripening, light and temperature can change dormancy through effects on hormone metabolism and sensitivity. The response varies among species and seed states, and it is not accurate to prescribe one sequence of treatment that releases all dormant seeds. In cereals, the GA-stimulated production of hydrolytic enzymes in aleurone tissue provides a classical illustration of reserve mobilization; ABA can antagonize this response. This is a tissue-specific mechanism, not a universal description of every seed.

The seed also provides a striking example of communication between tissues. The embryo and the surrounding endosperm can have different hormonal roles. In Arabidopsis, coordinated ABCG-mediated export from endosperm and import into embryo has been demonstrated, allowing ABA produced in one tissue to influence embryo growth. The transport route is relevant because measuring an average concentration from the entire seed does not identify the hormone exposure of the embryo. A tissue-specific genetic perturbation and a germination assay can be more informative than a bulk hormone measurement alone.

Maturation versus germination

ABA participates in acquisition of desiccation tolerance, storage reserve programmes and establishment of primary dormancy during seed development. These outcomes overlap but are not identical: some seeds tolerate drying yet have weak dormancy, and germination requires appropriate hydration and environmental conditions even after dormancy has been released. In Arabidopsis, developmental regulators including ABI3, FUS3 and LEC factors contribute to maturation programmes and interact with hormone signaling. After dispersal and imbibition, ABA metabolism and sensitivity change as environmental cues are integrated. Distinguish the maternal environment during seed formation from the conditions experienced by the mature seed in a germination assay.

How GA and ABA oppose each other

GA binding to GID1 promotes DELLA removal, supporting growth and, in cereals, reserve mobilization. ABA signaling promotes dormancy and can restrain germination-associated processes. In barley aleurone, GA stimulates alpha-amylase production, while ABA can antagonize GA-dependent induction. This classic model concerns a cereal tissue responding to hormones and should not be confused with the site of reserve storage in all species. Arabidopsis has a different seed organization; its embryo growth and endosperm weakening are key components of germination. Both examples demonstrate hormonal interaction rather than a single universal ABA-to-GA concentration threshold.

Light, temperature, nitrate and after-ripening

Environmental cues affect dormancy through regulation of hormone synthesis, catabolism and responsiveness. For example, phytochrome-dependent light signals can alter the balance of ABA and GA pathways, while nitrate can promote germination in some contexts partly through ABA catabolism. Temperature experienced during seed maturation and during imbibition can have different consequences. Dry after-ripening can reduce physiological dormancy even before the seed takes up water, with altered hormone responses becoming evident during imbibition. None of these cues guarantees germination across every species, genotype or dormancy class. A factorial design varying light and nitrate can reveal an interaction if their joint effect differs from that expected from their separate effects.

DOG1 and sensitivity rather than concentration alone

DELAY OF GERMINATION1 (DOG1) is an important determinant of dormancy in Arabidopsis. It participates in dormancy control in conjunction with ABA-related processes, including interactions involving specific PP2C proteins. DOG1 is not itself ABA, and dormancy cannot be reduced to the amount of ABA alone. Seeds with similar ABA concentration may differ in dormancy because of downstream regulators or developmental history. A germination experiment comparing freshly harvested and after-ripened seeds should therefore include ABA dose-response curves rather than only chemical measurement. A change in sensitivity can shift the response threshold without changing the concentration measured at the start of the assay.

Germination data and denominators

Suppose 100 seeds are sown per treatment. At day four, wild type has 80 germinated seeds and an ABA-treated group has 25. Germination percentages are 80 and 25; the absolute difference is 55 percentage points. Relative inhibition compared with the untreated group is (80−25)/80 = 68.75 percent. A day-four count does not distinguish permanent prevention from delayed germination. Follow the time course, test viability of remaining seeds and, where suitable, transfer them to hormone-free medium. Replicate dishes rather than treating seeds from a single dish as wholly independent environmental replicates. These distinctions matter when interpreting examination questions about percentages and rates.

9. Rapid responses, transcriptional responses and drought trade-offs

Stomatal movement can begin without the production of a new complement of channel proteins. Pre-existing signaling proteins are modified and ion transport changes, providing a response on the scale of minutes under appropriate conditions. More persistent acclimation often requires altered gene expression. ABA-activated SnRK2 proteins can phosphorylate AREB/ABF transcription factors belonging to the bZIP family. These factors recognize ABA-responsive regulatory sequences in suitable promoters and influence the expression of genes involved in dehydration responses. The two modes of action are not mutually exclusive: a plant can close stomata rapidly while starting a slower change in cellular protection.

A transcriptional response is not synonymous with an increase in hormone concentration. A transcription factor may be present but inactive until phosphorylated, and a promoter can require other regulatory factors in addition to an ABA-responsive element. Conversely, an increase in a particular transcript may be induced by ABA-independent stress pathways. To distinguish ABA-dependent and ABA-independent regulation, one can compare a wild-type plant with a signaling-deficient mutant exposed to the same water deficit. If induction is reduced in the mutant, ABA signaling contributes to the response; if induction persists, another pathway may be sufficient or may act in parallel. Neither result alone proves that ABA is the only upstream signal.

Reduced stomatal conductance usually limits transpirational water loss, but it also restricts diffusion of carbon dioxide into the leaf. A plant may conserve water at the cost of instantaneous assimilation and growth. The significance of this trade-off depends on drought duration, atmospheric humidity, root access to water, temperature and recovery conditions. Under a short deficit, transient closure may preserve tissue hydration until conditions improve. Prolonged closure can impose carbon limitation. Selecting plants solely for low stomatal conductance without measuring photosynthesis or yield therefore cannot establish agronomic superiority.

Under some moderate water deficits, ABA helps maintain primary root extension while restricting shoot expansion. The direction of this response depends on tissue, hormone concentration, stress severity and developmental state. Maintaining access to deeper water can be beneficial, whereas uncontrolled growth of large leaf surfaces may increase water demand. Under severe stress even roots can stop elongating. The important concept is allocation and context rather than the idea that ABA always inhibits or always promotes growth. ABA accumulation and signaling eventually need to decline or be adjusted as water conditions recover, so the mechanisms of catabolism and sensitivity are as physiologically important as those that initiate the signal.

Early and late responses use overlapping pathways

Channel regulation can occur rapidly because receptors, phosphatases, kinases and channels already exist in the cell. A slower response involves phosphorylation of transcription factors and accumulation of transcripts and proteins. ABA-responsive elements in promoters help recruit bZIP regulators such as ABFs. Products of induced genes can participate in osmotic adjustment, protection of macromolecules and maintenance of cellular function under stress. Late embryogenesis abundant proteins and dehydrins are familiar examples of protective proteins, but their expression alone is not proof of whole-plant drought tolerance. Rapid and slow responses can occur together and may share early signaling components.

Water use efficiency and carbon cost

Closing stomata decreases loss of water vapor but also restricts entry of carbon dioxide. Intrinsic water-use efficiency is often represented by assimilation divided by stomatal conductance to water vapor; whole-plant water-use efficiency may instead be biomass produced per unit water consumed. These are distinct metrics. If assimilation drops from 15 to 10 micromol per square meter per second and conductance drops from 0.30 to 0.10 mol per square meter per second, the numerical ratio rises from 50 to 100 micromol per mol. Yet assimilation has fallen by one third. A plant may conserve water more efficiently at a given moment while growing more slowly. Assess both short-term leaf measurements and longer-term biomass or yield under comparable conditions.

Drought avoidance and tolerance

Drought avoidance refers broadly to mechanisms that maintain favorable tissue water status, including stomatal restriction, while dehydration tolerance concerns the ability to function or survive at low tissue water content. ABA can contribute to both, through water conservation and gene regulation. In a pot experiment, a plant with low transpiration may deplete soil water more slowly than a comparator. If the plants are compared after the same number of days without watering, they may have experienced different soil moisture histories. To distinguish water-saving from cellular tolerance, compare plants at matched water status as well as during a realistic drying trajectory.

Growth and hormonal interactions

ABA is often described as antagonizing growth-promoting hormones under stress, but the outcome depends on tissue, timing and water status. Moderate ABA signaling can help maintain root elongation in some water-limited conditions while shoot expansion decreases. Ethylene and ABA can interact in root and seed responses; the signs of these interactions are context dependent. The fact that ABA closes stomata does not imply that its effects on every growth process are negative. In examination problems, identify the particular organ, environmental condition and measured phenotype before applying a general hormonal classification.

10. Experimental reasoning and quantitative interpretation

Suppose wild-type Arabidopsis, an ABA-deficient biosynthetic mutant and an ABA-insensitive receptor mutant are grown under matched conditions. A seed germination assay uses vehicle-treated and ABA-treated groups for each genotype. The biosynthetic mutant may germinate more readily than wild type without treatment, but an effective external dose can restore inhibition if uptake and signaling are intact. The receptor mutant may also germinate readily without treatment yet show reduced inhibition after ABA is supplied. This pattern distinguishes reduced hormone availability from a defect in response. Because families of receptors are redundant, the strength of the latter result depends on which and how many receptor genes are affected.

The same design can be applied to stomatal aperture measurements, but the experimental units must be defined properly. Many pores from a single leaf are not independent biological replicates of a genotype. Several leaves from independently grown plants and suitable randomization help reduce the risk of pseudoreplication. The initial aperture, time after treatment, temperature, light, solution composition and relative humidity can all influence the response. Measuring each group at matched intervals prevents differences in closure kinetics from being confused with differences in final aperture.

For an illustrative ABA treatment, consider a mean aperture of six micrometres before treatment and two micrometres after treatment. Relative closure, defined here as the change divided by the initial aperture, is (6 − 2)/6 = 0.667, or 66.7 percent. A mutant whose aperture changes from four to two micrometres has the same final value but only 50 percent relative closure. The starting values differ, so neither final aperture nor percentage alone proves that one genotype has a more sensitive signaling system. Full dose-response curves and time courses help distinguish altered baseline, maximal response and response sensitivity.

A response curve may shift horizontally without changing its maximum, suggesting altered sensitivity rather than necessarily altered maximal capacity. A lower plateau may instead reflect limited capacity of a downstream process, provided the test range truly reaches saturation. These are interpretations to be tested, not guaranteed identities. A plant that transports applied ABA poorly could produce an apparently right-shifted external dose-response curve even though its intracellular receptors function normally. Measuring internal hormone levels and bypassing the delivery step where feasible can help distinguish transport from perception.

Hormone concentration is often reported per gram of fresh mass or dry mass. If a leaf loses water, its fresh mass decreases and the value expressed per gram fresh mass may rise even if the total amount of ABA remains unchanged. A valid analysis should state the normalization basis and, where relevant, consider water content. The same principle applies to comparisons of drought-treated and well-watered tissues with different cell sizes. Accurate measurements alone are insufficient if the denominator is interpreted incorrectly.

Factorial interpretation of a signaling experiment

Imagine four guard-cell preparations: wild type without ABA has a mean aperture of 5 micrometers; wild type with ABA has 2; a mutant without ABA has 5; mutant with ABA has 4.5. The hormone effect in wild type is −3 micrometers and in the mutant −0.5. The difference of these effects is 2.5 micrometers, consistent with reduced response in the mutant. It is more informative than comparing only the treated groups because baseline aperture is explicitly considered. Replicate biological samples and estimate variability; the numbers alone cannot establish statistical significance. To locate the lesion, measure endogenous hormone, kinase activity and downstream channel function or complement the mutant.

Interpreting a phosphorylation time course

Suppose ABA exposure gives a peak of OST1 activity at five minutes, channel current at ten minutes, and transcript induction after sixty minutes. The temporal order is consistent with rapid kinase activation preceding downstream ion flux and later transcription. It does not prove that every transcript is directly controlled by OST1 or that the observed channel current is exclusively due to SLAC1. Specific channel mutants and phosphorylation-site mutants help test direct linkage. A transcription inhibitor may alter a later response while leaving the early response relatively intact, but toxicity controls are essential. Temporal sequence is necessary for a proposed causal chain, not sufficient evidence of causation.

A dilution and normalization problem

A hydrated leaf of fresh mass 200 mg contains 10 nmol ABA, giving 50 nmol per gram fresh mass. After water loss the same leaf has fresh mass 125 mg and still contains 10 nmol. The concentration becomes 80 nmol per gram fresh mass, an apparent rise of 60 percent without any net increase in ABA amount. If dry mass remains stable, dry-mass-normalized values may help identify the concentration artifact, although changes in dry matter and sampling must also be considered. A drought time course should report the normalization basis and, where appropriate, hormone content per organ as well as relative water content.

Epistasis and biochemical bypass

Consider a receptor-impaired line, an inhibitory PP2C allele and an engineered kinase that remains active despite phosphatase inhibition. If the active kinase restores a particular downstream reporter in a receptor-impaired background, the observation is consistent with the kinase functioning downstream of receptor signaling. It does not show that the kinase restores every ABA phenotype, because the response may involve other proteins and tissue-specific processes. A constitutively active protein may also have unintended effects. Interpret epistasis in the context of a defined output and specific allele, and complement genetic evidence with direct biochemical measurements.

Choosing a discriminating test

An ABA-insensitive seed can be caused by reduced uptake, defective ligand recognition, a phosphatase that remains inhibitory, loss of kinase function or failure of a downstream transcriptional regulator. A single external ABA dose does not distinguish these cases. Measure intracellular ABA after treatment, assay receptor-dependent PP2C inhibition in vitro, examine kinase phosphorylation and test a downstream reporter. Each test interrogates a separate level of the causal chain. If internal ABA and kinase activation are normal but the reporter is defective, a downstream transcriptional lesion is more plausible than a pure biosynthetic defect. The lesson is to design an experiment that separates competing hypotheses rather than merely confirming the phenotype.

Common misconceptions

ABA is synthesized directly from a C15 sesquiterpene precursor in plants.
The standard higher-plant pathway cleaves C40 carotenoids to form C15 xanthoxin.
NCED oxidizes abscisic aldehyde.
NCED cleaves epoxycarotenoids; AAO3 oxidizes the aldehyde.
ABA2 and ABA3 are interchangeable enzymes.
ABA2 participates in xanthoxin conversion; ABA3 supplies the sulfurated molybdenum cofactor.
ABA-GE and phaseic acid are the same storage form.
ABA-GE can release ABA by hydrolysis; phaseic acid arises from oxidative turnover.
Constant ABA concentration means no hormone turnover.
Balanced synthesis and removal can maintain a constant pool.
All leaf ABA originates in roots.
Local leaf synthesis and transport both contribute.
ABCG25 and ABCG40 have identical transport directions.
ABCG25 is an exporter example; ABCG40 is an importer example.
PYL is an ABA-activated membrane kinase.
PYL is a soluble receptor that modulates PP2C activity.
PP2C is the principal positive ABA kinase.
PP2C is a phosphatase and negative regulator in the core pathway.
Every abi1 mutation is a simple loss of function.
The dominant abi1-1 allele resists normal receptor-mediated inhibition.
ABA closes stomata by increasing guard-cell potassium content.
Closure commonly involves net anion and potassium release and reduced turgor.
All non-germinating seeds are dormant.
Viability and suitable germination conditions must be established.
High ABA necessarily means strong signaling.
Receptor competence and hormone distribution can limit response.
ABA always inhibits growth of every organ.
Growth effects vary by tissue and water-stress context.
A drought-resistant genotype must have the lowest stomatal conductance.
Water conservation must be evaluated together with carbon assimilation, recovery and yield.

Original examination practice

1. Part B reasoning

Which enzyme directly produces xanthoxin from a suitable epoxycarotenoid?

Answer and explanation

NCED. The oxidative cleavage is distinct from the subsequent cytosolic steps.

2. Part B reasoning

Which protein is a negative regulator of the core ABA signaling pathway?

Answer and explanation

Clade A PP2C. It restrains ABA-responsive SnRK2 activity; the ABA–PYL complex inhibits compatible PP2Cs.

3. Part B reasoning

Which compound is a reversible conjugate reservoir for free ABA?

Answer and explanation

ABA glucose ester. Beta-glucosidase-mediated hydrolysis can regenerate ABA from this ester.

4. Part B reasoning

Which kinase is especially associated with rapid ABA-induced guard-cell responses?

Answer and explanation

OST1/SnRK2.6. OST1 phosphorylates targets including the SLAC1 anion channel.

5. Part B reasoning

What is the expected direct effect of sustained net guard-cell solute loss?

Answer and explanation

Reduced osmotic water retention and turgor. Under suitable external water conditions the cells lose water and the pore tends to close.

6. Part C reasoning

A mutant has normal epoxycarotenoid pools and makes ABA after receiving xanthoxin. Which step is most strongly implicated?

Answer and explanation

Carotenoid cleavage or its regulation. Downstream conversion capacity persists; the available evidence favors the step producing xanthoxin, although transport or compartmentation should be examined.

7. Part C reasoning

Mutant A and mutant B have weak seed dormancy. Applied ABA restores inhibition only in A. What hypothesis best explains the difference?

Answer and explanation

A is biosynthetically deficient and B has impaired ABA response. External hormone bypasses production but cannot generally bypass a downstream perception or signaling lesion. Uptake and dose controls are required.

8. Part C reasoning

A tissue synthesizes 5 nmol ABA and imports 2 nmol in an hour; it conjugates 3 nmol and catabolizes 4 nmol. All other fluxes are negligible. What happens to the free pool?

Answer and explanation

No net change. Inputs of 7 nmol balance removal of 7 nmol, demonstrating that unchanged pool size can coexist with turnover.

9. Part C reasoning

A mutant loses the guard-cell ABA importer, but an assay of isolated intracellular signaling proteins shows normal ABA perception. Why could external ABA require a larger dose?

Answer and explanation

Reduced access to intracellular receptors. Transport limits the effective intracellular concentration, so external dose sensitivity cannot be equated with receptor affinity.

An ABA-treated line activates SnRK2 normally but releases fewer anions and shows weak closure. Which class of lesion is most plausible?

A downstream channel or channel regulation defect. Normal kinase activity places the likely lesion after kinase activation. A SLAC1 defect is one hypothesis, not uniquely established.

C. Receptor-resistant PP2C or defective receptor interaction. The assay directly implicates regulation at the receptor–phosphatase step.

33. Part C reasoning

A kinase-active allele restores an ABRE reporter in a receptor-impaired line. What inference is justified?

  1. Kinase can act downstream for this output
  2. Receptor is unnecessary in all tissues
  3. ABA biosynthesis is restored
  4. All stomatal phenotypes are rescued
Answer and explanation

A. Kinase can act downstream for this output. Bypass supports pathway ordering for the measured reporter, not universal rescue.

34. Part C reasoning

Wild type germination is 80%, treated is 20%. What is relative inhibition versus control?

  1. 60%
  2. 20%
  3. 80%
  4. 75%
Answer and explanation

D. 75%. (80−20)/80=0.75, whereas the absolute difference is 60 percentage points.

35. Part C reasoning

A guard cell has solute potential −1.0 MPa and pressure potential +0.6 MPa. Ignoring other terms, its water potential is?

  1. +0.4 MPa
  2. −0.4 MPa
  3. −1.6 MPa
  4. +1.6 MPa
Answer and explanation

B. −0.4 MPa. Water potential is the sum of solute and pressure potentials.

36. Part C reasoning

A mutant lacks an outward K+ channel but shows normal ABA-induced anion current. Which result is plausible?

  1. NCED necessarily fails
  2. ABA cannot bind PYL
  3. Reduced K+ loss and incomplete closure
  4. GA is absent
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced K+ loss and incomplete closure. The channel lesion is downstream of anion current and may impair osmotic solute loss.

37. Part C reasoning

Two genotypes have identical seed ABA levels but different ABA dose-response curves. Which explanation is most directly supported?

  1. Different effective sensitivity or access
  2. Identical signaling competence
  3. Both are necessarily dead
  4. ABA concentration alone explains the result
Answer and explanation

A. Different effective sensitivity or access. Differences in receptor/signaling function or uptake can shift apparent sensitivity.

38. Part C reasoning

Assimilation falls from 12 to 8, conductance from 0.24 to 0.08. How does the intrinsic water-use efficiency ratio change?

  1. 50 to 33
  2. 100 to 50
  3. Remains 50
  4. 50 to 100
Answer and explanation

D. 50 to 100. 12/0.24=50 and 8/0.08=100; carbon assimilation nonetheless decreases.

39. Part C reasoning

A drought experiment compares genotypes only after seven days without water. Which confound is especially important?

  1. Identical soil water in all pots is guaranteed
  2. Different transpiration can produce different soil moisture histories
  3. ABA never affects transpiration
  4. Stomatal density is always identical
Answer and explanation

B. Different transpiration can produce different soil moisture histories. Water-saving plants can experience milder soil drying over the same elapsed time.

40. Part C reasoning

A pulse of isotope label appears first in free ABA then in ABA-GE. Which inference is strongest?

  1. ABA-GE is necessarily inactive in every assay
  2. ABA-GE can only arise by de novo carotenoid cleavage
  3. Conjugation of labeled ABA is supported
  4. CYP707A is proven absent
Answer and explanation

C. Conjugation of labeled ABA is supported. Time-resolved appearance in the conjugate supports transfer of label; confirm chemical identity.

41. Part C reasoning

Assertion: ABA-bound PYL activates SnRK2 indirectly. Reason: it inhibits a PP2C that suppresses SnRK2. Choose the correct relationship.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true reason false
  4. Assertion false reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true and reason explains assertion. The two inhibitory steps explain the net positive response.

42. Part C reasoning

Assertion: All high-ABA mutants are drought tolerant. Reason: ABA can reduce stomatal water loss. Choose the correct relationship.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true reason false
  4. Assertion false reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion false reason true. Water conservation is one component of drought performance, not a universal guarantee.

43. Part C reasoning

A line retains normal pigment but fails to form xanthoxin. Xanthoxin feeding restores ABA. Which lesion is favored?

  1. AAO3
  2. NCED or regulation of cleavage
  3. ABI5
  4. CYP707A
Answer and explanation

B. NCED or regulation of cleavage. Feeding bypasses the cleavage step; downstream enzymes remain competent.

44. Part C reasoning

An ABA-treated plant has normal kinase activation but weak ABRE reporter induction. Which further test is most informative?

  1. Only measure carotenoid color
  2. Only count stomata
  3. Examine ABF phosphorylation and promoter competence
  4. Add more light without controls
Answer and explanation

C. Examine ABF phosphorylation and promoter competence. These tests interrogate the downstream transcriptional branch.

45. Part C reasoning

Germination is delayed on ABA but resumes after transfer to hormone-free medium. What does this show?

  1. At least some seeds remained viable and inhibition was reversible
  2. ABA caused permanent death in all seeds
  3. The seeds must have defective ABA receptors
  4. All dormancy mechanisms are identical
Answer and explanation

A. At least some seeds remained viable and inhibition was reversible. Recovery distinguishes reversible inhibition from death, but does not establish the entire dormancy mechanism.
