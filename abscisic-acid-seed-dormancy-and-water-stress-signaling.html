1. The physiological problem of waiting and conserving water

A seed lying in moist soil faces a decision that cannot be reversed easily. Once the radicle emerges and the young root begins to grow, the organism becomes dependent on an adequate supply of water and other resources. A mature plant faces a related decision every time it opens a stomatal pore: carbon dioxide enters through the pore, but water vapour escapes. Both situations involve the risk of committing resources at an unsuitable time. Abscisic acid (ABA) is one of the principal hormonal signals through which flowering plants coordinate these decisions. In developing seeds, it helps establish maturation programmes and prevents premature germination. In vegetative organs, it adjusts the exchange of gases and water and regulates the expression of genes that help tissues withstand dehydration.

A hormone is not the physical energy that accomplishes a biological task. ABA does not itself pull water out of a guard cell, build the seed coat or provide ATP for metabolic adaptation. Instead, it changes the activities of proteins that carry out those functions. Because cells differ in their proteins, a common chemical signal can generate different outcomes in different organs. An embryo that expresses seed-specific transcriptional regulators responds differently from a guard cell equipped with specialized membrane channels. Thus, the identity of a hormone is only the first part of explaining a physiological response. The amount of active hormone, its cellular location, the competence of the responding tissue and the duration of exposure are all important.

ABA was identified during the 1960s in studies of substances associated with dormancy and organ shedding. Its name reflects early observations, not a universal assignment of function. Ethylene is a major direct regulator of many abscission responses, and the contribution of ABA depends on the organ and species. Similarly, the older description of ABA as a growth inhibitor captures some familiar outcomes but conceals important differences between organs. When water availability declines, restriction of leaf expansion can coexist with maintenance of root elongation at moderate levels of stress. The adaptive value of a response must be considered in the environment in which it occurs.

Three distinctions will organize the rest of the chapter. First, hormone biosynthesis is not hormone perception: a plant may contain normal ABA yet be unable to respond. Second, dormancy is not simply a lack of water; a dormant viable seed fails to germinate under conditions that otherwise permit germination. Third, closure of a stomatal pore is not a guarantee of drought survival; it reduces one route of water loss but can also decrease photosynthetic carbon gain. Each distinction can be tested by designing an experiment in which one variable changes while the relevant alternatives are controlled.

2. From plastid carotenoids to active ABA

The naturally abundant active form of ABA in many higher plants is a fifteen-carbon oxygenated terpenoid derived from a larger carotenoid. A carotenoid is a lipid-soluble pigment constructed from isoprenoid units. Carotenoids have important roles in photosynthetic antennae and photoprotection, but selected oxygenated carotenoids, termed xanthophylls, also provide substrates for hormone synthesis. The plant route to ABA is therefore called an indirect or carotenoid-cleavage pathway. It is different from assembling a fifteen-carbon molecule directly from a cytosolic sesquiterpene precursor, even though the final product contains fifteen carbon atoms.

In plastids, zeaxanthin epoxidase (encoded by ABA1 in Arabidopsis) participates in the formation of epoxidized xanthophylls, including violaxanthin. The relevant nine-cis forms of violaxanthin and neoxanthin become substrates for a family of enzymes known as nine-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenases, abbreviated NCED. The geometric configuration of the carotenoid matters: the enzyme recognizes a suitable cis substrate rather than indiscriminately cleaving every photosynthetic pigment. Oxidative cleavage of the forty-carbon substrate produces the fifteen-carbon intermediate xanthoxin and a complementary twenty-five-carbon product. Xanthoxin represents the portion of the original molecule that proceeds towards ABA. Counting the carbon atoms before and after cleavage provides a simple check on the pathway.

The remaining conversion steps occur principally in the cytosol. The enzyme encoded by ABA2, a short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase, converts xanthoxin into abscisic aldehyde. Abscisic aldehyde oxidase, represented by AAO3 in Arabidopsis, oxidizes the aldehyde into the corresponding carboxylic acid, ABA. The aldehyde oxidase requires an appropriately sulfurated molybdenum cofactor; the ABA3 gene contributes to this cofactor maturation. A lesion affecting ABA3 can therefore impair aldehyde oxidation without changing the AAO3 coding sequence. This provides a useful distinction between loss of an enzyme and loss of a factor necessary for its activity.

NCED is frequently a major regulatory point because it directs the carotenoid pool towards ABA synthesis. Increasing its activity can increase hormone production, provided the relevant substrate and downstream enzymatic capacity are available. It is not correct to infer that all plants or tissues are limited by precisely the same step under every condition. In one tissue substrate delivery may be restrictive; in another, the rate of aldehyde conversion or hormone removal can determine the concentration of free ABA. A transcriptional increase in NCED must consequently be interpreted as evidence of potential biosynthetic regulation rather than a direct measurement of ABA production.

Different NCED family members can have contrasting developmental expression patterns. Arabidopsis NCED6 and NCED9, for example, are important in developing seeds, whereas NCED3 is associated with dehydration-induced ABA accumulation in vegetative tissue. This division of labour illustrates why a single mutant may have a stronger phenotype in one organ than another. It also explains why genetic experiments involving combinations of mutations can reveal functions masked by partially redundant enzymes. The terminology of Arabidopsis genes should not be applied without qualification to every crop species.

3. How a maize mutant identified the cleavage reaction

Vivipary in maize means that developing kernels germinate prematurely on the parent plant. Such a phenotype provides an opportunity to investigate the hormonal controls of seed development without beginning from a predetermined biochemical explanation. A viviparous embryo might fail to manufacture a germination inhibitor, might be insensitive to it, or might contain an altered developmental regulator that bypasses inhibition. The appearance of a germinated kernel alone does not distinguish among these possibilities. Comparing the hormone content and biochemical capabilities of wild-type and mutant embryos provides a way to locate the affected process.

The classical vp14 studies by Tan and colleagues and Schwartz and colleagues in 1997 illustrate complementary genetic and biochemical strategies. The mutant retained carotenoid substrates and could convert supplied xanthoxin to ABA, yet showed reduced ABA accumulation in developing embryos. The downstream machinery was therefore not completely disabled. The results focused attention on the reaction between the available carotenoid substrate and xanthoxin. Tests with recombinant VP14 protein demonstrated cleavage of suitable epoxycarotenoids and identified the products, connecting the gene directly to the predicted catalytic activity. The conclusion rests on several kinds of evidence rather than on a simple correlation between a gene and a phenotype.

Imagine three other mutants in this pathway. Mutant X cannot generate the appropriate epoxycarotenoid; mutant Y lacks NCED activity; mutant Z cannot oxidize abscisic aldehyde. All may be deficient in ABA, but their precursor profiles differ. Supplying xanthoxin to tissue extracts should bypass the early blocks in X and Y if the extract retains functional ABA2 and aldehyde oxidase. It cannot bypass a complete block of the final oxidation in Z. Providing ABA itself should bypass a purely biosynthetic defect if the responding tissue can receive and perceive the hormone. The logic is analogous to metabolic complementation: a product supplied downstream can overcome an upstream deficiency.

There are important qualifications when interpreting hormone rescue in intact plants. An externally supplied compound may fail to reach the target cells, may be rapidly metabolized or may be used at an unsuitable dose. A tissue extract removes some delivery barriers but changes the cellular environment and may lose an essential cofactor. Consequently, a convincing interpretation combines direct metabolite measurement, genetic complementation, appropriate vehicle controls and a test of downstream responsiveness. The objective is not merely to observe rescue; it is to identify why rescue is possible in one genotype and not another.

4. Storage, breakdown and free hormone homeostasis

The free concentration of ABA at any moment is the net result of processes that supply and remove it. A tissue can contain abundant ABA-related molecules while only a portion of them is immediately available to bind ABA receptors. One reversible mechanism is the formation of ABA glucose ester, often abbreviated ABA-GE. UDP-dependent glucosyltransferases catalyse conjugation of glucose to ABA, reducing the pool of free active hormone. ABA-GE can accumulate in cellular compartments and, under suitable conditions, beta-glucosidases can hydrolyse the conjugate to release free ABA. In Arabidopsis, examples include BG1 associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and BG2 associated with the vacuole.

A conjugate reservoir allows a tissue to mobilize ABA without waiting for every reaction in the carotenoid-cleavage pathway. This is advantageous when a rapid adjustment of water balance is required. Nevertheless, conjugate hydrolysis does not imply that biosynthesis becomes unnecessary. A reservoir can be depleted and must be replenished, and the relative contributions of hydrolysis and de novo synthesis vary between tissues and conditions. Measuring only the free hormone does not reveal whether it was newly made or recovered from a conjugate. Measuring both free ABA and ABA-GE over time offers more information, especially when combined with enzyme mutants or tracer experiments.

Oxidative breakdown is a distinct process. CYP707A enzymes are cytochrome P450 ABA eight-prime hydroxylases. Their product, eight-prime hydroxy-ABA, can rearrange to phaseic acid, which can subsequently be reduced to dihydrophaseic acid. These conversions generally lower the canonical ABA response, although phaseic acid can have signaling activity through some receptors. This qualification matters because catabolism is often taught as a switch from an active to a completely inert substance. It is more precise to describe oxidative turnover as a change in the abundance and signaling properties of the original hormone. Reversible ester hydrolysis and oxidative turnover should not be confused: the latter does not serve as the ordinary ABA-GE storage cycle.

A simple mass balance clarifies how apparently contradictory changes can arise. Let A represent the amount of free ABA in a specified tissue, in nanomoles. Let S be synthesis, H hydrolytic release, I import, C conjugation, D oxidative removal and E export, all measured in nanomoles per hour. The rate of accumulation is dA/dt = S + H + I − C − D − E. If the sum of the supply terms equals the sum of the removal terms, the measured amount is constant even though ABA molecules continually enter and leave the pool. A constant concentration therefore cannot establish that ABA metabolism is inactive. Concentration additionally depends on tissue volume, which can change during dehydration.

Consider an illustrative sample with ten nanomoles of free ABA. During a short interval the tissue produces six nanomoles, releases two from conjugate and imports one, while conjugating two, oxidizing three and exporting one. Assuming the amounts apply to the same tissue and interval, the free pool increases by three nanomoles to thirteen. An alternative sample that produces only three nanomoles but has less oxidative removal could finish with the same amount. The endpoint measurement alone cannot distinguish the two histories. This is why time courses and independent estimates of production and degradation are useful in interpreting hormonal regulation.

5. Transport links production sites to responding cells

A hormone can influence a neighbouring cell, the same cell that produces it or a remote tissue. ABA moves between cells and can travel in vascular tissues, including xylem and phloem. Roots may contribute ABA to transpiration-associated xylem transport, but ABA synthesized in leaves is also important in many drought responses. Neither the observation of ABA in xylem sap nor the expression of an ABA biosynthetic enzyme in roots establishes that all drought-responsive ABA in a leaf originated in the root. Vascular cells, mesophyll and guard cells can contribute to local regulation according to the experimental conditions.

ABA is a weak acid. The uncharged protonated form is more able to partition into membranes than the charged anion. As pH changes, the proportions of these forms change. The Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship gives the ratio of anionic to protonated ABA as 10 raised to the power of pH minus pKa. With an illustrative pKa of 4.7, approximately one seventh of free ABA is protonated at pH 5.5, but less than one hundredth is protonated at pH 7.0. These estimates assume aqueous acid-base equilibrium and do not directly measure transmembrane permeability. A membrane transport protein can facilitate movement even when most hormone is ionized.

The weak-acid behavior of ABA helps explain why the pH of the extracellular space can affect hormone distribution, but passive partitioning is not the only mechanism. In Arabidopsis, ABCG25 is an example of an ABA exporter and ABCG40 an example of an ABA importer. NPF4.6, formerly called AIT1, is another reported ABA uptake transporter. An exporter may help release ABA from a producing cell, and an importer may enhance access to cytosolic receptors in a responding cell. The families differ in their molecular mechanisms; the mere fact that a transporter belongs to an ABC or NPF family does not prove that every member transports ABA.

The direction of an individual transporter does not establish the direction of whole-organ flow. A tissue contains many cell types and possible routes, and the overall distribution depends on vascular movement, local synthesis, membrane transport and metabolic turnover. In a transport-defective mutant, a high ABA concentration in a producing tissue can coexist with a low concentration in a responding tissue. Treating the entire plant as a single homogeneous compartment would conceal this distinction. When designing an experiment, hormone measurements at the scale of whole leaves should therefore be complemented, where appropriate, by tissue-specific expression information or an assay of the target cell response.

6. The receptor–phosphatase–kinase switch

The core ABA signaling module contains three groups of proteins. PYR1, PYL and RCAR are related soluble ABA receptors. Clade A PP2Cs, including ABI1 and ABI2, are protein phosphatases that normally oppose signaling. Subclass III SnRK2 proteins are kinases that promote many ABA responses. A kinase transfers a phosphate group to a target protein, commonly using ATP, while a phosphatase removes phosphate groups. Phosphorylation can change enzyme activity, binding or cellular behavior. The particular targets of each kinase determine the final response.

At low ABA, PP2C-mediated inhibition prevents substantial activation of the ABA-responsive SnRK2 pathway. PP2C can dephosphorylate the kinase activation loop and directly restrain kinase activity. When ABA enters the receptor binding pocket, conformational changes allow the hormone-bound receptor to inhibit a compatible PP2C. The phosphatase is thereby less able to restrain SnRK2. Kinase activity increases through appropriate phosphorylation and regulatory events, and the activated SnRK2 phosphorylates downstream targets. The apparent paradox that inhibiting a protein activates a pathway is resolved by recognizing that the inhibited protein is itself an inhibitor. Two negative relationships yield a positive overall effect.

A receptor is not synonymous with a kinase. PYR/PYL proteins do not act as membrane receptor kinases like BRI1 in brassinosteroid signaling. Instead, ABA perception alters the interaction between soluble receptor and phosphatase. The resulting kinase activity belongs to SnRK2. Although some ABA receptor family members can interact with selected PP2Cs at low ABA in particular assays, the ligand-dependent inhibition of negative regulators is the central teaching model. It should not be interpreted as an identical quantitative response for every receptor–phosphatase combination.

The pathway predicts a series of genetic outcomes. Loss of one receptor can be partially masked by other family members, while loss of several relevant receptors may produce clear insensitivity. A PP2C allele that retains inhibitory activity but resists receptor inhibition can keep SnRK2 repressed even when ABA increases. The classical dominant abi1-1 allele exemplifies this logic; it must be distinguished from an ordinary loss-of-function allele in a negative regulator. Conversely, reducing relevant PP2C activity can enhance ABA responsiveness. Loss of downstream SnRK2 activity weakens the response even when receptors bind hormone normally. These predictions are valuable because they locate defects according to pathway position, not simply the direction of the visible phenotype.

Two complementary studies by Park and colleagues and Ma and colleagues in 2009 identified the receptor families through chemical genetic and protein interaction approaches. A chemical genetics experiment uses a compound whose physiological activity allows the investigator to find mutants altered in response to it. Biochemical tests then ask whether the candidate receptor recognizes the ligand and whether its interaction with a phosphatase changes enzymatic activity. Reconstitution with defined components can test whether the proposed proteins are sufficient for a particular biochemical step. A molecular interaction by itself, however, is not evidence that the same interaction accounts for every whole-plant response. Genetic and physiological assays connect the molecular mechanism to biological function.

7. From kinase activation to stomatal closure

A stoma is a pore in the epidermis bordered by two specialized guard cells. In many dicots the cells have a kidney-like outline; grasses possess a different guard-cell geometry and associated subsidiary cells. Changes in turgor produce differential deformation of cell walls and alter the aperture. When guard cells accumulate osmotically active solutes, water tends to enter and turgor rises. When sufficient solutes are released, water tends to leave and turgor falls. These effects depend on the surrounding water potential and mechanical properties of the stomatal complex, not on osmotic concentration alone.

ABA signaling in guard cells employs SnRK2.6, also known as OST1, as a major kinase. Activation of anion channels such as SLAC1 increases the release of negatively charged solutes. The exit of negative charge tends to depolarize the membrane, making the interior less negative relative to the extracellular solution. This favors opening of outward-rectifying potassium channels, including GORK under appropriate conditions. At the same time, ABA suppresses pathways that normally support solute accumulation. Loss of potassium and counterions reduces the osmotic driving force for water retention. The resulting water efflux lowers turgor and promotes closure.

Ion movements are coordinated rather than independent. Electrical potential changes influence channel opening, while concentration gradients and membrane potential jointly determine the electrochemical driving force. It would be incorrect to state that depolarization pushes all positively charged ions out irrespective of the gradients. Potassium efflux is favored when the membrane voltage is sufficiently positive relative to the equilibrium potential for potassium and when an appropriate outward pathway is available. Likewise, anion channel activation does not create anion molecules; it allows existing cellular anions to redistribute. Maintenance of these gradients before the closure response depends ultimately on cellular metabolism and membrane transport.

Calcium and reactive oxygen species participate in ABA signaling in guard cells. Activation of NADPH oxidases can contribute to reactive oxygen production, which can interact with calcium-permeable pathways and calcium-dependent kinases. Calcium-dependent signaling can reinforce channel regulation. These interconnected routes do not replace the central receptor–phosphatase–SnRK2 mechanism, and not every response requires an identical calcium transient. Experimental measurements of ion fluxes, calcium and aperture should be related in time rather than assumed to be interchangeable measures of ABA signaling.

Consider the effects of loss of SLAC1 compared with loss of OST1. The channel mutant may have impaired closure because one important anion efflux pathway is unavailable, despite retaining kinase activation and other downstream responses. The kinase mutant disrupts an upstream regulatory component and can therefore affect several outputs. Providing extra ABA cannot restore the function of a completely absent channel or kinase. However, partial closure can persist through other channels and regulatory pathways, so neither genotype should be described as necessarily eliminating every stomatal response. The appropriate inference is that the affected protein is necessary for the normal magnitude or timing of the response under the tested conditions.

8. Seed maturation, dormancy and gibberellin antagonism

Seed maturation prepares an embryo for survival between generations. Developing seeds accumulate reserves, acquire tolerance of drying in orthodox species and establish physiological conditions that restrain germination. ABA participates in coordinating these events. Seed-specific transcriptional regulators such as ABI3, ABI4 and ABI5 contribute to the response, with different roles and regulatory connections. ABA-responsive expression of protective proteins, including many late embryogenesis abundant proteins, helps prepare cells for water loss. Seed maturation and dormancy are related but not identical outcomes: a mature seed may possess desiccation tolerance and still germinate promptly under suitable conditions if its dormancy is weak.

Primary dormancy develops before dispersal, while secondary dormancy may arise in a previously nondormant seed under unsuitable environmental conditions. A viable dormant seed differs from a dead seed; lack of radicle emergence alone cannot distinguish them. It also differs from a nondormant seed that lacks sufficient water, oxygen or a suitable temperature. A dormancy assay must establish conditions that support germination in an appropriate nondormant control. Testing germination over time rather than at a single endpoint can separate a delayed response from a stable failure to germinate.

Gibberellins generally promote germination in many model systems by facilitating embryo growth and weakening tissues that oppose radicle emergence. ABA can restrain these events and maintain protective developmental programs. The balance between the pathways is useful, but a simple numerical ABA-to-GA ratio cannot explain all seeds because sensitivity, spatial distribution and developmental state also vary. A seed with a defective ABA receptor can germinate at a high hormone concentration; a seed unable to perceive GA may remain poorly germinating even after ABA declines. A complete interpretation therefore combines hormone metabolism with downstream signaling.

During imbibition of many nondormant seeds, oxidative ABA removal contributes to decreasing the inhibitory signal. CYP707A2 is an important example of a catabolic enzyme associated with early imbibition in Arabidopsis. After-ripening, light and temperature can change dormancy through effects on hormone metabolism and sensitivity. The response varies among species and seed states, and it is not accurate to prescribe one sequence of treatment that releases all dormant seeds. In cereals, the GA-stimulated production of hydrolytic enzymes in aleurone tissue provides a classical illustration of reserve mobilization; ABA can antagonize this response. This is a tissue-specific mechanism, not a universal description of every seed.

The seed also provides a striking example of communication between tissues. The embryo and the surrounding endosperm can have different hormonal roles. In Arabidopsis, coordinated ABCG-mediated export from endosperm and import into embryo has been demonstrated, allowing ABA produced in one tissue to influence embryo growth. The transport route is relevant because measuring an average concentration from the entire seed does not identify the hormone exposure of the embryo. A tissue-specific genetic perturbation and a germination assay can be more informative than a bulk hormone measurement alone.

9. Rapid responses, transcriptional responses and drought trade-offs

Stomatal movement can begin without the production of a new complement of channel proteins. Pre-existing signaling proteins are modified and ion transport changes, providing a response on the scale of minutes under appropriate conditions. More persistent acclimation often requires altered gene expression. ABA-activated SnRK2 proteins can phosphorylate AREB/ABF transcription factors belonging to the bZIP family. These factors recognize ABA-responsive regulatory sequences in suitable promoters and influence the expression of genes involved in dehydration responses. The two modes of action are not mutually exclusive: a plant can close stomata rapidly while starting a slower change in cellular protection.

A transcriptional response is not synonymous with an increase in hormone concentration. A transcription factor may be present but inactive until phosphorylated, and a promoter can require other regulatory factors in addition to an ABA-responsive element. Conversely, an increase in a particular transcript may be induced by ABA-independent stress pathways. To distinguish ABA-dependent and ABA-independent regulation, one can compare a wild-type plant with a signaling-deficient mutant exposed to the same water deficit. If induction is reduced in the mutant, ABA signaling contributes to the response; if induction persists, another pathway may be sufficient or may act in parallel. Neither result alone proves that ABA is the only upstream signal.

Reduced stomatal conductance usually limits transpirational water loss, but it also restricts diffusion of carbon dioxide into the leaf. A plant may conserve water at the cost of instantaneous assimilation and growth. The significance of this trade-off depends on drought duration, atmospheric humidity, root access to water, temperature and recovery conditions. Under a short deficit, transient closure may preserve tissue hydration until conditions improve. Prolonged closure can impose carbon limitation. Selecting plants solely for low stomatal conductance without measuring photosynthesis or yield therefore cannot establish agronomic superiority.

Under some moderate water deficits, ABA helps maintain primary root extension while restricting shoot expansion. The direction of this response depends on tissue, hormone concentration, stress severity and developmental state. Maintaining access to deeper water can be beneficial, whereas uncontrolled growth of large leaf surfaces may increase water demand. Under severe stress even roots can stop elongating. The important concept is allocation and context rather than the idea that ABA always inhibits or always promotes growth. ABA accumulation and signaling eventually need to decline or be adjusted as water conditions recover, so the mechanisms of catabolism and sensitivity are as physiologically important as those that initiate the signal.

10. Experimental reasoning and quantitative interpretation

Suppose wild-type Arabidopsis, an ABA-deficient biosynthetic mutant and an ABA-insensitive receptor mutant are grown under matched conditions. A seed germination assay uses vehicle-treated and ABA-treated groups for each genotype. The biosynthetic mutant may germinate more readily than wild type without treatment, but an effective external dose can restore inhibition if uptake and signaling are intact. The receptor mutant may also germinate readily without treatment yet show reduced inhibition after ABA is supplied. This pattern distinguishes reduced hormone availability from a defect in response. Because families of receptors are redundant, the strength of the latter result depends on which and how many receptor genes are affected.

The same design can be applied to stomatal aperture measurements, but the experimental units must be defined properly. Many pores from a single leaf are not independent biological replicates of a genotype. Several leaves from independently grown plants and suitable randomization help reduce the risk of pseudoreplication. The initial aperture, time after treatment, temperature, light, solution composition and relative humidity can all influence the response. Measuring each group at matched intervals prevents differences in closure kinetics from being confused with differences in final aperture.

For an illustrative ABA treatment, consider a mean aperture of six micrometres before treatment and two micrometres after treatment. Relative closure, defined here as the change divided by the initial aperture, is (6 − 2)/6 = 0.667, or 66.7 percent. A mutant whose aperture changes from four to two micrometres has the same final value but only 50 percent relative closure. The starting values differ, so neither final aperture nor percentage alone proves that one genotype has a more sensitive signaling system. Full dose-response curves and time courses help distinguish altered baseline, maximal response and response sensitivity.

A response curve may shift horizontally without changing its maximum, suggesting altered sensitivity rather than necessarily altered maximal capacity. A lower plateau may instead reflect limited capacity of a downstream process, provided the test range truly reaches saturation. These are interpretations to be tested, not guaranteed identities. A plant that transports applied ABA poorly could produce an apparently right-shifted external dose-response curve even though its intracellular receptors function normally. Measuring internal hormone levels and bypassing the delivery step where feasible can help distinguish transport from perception.

Hormone concentration is often reported per gram of fresh mass or dry mass. If a leaf loses water, its fresh mass decreases and the value expressed per gram fresh mass may rise even if the total amount of ABA remains unchanged. A valid analysis should state the normalization basis and, where relevant, consider water content. The same principle applies to comparisons of drought-treated and well-watered tissues with different cell sizes. Accurate measurements alone are insufficient if the denominator is interpreted incorrectly.

Common misconceptions

ABA is synthesized directly from a C15 sesquiterpene precursor in plants.
The standard higher-plant pathway cleaves C40 carotenoids to form C15 xanthoxin.
NCED oxidizes abscisic aldehyde.
NCED cleaves epoxycarotenoids; AAO3 oxidizes the aldehyde.
ABA2 and ABA3 are interchangeable enzymes.
ABA2 participates in xanthoxin conversion; ABA3 supplies the sulfurated molybdenum cofactor.
ABA-GE and phaseic acid are the same storage form.
ABA-GE can release ABA by hydrolysis; phaseic acid arises from oxidative turnover.
Constant ABA concentration means no hormone turnover.
Balanced synthesis and removal can maintain a constant pool.
All leaf ABA originates in roots.
Local leaf synthesis and transport both contribute.
ABCG25 and ABCG40 have identical transport directions.
ABCG25 is an exporter example; ABCG40 is an importer example.
PYL is an ABA-activated membrane kinase.
PYL is a soluble receptor that modulates PP2C activity.
PP2C is the principal positive ABA kinase.
PP2C is a phosphatase and negative regulator in the core pathway.
Every abi1 mutation is a simple loss of function.
The dominant abi1-1 allele resists normal receptor-mediated inhibition.
ABA closes stomata by increasing guard-cell potassium content.
Closure commonly involves net anion and potassium release and reduced turgor.
All non-germinating seeds are dormant.
Viability and suitable germination conditions must be established.
High ABA necessarily means strong signaling.
Receptor competence and hormone distribution can limit response.
ABA always inhibits growth of every organ.
Growth effects vary by tissue and water-stress context.
A drought-resistant genotype must have the lowest stomatal conductance.
Water conservation must be evaluated together with carbon assimilation, recovery and yield.

Original examination practice

1. Part B reasoning

Which enzyme directly produces xanthoxin from a suitable epoxycarotenoid?

Answer and explanation

NCED. The oxidative cleavage is distinct from the subsequent cytosolic steps.

2. Part B reasoning

Which protein is a negative regulator of the core ABA signaling pathway?

Answer and explanation

Clade A PP2C. It restrains ABA-responsive SnRK2 activity; the ABA–PYL complex inhibits compatible PP2Cs.

3. Part B reasoning

Which compound is a reversible conjugate reservoir for free ABA?

Answer and explanation

ABA glucose ester. Beta-glucosidase-mediated hydrolysis can regenerate ABA from this ester.

4. Part B reasoning

Which kinase is especially associated with rapid ABA-induced guard-cell responses?

Answer and explanation

OST1/SnRK2.6. OST1 phosphorylates targets including the SLAC1 anion channel.

5. Part B reasoning

What is the expected direct effect of sustained net guard-cell solute loss?

Answer and explanation

Reduced osmotic water retention and turgor. Under suitable external water conditions the cells lose water and the pore tends to close.

6. Part C reasoning

A mutant has normal epoxycarotenoid pools and makes ABA after receiving xanthoxin. Which step is most strongly implicated?

Answer and explanation

Carotenoid cleavage or its regulation. Downstream conversion capacity persists; the available evidence favors the step producing xanthoxin, although transport or compartmentation should be examined.

7. Part C reasoning

Mutant A and mutant B have weak seed dormancy. Applied ABA restores inhibition only in A. What hypothesis best explains the difference?

Answer and explanation

A is biosynthetically deficient and B has impaired ABA response. External hormone bypasses production but cannot generally bypass a downstream perception or signaling lesion. Uptake and dose controls are required.

8. Part C reasoning

A tissue synthesizes 5 nmol ABA and imports 2 nmol in an hour; it conjugates 3 nmol and catabolizes 4 nmol. All other fluxes are negligible. What happens to the free pool?

Answer and explanation

No net change. Inputs of 7 nmol balance removal of 7 nmol, demonstrating that unchanged pool size can coexist with turnover.

9. Part C reasoning

A mutant loses the guard-cell ABA importer, but an assay of isolated intracellular signaling proteins shows normal ABA perception. Why could external ABA require a larger dose?

Answer and explanation

Reduced access to intracellular receptors. Transport limits the effective intracellular concentration, so external dose sensitivity cannot be equated with receptor affinity.

10. Part C reasoning

An ABA-treated line activates SnRK2 normally but releases fewer anions and shows weak closure. Which class of lesion is most plausible?

Answer and explanation

A downstream channel or channel regulation defect. Normal kinase activity places the likely lesion after kinase activation. A SLAC1 defect is one hypothesis, not uniquely established.
