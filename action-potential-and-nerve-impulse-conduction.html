1. Why a neuron needs a regenerative signal

A sensory ending in the skin and a motor ending in a muscle can be separated by a distance many thousands of times greater than the diameter of either axon. Yet an animal can coordinate the movement of widely separated structures in a fraction of a second. The explanation is not that a substance manufactured in the cell body must travel all the way to the muscle before the muscle can respond. Rather, each region of an excitable membrane can generate a brief electrical event when it receives a suitable local stimulus. This property allows information to be passed progressively along a living fibre.

The biological challenge becomes clear if we first imagine a membrane that contains only channels whose conductances remain constant. Inject a small current at one point. The voltage nearby changes as charge accumulates on the two faces of the lipid bilayer. Some of the current travels along the cytoplasm, but some also crosses the membrane through open channels. With increasing distance from the point of injection, the voltage change gets smaller. Such passive spread is useful across short distances in dendrites or between nearby segments of an axon, but it cannot preserve an arbitrarily small signal across a long fibre. Increasing the initial stimulus does not eliminate the exponential decay of a passive response.

An action potential solves the distance problem by combining passive electrical spread with active local regeneration. The passive signal does not have to reach the final target; it only has to recruit another patch of excitable membrane. At this next patch, the ionic gradients already present on either side of the membrane supply the energy for another rapid voltage change. The new region then supplies current to its neighbours. In this manner, the travelling event is rebuilt rather than transported as a fixed packet of sodium ions. The distinction becomes important when considering why a long myelinated fibre can conduct rapidly despite the slow molecular diffusion of individual ions.

The term membrane potential, written Vm, refers here to the intracellular electrical potential minus the extracellular potential measured locally. A value of −70 mV therefore means that the inside is seventy millivolts more negative than the outside. Resting values vary with cell type, temperature and ionic composition. Depolarisation is movement of Vm in the positive direction, whether the membrane reaches zero or not. Repolarisation is movement back toward the original resting region after depolarisation, and hyperpolarisation is movement to a more negative potential relative to a specified reference. Overshoot means the action potential actually becomes positive inside relative to outside. These terms describe voltage, not which ion must move to create it.

An action potential differs from a graded potential in its regenerative character. The latter can vary smoothly with input strength and generally attenuates during passive spread. A conventional axonal spike begins when depolarisation recruits sufficient inward current to initiate positive feedback. Subsequent channel changes end this regenerative phase and return the membrane toward rest. A stimulus that just succeeds in initiating a spike and one that is somewhat stronger can therefore yield nearly the same local waveform in a given membrane state. That is the sense in which the event is all-or-none. It does not imply that every neuron in every species produces precisely the same peak voltage or duration.

The initial segment of many central neurons is particularly well equipped for spike initiation because of its high density and specialised distribution of voltage-gated sodium channels. A sensory axon may instead initiate impulses near its peripheral ending, where receptor-mediated changes first attain sufficient amplitude. After initiation, an action potential must still propagate successfully to its destination. Initiation and propagation are related but distinct: an axon can generate a normal proximal spike and still fail to transmit it past an injured or poorly insulated region.

Electrical signalling as a relationship between space and time

Imagine a receptor at the tip of a long sensory process that is briefly deformed by a mechanical stimulus. The receptor membrane may contain channels whose probability of opening depends on deformation. These channels create a local graded response because the strength and duration of the mechanical disturbance affect the number of conducting pathways. That response might last longer than an axonal spike. At the first excitable region of the sensory fibre, its electrical effect is converted into one or more spikes. Conversion is needed because the initial graded response and the subsequent propagated signal have different tasks: one represents local stimulus strength, whereas the other preserves a temporally identifiable event over distance. This conceptual transition precedes detailed receptor transduction in the sense-organ subunit and detailed synaptic integration in the next node.

An intracellular recording made at a fixed location provides a plot of voltage against time. At the onset of excitation the membrane potential at this recording site rises; later it returns toward rest. The experiment does not show directly which nearby regions of the fibre are excited at each instant. A spatial representation instead plots membrane potential along the axon at one selected time. In that picture the active region has a front, a peak and a region that is recovering behind it. If the wave travels without major change of shape at speed v, spatial displacement x and time displacement t are related by x = vt. For example, a pulse lasting approximately 1 ms occupies roughly 20 mm of an ideal uniform fibre conducting at 20 m/s. This spatial width is not the distance over which a single passive voltage perturbation can spread. It reflects the duration of the locally generated waveform combined with the speed of its translation.

This distinction also helps explain why a short segment of an axon is not necessarily all in the same physiological state. At one instant, some membrane patches may have nearly maximal sodium conductance, others may be inactivating sodium channels, and still others may be resting. The overall event is a travelling pattern of channel activity and voltage. A nerve impulse is therefore not equivalent to a simultaneous depolarisation of the entire neuron. For a long axon it may take many milliseconds for the terminal to experience the event generated at the initial segment. That timing matters when multiple pathways converge on a common target or when sensory feedback must reach muscles quickly.

2. Ionic gradients, conductance and electrical balance

The membrane potential changes when the net current across a membrane patch charges or discharges its capacitance. A membrane is a capacitor because the lipid interior separates conducting solutions. Ions cannot freely cross this insulating core, so a relatively small displacement of electrical charge alters the voltage across it. This surface charge is a minute fraction of the total ions in the adjacent solutions. Consequently, an action potential can alter Vm by around one hundred millivolts without making the bulk cytoplasm electrically positive by a large amount or eliminating the sodium and potassium gradients.

The electrochemical equilibrium potential E for an ion is the voltage at which the chemical and electrical contributions to its net movement balance. For an ion with valence z, the Nernst equation is E = (RT/zF) ln([ion]outside/[ion]inside), where R is the gas constant, T absolute temperature in kelvin and F the Faraday constant. Sodium typically has a positive equilibrium potential in neurons because its external concentration is higher, whereas potassium typically has a negative equilibrium potential. The sign and magnitude of these values specify the direction and magnitude of driving force at any given voltage, but the ions cannot produce a large current unless pathways are open.

For a simple ohmic description of a particular ionic current, Iion = gion(Vm − Eion). Conductance g is measured in siemens, potential difference in volts, and current in amperes. The sign convention is outward positive. At Vm = −50 mV and ENa = +60 mV, the sodium driving force is −110 mV; sodium current is inward if sodium conductance is nonzero. At the same Vm and EK = −90 mV, potassium driving force is +40 mV; potassium current is outward if potassium channels are open. Both processes can occur at the same moment. One cannot infer current direction merely from the sign of membrane voltage.

Consider a membrane patch with sodium conductance 10 nS and potassium conductance 4 nS at these voltages. Since a nanosiemens multiplied by a millivolt gives a picoampere, INa = 10 × (−110) = −1100 pA, while IK = 4 × 40 = +160 pA. Ignoring leak and applied current for this example, the net ionic current is −940 pA. Net inward positive charge depolarises the membrane. If sodium channels subsequently inactivate while potassium channels remain open, the net current may reverse sign even if Vm has not yet crossed zero. The current balance, not an arbitrary voltage landmark, determines whether voltage is rising or falling.

With outward ionic current positive and external injected inward current defined positive, the charge balance is C dVm/dt = Iinj − INa − IK − IL. C is capacitance in farads; dVm/dt is voltage change per second. This is a local, space-clamped description unless axial current is included in Iinj. For C = 100 pF and a net inward capacitive current of 200 pA, the instantaneous voltage slope is 2 V/s, equivalent to 2 mV/ms. This calculation concerns a particular instant. During a spike, conductances and driving forces change rapidly, so extending that slope linearly over the whole spike is unjustified.

The sodium-potassium ATPase maintains ion gradients over longer periods by moving three sodium ions outward and two potassium ions inward per ATP hydrolysed. Its electrogenicity contributes to membrane voltage, but the millisecond upstroke and falling phase are principally currents through channels rather than sudden reversals in pump operation. Blocking ATP production may leave gradients initially adequate for firing but will eventually compromise signalling, especially during repeated activity. This separation between stored electrochemical energy, channel-mediated discharge and metabolic recovery explains why an electrical signal remains inseparable from cellular physiology.

Deriving the effects of changing ion concentrations and membrane area

The factor RT/F in the Nernst equation has units of volts. At approximately 37°C, or 310 K, RT/F is about 0.0267 V, using R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and F = 96485 C mol⁻¹. Multiplication by 2.303 converts the natural logarithm coefficient to approximately 61.5 mV for a base-ten logarithm and monovalent ions. If the sodium concentrations outside and inside an illustrative neuron are 145 mM and 15 mM, their ratio is 9.67. The equilibrium potential calculated using 61.5 log10(9.67) is approximately +60.6 mV. If the outside concentration is halved while the inside remains fixed, the ratio becomes 4.83 and ENa becomes approximately +42.1 mV. The change of about −18.5 mV follows directly from 61.5 log10(0.5). This calculation predicts the shift in reversal potential; it does not assert that the spike peak shifts by exactly the same amount.

For potassium with outside concentration 4 mM and inside concentration 140 mM at the same temperature, EK is approximately 61.5 log10(4/140) = −95.0 mV. Doubling outside potassium to 8 mM raises this estimate by about 18.5 mV to −76.5 mV. At a voltage of −65 mV, the initial outward potassium driving force would be approximately 30 mV, whereas after this substitution it would be about 11.5 mV. If the conductance were held constant, the outward potassium current would be smaller. However, changing extracellular potassium also changes the resting voltage, which subsequently changes channel availability. The fixed-voltage calculation isolates one effect and provides a starting point rather than a complete prediction of a freely firing cell.

The distinction between permeability and conductance matters when interpreting quantitative expressions. Permeability describes how readily a substance crosses a membrane under a specified physical model, and the Goldman description incorporates concentration effects explicitly. Electrical conductance relates measured current to electrical driving force in an ohmic approximation. If an ion has an asymmetric current–voltage relationship, a single fixed conductance may not describe it adequately over the entire voltage range. The classical conductance model remains an instructive framework because it separates the changing availability of pathways from the direction of their electrochemical force. When applying it to a particular problem, follow the assumptions supplied in that problem rather than treating every channel as a perfect resistor.

Capacitance relates charge to voltage through Q = CΔV. Suppose a membrane patch with capacitance 1 pF changes its voltage by 100 mV. The capacitive charge change is 10⁻¹² F × 0.100 V = 10⁻¹³ C. Dividing by the elementary charge of approximately 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C gives roughly 6.24 × 10⁵ elementary charges. For a monovalent ion that number corresponds to the charge carried by approximately six hundred thousand ions. A real spike can involve substantially more ionic movement than this net capacitive charge because inward and outward currents overlap. The calculation estimates the difference in accumulated charge, not total sodium influx or ATP consumption.

Membrane capacitance and channel number also scale with membrane area. If two otherwise identical spherical compartments have different surface areas but the same channel density and specific capacitance, both their total conductance and total capacitance increase with area. Under a spatially uniform voltage and identical current density, the voltage change per time can therefore remain similar. Yet injecting the same absolute current into the larger compartment produces a smaller current density and initially a smaller rate of voltage change. This distinction between absolute current, current per unit area, total conductance and conductance density is essential when comparing cells experimentally. A bigger whole-cell current need not imply a higher channel density.

3. Threshold and regenerative sodium activation

The resting membrane contains sodium channels that are closed but able to open. These channels are different from the constitutively active leak pathways that dominate resting conductance. Voltage-gated channels contain charged voltage-sensing structures whose positions depend on the electric field across the membrane. A depolarising change in Vm alters the likelihood of their transition to conducting conformations. For a population of sodium channels, larger depolarisation generally recruits more available channels and increases inward sodium current over the initial range in which driving force remains substantial.

This recruitment establishes positive feedback. An initial depolarisation opens some sodium channels. Sodium entry makes the membrane more positive, which recruits additional channels. Their current in turn accelerates depolarisation. The critical distinction between a weak subthreshold response and a spike is whether this inward feedback can overcome opposing outward and leak currents sufficiently to become self-amplifying. The transition is often rapid enough that a voltage trace appears to leave its slow trajectory and rise sharply.

Threshold is not a universal voltage inscribed on every axon. The inward feedback depends on how many sodium channels are available, their voltage sensitivity and the size of the sodium driving force; opposing currents depend on potassium conductance, leak and membrane geometry. A cell recovering from a preceding spike may require stronger stimulation because some sodium channels remain inactivated and potassium conductance is still elevated. A slowly rising depolarisation can permit more sodium inactivation or potassium activation before the regenerative transition than a fast depolarising pulse of similar final amplitude. Thus the trajectory of the membrane as well as its instantaneous voltage can influence excitability.

A useful current-clamp experiment injects successive brief current pulses while recording Vm. At small amplitudes, each produces a smooth graded voltage deflection. A slightly larger pulse may evoke a spike, and further moderate increases in pulse amplitude produce a similar spike from the same initial state. Care must be taken to distinguish the current required for initiation from the resulting membrane voltage at the onset of rapid depolarisation. Input resistance controls how much voltage is generated by an injected current, while channel kinetics determine whether that voltage change recruits a regenerative response.

The idea of regenerative feedback explains why a modest reduction in sodium channel availability may impair transmission disproportionately near threshold. If outward current approximately balances inward current, losing a small portion of the inward component can prevent the transition entirely. Conversely, raising available inward conductance can make a previously ineffective stimulus successful. However, increasing excitability is not the same as guaranteeing indefinitely sustained firing. If the membrane remains strongly depolarised, sodium channel inactivation can ultimately remove the very inward conductance needed to generate further spikes. This provides a mechanism for depolarisation block and demonstrates why excitability depends on a cycle of activation and recovery rather than on a permanently positive voltage.

Why voltage history changes the stimulus required for excitation

Threshold can be explored with a strength–duration experiment in which a series of rectangular current pulses are delivered to a rested cell. For a given pulse duration, the investigator determines the smallest amplitude that evokes a reproducible spike. A very brief pulse must charge the membrane rapidly before it ends, whereas a longer pulse allows more time for voltage to build up. The required current amplitude therefore generally decreases as pulse duration increases, approaching a lower limiting value for sufficiently long pulses under a simple excitability model. This limiting current is called the rheobase; chronaxie is conventionally the pulse duration required at twice the rheobase. These measurements characterise excitability under the stated conditions and depend on the preparation and stimulation method.

The simplest passive explanation starts with ΔV(t) = IR(1 − e^(−t/RC)), where I is a constant injected current, R is membrane resistance and C is capacitance. Suppose a hypothetical cell rests at −70 mV and a fixed 15 mV depolarisation would bring it to a defined threshold. Let R = 100 MΩ and τ = RC = 5 ms. With an injected current of 200 pA, the steady passive change would be 20 mV. Reaching 15 mV requires 0.75 = 1 − e^(−t/5 ms), so t = 5 ln(4) ms ≈ 6.93 ms. A pulse lasting only 2 ms produces about 6.6 mV in this elementary model, insufficient to reach the assumed threshold. These are illustrative calculations, not measured data or a complete model of the active initiation process.

If the injected current were exactly 150 pA, the steady deflection would be 15 mV, approached asymptotically in the passive model. To reach a particular voltage within a finite time a larger amplitude would be needed. However, in an active neuron small inward currents may begin developing before the arbitrarily chosen threshold and alter the passive trajectory. The example therefore describes why stimulus duration matters without claiming that a real spike begins exactly when a passive formula predicts. A strength–duration curve is an experimental relationship that incorporates both passive charging and active channel kinetics.

A second approach compares a slowly rising current ramp with a rapidly rising pulse. The two stimuli can reach the same final amplitude while producing different firing responses. A slow rise may permit outward conductances to develop and some sodium channels to inactivate before the voltage reaches the region where a rapid input would have recruited strong inward feedback. This reduction of responsiveness to slowly developing stimulation is often described as accommodation. It is one reason why the stimulus threshold cannot be interpreted as a simple readout of a fixed voltage. The temporal characteristics of the input matter because the channel populations are themselves changing over time.

Changes in passive conductance also influence threshold. Opening additional channels whose reversal potential is near the resting voltage may have relatively little direct effect on the resting voltage yet can reduce input resistance. A given depolarising current then produces a smaller voltage excursion and may fail to initiate a spike. This principle anticipates shunting inhibition in the synaptic chapter: suppression can occur without a large hyperpolarisation. At this stage the central idea is that the efficacy of a current depends on the electrical load into which it flows.

A rigorous comparison of excitability between cells should therefore match temperature, baseline voltage and stimulation history as far as possible. If one cell requires a larger current, it might have a smaller input resistance, larger capacitance, a less favourable threshold voltage, fewer available sodium channels or stronger opposing currents. Recording both voltage responses and current–voltage relations helps separate these possibilities. A single threshold current does not reveal the molecular mechanism of excitability without additional observations.

4. The action potential as a sequence of channel states

A conventional fast axonal action potential begins with a depolarising input, often arriving from neighbouring active membrane. Once regenerative activation dominates, sodium conductance rises rapidly. The electrical and concentration gradients favour inward sodium movement as long as Vm is below ENa. The rising phase therefore reflects a large inward sodium current. The potential may overshoot zero, but positive Vm by itself does not require the sodium current to stop. At +25 mV with ENa = +60 mV, sodium still has an inward driving force of −35 mV.

Why does the voltage not continue rising indefinitely until all sodium gradients vanish? Two changes limit the upstroke. First, the magnitude of the sodium driving force diminishes as Vm approaches ENa. Second, many activated sodium channels rapidly become inactivated. Inactivation is a nonconducting state distinct from a resting closed state: an inactivated channel cannot simply reopen in response to another immediate depolarisation. Repolarisation and sufficient time are required to restore availability. Delayed voltage-gated potassium conductance also increases during the rising and peak portions of the event.

At the maximum of a locally recorded spike in the absence of net applied or axial current, the derivative dVm/dt is zero. The total inward ionic current then balances total outward ionic current. This is not equivalent to the sodium current being zero or the potassium current reaching its maximum. The peak generally lies below ENa because the sodium conductance is not the only membrane conductance and because sodium channel inactivation is developing. A spike peak is therefore a result of competing dynamic currents rather than a direct measurement of the sodium equilibrium potential.

Repolarisation follows because inward sodium current declines and outward potassium current becomes dominant. Potassium leaves through open channels while Vm lies above EK. The positive charge leaving the cytoplasm drives Vm downward. At more negative voltages, the delayed potassium channels progressively deactivate and sodium channels recover from inactivation. If potassium conductance remains elevated after Vm has reached its usual resting value, it continues driving Vm toward EK, producing an afterhyperpolarisation. In real neurons, additional potassium conductances, including calcium-dependent types, may contribute to afterpotentials; the classical delayed rectifier is the central explanatory example here.

The membrane returns toward its resting voltage as the transient conductances relax and the resting current balance is restored. The pump does not produce an immediate burst that directly cancels each individual spike. Instead it continuously maintains the gradients that make repeated firing possible. The initial and final states are approximately alike in voltage and ionic channel availability, but they are not identical immediately after the waveform ends. Some conductances recover more slowly than the visually obvious voltage excursion. For this reason, observing a return to the baseline on an oscilloscope does not establish that the membrane has fully recovered its ability to fire.

Not every excitable cell has the same spike mechanism. Some neurons have significant calcium currents, and some other excitable tissues possess long calcium-dependent plateau phases. A sodium-dependent action potential is the appropriate model for rapid conduction in many animal axons, not a definition that excludes every other regenerative ionic event. For examination questions the specified cell type, the relevant reversal potentials and the conductance changes must take precedence over memorised waveform values.

Interpreting overlapping currents and the energetic cost of a spike

Students sometimes draw sodium and potassium currents as nonoverlapping processes: sodium is imagined to enter only during the rising phase, then potassium is imagined to leave only after sodium channels have completely closed. Such a sequential description is useful for remembering the dominant direction of current, but it misrepresents the physiological overlap. At a point early in repolarisation, some sodium channels can still conduct inward current even while the total membrane current is outward. During the late upstroke, some potassium channels have already activated. The voltage trajectory is determined by the difference between these simultaneous currents and any axial or applied currents.

A quantitative example illustrates the peak condition in a simplified membrane. Suppose the sodium and potassium equilibrium potentials are +60 mV and −90 mV, and at one moment Vm = +30 mV. Let gNa = 12 nS and gK = 3 nS. The sodium current is 12 × (30 − 60) = −360 pA. The potassium current is 3 × (30 + 90) = +360 pA. Ignoring leak and applied or axial currents, the net ionic current is zero at that instant. Sodium still enters while potassium leaves at a compensating charge rate. A later decline in gNa or rise in gK moves the balance outward and initiates a falling voltage trajectory.

This simultaneous exchange also affects energetic efficiency. The minimum electrical charge needed to raise Vm is set by capacitance, but sodium entry that is offset by potassium exit contributes little to the net charge stored across the membrane. The ion gradients must nevertheless be restored after this opposing flux. If the sodium influx of a particular spike is known, its associated long-term pumping requirement can be estimated from pump stoichiometry. For example, if 3 million additional sodium ions entered an illustrative membrane region during a spike, exporting them through a pump that transports three sodium ions per ATP would require roughly one million ATP molecules for the sodium export component, assuming all those ions are eventually removed by that pump. This estimate does not include all energetic costs of the cell or imply ATP is directly consumed by the sodium channels.

Why might the action potential width change without a dramatic change in its peak amplitude? A perturbation that primarily alters potassium activation can have a much stronger effect on the falling phase than on the earliest positive feedback. If sodium activation remains sufficiently fast, the upstroke and peak may initially be relatively preserved, while reduced outward current prolongs the time spent at depolarised voltages. Such a waveform also affects subsequent channel availability and, at nerve terminals, the time over which voltage-sensitive processes may be activated. This is a natural connection to synaptic physiology without requiring the full mechanism of transmitter release in this chapter.

The initial baseline also matters. Consider comparing spikes beginning at −80 mV and −60 mV. If both attain +30 mV, their absolute peak voltages are the same but their amplitudes relative to baseline are 110 mV and 90 mV, respectively. If the second condition instead reduces sodium availability, the actual peak may also be smaller. Describing a trace requires reporting whether the measured quantity is absolute peak, excursion from baseline, rise slope, width or afterpotential. Each can be influenced by different channel properties.

A useful approximate measurement of spike width is the time between two crossings of a specified voltage level, such as half the excursion from baseline to peak. A change in peak amplitude can alter this measurement even if the underlying repolarisation kinetics are similar. When interpreting comparisons, it is therefore helpful to examine the full waveform rather than one numerical characteristic in isolation. The student should identify exactly which phase of the waveform is affected and then reason about the inward and outward currents that dominate during that phase.

5. Refractory periods and the timing of spikes

A second stimulus delivered soon after the first action potential reveals a temporary loss of excitability. This can be investigated by using pairs of stimuli. The first pulse initiates a spike and the second is applied after a variable interval. When the interval is extremely short, increasing the second pulse cannot trigger a normal second action potential in the same membrane region. This is the absolute refractory period, explained mainly by the reduced availability of voltage-gated sodium channels following their inactivation. A large injected current may nevertheless force the voltage to change; such a forced deflection is not evidence of a second regenerative action potential.

At somewhat longer intervals, an unusually strong second pulse can sometimes recruit a spike whereas the original pulse fails. This is relative refractoriness. More sodium channels have recovered, but their available fraction may still be smaller than at rest and potassium conductance may remain elevated. The latter contributes to a more negative starting voltage and a larger outward conductance that opposes depolarisation. A second spike during incomplete recovery can have a different maximum rate of rise, amplitude or propagation safety from a fully recovered spike.

Suppose an illustrative neuron has an absolute refractory interval of 2 ms. If no other limitation exists, a simple upper bound on repetitive firing is the reciprocal of that interval: 1/(0.002 s) = 500 spikes per second. This is not a prediction that the neuron will actually fire at 500 Hz. Relative refractoriness, stimulus strength, adaptation currents, metabolic constraints and the time necessary to reach threshold all lower the attainable rate in most physiological conditions. The distinction between an absolute upper bound and a measured steady firing rate is essential in quantitative questions.

A paired-pulse experiment also helps separate availability from the apparent return of membrane voltage. A conditioning pulse can depolarise a membrane under voltage clamp long enough to inactivate sodium channels, and a subsequent test pulse measures the remaining sodium current. Varying the recovery interval at a repolarised holding potential provides a time course for restoration of channel availability. If the voltage returns to its usual resting level but the test current is smaller than control, the channel population has not yet recovered. A stronger current stimulus might help during relative refractoriness but cannot substitute for sodium channel recovery during absolute refractoriness.

The refractory period contributes to normal forward propagation. Behind an advancing spike, the membrane has recently fired and is less excitable; ahead, resting channels are available. Nevertheless, the local current itself can spread in both directions. Stimulating the middle of a resting isolated axon can initiate waves in opposite directions because neither adjacent region is refractory at the beginning. Normal orthodromic conduction and experimentally evoked antidromic conduction thus obey the same membrane principles. A refractory wake helps maintain the direction of an already established travelling wave; it is not a structural one-way valve.

Separating recovery kinetics from outward current and testing impulse collisions

A sodium-channel availability experiment can be designed to distinguish the contribution of inactivation from the contribution of potassium conductance. A voltage-clamped cell first receives a conditioning depolarisation, then a recovery period at a chosen holding voltage, and finally a standardised test pulse. The test pulse always uses the same command voltage and thus the same sodium driving force if ionic concentrations are stable. If potassium and leak currents are independently accounted for, differences in the early sodium current reflect changes in available sodium conductance. Normalising the test current to a fully recovered control makes comparisons possible even when the absolute current varies between preparations.

Suppose the normalised peak test currents of an illustrative experiment are 0.18 after 1 ms of recovery, 0.55 after 4 ms and 0.88 after 12 ms. These numbers do not establish an absolute refractory period of 1, 4 or 12 ms by themselves. They indicate progressive restoration of sodium current under the imposed voltage history. To identify whether a second full spike is possible, one must also consider opposing currents and membrane charging in a current-clamp or propagation experiment. A population can recover a measurable inward current without yet having enough regenerative reserve to trigger a normal spike.

The recovery holding voltage is another variable. Repolarising to a more negative voltage often favours recovery from fast sodium inactivation. Thus two cells given the same recovery interval may produce different test currents if the intermediate voltage differs. This is the reason a deep afterhyperpolarisation can have competing effects on excitability: it initially places Vm farther below the threshold region, but it can also favour restoration of sodium channel availability. The outcome depends on the interval, the magnitude of outward conductance and the voltage dependence of the channels. In examination reasoning, do not assume a more negative voltage is always inhibitory in every subsequent time window.

The consequences of refractoriness become striking when two action potentials travel toward each other in a uniform axon. Imagine stimulation at opposite ends of a rested isolated fibre, with the timing arranged so that each launches a wave toward the other. Each wave advances by depolarising resting tissue in front of it and leaves recovering membrane behind. When the two fronts encounter one another, there is no recovered tissue immediately beyond the meeting point in which either wave can regenerate. Under ordinary conditions they extinguish rather than pass through each other. This phenomenon provides an intuitive way to distinguish a regenerative excitable wave from independent linear waves, such as low-amplitude disturbances that can superimpose.

A related practical experiment uses a conditioning impulse followed by a test impulse from the same end. At very short intervals the second stimulus may fail at the initiation site. At intermediate intervals the second impulse may initiate but propagate less reliably or at altered speed because downstream membrane has not fully recovered from the first impulse. Measuring only the proximal trace could miss this propagation problem. Comparing proximal and distal records across different stimulus intervals can reveal whether the limiting factor is initiation, propagation or both.

Refractory periods also establish an upper limit on the density of distinct spikes along an axon. If conduction velocity is 10 m/s and the effective minimum interspike interval for successful transmission is 2 ms, successive fronts in a regular train would be separated by about 0.020 m, or 20 mm, assuming constant velocity. This does not mean the entire 20 mm is absolutely refractory; it is the spatial separation between equivalent waveform positions under the stated assumptions. The refractory state varies continuously behind each wave. During very rapid firing, the later wave can encounter partially recovered regions, which can modify its speed and waveform.

6. All-or-none responses, rate coding and spike trains

Neural information cannot be understood solely by examining a single action potential. Within an ordinary firing range, a stronger sustained depolarising input often produces more spikes per unit time instead of substantially increasing the amplitude of each spike. A continuously stimulated neuron may repeatedly charge toward threshold, fire and recover, generating a spike train. The interval between consecutive spikes determines the instantaneous firing rate; the number counted over a specified observation interval determines the average firing rate.

For example, an illustrative recording contains twelve spikes during a 300 ms stimulus. Its average rate during that observation window is 12/0.300 = 40 Hz. A pair of spikes separated by 10 ms corresponds to an instantaneous interval-based rate of 100 Hz, even though the average across the whole window may be lower. If a stronger stimulus evokes twenty-four spikes over the same interval, the mean rate doubles to 80 Hz. It would be incorrect to infer that each action potential must now carry twice as much sodium current or reach twice its former peak.

Rate coding is important but not the only coding strategy. The precise timing of individual spikes and the relative timing of spikes in different neurons can also convey information. In sensory systems, recruitment of additional afferent fibres can increase the number of active axons in a population as stimulus intensity increases. This can change the amplitude of a compound extracellular signal without any individual axon violating the all-or-none principle. A measured compound action potential is a sum of the electrical contributions of multiple fibres, and its peak also depends on their synchrony and recording geometry.

Adaptation can reduce firing rate even during a constant input. If an additional outward conductance builds up across several spikes, the membrane may take longer to return to threshold following each spike. Some neurons consequently fire rapidly at the beginning of a stimulus and then settle to a lower rate. Other neurons sustain repetitive firing more steadily. Comparing two firing records requires distinguishing the initial transient response from steady behaviour, and also distinguishing a change of spike rate from a change of individual spike shape.

A high firing rate is not always a reliable indicator that the input is stronger. An inhibitory input can lower rate without changing the excitatory stimulus; a neuron with damaged channels may fail to fire regularly despite a large depolarising drive. During severe maintained depolarisation, too many sodium channels may remain unavailable and repetitive firing may cease. Examination problems that provide a sequence of increasing stimulus strengths followed by cessation of spikes therefore need a mechanistic explanation, not automatic extrapolation of a straight stimulus-frequency relationship.

Interpreting stimulus–response relations without confusing coding and conduction

A stimulus–response experiment can begin with several repeated trials at each of a series of constant current intensities. For each trial the experimenter records whether a spike occurred, its latency after stimulus onset, the number of spikes and their temporal distribution. Near threshold, variability in membrane state or channel activity may lead to firing on some trials but not others. The average number of spikes per trial then changes gradually with input even though individual successful spikes remain relatively stereotyped. Consequently, a smooth relationship between firing probability and input does not contradict regenerative threshold behaviour.

For a sustained input, the first-spike latency may become shorter when the depolarising drive is stronger, even before a steady firing rate can be defined. A stronger current charges the membrane more quickly in a simple model, allowing it to enter the regenerative region earlier. The first-spike latency can therefore be a useful information-bearing variable in addition to average firing rate. In a short sensory stimulus, the duration may be too brief for a long spike train; timing of the first few spikes can then be especially informative. The relation between latency and stimulus depends on the membrane's initial state and is not universal across neurons.

A hypothetical pair of spike trains each contains eight spikes within a one-second recording window. The first is approximately regular across the entire second; the second contains two short groups of four spikes separated by a long silent interval. Their mean frequencies are equal at 8 Hz, but the temporal arrangements differ greatly. A downstream cell with a short integration time may respond strongly to the clustered train, while a different downstream circuit may be more sensitive to overall rate. This example shows why rate is a summary statistic rather than a complete representation of neural information. Detailed summation is treated in the following chapter.

A distinction must also be made between rate coding in one fibre and recruitment in a nerve population. Imagine an isolated nerve with low-threshold fibres and high-threshold fibres. At a weak electrical stimulus only some of the low-threshold fibres fire. When the stimulus becomes stronger, more fibres are recruited. The resulting extracellular signal may become larger because the activity of multiple fibres is combined. If the stimulus is a single brief pulse, the change in signal size does not imply that each fibre produced a train of action potentials. Recruitment and repetitive firing are two different dimensions of the response.

To examine adaptation, one could compare the first two interspike intervals of a sustained current-clamp response with those later in the trial. Suppose the first interval is 20 ms and a later interval is 50 ms. The interval-based firing rates are 50 Hz and 20 Hz, respectively. The input may have remained constant; a fall in firing rate can result from changes within the neuron itself. The investigator might test this explanation by varying the time between trials, examining afterpotentials or altering a relevant conductance. The observation alone establishes the change in timing, not the unique identity of the channel responsible.

Conduction speed and firing rate are frequently confused because both describe rapid nervous signalling. Their units provide a useful check: speed is distance per time, usually metres per second, whereas rate is events per time, usually hertz. A fibre can conduct rapidly and fire infrequently. A slow fibre can produce a high frequency of spikes if its channel kinetics permit adequate recovery. The distance between successive fronts in an ideal regular train depends on both variables, but neither can be inferred from the other without more information.

7. How intracellular recordings and voltage clamp established the mechanism

Before direct electrophysiological measurements, an electrical disturbance travelling through nerve tissue could be detected without knowing how it was generated at a particular membrane patch. A major experimental advance came from working with the exceptionally large squid giant axon. Its diameter made it possible to manipulate and record from the interior much more readily than in small mammalian nerve fibres. The preparation also provided a useful example of an unmyelinated fibre adapted for relatively rapid signalling, linking electrophysiological mechanism with the biological need for a quick escape response.

The first crucial distinction is between extracellular recording and intracellular recording. An extracellular electrode measures electrical potential outside the fibre relative to a reference. A propagating wave may produce a biphasic or multiphasic extracellular trace because the regions contributing current change position relative to the electrode. An intracellular electrode measures the internal voltage relative to the extracellular reference and can directly reveal the voltage trajectory across the membrane. A negative extracellular deflection should not automatically be called intracellular depolarisation without understanding the electrode arrangement.

The finding that the intracellular potential could overshoot zero placed important constraints on explanations based on a simple disappearance of resting selectivity. If excitation merely made the membrane equally permeable to all ions, a reproducible positive overshoot would be difficult to explain. The sodium hypothesis instead proposed a transient increase in sodium permeability, giving the positive sodium equilibrium potential a dominant influence. Changing external sodium concentration provides a way to test a prediction of this hypothesis: lowering external sodium tends to reduce the sodium equilibrium potential and diminish the spike overshoot when other relevant conditions are controlled.

A fundamental difficulty in studying a freely changing action potential is that voltage and conductance influence each other. If sodium channels open, they change the voltage; that changing voltage then alters the channels. A voltage-clamp system breaks this particular feedback loop experimentally by maintaining the membrane potential near a chosen command value. A feedback amplifier measures Vm and injects compensating current when it deviates from the command. Recording the current needed to maintain the voltage reveals the membrane currents once capacitive and other contributions have been taken into account. Holding voltage fixed also fixes each ionic driving force if concentrations remain constant.

A typical protocol begins at a negative holding potential, steps to a more positive command potential and then returns to the holding voltage. Immediately after the step, charging of the membrane capacitance creates a brief current transient. The subsequent ionic current may show an inward component that develops rapidly and declines, followed by a more sustained outward component. Because the command voltage remains fixed during the step, the time dependence cannot be explained by the voltage itself simply returning toward rest. It indicates a time-dependent change of membrane conductance or channel availability at the fixed voltage.

Hodgkin and Huxley used a series of such measurements in the squid giant axon to formulate their quantitative account of excitation and conduction in 1952. Their analysis separated sodium and potassium components and described the voltage and time dependence of the conductances mathematically. A powerful feature of this approach was that the model derived from controlled voltage steps could predict a freely evolving spike when the voltage was no longer clamped. The research thus connected separately measured membrane properties to emergent electrical behaviour, rather than merely fitting a curve to an existing spike shape.

The clamp itself does not identify an ion merely because a current is inward. Ion substitution, reversal potentials, and later selective pharmacological interventions provide complementary evidence. Imperfect control of voltage along a spatially extended cell can also distort measurements, especially when distal processes escape the voltage command. The squid preparation and appropriate electrode configuration helped reduce such problems. A strong experimental interpretation therefore distinguishes the measured current, the assumptions required to isolate it and the inference about underlying ion-selective pathways.

Experimental controls, reversal of currents and the logic of ion identification

A useful voltage-clamp analysis compares currents during several steps from an identical holding voltage. Suppose the investigator obtains the transient current at command voltages of −40, 0, +40 and +70 mV. An inward current that increases in magnitude over some depolarising steps but then decreases and becomes outward at the highest command can be consistent with activation of a sodium-selective pathway. Activation becomes stronger over part of the voltage range, but the driving force becomes smaller as voltage approaches the sodium equilibrium potential. At a command above that equilibrium potential, the open channels carry outward rather than inward sodium current.

A current–voltage curve is therefore not the same as a conductance–voltage curve. In the former, measured current depends on both conductance and driving force. In the latter, conductance is estimated by dividing current by the appropriate driving force where the approximation is valid. This division becomes problematic near reversal because both the numerator and denominator may be very small and recording error becomes disproportionately important. A good experiment estimates the reversal from the pattern across several voltages rather than trying to calculate a precise conductance by dividing a noisy near-zero current by a near-zero voltage difference.

Ion substitution offers a logically independent test. If external sodium is decreased and the reversal of an isolated early current shifts in the direction predicted for ENa, the result strengthens the sodium selectivity interpretation. A suitable substitute must preserve other relevant properties of the solution as far as possible, including osmotic conditions and the concentrations of ions not being tested. A matched control and a reversible recovery after restoring the original solution can help distinguish the desired ionic effect from progressive deterioration of the axon. A reduction of the overshoot alone is supportive but not decisive because solution changes might affect other properties as well.

A second method subtracts currents obtained under two conditions. In a conceptual experiment, record the total current during a command step before and after blocking a class of sodium channels. If the blocker removes only the susceptible sodium component and all other conditions remain unchanged, subtracting the blocked trace from the control trace estimates the affected current. This reasoning requires the same command, holding history, temperature and stable cell properties across the measurements. A failure of specificity or deterioration between trials would undermine the interpretation. The subtraction is a testable inference rather than an automatic chemical identification.

A capacitive transient and a leak current are separate experimental concerns. The ideal capacitive current is proportional to the rate of change of voltage, so a rapid command step produces a brief transient even in the absence of an active channel response. A linear background leak can persist after charging is complete. If these components are not distinguished, an early inward or outward feature may be incorrectly assigned to a voltage-gated channel. A suitable analysis compares the time course, voltage dependence and response to ionic or pharmacological interventions. The measured compensation current also needs a clear sign convention: an amplifier may report current flowing through its own circuit rather than the investigator's chosen outward membrane sign.

8. The Hodgkin–Huxley conductance model

In the classical model, the membrane current is represented as the sum of capacitive current and currents through sodium, potassium and leak pathways. The sodium current is written INa = gNa,max m³h(Vm − ENa), and the potassium current is IK = gK,max n⁴(Vm − EK). The leak current can be approximated by IL = gL(Vm − EL). The quantities m, h and n vary between zero and one and describe activation or availability in a phenomenological model; the maximal conductances give the scale when the relevant factors permit conduction.

The variable m increases rapidly with depolarisation and accounts for activation of sodium conductance. The variable h decreases during maintained depolarisation and represents loss of sodium availability through inactivation. Potassium activation, described by n, develops more slowly. The product m³h therefore allows sodium conductance to be transient even at an unchanged depolarised command voltage. The n⁴ term describes the delayed development of potassium conductance. The exponents were chosen to represent the macroscopic kinetics, not as a direct determination of the number or arrangement of molecular channel subunits.

Each gating variable x can be expressed conceptually as dx/dt = αx(Vm)(1 − x) − βx(Vm)x. The first term increases the fraction x and the second decreases it. At fixed Vm the coefficients have units of inverse time and are constant in this formulation. At equilibrium, setting dx/dt = 0 yields x∞ = α/(α + β). The time constant is τ = 1/(α + β), and the variable approaches its steady value exponentially if the voltage is held fixed. A larger rate sum therefore implies faster adjustment, whereas the ratio determines the final fraction.

For an illustrative gating variable with α = 3 ms⁻¹ and β = 1 ms⁻¹, x∞ = 3/4 = 0.75 and τ = 1/4 ms = 0.25 ms. Starting from x = 0, after one time constant the variable has achieved approximately 63% of the total approach toward its steady value: 0.75(1 − e⁻¹) ≈ 0.474. The current associated with that variable need not reach 63% of its final value at the same moment because its conductance may involve a power of x or a product with another time-varying variable.

At a fixed voltage, imagine m = 0.8 and h = 0.5. The fractional sodium conductance is m³h = 0.512 × 0.5 = 0.256. If h subsequently falls to 0.1 while m stays unchanged, the fraction becomes 0.0512, only one-fifth as large. This exercise demonstrates how activation and availability can change independently. Likewise, a potassium activation value n = 0.5 corresponds to n⁴ = 0.0625, showing why moderate changes in the gating variable can have a substantial nonlinear effect on conductance.

The model is powerful because it allows a student to predict the sign and time course of current changes using voltage-dependent rules. It is not a universal parameter set for every cell. The squid giant axon measurements were made under particular experimental conditions, including temperature, species and ionic media. Mammalian fibres and other excitable tissues have different channel distributions and kinetics. The general lesson is the interaction of voltage, ionic driving forces, activation, inactivation and capacitance; detailed fitting or computational simulation beyond this conceptual level is not required to understand the examination mechanisms.

Reasoning from the model without treating fitted parameters as universal

The gating equation at a fixed command voltage can be rearranged as dx/dt = (α + β)(x∞ − x). This makes the reason for the exponential approach transparent: the rate of change is proportional to the difference between the current value and its steady-state value. If the voltage changes abruptly, the target steady value and time constant change with it, while the gating variable retains continuity across the instant of the step in the ordinary differential-equation description. Thus after a step the variable does not immediately acquire its new equilibrium value. It approaches that value at a voltage-dependent rate.

For a variable that starts at x0 and moves toward x∞, the solution at fixed voltage is x(t) = x∞ + (x0 − x∞)e^(−t/τ). Suppose x0 = 0.20, x∞ = 0.80 and τ = 2 ms. At t = 2 ms, x is 0.80 − 0.60/e ≈ 0.579. After 6 ms, x is about 0.770. The final value is never exceeded in this simple single-variable model under a constant command. If an experimental current exhibits a transient peak above its eventual steady level, this need not contradict exponential kinetics of individual variables. A product of two variables moving in opposite directions can naturally produce a peak, as occurs with activation and inactivation in sodium conductance.

A simplified numerical example makes that point. Let m begin low and increase from 0.1 toward 0.9 during a depolarising step, while h decreases from 0.9 toward 0.1. At an early instant with m = 0.6 and h = 0.8, m³h = 0.1728. At a later instant with m = 0.9 and h = 0.1, m³h = 0.0729. Sodium conductance can therefore be smaller later even though activation is greater. If the voltage remains fixed, this decline is not attributable to a change in driving force. In contrast, during a free action potential both the driving force and the conductance vary.

The potassium expression demonstrates an additional nonlinearity. Suppose n increases from 0.4 to 0.6. Its fourth power increases from 0.0256 to 0.1296, a ratio of roughly 5.06, even though n itself increases by a factor of only 1.5. If the potassium driving force is fixed, its current changes in the same ratio. If voltage is also changing, the ratio of currents includes the ratio of driving forces. These examples are not a derivation of the original fitted parameters; they demonstrate how to use the model as a mechanistic reasoning tool.

One can explore qualitatively what happens if the maximal sodium conductance is reduced while the gating kinetics remain the same. For a given Vm and identical m and h, the sodium current becomes proportionally smaller. During a free spike, however, Vm will not necessarily follow the original trajectory: reduced inward current leads to slower or smaller depolarisation, which changes m, h and n in turn. This feedback can produce a nonlinear loss of excitability. Consequently, a fifty percent reduction in maximal conductance need not yield a fifty percent reduction in spike height. It might cause only a modest change in waveform in a fibre with substantial reserve, or prevent firing in a marginally excitable preparation.

Similarly, slowing potassium activation might prolong a spike because outward current becomes dominant later. Yet a delayed or prolonged waveform changes sodium inactivation and subsequent recovery, so predictions about sustained firing require more than the first-spike waveform. A model with the necessary parameters can calculate these coupled effects; without those parameters, a qualitative answer should acknowledge the limits of the prediction. This is a key skill for Part C problems: specify which variables are being held constant and which are free to change in response to the intervention.

The original formulation represents a particular squid axon in a defined experimental context. Voltage conventions sometimes differ between reproductions of the original equations, especially in how resting potential and depolarisation are referenced. The expressions in this chapter consistently use inside minus outside with outward ionic current positive. A problem may use a different convention; if so, translate the driving force and the sign of injected current coherently. Equations are not useful without a declared convention.

9. From macroscopic currents to single-channel evidence

Macroscopic current is the combined electrical contribution of many channels. A smoothly changing current trace may look continuous even if each constituent channel opens and closes in discrete transitions. Patch-clamp recording provided a way to examine electrical activity from extremely small regions of membrane. A fine glass pipette contacts the membrane and forms a high-resistance seal, limiting electrical leakage between the pipette and the surrounding solution. The recording apparatus can then resolve very small currents, in favourable conditions corresponding to the opening of individual ion channels.

Neher and Sakmann reported single-channel currents in denervated frog muscle fibres in 1976, studying acetylcholine receptor channels. This landmark work should not be misidentified as the original proof of the fast voltage-gated sodium current in the squid giant axon. It established the experimental ability to resolve discrete channel events and opened the way to analogous investigations of many channel types. Later studies of squid axon sodium channels provided complementary microscopic evidence for voltage-dependent sodium channel behaviour.

At a fixed membrane voltage, one open ion channel may produce a current step of approximately reproducible amplitude. The step duration and time between steps can vary. When a large number of channels are recorded simultaneously, the individual events are summed and the resulting trace becomes smoother. This explains how population gating variables in a macroscopic model can represent probabilistic transitions without requiring each channel to move gradually through a partially open state. Some real channels possess multiple conducting states, so a single fixed step amplitude is a useful first approximation rather than a universal rule.

For N identical independent channels, each with unitary current i and probability Po of being open, the expected macroscopic current is I = NPo i. Here N is a count, Po is dimensionless and i and I have current units. Consider 500 channels, Po = 0.2, and i = −2 pA at the chosen voltage. The expected current is 500 × 0.2 × (−2) = −200 pA. If a perturbation doubles Po while N and i remain unchanged, the expected current becomes −400 pA. A reduced macroscopic current might instead reflect a smaller N or a reduction in unitary conductance or driving force. A whole-cell trace alone cannot distinguish these alternatives.

A channel conductance can be estimated from its current and driving force if the current-voltage relationship is approximately ohmic. A single channel carrying −2 pA at a driving force of −100 mV has a conductance of 20 pS, because pA/mV is nS. Measuring the current at several voltages can establish where it reverses, which helps identify its ionic selectivity. A channel that is open but at its reversal potential gives no net current, so failure to resolve a step at that voltage does not necessarily mean the channel never opened.

Reading unitary traces and designing a complementary channel experiment

Consider a patch containing at most two identical channels that are independently active. At a fixed negative driving force, each opening contributes −2 pA. The possible ideal total current levels are 0, −2 and −4 pA. A trace that visits all three levels gives direct support for multiple simultaneous conducting events under the assumed conditions. The durations of the levels and the frequencies of transitions can be used to estimate how often channels are open. However, if multiple channels are present, the identity of the particular channel that opened is generally not apparent from the current level alone.

If each of two independent identical channels has open probability 0.30, the probability that neither is open is 0.7² = 0.49. The probability of exactly one open is 2 × 0.3 × 0.7 = 0.42, and the probability that both are open is 0.3² = 0.09. The expected number of open channels is 0 × 0.49 + 1 × 0.42 + 2 × 0.09 = 0.60, consistent with NPo = 2 × 0.30. The mean current is therefore 0.60 × (−2 pA) = −1.2 pA. This simple statistical example connects discrete microscopic current levels to macroscopic conductance without assuming that a single channel must physically conduct a fractional current of −1.2 pA.

A change in current amplitude per opening and a change in opening frequency provide different clues. If a voltage step increases the absolute magnitude of unitary current while opening probability is unchanged, the change might reflect an increased driving force. If the unitary amplitude stays constant but the channel spends more time open, the average current changes through probability. The distinction is particularly useful in comparing conditions at the same command voltage and ionic gradients, where a change in step amplitude may indicate altered channel conductance or selectivity. At different voltages, changes in driving force must be considered before inferring a molecular change.

Patch configurations differ in what they measure. A cell-attached patch allows recording from a small membrane region while the cell remains largely intact; the exact membrane voltage across that patch can depend on the cell's resting potential as well as the pipette voltage. Whole-cell recording electrically accesses the intracellular space and can measure combined currents from many channels. Excised patches expose a chosen side of a membrane patch to controlled solutions. These conceptual distinctions explain why recordings from different configurations answer complementary questions, without requiring specialist instructions for carrying out the procedures.

A convincing experiment investigating a reduced sodium current would compare whole-cell and unitary behaviour under controlled voltages and ionic conditions. If whole-cell current decreases while unitary current and measured opening probability remain comparable, a reduction in functional channel number becomes plausible. If unitary current is unchanged but the fraction of time open decreases, altered gating is plausible. If unitary amplitude decreases at the same driving force, altered single-channel conductance is plausible. None of these observations necessarily determines the molecular origin of the alteration by itself, but the combination greatly narrows the explanations.

Real recordings have background noise, limited temporal resolution and potential seal leakage. Very brief openings may be missed; overlapping openings may be difficult to resolve. Estimating open probability from observed open time assumes that the relevant events are adequately detected and that the recording window is representative. The physiological interpretation should therefore distinguish between absence of evidence for openings and evidence that a channel cannot open. Single-channel data complement macroscopic studies rather than automatically replacing the need for careful whole-cell controls.

10. Continuous conduction and local circuit currents

To understand propagation, imagine a short active region within a long axon. Its interior becomes relatively positive compared with the resting interior nearby. The resulting voltage difference drives conventional positive current through the conducting cytoplasm toward adjacent regions. Return current travels through the extracellular solution, completing local circuits. This flow charges the neighbouring membrane and moves its voltage in the depolarising direction. If that region reaches the regenerative threshold, its own voltage-gated channels produce the next active event.

In an unmyelinated axon, the process is continuous in the sense that closely adjacent regions of excitable membrane are successively recruited. It is not necessary for an individual sodium ion that entered at the beginning of the axon to migrate to the terminal. Local electrical charge redistribution communicates the voltage disturbance, and the local ion gradients power the new spike. The conduction wave can move much faster than diffusion of a particular ion through the full length of an axon.

The safety factor of conduction describes, in a general physiological sense, the reserve by which the depolarising current available to excite the next region exceeds the minimum needed to recruit it. A large reserve promotes reliable conduction despite variation in membrane properties. Reduced sodium channel density, incomplete recovery during rapid firing, or excessive loss of current through a leaky membrane can reduce that reserve. Once it falls below what is needed, the next region may show only a passive subthreshold response. The upstream spike can remain normal while distal conduction fails.

Passive cable properties help explain the recruitment of adjacent membrane. In an ideal uniform cylindrical cable, with membrane resistance parameter rm and axial resistance per unit length ri, the length constant is λ = √(rm/ri), in units of distance when these quantities are consistently defined. A steady passive voltage deflection decays approximately as ΔV(x) = ΔV(0)e^(−x/λ) along a sufficiently long cable under the appropriate boundary conditions. At one length constant the deflection is about 37% of its initial value. This describes passive spread, not the decreasing amplitude of a successfully regenerated travelling action potential.

Suppose a local passive depolarisation is 30 mV and the length constant is 1 mm. At 1 mm the predicted change is 30e⁻¹ ≈ 11.0 mV, and at 2 mm it is 30e⁻² ≈ 4.1 mV. A hypothetical neighbouring region needing 15 mV of depolarisation would not reach threshold at either of these distances from that particular steady source. A greater length constant would improve passive reach. In an actual conducting fibre, temporal charging, changing conductances and multiple neighbouring active regions must also be considered, so this simplified calculation should not be confused with an exact prediction of action potential conduction velocity.

The membrane time constant τm = RmCm expresses how rapidly a simple passive membrane charges in response to a constant current, where resistance and capacitance refer to the same patch or consistently normalised membrane area. In the elementary single-compartment case, a voltage step develops as ΔV(t) = ΔV∞(1 − e^(−t/τm)). One time constant corresponds to about 63% of the final change. A propagating wave depends on both how far current can spread and how quickly the next excitable region is charged. Neither the length constant nor the time constant alone uniquely specifies conduction velocity.

The electrical geometry of an axon and the limits of a passive model

The passive cable equation has a simple physical origin. Along a cable, current flowing through the cytoplasm decreases with distance because part of it leaves through the membrane. The longitudinal voltage gradient drives axial current, whereas the difference between the local voltage and the resting reference drives transverse leak. In a uniform steady-state cable the combination yields an exponential spatial dependence. This is the basis of the length constant. In the time-dependent situation, some current also charges membrane capacitance, and in an active cable the transverse current includes voltage-dependent ionic components.

For a cylinder of radius a, the membrane surface per unit length is 2πa, whereas cross-sectional area is πa². If the specific membrane resistance is Rm in Ω m² and intracellular resistivity is ρi in Ω m, the membrane resistance per unit length used in the cable relation is rm = Rm/(2πa), with units Ω m, and axial resistance per length is ri = ρi/(πa²), with units Ω/m. Their ratio is Rm a/(2ρi), with units m². Thus λ = √(Rm a/(2ρi)). This derivation explains why increasing radius increases passive reach despite also increasing membrane surface available for leakage: the cross-sectional area grows faster than the circumference.

Suppose a passive cylindrical fibre is represented by Rm = 0.2 Ω m², ρi = 1 Ω m and a = 5 µm = 5 × 10⁻⁶ m. Then λ² = 0.2 × 5 × 10⁻⁶/(2 × 1) = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m², giving λ ≈ 7.07 × 10⁻⁴ m, or 0.707 mm. If radius is increased fourfold and all specific properties remain the same, λ doubles to approximately 1.414 mm. These numbers are an illustrative calculation, not a measured cable constant for a named biological fibre. The relation assumes a uniform passive cylinder and a sufficiently simple extracellular return pathway.

The membrane time constant for an ideal local patch can also be expressed in terms of specific resistance and capacitance because area cancels. For an area A, total resistance is Rm/A and total capacitance is CmA, where Cm here denotes specific capacitance in F/m². Their product is RmCm. This is why doubling the size of an otherwise identical isopotential patch does not necessarily double its intrinsic passive time constant. It changes total current demand and electrical coupling to other regions. In a spatially extended cable, the observed voltage time course at a recording position also depends on current arriving from neighbouring positions, so it need not follow a single elementary exponential.

The apparent relation between conduction speed and length constant can be misleading if applied without the kinetic dimension. A fibre with large passive reach but very slow active channel recruitment may not conduct as rapidly as expected from its λ. Conversely, rapid channel activation does not guarantee successful long-distance transmission if excessive current leaks away before the next region is excited. Propagation is an interaction between geometry, charging, leakage and regenerative conductance. This is why two fibres of equal diameter may have different velocities when their channel properties or myelin differ.

A simple branch illustrates current loading. A parent axon supplies axial current to two daughter branches rather than one. Each daughter has a membrane that must be charged and an axial resistance that affects the distribution of current. If the two branches present different electrical loads, the same parent spike may recruit one more easily than the other. Branch geometry can therefore be important for reliability, particularly during repetitive activity or under conditions that reduce sodium availability. The concept is sufficient for examination reasoning; precise propagation through a branching cable requires more detailed modelling than the present node.

11. Saltatory conduction, diameter and electrical insulation

Myelinating glial cells wrap layers of membrane around particular regions of vertebrate axons. Schwann cells perform this role in the peripheral nervous system, whereas oligodendrocytes form central nervous system myelin. The exposed gaps between adjacent myelinated regions are called nodes of Ranvier. Nodes are enriched in voltage-gated sodium channels, allowing a concentrated region of membrane to regenerate the travelling spike. The intervening myelinated regions, called internodes, provide an efficient path for passive spread of axial current.

Myelin raises the effective transverse electrical resistance of the internodal membrane, reducing the fraction of axial current lost through it. It also lowers effective capacitance per unit membrane area because the insulating separation between conducting regions is increased. Less charge must then be transferred for a given internodal voltage change, and the next node receives a greater fraction of the available current. These properties support rapid node-to-node recruitment. Myelin does not conduct the nervous impulse as a metallic wire, nor does it eliminate the need for axial current underneath the insulating layers.

The name saltatory conduction refers to the apparent movement of the spike between successive nodes. In an ideal textbook representation, major regeneration occurs at the nodes and the internode is primarily passive. The internodal voltage does change as current spreads through the axoplasm. Therefore it is inaccurate to imagine an electrical event disappearing entirely at one node and appearing instantaneously at the next without any intervening process. A finite time is needed for the adjacent node to charge to its threshold.

Internode length has competing consequences. A larger separation between nodes reduces the number of regeneration steps needed to travel a given distance, potentially increasing speed. But increasing the distance also gives passive current more opportunity to attenuate and delays recruitment of the next node if it is too far away. Natural fibre architecture balances myelin thickness, axon diameter, nodal channel distribution and internodal spacing. It is not generally correct to claim that increasing node spacing indefinitely must produce indefinitely increasing velocity.

Axon diameter also influences conduction. For a cylindrical axon with intracellular resistivity ρi and radius a, axial resistance per unit length is ρi/(πa²). Increasing radius enlarges the cross-sectional area available for current, thereby reducing axial resistance per unit length. In a simple family of unmyelinated fibres with comparable specific membrane properties, conduction velocity scales approximately with the square root of diameter. In many myelinated fibres with geometrically comparable architecture, velocity is approximately proportional to diameter. These are approximate scaling relationships, not universal laws across species, temperatures and channel compositions.

Consider an illustrative unmyelinated fibre of diameter 1 µm and velocity 0.8 m/s. Under the simplified square-root relationship, a fibre with four times its diameter would conduct at about 1.6 m/s. For a comparable family of myelinated fibres showing approximate linear scaling, a doubling of diameter would instead approximately double velocity. Such calculations are valid only under the stated assumptions; a myelinated fibre is not obtained by simply substituting a different exponent into the unmyelinated model. The electrical organisation of the membrane itself differs.

Comparative animal physiology reveals two strategies for fast conduction. Some invertebrates use very large axons, such as the squid giant axon, to reduce internal resistance. Many vertebrate fibres use myelination to achieve rapid transmission without the same extreme increase in axonal diameter. Both approaches address the problem of bringing sufficient depolarising current to the next excitable region. They have different space, material and metabolic trade-offs, and neither implies that every fast animal response uses one strategy alone.

Nodal regeneration, internodal trade-offs and comparative reasoning

The apparent speed advantage of myelin is best understood by following a current pulse leaving one active node. Some current charges the downstream axonal membrane, some may leak through membrane pathways, and some travels onward along the axial resistance. An unmyelinated membrane presents continuous capacitive and conductive load along its length. In a myelinated region, multiple insulating layers reduce transverse leakage and the effective capacitive load. More of the local depolarising disturbance therefore reaches the next exposed node in a useful time. At that node, the high density of available sodium channels can regenerate a large inward current and create a new active source.

Electrical resistance and capacitance must not be treated as a single property. If effective resistance increases while capacitance decreases, the product RC may increase, decrease or remain similar depending on the magnitudes. It is therefore unsafe to explain myelination by simply asserting that its local membrane time constant must decrease. The important changes are the reduction of leak, the reduced charge required to alter the internodal voltage, and the altered distribution of current between nodes. A student given specified changes in R and C can calculate the product, but cannot infer a universal time constant trend solely from the fact that myelin is present.

An illustrative comparison demonstrates this. An uninsulated patch has an effective resistance of 10 MΩ and capacitance of 100 pF, giving RC = 1 ms. A simplified insulated patch of the same reference area has resistance 100 MΩ and capacitance 10 pF, again giving RC = 1 ms. Despite identical products, a given voltage excursion leaks less steady current through the insulated patch and requires less capacitive charge. A real internode cannot be represented completely by this pair of lumped elements, but the comparison shows why examining RC alone is insufficient.

The physical spacing of nodes also affects the total conduction delay. Imagine an ideal axon with internodes of 1 mm, each producing a combined passive transfer and recruitment delay of 0.05 ms. The average effective velocity in this illustrative discrete-step model is 0.001 m/0.00005 s = 20 m/s. If internode length increased to 2 mm but the delay increased only to 0.08 ms, the corresponding velocity would be 25 m/s. If a further increase to 3 mm resulted in a delay of 0.20 ms, velocity would decline to 15 m/s. If threshold could not be reached, the impulse would fail entirely. These numbers are invented teaching examples, not biological measurements, and they illustrate the existence of a trade-off rather than a specific optimum internodal length.

There is also a distinction between axon diameter and total myelinated fibre diameter. The latter includes the thickness of the insulating sheath. Comparisons of conduction velocity with diameter should specify which measurement is used, because two fibres with equal external diameters might have different internal axon radii and myelin thicknesses. The axial resistance depends mainly on the conducting axoplasmic cross-section, while the transverse electrical properties depend on the sheath and membrane architecture. Approximate linear velocity–diameter trends in myelinated fibres assume related geometries rather than arbitrary mixtures of these variables.

12. Measuring conduction speed and distinguishing compound signals

Conduction velocity is a distance travelled by an electrical event divided by its propagation time. In a nerve recording experiment a stimulus is applied to excite fibres, and electrodes detect the propagating electrical activity. Measuring time from stimulus onset to one recording site can include stimulus activation delay, recording equipment delay and conduction along the intervening nerve. Using two recording positions and comparing corresponding signal features is a way to remove a common initiation delay.

For an illustrative experiment, recording electrodes are separated by 60 mm. The same identifiable waveform component appears at the first electrode after 2.5 ms and at the second after 5.5 ms. The time difference is 3.0 ms. Converting units, the distance is 0.060 m and the interval is 0.003 s, giving v = 20 m/s. The calculation does not require assuming that the action potential started exactly when the electrical stimulus began. It does require that the identified waveform component represents the same set of conducting fibres at both positions.

A mixed nerve contains fibres with different diameters, degrees of myelination and propagation speeds. A compound action potential can therefore show several peaks or a broadened waveform rather than a single clear spike. Larger fast-conducting components usually arrive before slower ones over a sufficiently long conduction distance, though the observed amplitudes depend on recruitment and recording arrangement. The latency of a compound peak is not automatically the mean velocity of every axon in the nerve. In an experiment where individual components cannot be identified, a more limited interpretation is necessary.

Consider two fibres travelling 0.12 m at 40 m/s and 4 m/s. Their propagation times are respectively 3 ms and 30 ms. Even if they are activated simultaneously, the signals arrive 27 ms apart. At a shorter recording distance their arrival times are closer. Increasing the distance thus produces temporal dispersion in a mixed nerve. The compound peak may decline and broaden without loss of individual spikes, because contributions formerly arriving together now arrive at different times. This distinction is central to interpreting experiments that measure only the maximum amplitude of a compound response.

A reduction in peak amplitude can also occur because some axons cease to conduct, because the stimulus no longer recruits them, or because recording conditions change. To distinguish loss of propagation from temporal dispersion, one can compare waveform shape, component timing and the size of responses obtained after stimulation at different locations under controlled conditions. The key is to identify which measurement reflects single-fibre amplitude, population recruitment, conduction time or synchrony. These are different physiological variables that may change together but must not be treated as interchangeable.

Interpreting a multi-site recording and separating electrical and synaptic delays

For a conduction experiment with several recording sites, latency can be plotted against distance from a common stimulation point. If the same fibre or identifiable component travels at constant velocity and the initiation delay is common, the relation is t = t0 + x/v. Here t0 is the common delay, x is distance in metres and v is velocity in metres per second. A graph of latency against distance has a slope of 1/v. If distance is plotted in millimetres and latency in milliseconds, the numerical ratio mm/ms equals m/s when inverted appropriately. A nonzero intercept does not imply propagation failure; it can represent initiation or recording delay.

Consider illustrative latencies of 2.0, 3.5 and 5.0 ms at distances of 20, 50 and 80 mm. The increase in latency across each 30 mm interval is 1.5 ms, giving v = 30 mm/1.5 ms = 20 m/s. Extrapolating back to zero distance, t0 = 2.0 − 20/20 ms = 1.0 ms. A student who calculates 20 mm/2 ms obtains 10 m/s and incorrectly includes the common delay. Using a difference or a regression slope is more informative when the onset delay is unknown.

If a particular nerve region has become slow, the latency–distance plot need not be a single straight line. Suppose the first 40 mm of a fibre conducts at 20 m/s and the next 40 mm at 5 m/s. The propagation times are 2 ms and 8 ms respectively. Total propagation time is 10 ms over 80 mm, corresponding to an average speed of 8 m/s rather than the arithmetic mean of 20 and 5 m/s. The slow segment dominates the time spent travelling. By adding recording positions, an investigator can identify where the slope changes and infer which region accounts for the increased delay, though identifying its molecular cause requires other evidence.

Timing of a response measured in a target muscle or another neuron can include additional delays at connections. An electrical signal recorded at a distal muscle after nerve stimulation includes nerve conduction time, transfer across the neuromuscular junction and initiation or spread of electrical activity in the muscle. A mechanical contraction measured afterward includes additional excitation–contraction and force-development delays. Dividing total stimulus-to-contraction latency by nerve length therefore does not isolate axonal conduction velocity. The logic of difference measurements is especially valuable when several downstream processes add a common delay.

In a population recording, an investigator must specify which feature of the waveform is used for latency. The earliest detectable deflection may indicate the fastest recruited fibres, whereas a later prominent peak may reflect the greatest synchronous contribution of another group. A change of stimulus intensity can recruit different groups and thereby change which peak is most prominent. Comparing the largest peak at two distances without verifying correspondence may produce a misleading velocity estimate. An appropriate comparison tracks the same component, uses matched stimulation and considers temporal dispersion.

Controls are also needed for temperature and recording geometry. If a nerve is cooled between measurements, both conduction speed and waveform shape may change. If an electrode is moved, the apparent amplitude can change because its position relative to active current sources differs. A robust conclusion about conduction should rely on repeated observations, consistent distances and a defined latency criterion. Evidence of slowed propagation alone does not prove demyelination, just as a diminished compound peak alone does not prove the loss of axons.

13. Predicting channel blocks, temperature and demyelination

A selective reduction of fast voltage-gated sodium current in an ordinary sodium-dependent axon reduces the inward positive feedback that produces the spike. Tetrodotoxin blocks many voltage-gated sodium channels and is a classic experimental tool for distinguishing sodium-dependent currents, though channel isoforms vary in sensitivity. In voltage-clamp records it can remove a susceptible transient inward component, leaving delayed outward components visible. In current-clamp experiments strong enough suppression may prevent action potential initiation or propagation. The absence of a spike does not by itself prove that potassium channels have stopped functioning.

Reducing delayed potassium conductance has a different immediate effect. With less outward current opposing the remaining inward current, repolarisation can be slower and the spike can become broader. The afterhyperpolarisation attributable to that conductance may decrease. Tetraethylammonium can block certain potassium channels and is a classical means of studying outward current; it does not selectively and completely block every potassium channel type. The net effect on repetitive firing is not invariably an increase: prolonged depolarisation may delay recovery of sodium channels or recruit other conductances.

Changing extracellular potassium concentration modifies the potassium equilibrium potential. Increasing external potassium usually makes EK less negative, which tends to depolarise a membrane whose resting conductance is dominated by potassium. Mild depolarisation may bring it nearer the regenerative threshold. More severe or sustained depolarisation, however, can inactivate sodium channels and reduce excitability. A biphasic change from increased responsiveness to conduction failure is therefore physiologically plausible. This prediction relies on the difference between the resting distance to threshold and the available conductance needed for regenerative activation.

Temperature alters channel kinetics and other physical properties. Within an appropriate physiological range, warming generally speeds many channel transitions and often increases nerve conduction velocity; cooling generally slows them, broadens the time scale of action potentials and can increase refractory intervals. An approximate temperature coefficient Q10 describes the ratio by which a measured rate changes for a ten-degree Celsius rise over a restricted range. If an illustrative gating rate is 100 s⁻¹ at 20°C and Q10 = 2 over the next ten degrees, the predicted rate at 30°C is 200 s⁻¹. This empirical relation cannot be safely extrapolated to damaging temperatures or used to assign the same coefficient to every channel process.

When myelin is lost, transverse current leakage increases and effective membrane capacitance may rise. The axial current reaching the next node can be insufficient or too slow to recruit a regenerative event. Thus demyelination can slow conduction, cause temporal dispersion across a nerve population, or block conduction completely. An axon may partially compensate through redistribution of channels, but the altered geometry and increased energetic demand can limit the effectiveness of that compensation. Myelin is consequently important not only for speed but also for reliable transmission.

Integrated predictions from matched interventions and control measurements

A useful way to compare ionic interventions is to ask first whether they change reversal potential, conductance, availability or passive geometry. Lowering extracellular sodium changes the sodium equilibrium potential and thus the driving force at a given Vm. Blocking a population of sodium channels primarily reduces the available conductance through those pathways. Prolonged conditioning depolarisation may reduce availability through inactivation without changing the number of channel proteins. Loss of myelin changes the electrical load and transfer of local current. These interventions can all reduce excitability or conduction reliability, but they need not produce the same voltage-clamp and propagation records.

Imagine an experiment in which a perturbation decreases the early inward current and reduces spike height. If the reversal potential of the isolated early component shifts toward less positive voltage, an altered sodium gradient is a possible explanation. If the reversal remains similar but maximal current is smaller at several command voltages, reduced available sodium conductance is more plausible. If the test current returns to normal after prolonged recovery at a negative holding voltage, reversible inactivation is a plausible contributor. This sequence of tests illustrates how multiple measurements can discriminate between alternative mechanisms that would otherwise look similar in a current-clamp trace.

An intervention that reduces a potassium conductance can sometimes make the membrane more excitable during an initial stimulus because opposing current is smaller. It can also broaden the spike and extend the period of depolarisation, thereby increasing sodium inactivation or delaying recovery. Repeated stimuli may then reveal a lower ability to follow rapid input. Thus increased initial excitability and decreased capacity for high-frequency repetitive firing are not contradictory outcomes. Their mechanisms are separated by time: an initial stimulus tests the rested membrane, whereas a repetitive train tests its recovery between successive events.

Temperature introduces another time-dependent comparison. If a hypothetical kinetic process has a time constant of 4 ms at one temperature and its rate doubles after warming by ten degrees, its time constant becomes 2 ms, because time constant is the reciprocal of the relevant rate in a simple first-order process. A velocity might also increase, but it cannot generally be predicted by the same factor without a propagation model. Temperature can alter passive resistance, ion equilibrium potentials and multiple channel kinetics, so the Q10 of one process is not the Q10 of an entire fibre. In an injured or demyelinated axon, the safety reserve may be limited, and altered timing can produce responses different from those of an intact fibre.

When studying insulation loss, it is informative to record an upstream node and a downstream node simultaneously during controlled stimuli. A normal upstream action potential followed by a delayed downstream spike indicates that local initiation is preserved but transfer is slowed. A normal upstream action potential with no downstream spike indicates failure somewhere in the intervening path, if recording and stimulus conditions are valid. Observing a small downstream subthreshold depolarisation strengthens the interpretation that current reached the region but failed to recruit regenerative activation. Such observations do not by themselves prove that myelin is the only altered structure; they identify a functional pattern.

Finally, the relation between action potentials and subsequent physiological outcomes can be understood without leaving the chapter's boundaries. A motor axon that fails to propagate a spike cannot reliably deliver the electrical event to its terminal even if the muscle itself remains excitable. A sensory fibre with altered sodium recovery may transmit occasional signals yet fail during a rapid stimulus sequence. A neural circuit receiving inputs through axons with different propagation delays may experience a changed pattern of arrival times even when each individual axon still fires. These connections motivate subsequent chapters on synaptic integration, reflexes and coordinated movement. The essential unifying principle is that membrane excitability determines whether a regenerative event can be created, while electrical coupling and recovery determine whether that event reaches its destination at the appropriate time.

15. Misconceptions and examination traps

Threshold is always −55 mV

It varies with channel availability, membrane conductance and prior voltage history. Treat numerical textbook thresholds as examples.

Textbook illustrations often assign one representative numerical threshold because it makes the waveform easier to label. Compare the initial membrane state and current balance instead of choosing a value solely because it appears in a diagram.

A positive Vm means sodium must leave

Compare Vm with ENa; sodium still enters if Vm is below ENa and its channels conduct.

Students commonly confuse the sign of membrane voltage with the sign of electrochemical driving force. Subtract the ion's reversal potential from Vm before deciding the direction of current.

The spike peak equals ENa

The peak is a dynamic current balance, typically below ENa in a conventional fast axon.

Dominant sodium permeability during the upstroke is often simplified to a pure sodium membrane. At the peak consider all simultaneous currents and whether external or axial current is present.

The pump repolarises every spike

Rapid repolarisation mainly follows channel-mediated outward current and loss of inward current; the pump maintains gradients.

The pump restores gradients over long periods and is often confused with the channel currents that temporarily use those gradients. Distinguish millisecond events from long-term metabolic maintenance.

All sodium channels open together

Population conductance reflects changing probabilities of activation and availability.

Smooth population currents can conceal probabilistic individual events. Use channel availability and open probability instead of assuming perfect synchrony.

Closed and inactivated channels are equivalent

Both may be nonconducting, but available closed channels can activate while inactivated channels need recovery.

Both states lack current, so current alone may not reveal which state exists. A conditioning and recovery protocol tests availability at a matched command voltage.

Absolute refractory means voltage cannot be changed

An imposed current may change voltage without creating a second normal regenerative spike.

A large stimulus can force charge onto the membrane even when regenerative channels cannot recover. Look for a self-sustaining spike rather than a stimulus-shaped voltage deflection.

Relative refractory means no stimulus works

A sufficiently strong stimulus can sometimes recruit a second spike because some channels have recovered.

Refractory is sometimes incorrectly treated as a single all-or-none interval. Compare the response to a normal and an increased test pulse.

All-or-none means compound nerve amplitude never changes

Recruitment and synchrony change the summed extracellular signal.

Extracellular recordings combine multiple sources; a single-fibre intracellular waveform is a different measurement. Identify recruitment and synchrony before attributing amplitude changes to spike height.

A stronger stimulus must increase each spike amplitude

Within a usual operating range it changes firing rate or recruitment more reliably.

Graded input and regenerative output are different. For a sustained input compare the number or timing of spikes, not just the amplitude of the first spike.

Current only moves forward

Axial current spreads in both directions; refractory membrane behind the wave limits backward regeneration.

Diagrams often draw one direction arrow to show an advancing impulse, not the direction of every local current. Ask whether the adjacent tissue has recently fired.

Saltatory conduction skips all electrical events in internodes

Passive axial current and voltage spread connect active nodal events.

The word saltatory suggests a discontinuous jump, but the next node must charge through intervening axial current. A gap in active regeneration does not mean a gap in electrical continuity.

More myelin always increases velocity without limit

Geometry, internode length, nodal properties and physiological limits jointly determine velocity.

Insulation has clear benefits, yet nodal spacing and the conductive core also matter. Predict the effect only under specified geometrical assumptions.

A sodium channel blocker directly blocks potassium channels

Selective blockers isolate distinct currents; effects must be interpreted with their actual specificity.

Pharmacological block is defined by actual sensitivity and specificity, not by the appearance of the whole trace. Compare control and blocked records at matched commands.

High extracellular potassium always increases firing

Mild depolarisation may facilitate firing; sustained severe depolarisation can cause inactivation and block.

Near-threshold depolarisation and maintained depolarisation are different states. Ask how the new resting voltage changes sodium availability over time.

An extracellular spike is the intracellular Vm waveform

Recording geometry and return currents shape extracellular traces.

An extracellular potential is relative to an external reference and depends on the positions of sources and sinks. Interpret the electrode geometry before assigning phases.

The Hodgkin–Huxley exponents prove a subunit count

They are phenomenological parameters describing macroscopic gating kinetics.

The conductance equations predate direct knowledge of the protein structures. Treat their exponents as fitted functional relationships, not as structural proof.

16. Original CSIR-NET Part B practice

B1. Which variable defines membrane potential?

  1. Intracellular minus extracellular potential
  2. Extracellular sodium concentration
  3. Total cytoplasmic charge
  4. Current through potassium alone
Answer and explanation

A. Vm is a voltage difference; ionic concentrations and currents influence it but are not its definition. A concentration is measured in molar units, charge in coulombs and current in amperes; none is itself a voltage difference.

B2. Which process provides the positive feedback of a typical axonal upstroke?

  1. Pump acceleration
  2. Voltage-dependent sodium activation
  3. Potassium channel opening
  4. Sodium inactivation
Answer and explanation

B. Sodium activation increases inward current, which further depolarises the membrane and recruits additional channels. Potassium opening and sodium inactivation instead tend to limit inward feedback; pump transport does not directly supply the rapid upstroke.

B3. At Vm below ENa, open sodium channels usually carry which current?

  1. Outward
  2. No net current
  3. Inward
  4. Only capacitive
Answer and explanation

C. Vm − ENa is negative, giving inward conventional current for sodium conductance. Zero net sodium current requires Vm = ENa or zero conductance; the fact that Vm is negative or positive is not sufficient.

B4. Which event mainly causes rapid repolarisation?

  1. Delayed outward potassium current and declining inward sodium current
  2. Reverse sodium pump
  3. Increased chloride concentration only
  4. Loss of all ion gradients
Answer and explanation

A. The falling phase reflects net outward current as sodium conductance declines and potassium conductance remains elevated. A large bulk change in ionic gradients is unnecessary; the pump and chloride alone do not account for the normal rapid falling phase.

B5. Which sodium channel state must recover before activation can recur?

  1. Resting available
  2. Inactivated
  3. Open at reversal potential
  4. Leak
Answer and explanation

B. Inactivation reduces channel availability until recovery, typically favoured by repolarisation. Closed available channels can respond to depolarisation; lack of current at reversal is different from inactivation.

B6. Which finding identifies absolute refractoriness?

  1. A stronger pulse reliably evokes a spike
  2. A normal second spike cannot be elicited even by a stronger pulse
  3. Resting voltage is positive
  4. Potassium has no gradient
Answer and explanation

B. The regenerative mechanism lacks sufficient sodium availability, not merely a higher stimulus threshold. A larger forced voltage excursion is not necessarily a regenerative response; a successful second spike at higher strength indicates relative refractoriness.

B7. What can contribute to relative refractoriness?

  1. Persistent elevated potassium conductance
  2. Permanent absence of all sodium channels
  3. Elimination of capacitance
  4. Myelin acting as a wire
Answer and explanation

A. Outward potassium current and incomplete sodium recovery make a second spike harder to initiate. Complete permanent absence of sodium channels is not a reversible recovery interval, and the passive membrane and myelin are not the defining causes here.

B8. What normally happens to the amplitude of a single recovered spike as a moderate stimulus becomes stronger?

  1. It doubles
  2. It becomes zero
  3. It stays approximately stereotyped
  4. It equals stimulus amplitude
Answer and explanation

C. Once threshold is crossed, regenerative channel changes largely determine spike shape. Stimulus amplitude determines whether threshold is reached, not the peak voltage in a one-to-one relationship; partial recovery is an important exception.

B9. Which is a common code for increased sustained input?

  1. Greater number of sodium ions travelling from soma to terminal
  2. Higher firing rate
  3. Permanent positive membrane voltage
  4. Lower axial resistance within each spike
Answer and explanation

B. More frequent spikes can encode stimulus intensity within the normal firing range. Axonal propagation velocity and the rate of individual spikes have different dimensions; sustained depolarisation can also eventually cause block.

B10. A voltage clamp directly controls which quantity?

  1. Membrane voltage
  2. Channel number
  3. Potassium concentration
  4. Axonal radius
Answer and explanation

A. Feedback holds Vm near a commanded value and records compensating current. A clamp neither fixes channel number nor controls all ion concentrations. Those are separate experimental variables.

B11. What causes an immediate capacitive transient after a voltage step?

  1. Bulk sodium gradient collapse
  2. Charging membrane capacitance
  3. Long-range neurotransmission
  4. Myelin synthesis
Answer and explanation

B. Changing voltage requires charge transfer to the membrane capacitor before the voltage becomes steady. The transient follows C dV/dt and does not require an active ion-selective channel to open.

B12. What does h represent in the classical sodium conductance expression?

  1. Axon diameter
  2. Potassium activation
  3. Sodium availability
  4. Temperature alone
Answer and explanation

C. The h factor decreases with inactivation, reducing conductance despite activation. m describes rapid sodium activation and n describes delayed potassium activation; h does not represent a physical diameter.

B13. Which expression represents the delayed potassium current in the classical model?

  1. gK,max n⁴(Vm − EK)
  2. gNa,max m³h
  3. CmVm alone
  4. ENa/EK
Answer and explanation

A. The n⁴ activation factor scales maximal potassium conductance and is multiplied by driving force. The sodium m³h term is a conductance factor and must be multiplied by the sodium driving force; capacitance belongs to the separate charge-balance term.

B14. What does patch clamp permit under suitable conditions?

  1. Direct measurement of action potential conduction distance only
  2. Resolution of discrete single-channel currents
  3. Direct measurement of protein sequence
  4. Elimination of all current noise
Answer and explanation

B. A high-resistance seal permits very small channel currents to be recorded. Patch clamp does not directly reveal amino acid sequence; finite bandwidth and noise remain experimental limitations.

B15. Which structures regenerate the spike along a typical myelinated axon?

  1. Internodal glial nuclei
  2. Myelin layers
  3. Nodes of Ranvier
  4. Extracellular matrix
Answer and explanation

C. Nodes contain high densities of sodium channels, whereas internodes mainly support passive spread. Myelin and internodes support passive spread rather than the major regenerative inward currents at the nodes.

B16. How does myelin change effective internodal electrical properties?

  1. Raises transverse resistance and lowers capacitance
  2. Lowers resistance and raises capacitance
  3. Removes axial current
  4. Raises both without exception
Answer and explanation

A. Insulation reduces current leak and decreases effective capacitance per unit area. Reducing transverse resistance would increase leakage, the opposite of myelin insulation; axial current remains essential.

B17. Which effect follows from increasing axon diameter with resistivity held constant?

  1. Greater axial resistance per length
  2. Lower axial resistance per length
  3. No change in axial resistance
  4. Zero membrane capacitance
Answer and explanation

B. Cross-sectional area increases with the square of radius, reducing axial resistance. A larger radius means more conducting area, not zero membrane capacitance or increased axial resistance.

B18. What primarily supplies energy for the fast ionic flux of a spike?

  1. Immediate ATP hydrolysis by each sodium channel
  2. The existing electrochemical gradients
  3. Diffusion from the soma
  4. Myelin metabolism alone
Answer and explanation

B. Channels permit movement down gradients maintained by metabolism over longer periods. ATP supports maintenance of the gradients; ion channels do not hydrolyse ATP for each passive crossing in the ordinary spike.

B19. What is a compound action potential?

  1. An action potential of one sodium channel
  2. A sum of recorded contributions from multiple activated fibres
  3. The voltage of a single mitochondrion
  4. A resting potential with no stimulus
Answer and explanation

B. Extracellular nerve recordings sum contributions and depend on recruitment and temporal dispersion. This is not a single-channel or single-membrane record; the sum depends on which fibres are recruited and their timing.

B20. What usually happens to nerve conduction with moderate cooling?

  1. Velocity increases indefinitely
  2. Kinetics and conduction tend to slow
  3. All membrane conductances become zero
  4. ENa becomes identically zero
Answer and explanation

B. Cooling slows many voltage-dependent channel transitions and delays propagation. The temperature dependence is valid only within a physiological range and need not be identical for every channel.

  2. Repolarising
  3. Zero

  4. 200 mV/ms
Answer and explanation

B. 100 pA / 50 pF = 2 V/s = 2 mV/ms; this is an instantaneous slope. The pA and pF prefixes cancel; converting V/s to mV/ms leaves the numerical value unchanged.

C3. An isolated sodium current reverses at +55 mV. At a +75 mV command, predict its sign if channels are open.

  1. Inward
  2. Zero
  3. Outward
  4. Undefined
Answer and explanation

C. Vm − ENa = +20 mV; the driving force reverses despite depolarisation. At +55 mV the current would vanish if the channel remained open; above that value the sodium current is outward, not inward.

C4. A maintained depolarising voltage step produces a transient inward current followed by delayed outward current. What best explains this?

  1. Voltage was necessarily changing
  2. Time-dependent conductances at fixed voltage
  3. Only pump reversal
  4. No ionic channels
Answer and explanation

B. The clamp keeps Vm fixed; sodium activation/inactivation and delayed potassium activation change conductance. A capacitive transient occurs at the step onset, not throughout the steady command. A pump reversal is unnecessary.

C5. External sodium is reduced while internal sodium remains constant. What is expected for ENa?

  1. More positive
  2. Less positive
  3. No change
  4. Always equal EK
Answer and explanation

B. The concentration ratio outside/inside decreases, making the sodium equilibrium potential less positive. Neither a fixed sodium reversal nor equality with EK follows from changing extracellular sodium; use the sodium concentration ratio.

C6. Two equivalent test steps are applied after recovery intervals of 1 ms and 20 ms. The later sodium current is larger. Which explanation fits?

  1. Recovery from inactivation
  2. Increase in axial diameter within 19 ms
  3. Complete K gradient reversal
  4. More ATP hydrolysis by the recording electrode
Answer and explanation

A. At the same test potential, more channels may have recovered from inactivation during the longer interval. Channel number and axial geometry need not change over the short recovery interval. Matched commands hold the driving force constant if concentrations remain stable.

C7. For m = 0.5, h = 0.8, what fraction of maximum sodium conductance follows m³h?

  1. 0.40
  2. 0.10
  3. 0.80
  4. 0.25
Answer and explanation

B. 0.5³ × 0.8 = 0.125 × 0.8 = 0.10. Multiplying m directly by h would ignore the cubic activation factor; neither variable alone gives the sodium conductance fraction.

C8. A gate has α = 2 ms⁻¹ and β = 3 ms⁻¹. Find its steady fraction and time constant.

  1. 0.6; 5 ms
  2. 0.4; 0.2 ms
  3. 0.4; 2 ms
  4. 0.2; 0.4 ms
Answer and explanation

B. x∞ = 2/5 = 0.4 and τ = 1/5 ms = 0.2 ms. The sum of rates is 5 per ms, not 5 ms. The steady fraction depends on the forward-rate fraction, not the backward-rate fraction.

C9. A 1 ms absolute refractory interval gives what theoretical rate ceiling?

  1. 100 Hz
  2. 1000 Hz
  3. 10 Hz
  4. 1 Hz
Answer and explanation

B. 1/0.001 s = 1000 Hz, a ceiling rather than a predicted sustainable firing rate. The reciprocal of a millisecond is 1000 per second, but recovery and other conductances impose additional constraints.

C10. A nerve is stimulated in the centre while both adjacent regions are rested. What occurs?

  1. Only forward propagation is possible
  2. Two waves may travel in opposite directions
  3. No impulse can start
  4. Both regions are absolutely refractory
Answer and explanation

B. Both neighbours are initially excitable; established waves leave refractory membrane behind. Direction depends on initial excitability, not a universal structural valve. Both waves can start before adjacent patches become refractory.

C11. In a cable with λ = 2 mm, a 40 mV passive deflection is measured at its origin. Estimate the steady deflection 2 mm away.

  1. 40 mV
  2. 27 mV
  3. 14.7 mV
  4. 0 mV
Answer and explanation

C. At x = λ the value is 40/e ≈ 14.7 mV. The passive cable equation is exponential, not linear; the signal at a distance equal to λ is not zero.

C12. Two fibres have equal resistivity, but fibre B has twice the radius. Compare its axial resistance per length.

  1. Fourfold greater
  2. Half
  3. One quarter
  4. Equal
Answer and explanation

C. Axial resistance per length is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area, so doubling radius divides resistance by four. The cross-sectional area, not circumference, determines the axial resistance for a cylinder; a twofold radius increase is fourfold in area.

C13. At two sites 90 mm apart, corresponding peaks occur 3 and 6 ms after stimulus. Find velocity.

  1. 15 m/s
  2. 30 m/s
  3. 45 m/s
  4. 90 m/s
Answer and explanation

B. 0.09 m / (0.006 − 0.003 s) = 30 m/s. Using absolute arrival time rather than the difference can incorrectly include initiation delay.

C14. Two fibres travel 0.1 m at 10 and 50 m/s. Find their difference in propagation times.

  1. 2 ms
  2. 8 ms
  3. 10 ms
  4. 40 ms
Answer and explanation

B. The times are 10 ms and 2 ms; difference is 8 ms. Both velocities must first be converted to travel times over the same distance. Subtracting velocities would not give a time.

C15. A mixed nerve response broadens and loses peak height with increasing distance, while identifiable components persist. What is a plausible explanation?

  1. Temporal dispersion
  2. Every spike lost all sodium
  3. Resting pump stopped immediately
  4. Extracellular sodium doubled
Answer and explanation

A. Different fibre velocities spread arrival times, reducing synchrony and peak height without requiring axon loss. The observation does not prove conduction is intact for every fibre, but preserved components and broadening support temporal dispersion over a simple proportional loss of all responses.

C16. One channel carries −3 pA at Po = 0.25 in a population of 200 identical channels. Expected macroscopic current?

  1. −600 pA
  2. −150 pA
  3. +150 pA
  4. −50 pA
Answer and explanation

B. I = NPo i = 200 × 0.25 × (−3 pA) = −150 pA. The maximum possible simultaneous current would be −600 pA if every channel were open. Multiplying by Po gives the expected rather than maximal current.

C17. A drug reduces whole-cell sodium current but does not change unitary current. Which interpretation remains possible?

  1. Only altered ion valence
  2. Reduced channel availability or open probability
  3. No channels present originally
  4. Reversal potential must equal Vm
Answer and explanation

B. Macroscopic current can decrease through N or Po without changing single-channel amplitude. A reduced whole-cell current does not imply zero driving force because the unitary current is measured as unchanged. Distinguishing N from Po needs further measurement.

C18. During control a spike is narrow. Reducing delayed potassium conductance prolongs the falling phase. Why?

  1. Outward repolarising current is reduced
  2. Sodium equilibrium potential must become zero
  3. Membrane capacitance becomes zero
  4. Axial resistance always increases
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced outward current delays return of Vm; the effect on firing rate depends on recovery and other currents. A broader spike does not require a change in capacitance or reversal potentials. The intervention directly reduces one component of the charge balance.

C19. Moderately raising extracellular K increases excitability, but a larger sustained rise abolishes spikes. Which mechanism reconciles both?

  1. Increased K gradient at high K
  2. Initial depolarisation followed by reduced sodium availability
  3. Pump generates each spike
  4. Myelin gets thicker immediately
Answer and explanation

B. Depolarisation initially brings Vm nearer threshold but prolonged strong depolarisation can inactivate sodium channels. Increasing extracellular potassium reduces the outward concentration gradient, rather than strengthening it. The time dependence of sodium availability explains the biphasic effect.

C20. A proximal spike remains normal after myelin injury, while the distant node exhibits only a small subthreshold deflection. What is most directly supported?

  1. Loss of local sodium gradient at all nodes
  2. Conduction failure from insufficient recruitment of the next region
  3. Higher firing rate
  4. A normal distal spike
Answer and explanation

B. The active event can be initiated proximally, but leakage and charging demands prevent threshold at a downstream node. The proximal spike establishes that initiation can occur; the downstream voltage response does not justify an inference about every nodal sodium gradient.

C21. Two spikes occur at 12 and 17 ms in a trace containing 15 spikes in 0.5 s. What are the interval rate and mean rate?

  1. 30 Hz and 200 Hz
  2. 200 Hz and 30 Hz
  3. 5 Hz and 15 Hz
  4. 15 Hz and 500 Hz
Answer and explanation

B. Interval is 0.005 s, giving 200 Hz; mean is 15/0.5 = 30 Hz. A short interspike interval describes a local instantaneous rate; the longer observation window gives a distinct mean rate.

C22. A myelinated fibre develops unusually long internodes without compensatory changes. Why might conduction eventually fail?

  1. Current attenuation can prevent the next node reaching threshold
  2. Nodes stop needing sodium
  3. Axial resistance becomes zero
  4. Every longer internode must increase speed
Answer and explanation

A. Passive spread has finite reach; a longer internode can compromise conduction safety. Longer internodes can reduce the number of nodal events but also weaken transfer to the next node; the latter effect may dominate.

C23. At a fixed command voltage, an isolated sodium current first increases in magnitude and then decreases. In a repeat at the same command after a conditioning depolarisation, the peak current is smaller. Consider: I. The decline within the first step can reflect inactivation. II. A change of driving force is required to explain the first decline. III. The smaller second peak can reflect incomplete recovery. Which combination is best supported?

  1. I and II only
  2. I and III only
  3. II and III only
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

B. I is consistent with a decrease in availability at constant Vm; III follows because the matched second step has less available sodium current. II is unnecessary and contradicts the fixed voltage and unchanged concentration assumption. A control with a longer recovery interval would help test III.

C24. Use the schematic voltage-clamp current separation diagram. After selective suppression of the early transient sodium component, the delayed positive current remains. Which interpretation is most justified?

  1. The delayed current is necessarily carried by sodium because the command is depolarising.
  2. The remaining delayed current is consistent with outward potassium conductance; measure its reversal or ionic sensitivity to confirm.
  3. The membrane voltage has necessarily fallen below EK.
  4. All channel proteins have become inactive.
Answer and explanation

B. The schematic uses outward positive, so the late current is compatible with potassium efflux at a command above EK. Its timing and persistence under sodium block support a separate component, but ionic identity requires reversal or substitution controls. A clamp command does not need to fall during the trace.

C25. In the illustrative recovery table, the sodium test current has returned to 0.55 of control after 4 ms. Under a matched current-clamp protocol the second spike still fails. Which inference is warranted?

  1. No sodium channels can conduct after 4 ms.
  2. The recording proves that absolute refractoriness lasts exactly 4 ms.
  3. Partial inward-current recovery need not provide enough regenerative reserve to overcome opposing currents.
  4. The sodium equilibrium potential must be zero.
Answer and explanation

C. A measurable inward current demonstrates some channel function, but a spike requires adequate net positive feedback. The data do not distinguish absolute from relative refractoriness without stronger test stimuli, and do not measure ENa.

C26. Use the latency table. If a drug adds 1 ms of common initiation delay but does not alter propagation, what are the new latencies and the slope-derived conduction velocity?

  1. 3.0, 4.5, 6.0 ms; 20 m/s
  2. 3.0, 4.5, 6.0 ms; 10 m/s
  3. 2.0, 3.5, 5.0 ms; 20 m/s
  4. 3.0, 5.5, 8.0 ms; 12 m/s
Answer and explanation

A. The drug adds 1 ms to each latency. Differences remain 1.5 ms per 30 mm, so the slope-derived velocity is 20 m/s. A change in intercept is not evidence of slower conduction, and a change in slope would contradict the stated unchanged velocity.

18. Original SET and integrated practice

S1. Assertion: the Na/K ATPase is essential for sustained excitability. Reason: it maintains electrochemical gradients used by channels.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false
Answer and explanation

A. The pump supports long-term gradients rather than directly generating each rapid phase. The reason provides the causal explanation, not just a parallel association. ATP-dependent pumping restores the gradients that channels subsequently use.

S2. Assertion: sodium channels may carry inward current when Vm is positive. Reason: ENa may be more positive than Vm.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

A. A positive absolute voltage does not eliminate the inward sodium driving force if Vm is below ENa. The two statements are causally related by the equation I = g(Vm − E). Positive Vm alone is not sufficient to identify direction.

S3. Which experiment best tests whether reduced extracellular sodium changes overshoot?

  1. Measure paired intracellular spikes before and after controlled sodium substitution
  2. Count glial nuclei only
  3. Measure unrelated muscle length
  4. Change every extracellular ion at once
Answer and explanation

A. Controlled substitution and a matched reference recording isolate sodium-dependent changes while preserving other conditions as far as possible. Changing several ions together would confound the inference. A repeated controlled sodium substitution compares the predicted change with the same preparation at baseline.

S4. Which evidence separates regenerative propagation from passive spread?

  1. Comparable spikes at distant excitable regions while subthreshold potentials attenuate
  2. Only membrane voltage at rest
  3. Only cell-body morphology
  4. Only extracellular ion totals
Answer and explanation

A. Persistence of regenerated waveforms despite passive attenuation is direct evidence of active recruitment. Morphology and resting voltage cannot demonstrate regeneration. Direct comparison of propagated spikes and passive deflections tests the defining distinction.

S5. If Q10 = 2 for a process over 20–30°C and the rate at 20°C is 60 s⁻¹, estimate rate at 30°C.

  1. 30 s⁻¹
  2. 60 s⁻¹
  3. 120 s⁻¹
  4. 600 s⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. The stated rate ratio for a ten-degree rise is two, giving 120 s⁻¹. Q10 describes a multiplicative rate ratio for the specified temperature interval; the same assumption cannot automatically be extended beyond it.

S6. Which trace most strongly suggests relative rather than absolute refractoriness?

  1. A normal second spike with any weak pulse
  2. No regenerative response to any pulse
  3. A second spike only with an increased stimulus
  4. A voltage step with no test pulse
Answer and explanation

C. Partial recovery permits firing if sufficient depolarisation is achieved. Failure at every stimulus strength is characteristic of absolute refractoriness; success only at increased strength identifies a partially recovered membrane.

S7. Why can a model predict a free action potential from voltage-clamp measurements?

  1. Measured conductance kinetics can be coupled to the membrane charge-balance equation
  2. Voltage never changes in a free spike
  3. Clamp proves all neurons identical
  4. Gating variables equal ion concentrations
Answer and explanation

A. The current balance determines voltage dynamically from experimentally characterised conductances. The model predicts time-dependent voltage because charge balance couples channel currents back to Vm. It need not assume identical channels across all neurons.

S8. A larger compound nerve signal follows stronger stimulation. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Each fibre necessarily generates a taller spike
  2. More fibres may have been recruited
  3. All refractory periods have vanished
  4. Myelin thickened within a millisecond
Answer and explanation

B. Compound amplitude reflects summed contributions and cannot directly establish individual spike amplitude. Population amplitude can change through recruitment and synchrony; individual spike height requires a single-fibre or equivalent measurement.

19. Revision and mastery

A spike is a regenerative, time-dependent event powered by existing ionic gradients. Fast inward current initiates positive feedback; sodium inactivation and delayed outward conductance terminate it. Refractoriness shapes spike timing, while local axial currents recruit adjacent membrane. Myelination improves passive transfer between active nodes.

High-yield terminology

Threshold
The transition to regenerative inward feedback under specified conditions, not a universal voltage.
Driving force
The difference Vm − E for a specified ionic pathway.
Inactivation
Loss of channel availability even though depolarisation may persist.
Safety factor
The reserve of depolarising current available for recruitment of the next region.
Node of Ranvier
Exposed axonal region where active inward current regenerates the impulse.
Temporal dispersion
Separation of arrival times of different components of a compound nerve response.
