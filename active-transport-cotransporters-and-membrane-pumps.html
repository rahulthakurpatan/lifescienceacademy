1. Why living cells cannot rely on diffusion alone

A freshwater cell, an intestinal epithelial cell and a contracting muscle fibre face different problems, yet each must maintain a chemical interior unlike its surroundings. An animal cell commonly contains much more potassium than the surrounding fluid, much less sodium and an extremely low concentration of free calcium in its cytosol. These differences are not the natural outcome of leaving a permeable membrane in a solution. If all relevant ions were allowed to equilibrate independently, the gradients would tend to dissipate. The cell would lose a means of controlling its volume, accumulating nutrients and producing electrical or chemical signals.

The lipid bilayer solves one part of this problem. Its hydrophobic interior restricts charged and strongly polar molecules, giving the cell control over when particular solutes cross. But a barrier cannot on its own restore a gradient once ions have escaped. Channels and facilitated diffusion carriers provide selective routes, but their net movement is downhill in electrochemical free energy. A carrier that merely reduces the activation barrier accelerates arrival at equilibrium; it cannot move the equilibrium itself. To build a gradient, the cell needs a molecular mechanism that couples solute movement to an independent source of free energy.

Consider an intestinal cell that is already richer in glucose than the gut lumen. A glucose-selective passive carrier cannot continue accumulating free glucose in that cell under those conditions. Yet the same cell has abundant extracellular sodium, and the negative potential of its interior favours sodium entry. A protein can link sodium entry and glucose entry within a single conformational cycle. Glucose then gains free energy while sodium loses a greater amount. This is not a violation of the second law: the combined process is energetically favourable. The sodium gradient itself exists because another protein spends ATP on sodium extrusion. Energy therefore passes through an intermediate store before it is used for nutrient uptake.

The term active transport refers to the uphill component of such a coupled process. It does not mean that every molecule transported by an active transporter necessarily moves uphill at every instant. A pump may encounter unusually favourable ionic gradients, or a cotransporter may reverse if the gradients are reversed. We must distinguish the usual physiological direction, the direction favoured in a stated experiment and the molecular mechanism by which energy is coupled. This distinction will prevent many apparently contradictory statements later.

To understand the mechanisms, recall three prerequisites: concentrations specify amounts per volume, voltage specifies electrical energy per unit charge, and Gibbs free energy predicts the favoured direction of a process at constant temperature and pressure. A transport protein changes how quickly a process can occur; a coupled energy source changes the free-energy balance of the complete cycle. We will first construct that balance, then examine the molecular machines that implement it.

2. The thermodynamic price of moving a solute

Imagine transferring a small amount of a neutral solute from compartment A to compartment B while keeping both compartments large enough that their concentrations hardly change. In a dilute solution, its chemical potential is μ = μ° + RT ln a, where a is dimensionless activity relative to a standard state. Chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs free energy: it measures the energy change associated with adding an infinitesimal amount of material. Subtracting the chemical potential in A from that in B cancels the common standard-state term when the solute and solvent standard state are identical. The molar cost of transfer is consequently RT ln(aB/aA). For sufficiently dilute ideal solutions the ratio of activities may be approximated by the ratio of concentrations in identical units.

If the destination is ten times as concentrated, the logarithm is positive. Moving one mole there requires energy, even though some molecules will still travel in that direction because of random motion. If the destination is less concentrated, the chemical contribution is negative. For a charged solute we must also account for electrical energy. A mole of ions of charge number z carries zF coulombs, where F is approximately 96,485 C mol⁻¹. Moving that charge through a potential difference ψB − ψA adds zF(ψB − ψA) joules per mole. The charge number includes its sign: +1 for sodium, +2 for calcium and −1 for chloride.

Electrochemical free energy of transfer

ΔGA→B = RT ln(aB/aA) + zF(ψB − ψA).

R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in kelvin; voltage is in volts. Divide the answer by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹. A negative ΔG favours A→B, a positive ΔG opposes it, and zero marks equilibrium for that transfer.

In the rest of the chapter define Vm = ψin − ψout. Inward ion movement therefore has the voltage contribution zFVm, while outward movement has −zFVm. A negative interior favours inward movement of cations and outward movement of anions electrically. It does not automatically determine the net direction because concentration may oppose voltage. The formula applies to transfer between two defined aqueous phases, assuming temperature is the same, activity approximations are appropriate and extra terms such as major pressure differences need not be considered.

At 310 K, RT is 2.577 kJ mol⁻¹. For a tenfold concentration ratio the chemical work is RT ln 10 = 5.93 kJ mol⁻¹; a 60 mV potential difference corresponds to 5.79 kJ mol⁻¹ for a monovalent charge. Thus even a seemingly small membrane voltage can compete with a tenfold concentration difference. For calcium the same potential gives twice the electrical work because z = +2. For an uncharged sugar voltage contributes nothing directly, although voltage can indirectly affect its transport by changing the driving force on a coupled ion.

As an example, suppose extracellular chloride is 120 mM, intracellular chloride 30 mM and Vm = −70 mV at 310 K. Inward chemical work is 2.577 ln(30/120) = −3.57 kJ mol⁻¹. Inward electrical work is (−1)(96.485)(−0.070) = +6.75 kJ mol⁻¹. Their sum is +3.18 kJ mol⁻¹, so passive chloride entry is unfavourable despite the higher extracellular concentration. A chloride pathway under these conditions favours net outward flux, provided the channel is open. This is why the phrase against a concentration gradient is not a sufficient definition of active ion transport.

Setting ΔG to zero and solving for voltage would yield the equilibrium potential of the ion. That calculation belongs to the next node, but we already have the tool required to evaluate every pump and cotransporter: write the movement of each substrate in its actual direction, multiply each free-energy change by its stoichiometric coefficient, and sum. The cost of a repeated complete cycle, not the isolated cost of a single microscopic conformational step, determines its thermodynamic direction.

3. Primary and secondary active transport: coupling the whole cycle

ATP hydrolysis in an aqueous cellular environment is thermodynamically favourable because the chemical potentials of ADP and inorganic phosphate, relative to ATP and water, make the products lower in Gibbs free energy under typical cellular conditions. The process is not explained by claiming that breaking one phosphate bond releases energy in isolation: bond breaking costs energy, and formation, solvation and stabilization of products contribute to the net free-energy change. For biochemical standard-state notation, ΔGhyd = ΔG°′hyd + RT ln Q, with Q expressed using the activities of ADP, phosphate and ATP under the specified biochemical convention. Water is taken as constant. The familiar −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹ is an approximate biochemical standard value, not a universal intracellular value. In living cells the actual hydrolysis free energy is often substantially more negative, with magnitude varying with the ATP/ADP/phosphate state and other conditions.

If a machine hydrolyses m ATP per turnover and moves n molecules of one solute uphill, the cycle balance is ΔGcycle = mΔGhyd + nΔGtransport. Additional moved species must each be included. For spontaneous forward cycling this sum must be negative; equality defines the reversible thermodynamic limit. A real pump running at appreciable speed has a negative driving force and dissipates some free energy. An energetically possible gradient is not proof that a particular transporter can achieve it: binding, turnover, leaks and other pathways affect the realised state.

In primary active transport, the transporting machinery directly couples movement to a chemical or physical source of energy. ATP-driven pumps dominate the examples in this node. Light-driven proton pumps and respiratory redox-driven proton translocation illustrate that primary transport is not restricted to ATP hydrolysis, although their detailed photochemistry and electron transport belong elsewhere. Secondary active transport instead harnesses the free energy of a previously established electrochemical gradient. It does not need an ATP-hydrolysing site on the cotransporter itself. This distinction can be tested with isolated membranes: if an imposed sodium gradient can drive a sugar uptake pulse even without ATP, the immediate energy source is the sodium gradient rather than sugar-associated ATP hydrolysis.

Transport terminology describes two independent properties. A uniporter translocates one species; a symporter couples two or more species moving in the same direction; an antiporter couples species moving in opposite directions. These words describe relative directions, not the source of energy. A GLUT uniporter is passive, but a primary pump moving one kind of ion can also be called a single-species transporter. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase exchanges ions in opposite directions yet is classified as primary active transport because it directly consumes ATP. An exchanger may operate downhill overall even when one participant moves uphill. Similarly, an electroneutral carrier can consume a gradient, and an electrogenic channel can be passive. Neither electrical current nor the number of substrates by itself identifies the energy mechanism.

Coupling requires molecular constraints. If a protein could bind ATP and hydrolyse it freely while allowing ions to diffuse through an always-open pore, most of the chemical energy would be wasted. A useful transporter coordinates binding, gating and release. The protein cycles through conformations whose binding affinities and access to the membrane faces differ. In an ideal tightly coupled machine, the favourable and unfavourable reactions are linked so that completion of one full forward cycle necessarily includes the other. Imperfect coupling and leak are biologically important exceptions, especially in damaged or experimentally altered proteins. We will return to them when interpreting ATP consumption and flux measurements.

4. Alternating access: turning energy into directional movement

What prevents a transported ion from simply escaping back to its original compartment? A channel, when open, provides a continuous aqueous pathway across a membrane. A conventional alternating-access carrier instead exposes its binding site to one side at a time. In an outward-facing state it can acquire substrates from outside, then pass through an occluded state in which the bound substrates cannot exchange with either bulk compartment, and subsequently become inward-facing. Occlusion protects the energy investment by preventing the protein from becoming an uncontrolled pore. The empty carrier eventually returns to its original conformation.

The carrier's binding site is not a rigid pocket with a permanently fixed affinity. Local changes in helices, packing and charged residues can make a binding site favourable in one conformation and less favourable in another. A downhill ion can stabilize a loaded conformation that is otherwise rarely occupied. The presence of the coupled substrate may permit the gate to close and the protein to change orientation. Release occurs because affinity and access have changed and because the destination concentrations influence binding and unbinding. Thermal motion enables the conformational transitions, while the free-energy difference biases the distribution and net cycling direction. There is no need to imagine a protein consciously sensing which side is downhill.

ATP-driven machines use an analogous logic but have an additional chemical transition. ATP binding, hydrolysis or transient phosphorylation changes the relative energies of conformations. In P-type pumps a covalent phosphorylated intermediate coordinates ion access; in ABC proteins ATP binding and hydrolysis change the arrangement of cytosolic nucleotide-binding domains; in rotary ATPases chemical changes generate torque. Their detailed structures differ, but all must prevent productive energy input from being uncoupled from vectorial movement.

Transport is reversible in thermodynamic principle. At equilibrium microscopic events still occur, but forward and reverse productive cycles balance. When the coupled driving force changes sign, a reversible transporter can reverse net direction if its kinetic mechanism allows it. Some ATPases have biological regulatory mechanisms that restrict wasteful reversal. A slow reverse rate is therefore a kinetic observation, not evidence that a protein can drive an unfavourable cycle without a source of energy. Similarly, a large favourable ΔG does not imply instantaneous movement. Flux depends on transporter number, substrate availability, affinities and transition rates. An energetics calculation tells us whether forward transport is possible, whereas a rate experiment tells us how much transport actually occurs.

5. P-type ATPases: phosphorylation as a mechanical switch

P-type ATPases form a large family whose name comes from the transient phosphorylation of a conserved aspartate in the protein during the transport cycle. This is chemically distinct from phosphorylation of a regulatory serine residue by a kinase. The pump itself accepts a phosphoryl group from ATP and later releases inorganic phosphate. The intermediate is part of the catalytic cycle and is repeatedly created and removed. The transported ion is not chemically phosphorylated. P-type pumps include animal sodium–potassium pumps, calcium pumps, gastric H⁺/K⁺ ATPases, plant plasma membrane proton pumps and certain lipid flippases. Other members transport selected metals. The family is defined by its catalytic mechanism, not by a single transported substrate.

The membrane domain contains binding sites formed by residues on transmembrane helices. Cytosolic domains carry out ATP binding, phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. In the classical Post–Albers model, the E1 ensemble exposes high-affinity sites to the cytoplasm for the substrate that will be exported from that side. Binding the correct ion favours phosphorylation. The phosphorylated protein moves through an occluded configuration and toward an E2-P state, in which access and affinity have changed so that substrate is released on the other side. In a countertransporting pump, a second substrate binds to the E2-P configuration from the opposite side. The pump then dephosphorylates, occludes the second substrate and returns toward E1. The details, including counterions and the timing of events, differ between family members; E1 and E2 should be understood as ensembles, not two static snapshots covering all intermediate states.

Three observations distinguish this mechanism from simple facilitated diffusion. First, ATP hydrolysis depends on occupancy of appropriate transport sites. Second, a transient covalent phosphoenzyme can be detected with labelled phosphate transferred from ATP. Third, experimentally changing ions on the relevant membrane face changes the distribution or decay of phosphorylated intermediates. In the classic calcium pump, for example, the amount of labelled phosphoenzyme is influenced by calcium binding to the cytosolic sites and by subsequent catalytic transitions. These observations link the chemical reaction to the vectorial transport mechanism. Detection of an ATP-binding protein alone would not establish primary active transport.

Orthovanadate, a phosphate analogue, is a useful broad inhibitor of many P-type ATPases because it stabilizes phosphate-like catalytic states. Its presence in a cell experiment is not sufficient to identify a particular transporter, since different enzymes may also respond to it. Specific substrates, orientation and more selective inhibitors are required to discriminate members. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump offers the best example of how the general E1/E2 mechanism produces a precise and physiologically essential ion exchange.

6. The sodium–potassium pump: mechanism, discovery and stoichiometry

In 1957, Jens Christian Skou described an ATPase from crab peripheral nerve membranes whose activity depended on sodium, potassium and magnesium. The important insight was not simply that the membrane contained an ATP-hydrolysing enzyme; ATPases already existed in many contexts. Rather, the dependence on the very ions known to undergo active transmembrane exchange made it plausible that the enzyme was the biochemical machinery responsible for the exchange. Later work connected ion flux, the sidedness of ion effects, ATP hydrolysis, inhibition by cardiac glycosides and the phosphoenzyme intermediate. Skou's Nobel lecture describes the progression from correlated observations to a mechanistic identification. This historical sequence illustrates a valuable exam principle: an association with ATP hydrolysis is evidence, but direct coupling must be established by additional tests.

In most animal plasma membranes the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase uses one ATP to export three Na⁺ from the cytosol and import two K⁺. The catalytic α subunit contains ten transmembrane helices and cytosolic catalytic domains; a β subunit supports proper folding, membrane delivery and function. FXYD regulatory proteins modulate activity in many tissues. Different isoforms have distinct kinetic and regulatory properties. The essential teaching point is that both ion movements are coordinated in a single catalytic cycle. The stoichiometry is not three independent sodium pumps plus two potassium channels.

Start in E1, where binding sites face the cytosol and preferentially bind three intracellular sodium ions. ATP binds at the nucleotide-binding domain and transfers a phosphoryl group to the conserved aspartate. The pump occludes the ions; they are temporarily shielded from both aqueous compartments. Structural changes then expose the sodium binding region to the outside in the E2-P state. The altered affinity allows sodium to dissociate into extracellular fluid even when extracellular sodium concentration is high. The chemical energy investment has changed the conformational landscape rather than directly pushing the ions with a phosphate group.

Two extracellular potassium ions now bind to sites accessible from outside. Their binding stimulates dephosphorylation and formation of an occluded potassium-containing state. The protein returns toward E1, exposes the ions to the cytoplasm and releases them. ATP can facilitate particular conformational transitions in addition to its role in phosphorylation; the simplified stoichiometric ledger remains three Na⁺ out, two K⁺ in, one ATP hydrolysed in the usual forward cycle. There is a difference between the number of individual ion-binding events and the number of ions transported per complete turnover.

Since three positive charges leave while two enter, the net transport is one positive charge out per cycle. The pump is therefore electrogenic. Its direct current tends to make the interior more negative. However, it is incorrect to attribute the entire negative resting potential to that net charge transfer. The major indirect role is to establish the sodium and potassium gradients that passive ion permeabilities can exploit. A cell may show a small immediate depolarization after pump inhibition and a much larger delayed change as gradients dissipate. The exact time course depends on the conductances, cell volume, ion reservoirs and other transport systems. The next chapter will describe the quantitative relationship between selective permeability and voltage.

Maintaining the gradients also protects the cell against osmotic swelling. Intracellular impermeant anions attract counterions and contribute to the osmotic burden. With passive ion equilibration alone, many animal cells would accumulate excess solute and water. Continuous sodium extrusion, together with potassium permeability and other ionic mechanisms, supports a non-equilibrium steady state compatible with stable volume. In a pump–leak model, the pump balances inward sodium leakage and outward potassium leakage over time. No net change in intracellular sodium concentration at steady state does not imply zero sodium transport. It means the rates of opposing pathways balance. ATP is consumed even when concentration measurements appear constant.

The gradient is also a reserve of energy used by sodium-coupled nutrient uptake and calcium extrusion. When ATP generation is impaired, the sodium pump slows, sodium entry through existing pathways may continue, and the intracellular concentration of sodium rises. The sodium driving force falls; cotransport becomes weaker and some exchangers may reverse. Calcium homeostasis, cell volume and signalling can all be disrupted before the membrane itself loses structural integrity. These secondary consequences explain why a single ATPase can have effects on transport processes that never bind ATP.

Ouabain and related cardiac glycosides inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase by interacting with its externally accessible region, favouring an E2-P-like state. In an appropriate controlled experiment, ouabain-sensitive ATPase activity or rubidium uptake can provide evidence for sodium-pump operation. Rubidium can substitute for potassium in many such assays. The interpretation requires attention to species and isoform sensitivity and to other rubidium transport routes. In cardiac cells partial inhibition can raise intracellular sodium, weaken sodium-driven calcium extrusion and increase cellular calcium availability. This is a mechanistic example of connected gradients, not a recommendation concerning medication.

7. Worked energy accounting for the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase

Use a simplified animal cell at 310 K: [Na⁺]out = 145 mM, [Na⁺]in = 15 mM, [K⁺]out = 4 mM, [K⁺]in = 140 mM and Vm = −70 mV. Treat concentration ratios as activity ratios. Assume the pump hydrolyses ATP with ΔGhyd = −55 kJ mol⁻¹ under these conditions. First calculate work for one mole of each ion in the pump direction. Sodium moves from inside to outside, so its voltage difference is +0.070 V. Its chemical work is 2.577 ln(145/15) = 5.85 kJ mol⁻¹; its electrical work is +6.75 kJ mol⁻¹. The total is +12.60 kJ per mole of Na⁺ exported.

Potassium enters, so its chemical work is 2.577 ln(140/4) = 9.16 kJ mol⁻¹ and electrical work is −6.75 kJ mol⁻¹. Its total inward work is +2.41 kJ per mole of K⁺. Both species are moved against their electrochemical gradients in this example, although inward potassium is electrically favoured. Multiply each by its coefficient: 3(12.60) + 2(2.41) = 42.62 kJ per mole of forward cycles. Add ATP hydrolysis: ΔGcycle ≈ 42.62 − 55 = −12.38 kJ mol⁻¹ cycles. Thus forward cycling is allowed under the assumptions. The ideal work divided by the supplied chemical energy is about 77.5%; this is a state-dependent energetic fraction, not a measured universal efficiency or ATP cost.

The electrical terms can be simplified before substitution. Three monovalent cations exported cost −3FVm; two imported cost +2FVm. The net is −FVm, consistent with one positive charge outward. The concentration contribution is RT[3 ln(Naout/Nain) + 2 ln(Kin/Kout)]. This form makes the effect of voltage easy to predict. Hyperpolarizing the inside makes the net outward positive-charge movement more costly, even though it favours the inward potassium step.

For a second calculation suppose 6.0 × 10⁶ sodium ions per second leak into a cell, and this influx is the only sodium entry route. At steady state a pump that is tightly coupled must export the same number of sodium ions per second. Since three sodium ions leave per cycle, the required 2.0 × 10⁶ cycles s⁻¹ consume 2.0 × 10⁶ ATP molecules s⁻¹ and import 4.0 × 10⁶ potassium ions s⁻¹. Dividing the ATP number by Avogadro's constant gives 3.32 × 10⁻¹⁸ mol ATP s⁻¹. With a 55 kJ mol⁻¹ hydrolysis magnitude, the associated chemical power is approximately 1.83 × 10⁻¹³ W. A stable steady state also requires matching potassium efflux or another compensating transport mechanism. We cannot infer total cellular ATP consumption from this isolated pump flux because the cell spends ATP on many processes.

Notice that the stoichiometric calculation concerns the number of productive cycles, whereas the energy calculation concerns the driving force per cycle. A transporter might exhibit a substantial ATPase rate yet a much lower transport rate if it is uncoupled. Conversely, the energy per cycle can change without a change in integer coupling ratio. This separation of counting from thermodynamics is essential for interpreting data.

8. Calcium ATPases: maintaining a low cytosolic calcium signal

Calcium differs from sodium and potassium in that the free cytosolic concentration in resting cells is usually near 100 nM, while extracellular free calcium may be around 1–2 mM. The endoplasmic reticulum and sarcoplasmic reticulum can store much higher concentrations than the cytosol. The steep gradient allows even brief opening of calcium-permeable pathways to raise cytosolic calcium substantially. Calcium can then bind regulatory proteins, trigger secretion or contribute to muscle contraction. To make calcium a reusable signal, the cell must actively remove it from the cytosol after each response.

Two major P-type calcium pumps perform complementary tasks. The plasma membrane Ca²⁺ ATPase (PMCA) extrudes calcium into extracellular fluid; it commonly couples one Ca²⁺ transported to ATP hydrolysis, with proton countertransport depending on conditions and isoform. The sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ ATPase (SERCA) moves cytosolic calcium into the ER or SR lumen, normally transporting two Ca²⁺ per ATP in a tightly coupled cycle. They are not redundant: PMCA lowers total cellular calcium when calcium has entered across the plasma membrane, whereas SERCA redistributes calcium into an intracellular store that can later release it. A sodium/calcium exchanger can also remove calcium from the cell using a sodium gradient, but its mechanism is secondary rather than ATP-driven.

In SERCA the E1 state presents two high-affinity cytosolic calcium sites formed by transmembrane residues. Binding of two calcium ions enables the ATP-dependent phosphorylation of the catalytic aspartate. The protein occludes the ions and shifts toward the E2-P conformation, exposing lower-affinity binding sites to the lumen. Calcium dissociates and proton binding helps stabilize subsequent transitions. Dephosphorylation and return toward E1 restore cytosolic calcium affinity, with protons released toward the cytosol. Proton countertransport is part of the real cycle; avoid treating SERCA as a universal simple four-charge current source because the actual net electrical transfer depends on counterions and coupling conditions.

The pump must overcome both chemical and possibly electrical costs. For calcium moving out of a cell with 100 nM free cytosolic calcium into 1 mM extracellular calcium at 310 K and Vm = −60 mV, the concentration ratio is 10,000: the concentrations must first be expressed in identical units. Chemical work is 2.577 ln(10,000) = 23.73 kJ mol⁻¹. Electrical work for Ca²⁺ export is 2 × 96.485 × 0.060 = 11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. Their sum is 35.31 kJ mol⁻¹ Ca²⁺, ignoring any coupled proton movements. Export of one Ca²⁺ by ATP hydrolysis of magnitude 55 kJ mol⁻¹ is energetically plausible under those simplified conditions. For SERCA, do not reuse plasma membrane voltage or extracellular calcium: use free calcium activities and electrical potential on the ER/SR faces and include proton coupling if provided. Total stored calcium, much of which can be buffer-bound, is not the same as free calcium activity.

Calmodulin activation of PMCA illustrates how a rise in cytosolic calcium can enhance its own clearance. In cardiac muscle the small regulatory protein phospholamban inhibits SERCA in its unphosphorylated state; phosphorylation relieves this inhibition, promoting calcium sequestration and relaxation. Thapsigargin inhibits SERCA and can cause ER calcium stores to decline as leak continues without effective refilling. A prolonged increase in cytosolic calcium after thapsigargin should not automatically be assigned to direct opening of a plasma membrane channel: store depletion may subsequently activate calcium entry through a distinct pathway. This example separates primary pump inhibition from downstream signalling responses.

9. Proton and other P-type pumps at the cell surface

Animals commonly use sodium gradients as an intermediate energy store, whereas plants and fungi commonly use a proton gradient across the plasma membrane. A plant plasma membrane H⁺ ATPase exports protons from the cytoplasm to the apoplast by ATP hydrolysis. This removes positive charge and tends to make the cell interior electrically negative; it also acidifies the outside relative to the cytosol. Both effects can favour inward proton movement through appropriate secondary transporters. Plant root cells use this stored energy to take up mineral nutrients, and many plant tissues use it to load sucrose. A proton pump is therefore not merely a pH regulator: it provides a common energy currency for multiple chemically different substrates.

Plant H⁺ ATPases belong to the P-type family, so their proton transfer is linked to a phosphoaspartate catalytic intermediate rather than a rotating membrane ring. Their activity can be modulated by an autoinhibitory C-terminal region; phosphorylation of a regulatory residue near the C-terminus and association with 14-3-3 proteins can relieve inhibition. This regulatory phosphorylation should not be confused with the transient catalytic phosphorylation of aspartate. The first changes the probability or rate of pump activity in response to signals; the second is part of each productive transport turnover. Changes in pump activity can alter proton motive force and thereby change the activity of a broad set of transporters.

The gastric H⁺/K⁺ ATPase exchanges cytosolic hydrogen ions for external potassium at the apical membrane of acid-secreting parietal cells. It uses ATP to drive acid accumulation in a strongly acidic lumen. The exchange is generally treated as electroneutral in the ideal equal-charge cycle; effective stoichiometry can depend on conditions. Potassium recycling and chloride movement are necessary parts of sustained secretion at the cell level. The key principle is that electroneutrality does not make transport passive: moving H⁺ across an enormous activity gradient has a large chemical cost. Other P-type pumps include P4 ATPases that move selected phospholipids between leaflets, supporting membrane asymmetry. They demonstrate that the family name does not imply cation transport exclusively. A lipid flippase changes leaflet composition and is not equivalent to a passive scramblase that dissipates asymmetry.

10. Proton motive force: connecting voltage, pH and transport

Proton transport deserves special attention because biological proton concentrations are usually expressed as pH rather than molarity. Since pH = −log10 aH⁺, the activity ratio aB/aA = 10pHA−pHB. Substituting this into the general electrochemical equation for z = +1 gives ΔGH,A→B = 2.303RT(pHA−pHB) + F(ψB−ψA). This is a signed molar energy. For an inward movement define ΔpH = pHin − pHout; dividing by F yields ΔGH,in/F = Vm − (2.303RT/F)ΔpH. This voltage-equivalent quantity is one common signed convention for proton motive force. Other texts may report its magnitude or define the two sides in the opposite order; always establish the convention before using a formula.

At 298 K, one pH unit corresponds to approximately 59.2 mV; at 310 K, 61.5 mV. Suppose a plant cell has pH 7.2 inside, pH 5.7 outside and an inside-negative potential of −120 mV at 298 K. Proton entry has a chemical contribution 2.303RT(5.7−7.2) = −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹ and an electrical contribution 96.485(−0.120) = −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. Their sum is approximately −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹. A one-to-one proton/sucrose symporter can therefore use this favourable entry to accumulate neutral sucrose uphill, in principle until sucrose chemical work offsets the proton free energy. At the ideal zero-net-flux boundary, ln(Sin/Sout) = 20.14/2.478 ≈ 8.13, giving a ratio around 3.4 × 10³. This is a theoretical reversible maximum for fixed pH and voltage, not a typical measured sucrose concentration ratio.

A pH difference reflects free proton activity, not total acid stored in a compartment. Buffers bind or release protons and can absorb many transported protons with only a modest pH change. The rate of pH change therefore depends on buffering capacity and volume as well as proton flux. When comparing an acidic lysosome and a plant vacuole, do not infer their total proton contents from pH alone. Similarly, a proton pump that creates an electrical gradient without a large pH gradient can still provide energy for proton-linked processes. The contributions can even oppose each other, depending on the compartment and voltage.

In bacteria a respiratory chain or, in certain species, light-driven pumps may create a proton electrochemical gradient. That gradient can supply uptake of nutrients, ion exchange, flagellar rotation or ATP synthesis. Some bacteria instead use sodium motive force for selected processes. These are variations on the same principle: primary energy transduction generates a gradient, while downstream machines convert the stored free energy into useful work. The detailed respiratory chain has been considered in Unit 1, and organism-level adaptations belong to microbial physiology rather than this chapter.

11. V-type ATPases and the acidification of compartments

Endosomes and lysosomes contain enzymes, receptors and cargo whose behaviour is pH dependent. An early endosome is moderately acidic and a lysosome is more acidic; the exact values vary with cell type and conditions. The low luminal pH is not an inevitable property of a lipid membrane. It is maintained dynamically by proton transport, buffering and leak. A vacuolar-type H⁺ ATPase, abbreviated V-ATPase, hydrolyses ATP on the cytosolic side and moves protons into the compartment. Plant vacuoles and many fungal vacuoles also use this mechanism. Some specialized cells position V-ATPases at the plasma membrane to acidify an extracellular space.

The machine contains a soluble peripheral V1 domain and a membrane-embedded V0 domain. V1 contains catalytic sites for ATP hydrolysis, arranged around a central rotor. Energy-dependent conformational changes rotate the central shaft and membrane proteolipid ring relative to stationary elements. A proton enters a half-channel, binds a protonatable residue on the rotor ring, and is carried around by rotation until it reaches the other half-channel, where release becomes possible. The separated half-channels prevent free proton passage straight through the protein. The stator keeps the catalytic head from rotating with the shaft, enabling torque to produce relative motion. V-ATPases do not use the phosphoaspartate cycle characteristic of P-type pumps.

As protons enter a lumen, its positive charge tends to oppose further entry. Without an electrical compensation pathway, even a pump capable of large chemical work would rapidly face a growing voltage cost. Anion entry or cation exit can help dissipate that charge separation and allow continuing acidification. The real ion pathways depend on the organelle and can be exchangers rather than simple chloride channels. For example, lysosomal chloride/proton exchange can contribute to ion homeostasis in a more complex way than a passive chloride conductance. The central inference is that steady pH depends on the coupled behaviour of proton influx, counterion movement, buffering and proton leak.

Suppose a lysosomal lumen has pH 5.0, cytosol pH 7.2, and ψlumen − ψcytosol = +30 mV at 310 K. For H⁺ movement from cytosol to lumen the chemical work is 2.303(2.577)(7.2−5.0) = 13.05 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical work is +2.89 kJ mol⁻¹, giving +15.94 kJ mol⁻¹ per proton. Three such proton transfers would cost 47.82 kJ per mole of cycles; four would cost 63.76 kJ. If the available ATP hydrolysis magnitude were 55 kJ mol⁻¹, three would be possible and four impossible for that hypothetical perfectly coupled cycle, absent other energy sources. Actual rotary coupling is governed by ring size and catalytic stoichiometry rather than automatically adjusting to the nearest integer calculated from energy; this example is an energy ceiling only.

V-ATPase inhibition with bafilomycin A1 reduces ATP-dependent acidification. A pH-sensitive probe in an intact cell may reveal gradual alkalinization, as passive leak and other pathways continue after pumping stops. A dye accumulation change can also reflect vesicle number, probe loading or membrane potential, depending on the probe. A calibrated luminal pH measurement is therefore more diagnostic than raw fluorescence alone. V-ATPase assembly and disassembly, pump abundance in membranes and catalytic regulation can alter acidification. These regulatory mechanisms allow compartments to change their identity and function without changing the chemistry of the proton itself. Detailed vesicular sorting and organelle-specific pathways are reserved for 2.A.10 and 2.B.

12. F-type ATP synthase: the same energy logic in reverse

F-type ATP synthases are related rotary machines found in bacterial plasma membranes and the energy-transducing membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts. Their usual physiological task in those energy-producing settings is to synthesize ATP using downhill ion movement, most commonly proton movement. The membrane F0 sector contains the rotor ring and half-channel arrangement, and the F1 sector contains three major catalytic β sites arranged around a rotating central γ shaft. Rotation changes the conformations of those sites, favouring ADP and phosphate binding, ATP formation and ATP release in sequence. A complete rotation typically produces three ATP molecules under ideal coupling. The number of ions transported per rotation depends on the number of ion-binding rotor subunits, so there is no universal H⁺/ATP ratio.

The chemistry is particularly instructive because it can run in either direction. If the proton motive force is sufficiently large, downhill H⁺ movement provides the energy needed to drive ATP synthesis. If the gradient collapses while ATP is abundant, some F-type machines can operate in reverse, hydrolysing ATP and pumping ions against their gradient. Biological control mechanisms can limit wasteful reverse activity; direction must be inferred from free energy and the physiological context, not from the name ATPase alone. In the mitochondrial inner membrane, protons normally move from the intermembrane space toward the matrix through ATP synthase. In chloroplasts they usually move from the thylakoid lumen toward the stroma, where the catalytic head is located. This is consistent with ATP production on the side that contains the catalytic sector.

Take an idealized c-ring with 12 proton-binding subunits. One complete revolution transfers 12 protons and yields three ATP, so the rotor coupling is 4 H⁺ per ATP. A proton gradient providing 15 kJ mol⁻¹ per proton could supply 60 kJ mol⁻¹ ATP, sufficient to support synthesis costing 50 kJ mol⁻¹ in that model. If the ring contained nine subunits, the ideal ratio would be three protons per ATP and the same gradient would supply only 45 kJ mol⁻¹, insufficient at that stated ATP synthesis cost. The number need not be an integer per ATP molecule because it is an average over a complete rotation. A mitochondrial accounting exercise must also distinguish this rotor ratio from the energetic costs of importing phosphate, exchanging nucleotides and maintaining the gradient. Those processes were studied in the oxidative phosphorylation chapter rather than being duplicated here.

Oligomycin blocks proton conduction through mitochondrial F0 in susceptible systems. In intact coupled mitochondria, inhibition reduces ATP synthesis and can slow respiratory electron transport as the proton gradient rises and resists additional pumping. A protonophore instead dissipates the gradient and may accelerate electron transfer while reducing ATP synthesis. These different responses illustrate why ATP production and oxygen consumption are not interchangeable readouts. The distinction between F-type and V-type is their usual direction of operation and physiological role, not an assertion that rotary energy coupling itself is fundamentally irreversible.

13. ABC transporters: ATP binding cassettes and diverse cargo

Some molecules that must cross membranes are neither simple ions nor small hydrophilic nutrients. Hydrophobic drugs, lipids, peptides and many metabolites may require specific active transport proteins. ATP-binding cassette (ABC) proteins include transporters with a characteristic conserved nucleotide-binding domain on the cytosolic side. A typical functional transporter has two transmembrane domains that form the cargo pathway and two nucleotide-binding domains that bind ATP. These units can occur within a single polypeptide or be supplied by separate polypeptides that assemble into a functional complex. Their membrane domains vary substantially because their cargo is diverse.

In a simplified exporter cycle, an inward-facing conformation has access to cytosol or the inner membrane leaflet, allowing substrate binding. ATP binding promotes association of the nucleotide-binding domains. This rearranges transmembrane helices, closes access to the original side and favours an outward-facing or release-competent state. Cargo dissociates on the other side; ATP hydrolysis and product release then help reset the nucleotide-binding domains and the membrane pathway. This model connects ATP-dependent conformational change to alternating access. The ATPase cycle is not a P-type phosphorylation reaction, even though both consume ATP, and it is not rotary pumping. The two nucleotide-binding sites can differ in catalytic behaviour, and coupling ratios differ among proteins and conditions; do not assume all ABC proteins hydrolyse exactly two ATP for every transported molecule simply because they have two nucleotide-binding domains.

Bacterial ABC importers often use a soluble or membrane-associated substrate-binding protein that captures nutrients outside the cytoplasmic membrane and presents them to the transporter. In Gram-negative bacteria such proteins can reside in the periplasm. This arrangement is particularly useful when the target nutrient is scarce because selective capture increases access to substrate. Bacterial exporters and many eukaryotic ABC transporters do not use this arrangement. Direction is specified by the organization of the complete transport cycle, not merely by which side contains ATP.

P-glycoprotein (ABCB1) exports many structurally diverse hydrophobic compounds and can lower intracellular drug accumulation. Increased expression of this transporter in some tumour cells can contribute to multidrug resistance, although drug resistance has many other causes. The TAP transporter uses ATP-dependent peptide transport into the ER, providing peptide substrates for MHC class I antigen presentation. ABCA1 participates in lipid efflux and HDL formation. These examples illustrate how a conserved ATP-utilizing module can be combined with membrane domains adapted to distinct cellular functions. Detailed immune processing and lipid metabolism belong to their dedicated chapters.

An important exam exception is CFTR (ABCC7). Although it belongs to the ABC protein family and its opening and closing are regulated by phosphorylation and nucleotide binding/hydrolysis, it functions mainly as an anion channel, permitting chloride and bicarbonate flow down their electrochemical gradients. ATP controls gating; it does not obligatorily drive each chloride ion uphill through an alternating-access pump cycle. This distinction is why ATP dependence alone cannot establish that an ion pathway is an active transporter. Measure the direction of net ion movement and its dependence on electrochemical gradients before assigning mechanism.

14. Secondary transport: deriving the accumulation limit

Suppose an ideal symporter transfers n sodium ions and one neutral substrate S from outside to inside during one forward cycle. No ATP is directly consumed by that protein. The cycle's free energy is n[RT ln(Nain/Naout) + FVm] + RT ln(Sin/Sout). The first bracket is often negative because sodium is more concentrated outside and the interior is negative. If the substrate is already concentrated inside, the last term is positive. The forward cycle continues to be thermodynamically favoured as long as the sodium energy decrease is larger than the substrate energy increase.

At the ideal reversible boundary, set the sum to zero. Moving the sodium term to the other side and dividing by RT gives ln(Sin/Sout) = n ln(Naout/Nain) − nFVm/RT. Taking the exponential yields Sin/Sout = (Naout/Nain)n exp(−nFVm/RT). This formula explains the importance of coupling stoichiometry: doubling the number of driving ions doubles the available free energy and squares the ideal accumulation ratio, other conditions unchanged. If the transported solute is charged, add its own electrical term and solve again rather than blindly applying the neutral-solute formula.

At 310 K let sodium outside be 140 mM and inside 14 mM, with Vm = −60 mV. One inward sodium has ΔG = 2.577 ln(0.1) + 96.485(−0.060) = −5.93 − 5.79 = −11.72 kJ mol⁻¹. A 1:1 symporter could theoretically sustain Sin/Sout = exp(11.72/2.577) ≈ 94.4. A 2:1 symporter could sustain exp(23.44/2.577) ≈ 8.9 × 10³. At zero voltage the corresponding ratios are merely 10 and 100. This calculation shows why voltage and stoichiometry strongly influence secondary transport. At the limiting ratios, however, net forward flux is zero, not maximal. Real systems reach a lower steady state due to leak, metabolism, competing flux and incomplete preservation of the driving gradient.

For antiport, the correct procedure is identical except that the transported species cross in opposite directions. Write a separate destination/source ratio and voltage difference for each one. A common mistake is to substitute all intracellular/extracellular ratios without changing signs. Another is to treat two opposite directions as necessarily electroneutral. Opposite movement of a divalent cation and a monovalent cation transfers different total charge unless their stoichiometries compensate. We now apply these rules to major physiological cotransporters.

15. Sodium–glucose cotransport and epithelial directionality

Sodium-glucose linked transporters (SGLTs) bring glucose into cells along with sodium. Human SGLT1 typically couples two Na⁺ to one glucose, whereas SGLT2 couples one Na⁺ to one glucose. Because the sugar is neutral and the coupled sodium ions carry positive charge inward, both are electrogenic in their normal uptake direction. Their different stoichiometries have physiological consequences: for the same sodium gradient and voltage, a two-sodium cycle can drive a higher limiting glucose concentration ratio than a one-sodium cycle, at the expense of bringing more sodium in per sugar molecule. Actual affinity and turnover are separate kinetic properties and cannot be calculated from the coupling ratio alone.

In the intestine, SGLT1 is localized in the apical brush-border membrane of absorptive epithelial cells. It takes up glucose or galactose together with sodium from the lumen. On the basolateral side glucose can leave through a facilitative carrier such as GLUT2 when the glucose gradient favours exit. Meanwhile the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase continuously removes cytosolic sodium into the interstitial fluid. Thus two physically separated transport proteins and a pump cooperate to produce vectorial transfer across the epithelium. If all three were randomly distributed over the cell surface, local gradients might still be maintained but efficient directional transfer from lumen to blood would be compromised. Cell polarity is as important as the properties of each carrier.

Renal proximal tubule cells use SGLT2 in early segments to recover much filtered glucose; SGLT1 in later segments can recover glucose remaining in the tubular fluid. The different coupling ratios contribute to the ability to recover glucose at low luminal concentration, while expression, affinity and capacity also determine each segment's actual contribution. This is a transport example rather than a complete kidney physiology treatment. The same mechanism explains why oral rehydration solutions containing appropriate glucose and sodium can support absorption: solute uptake promotes water movement through osmotic effects in the epithelium. SGLT is not an ATP-driven water pump, nor does glucose uptake alone guarantee net water movement independently of all other gradients.

A simplified coupled cycle starts with sodium binding to an outward-facing transporter, which helps favour glucose binding and productive occlusion. Conformational changes expose the loaded site to the cytosol, where the favourable sodium gradient and changed affinities favour release. Return of the unloaded transporter completes the cycle. This explanation is deliberately a general alternating-access model; individual microscopic binding orders and gating intermediates should not be assumed identical for every SGLT. Removing sodium outside inhibits normal glucose uptake even when ATP is supplied to the cell, because ATP is not the immediate energy source of the SGLT cycle. Adding a sodium gradient to an isolated vesicle containing SGLT can generate transient glucose uptake without ATP, provided orientation, voltage and other conditions are suitable.

Phlorizin inhibits sodium–glucose transporters and has been useful in distinguishing sodium-dependent uptake from facilitative glucose transport. SGLT2-selective drugs target renal glucose reabsorption, showing how the site of transporter expression gives physiological specificity to an intervention. For exam reasoning, compare a rapid inhibition of SGLT with inhibition of the basolateral sodium pump. The first can immediately stop coupled uptake while sodium gradients remain; the second progressively reduces the gradient that powers uptake. With a deliberately maintained gradient, the immediate secondary carrier can continue functioning after ATPase inhibition. Distinguishing direct and indirect effects avoids misidentifying a secondary carrier as an ATPase.

16. Sodium–hydrogen and sodium–calcium exchangers

The plasma membrane sodium–hydrogen exchanger NHE1 typically brings one Na⁺ into a cell in exchange for one H⁺ leaving. It is electroneutral: the positive charge entering balances the positive charge leaving. Its cycle still moves hydrogen ions against a chemical gradient when the extracellular medium is more acidic than the cytoplasm. By using the sodium gradient, NHE1 helps recover from intracellular acidification. Activation increases proton extrusion after an acid load; the rate depends on intracellular pH and regulatory factors, not simply on the free-energy difference. At physiological conditions there can be competing acid-loading and buffering processes that prevent pH from reaching the theoretical equilibrium of the exchanger.

Derive the ideal equilibrium for the 1:1 exchange. ΔG = RT ln(Nain/Naout) + FVm + RT ln(Hout/Hin) − FVm. Voltage terms cancel, leaving RT ln[(Nain/Naout)(Hout/Hin)]. At zero net driving force, Hout/Hin = Naout/Nain. A tenfold outward sodium concentration ratio could theoretically sustain a tenfold higher proton activity outside, or one pH unit more alkaline inside. For instance, at pH 7.0 inside and 6.5 outside with a tenfold sodium ratio, Hout/Hin = 100.5 = 3.16; the product with Nain/Naout is 0.316, and ΔG is negative. Forward acid extrusion is favourable. Electrically neutral exchange has not escaped thermodynamics; it simply has no net voltage term in the ideal fixed cycle.

Some Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers have different coupling stoichiometries or occur on organellar membranes, where substrate and proton gradients may favour the opposite physiological direction. Bacteria can use proton-driven sodium export, for example. Do not generalize the NHE1 ratio and direction to all family members. Amiloride derivatives can inhibit particular Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers, but amiloride also affects other transport proteins; specificity matters in interpreting experiments. The direction of pH change after inhibition depends on which membrane the exchanger occupies and on other acid-base pathways.

The sodium–calcium exchanger NCX is a different kind of antiporter. In the common plasma membrane forward mode, three Na⁺ enter in exchange for one Ca²⁺ leaving. Three positive charges enter and two positive charges leave, so the net charge is one positive inward. Its voltage dependence therefore differs from that of NHE1. NCX uses the stored sodium electrochemical gradient to help remove cytosolic calcium. Because it can carry relatively large calcium fluxes, it is particularly important in excitable cells, where transient calcium entry must be cleared. PMCA and SERCA use ATP directly, whereas NCX depends on the sodium gradient, which itself is maintained in part by the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase.

With sodium 140 mM outside and 14 mM inside, calcium 1 mM outside and 0.0001 mM inside, Vm = −60 mV and T = 310 K, the energy gained from three inward sodium ions is 3(−11.72) = −35.16 kJ mol⁻¹ cycles. The outward calcium work is +35.31 kJ mol⁻¹ as calculated earlier. The sum is close to +0.15 kJ mol⁻¹: under these deliberately chosen concentrations forward calcium extrusion is slightly unfavourable. Near the balance point small concentration or voltage changes can reverse direction. Raising intracellular calcium lowers the outward calcium chemical cost and favours extrusion; raising intracellular sodium reduces the sodium driving force and can favour reversal. Depolarization also makes the net inward positive-charge transfer less favourable. Therefore one cannot assume NCX always exports calcium merely because it is named a calcium exchanger.

17. Other coupled systems: proton symport, chloride and group translocation

The plant proton gradient powers uptake of many solutes. For an H⁺/sucrose symporter, a proton enters down its electrochemical gradient while neutral sucrose may enter uphill. Proton/nitrate transport is another useful example: nitrate bears negative charge, so a negative cell interior electrically opposes nitrate entry. Coupling nitrate entry to protons can compensate for this cost. The net electrical character depends on the actual stoichiometry: one H⁺ and one NO₃⁻ entering would carry zero net charge, whereas two protons with one nitrate would carry one positive charge inward. It is not appropriate to assign a single ratio to every nitrate transporter. In bacteria the lactose permease LacY couples lactose uptake to inward proton movement. Supplying a proton gradient to a membrane with LacY can drive transient sugar accumulation; collapse of the gradient removes the immediate driving force even if lactose binding remains possible.

Several animal ion cotransporters move multiple ionic species together. The Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter (NKCC) normally carries one sodium, one potassium and two chloride ions in the same direction. Its ideal cycle is electroneutral: +1 +1 −2 = 0. Yet its cycle may use the sodium gradient to bring chloride above the concentration at which passive chloride channels would equilibrate it. The related K⁺–Cl⁻ cotransporter (KCC) moves potassium and chloride together and often favours their outward movement in animal cells. These transporters help regulate cell volume and chloride concentration. Chloride transport direction cannot be guessed simply from the membrane voltage because the complete electroneutral cycle has no net voltage contribution. Changes in the driving concentrations or regulation may alter direction. The Na⁺–Cl⁻ cotransporter (NCC) is another electroneutral example.

Consider NKCC with sodium outside/inside 140/14, potassium outside/inside 5/100, and chloride outside/inside 110/20 mM, at 310 K. Inward free energy is RT ln[(14/140)(100/5)(20/110)²], because voltage terms cancel. The product is 0.0661, so the energy is about −7.00 kJ mol⁻¹ cycles. Despite inward potassium being chemically uphill, the combined inward transport is favourable. If intracellular chloride rises while sodium and potassium gradients stay fixed, the product increases and approaches unity. The ideal chloride equilibrium ratio can be solved by taking the square root of the inverse sodium and potassium ratios. This predicts a limiting chloride inside concentration of 110 × √[(140/14)(5/100)] ≈ 77.8 mM, not the chloride channel equilibrium. Net movement at this ratio is zero for the ideal NKCC cycle. In a real cell chloride channels and KCC can establish a different steady concentration.

A related but distinct mechanism is bacterial group translocation, exemplified by the phosphoenolpyruvate-dependent phosphotransferase system (PTS). The incoming sugar is chemically modified during entry through sequential transfer of phosphate from phosphoenolpyruvate via proteins of the PTS. This is not a conventional ATP-driven membrane pump or an ordinary ion-gradient symporter. The product, a phosphorylated sugar, is chemically different from the extracellular substrate and can be retained in the cell and used in metabolism. A calculation comparing free glucose concentrations alone does not account for the full reaction. The important distinction is that transport can be coupled to chemical transformation of the substrate itself. Detailed PTS regulation and bacterial metabolic adaptation belong in microbial physiology.

18. Electrogenicity, electroneutrality and the difference between rate and energy

To calculate net charge transfer, assign an inward direction as positive and sum the signed ion charge numbers multiplied by the signed number transported. For an outward ion use a negative movement coefficient. Thus the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase transfers −3 +2 = −1 elementary positive charge inward-equivalent per cycle, SGLT1 transfers +2, NHE1 transfers +1 −1 = 0, NCX transfers +3 −2 = +1 and inward NKCC transfers +1 +1 −2 = 0. For chloride, an inward chloride ion contributes negative charge. This arithmetic is distinct from a count of the total number of molecules that cross the membrane. An exchanger may move multiple charged substrates yet transfer zero net charge, and a channel that is energetically passive may transfer substantial charge.

For a molar cycle rate J in mol s⁻¹, the net inward positive-charge current equivalent is qFJ, where q is the signed charge transfer per cycle. If using an electrophysiology convention in which outward positive current is positive, reverse the sign. For example, 2 × 10⁶ Na⁺/K⁺ pump cycles per second in one cell correspond to one outward elementary charge each, giving a current magnitude 2 × 10⁶ × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C s⁻¹ = 3.20 × 10⁻¹³ A, or 0.320 pA. This direct current can influence voltage, but the cell's voltage is also controlled by all other currents and the membrane capacitance. An electroneutral transporter may still alter voltage indirectly by changing intracellular ion concentrations that influence channel currents. Detailed current–voltage and equilibrium potential analysis follows in 2.A.09.

Transport proteins also exhibit saturation. If a fixed number of transporters carry solutes by binding and conformational cycling, raising substrate concentration eventually leaves nearly all productive sites occupied, and the rate approaches a finite limit. Under suitably simplified initial-rate conditions one may use v = Vmax[S]/(Km,app + [S]) at fixed driving-ion concentration and voltage. This resembles Michaelis–Menten kinetics but the apparent parameters depend on membrane conditions and on the rates of individual transport steps. A two-substrate cotransporter cannot generally be described by a single universal Km. When sodium or voltage changes, a glucose uptake curve can change even if the intrinsic glucose-binding chemistry is unaltered.

For example, suppose a cell has a glucose transport Vmax of 120 nmol min⁻¹ and an apparent Km of 2 mM at fixed sodium and voltage. At 2 mM glucose, the predicted initial rate is 60 nmol min⁻¹; at 8 mM it is 96 nmol min⁻¹. Doubling the number of identical functional transporters at the same conditions doubles Vmax and both rates, without necessarily changing Km,app. It does not double the ideal equilibrium accumulation ratio, which depends on thermodynamics and coupling stoichiometry. In a leaky cell more transporter may increase the observed steady state by better competing with leakage; that is a kinetic consequence, not a change to the thermodynamic limit.

Similarly, a Hill coefficient fitted to sodium-dependent uptake does not by itself prove an integer sodium:substrate transport ratio. It reports apparent cooperativity under a model. A stoichiometric claim needs independent flux ratios, charge-to-substrate ratios or thermodynamic reversal measurements, with confounding pathways controlled. This is a common Part C reasoning distinction between parameters of a fitted curve and quantities that follow from conservation of matter or charge.

19. How experiments distinguish pumps from cotransporters

A transport claim becomes strong when several independent observations support the same mechanism. Consider reconstituting a purified membrane protein into artificial lipid vesicles. If a selected substrate accumulates uphill inside only when ATP is supplied to the face bearing the catalytic domain, and uptake is accompanied by ATP hydrolysis in a reproducible ratio, that is strong evidence of primary active transport. Vesicle orientation matters: ATP cannot readily cross an intact lipid bilayer, and a random orientation can make a fraction of proteins inaccessible to externally supplied ATP. An experiment with no uptake might therefore reflect wrong orientation rather than an inactive protein. Protein-free vesicles and a substrate-impermeability control are essential to establish background movement.

To identify secondary active transport, impose a driving ion gradient across vesicles containing the candidate carrier while withholding ATP. If labelled sugar is taken up and transiently accumulates above its equilibrium concentration for a passive sugar uniporter, the gradient provides evidence for energy coupling. As the ion gradient dissipates, the accumulated sugar may leave again, creating an overshoot in the uptake curve. The peak is not caused by a change in the chemical identity of the sugar when a nonmetabolizable tracer is used; it reflects a transient driving force. Replacing the driving ion with an impermeant substitute while controlling osmolarity and voltage tests ion dependence. A protonophore can collapse a proton gradient, but it can also alter voltage; to assign the separate pH and electrical contributions, control or measure both components.

For a V-ATPase, an acidic luminal pH alone is not sufficient proof of ATP-driven pumping. An ATP-dependent acidification assay should include a pH-sensitive reporter, calibration and controls for passive proton leak. Inhibiting V-ATPase while measuring luminal pH and ATP turnover can connect proton movement to its immediate energy source. Adding a suitable counterion pathway may increase acidification without increasing pump abundance because the electrical cost is reduced. Such a result is not evidence that the counterion pathway itself is the proton pump.

In intact cells ATP depletion is a useful perturbation but a poor stand-alone classifier. It affects primary pumps, gradient maintenance, metabolism, cytoskeletal functions and possibly transporter trafficking. A secondary transporter may fail indirectly after ATP depletion. Conversely, an ATP-dependent channel may remain passive with respect to the transported ion. The strongest examination answer proposes controls that isolate immediate energy sources, distinguish initial from late effects and account for alternative pathways. This reasoning is more informative than memorizing an inhibitor list.

20. Regulation, inhibitors and the integrated pump–leak steady state

A cell continuously adjusts transport to its needs. An increase in intracellular sodium increases substrate availability for the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase; a rise in calcium activates calcium clearance pathways; an acid load activates proton extrusion. Changes in phosphorylation, protein interactions, trafficking to the plasma membrane and long-term transporter expression can alter the maximum transport capacity or apparent substrate affinity. A regulator that increases the number of transporters at the surface changes the speed with which the cell approaches its steady state. It does not provide extra free energy to an individual cycle unless it also changes energy coupling, gradients or ATP chemical potential.

For a well-mixed cell of constant volume V, the rate of change of intracellular solute concentration follows dC/dt = (inward molar flux − outward molar flux + intracellular production − consumption)/V, where fluxes are in mol s⁻¹ and V is in litres if concentration is in mol L⁻¹. If volume varies, track moles first and use C = amount/V. At steady state this net balance is zero, but individual pathway rates may be nonzero. For sodium, pump extrusion must balance entry through leak channels, cotransporters and exchangers. For glucose, apical entry, metabolic consumption and basolateral exit may all occur simultaneously. For a lysosome, proton pumping, buffering and leak determine the pH response. This mass-balance framework connects membrane transport to homeostasis and explains why the disappearance of a steady-state concentration difference is often delayed after inhibiting a pump.

When an inhibitor changes the flux of another transporter, distinguish the direct molecular target from the secondary physiological effect. Ouabain directly inhibits the sodium pump in susceptible systems, and glucose transport may fall later because the sodium driving force weakens. Bafilomycin inhibits V-ATPase and may secondarily perturb acid-dependent cargo processing. Oligomycin inhibits mitochondrial ATP synthase, altering ATP supply and gradient dynamics. Thapsigargin inhibits SERCA, leading to store depletion and possible downstream calcium influx. In each case identify the first affected machine, the changed gradient or energy state and the later consequences. Drugs and experimental inhibitors are valuable evidence when combined with appropriate concentration, specificity, temporal and genetic controls; none is a perfect proof by itself.

21. Part B practice: concepts and assertion–reason

These original questions test classification and mechanism. For assertion–reason questions use the options stated in each item. Attempt the question before opening the explanation.

1. Which observation best demonstrates secondary active transport? A. ATP binding to a channel. B. Sugar accumulates uphill in vesicles with a Na⁺ gradient but no ATP. C. Sugar diffuses downhill through GLUT. D. Membrane ATP hydrolysis without flux.

Answer and reasoning. B. A maintained ion gradient can drive uphill sugar uptake without direct ATP hydrolysis. A describes gating, C is passive transport, and D lacks evidence linking hydrolysis to translocation.

2. The defining intermediate of a P-type ATPase is: A. phosphorylated substrate sugar; B. covalently phosphorylated aspartate on the pump; C. rotating c-ring; D. ATP-gated open chloride pore.

Answer and reasoning. B. The catalytic aspartyl phosphate distinguishes the family. A resembles group translocation, C is rotary ATPase architecture, and D describes CFTR-like gating.

3. For a complete Na⁺/K⁺ pump cycle, which net movement occurs? A. 1 positive inward; B. no charge; C. 1 positive outward; D. 5 positive outward.

Answer and reasoning. C. Three Na⁺ exit and two K⁺ enter; net +1 leaves. A reverses the sign, B ignores the unequal stoichiometry, D counts all ions as outward.

4. Which is normally electroneutral? A. SGLT1; B. NCX 3:1; C. NHE1 1:1; D. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase.

Answer and reasoning. C. One inward Na⁺ offsets one outward H⁺. A carries +2 inward, B +1 inward and D +1 outward.

5. Which comparison is correct? A. SERCA removes calcium to blood; B. PMCA fills the ER; C. SERCA sequesters calcium into ER/SR and PMCA extrudes calcium; D. both are Na⁺-coupled exchangers.

Answer and reasoning. C. Their destination compartments differ; both are P-type ATPases. A and B swap locations, and D confuses them with NCX.

6. Assertion: CFTR can use ATP in its gating cycle yet chloride movement through it is passive. Reason: ATP can regulate channel opening without coupling hydrolysis to each translocated chloride ion. Choose: A both true, reason explains; B both true, reason does not explain; C assertion true, reason false; D assertion false.

Answer and reasoning. A. The gating cycle controls access to a passive conductive pathway. ATP dependence does not establish uphill ion pumping.

7. Assertion: NHE1 is not influenced by an electrochemical sodium gradient because it is electroneutral. Reason: a 1:1 Na⁺/H⁺ exchange has cancelling voltage terms. A both true; B assertion true, reason false; C assertion false, reason true; D both false.

Answer and reasoning. C. Sodium chemical energy still drives the coupled process. Cancellation of net electrical work does not eliminate chemical free energy.

8. In a lysosome, increasing an appropriate counterion conductance can promote acidification because: A. chloride hydrolyses ATP; B. it reduces opposing charge buildup; C. it phosphorylates the V-ATPase; D. it removes all proton buffering.

Answer and reasoning. B. Counterion movement can limit luminal positivity. A and C are unsupported mechanisms, and D is not a consequence of conductance.

9. An ideal F-type rotor with 10 ion-binding subunits produces three ATP per rotation. What is the mean ion/ATP ratio? A. 1; B. 3; C. 10/3; D. 30.

Answer and reasoning. C. Ten ions per full revolution divided by three ATP. The mean need not be an integer. Other options misread the rotary stoichiometry.

10. A competitive inhibitor of a sugar binding site may change: A. apparent affinity and uptake kinetics; B. the sign of the universal gas constant; C. ion charge; D. the definition of chemical potential.

Answer and reasoning. A. Kinetic parameters can change without altering thermodynamic constants or the charge of transported ions.

11. Which immediately supplies energy to LacY? A. ATP bound to LacY; B. downhill proton movement; C. glucose phosphorylation; D. K⁺ hydrolysis.

Answer and reasoning. B. LacY is a proton–lactose symporter. A confuses primary and secondary transport, C resembles group translocation, and D is not a chemical energy source.

12. Assertion: a cell with constant intracellular sodium can still consume ATP at the sodium pump. Reason: sodium influx and pump-mediated extrusion can balance. A both true and reason explains; B both true without explanation; C reason false; D assertion false.

Answer and reasoning. A. A non-equilibrium steady state differs from equilibrium; fluxes may be substantial despite constant concentration.

22. Part C practice: quantitative and experimental reasoning

Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹ and RT = 2.577 kJ mol⁻¹ at 310 K unless specified. Assume ideal dilute activity ratios and tight coupling when explicitly indicated. These are original questions, not claimed previous-year questions.

1. At 310 K, sodium is 150 mM outside and 15 mM inside; Vₘ = −50 mV. Calculate inward sodium energy.

Answer and reasoning. Chemical = 2.577 ln(0.1) = −5.93 kJ mol⁻¹. Electrical = 96.485(−0.050) = −4.82. Total −10.75 kJ mol⁻¹; both favour entry. Reversing the transfer reverses both terms.

2. At 310 K an uncharged solute is 2 mM outside and 200 mM inside. What is the inward chemical cost? Can one sodium in the preceding problem power this uptake?

Answer and reasoning. RT ln(100) = 11.87 kJ mol⁻¹. One sodium provides 10.75 kJ mol⁻¹; sum +1.12, so not in a tightly coupled 1:1 forward cycle. Two sodium ions would provide 21.50, making the sum negative.

3. A 2:1 Na⁺/neutral substrate symporter operates at zero voltage and Naₒ/Naᵢ = 20. Determine the ideal maximum substrate ratio.

Answer and reasoning. Set 2RT ln(1/20) + RT ln(Sᵢ/Sₒ)=0. The ratio is 20² = 400. At this ratio net cycle flux vanishes; 400 is not the ratio at maximum speed.

4. At 310 K, pH outside 6.0 and inside 7.0, voltage −90 mV. Compute inward proton energy.

Answer and reasoning. Chemical = 2.303 × 2.577 × (6−7)=−5.93 kJ/mol; electrical = 96.485 × −0.090 = −8.68. Total −14.61 kJ/mol. For two protons per cycle multiply this by two.

5. A pump hydrolyses ATP with ΔG = −48 kJ mol⁻¹. It moves three ions each costing 17 kJ mol⁻¹. Is the specified cycle feasible?

Answer and reasoning. Transport costs 51 kJ mol⁻¹; net +3. It is not forward-favourable as stated. A change in gradients, hydrolysis potential, coupling ratio or additional energy would be required; increasing protein number alone cannot fix the balance.

6. A Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase exports 9 × 10⁷ Na⁺ per second. Find K⁺ influx, ATP consumption and net charge current magnitude.

Answer and reasoning. Divide by 3: 3 × 10⁷ cycles/s and ATP/s. K⁺ influx = 6 × 10⁷/s. Net outward current = 3 × 10⁷ × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.806 × 10⁻¹² A = 4.81 pA. Do not use total ion count for current.

7. An inward NKCC cycle moves Na⁺, K⁺ and 2 Cl⁻. At 310 K the inside/outside ratios are 0.1, 10 and 0.5 respectively. Compute ΔG at any voltage.

Answer and reasoning. The charge sum is zero; voltage cancels. Product 0.1 × 10 × 0.5² = 0.25; ΔG = 2.577 ln 0.25 = −3.57 kJ/mol cycles. Inward is favoured despite K⁺ chemical cost.

8. An NHE1 cell has Naₒ/Naᵢ = 5, pH inside 7.4 and outside 6.4. Predict direction of its ideal exchange.

Answer and reasoning. Hₒ/Hᵢ = 10; multiply by Naᵢ/Naₒ = 0.2 to obtain 2. Forward Na⁺ entry and H⁺ extrusion has positive ΔG = RT ln 2; reverse is favoured thermodynamically. Regulation can restrict actual reversal.

9. Under identical concentrations, hyperpolarization of an animal cell changes the forward driving force of which most directly: NHE1 or NCX? Explain.

Answer and reasoning. NCX has a net +1 inward charge, so hyperpolarization makes its forward cycle more favourable. NHE1 is ideal electroneutral and has no direct cycle voltage term. Changes in concentrations can still indirectly affect NHE1.

10. A pump-containing vesicle acidifies on ATP addition. Adding a protonophore collapses pH difference while ATP hydrolysis continues. Does this prove the pump stopped?

Answer and reasoning. No. The protonophore allows gradient dissipation while pumping may continue. Measure proton flux and ATP turnover independently; lack of net acidification can reflect a leak balancing pump influx.

11. In a vesicle experiment total Na⁺ uptake is 130 and inhibitor-resistant uptake is 30 nmol/min. Sugar uptake is 55, inhibitor-resistant uptake 5. Estimate coupling.

Answer and reasoning. Corrected fluxes are 100 and 50 nmol/min. Ratio 2 Na⁺ per sugar. Requires the inhibitor to isolate the coupled pathway and no substantial uncoupled ion flux. A fitted Hill slope alone would not establish this ratio.

12. A mutation retains 90% of normal ATP hydrolysis but only 15% of ion uptake in reconstituted vesicles. State a likely mechanism and two checks.

Answer and reasoning. Possible uncoupling of hydrolysis from translocation. Check vesicle integrity/leak and protein orientation/abundance; measure corrected ion flux and ATPase simultaneously. The numbers alone do not establish a structural mechanism.

13. A cell at fixed volume 2 pL has net Na⁺ entry 4 × 10⁶ ions/s after pump inhibition. Estimate initial change in intracellular Na⁺ per second.

Answer and reasoning. 4 × 10⁶/6.022 × 10²³ = 6.64 × 10⁻¹⁸ mol/s. Divide by 2 × 10⁻¹² L = 3.32 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L/s = 0.00332 mM/s. Assumes fixed volume and initial net flux; later gradients can alter rate.

14. In a neutral-sugar symporter experiment doubling transporter expression doubles initial uptake but does not double the fixed-gradient zero-flux substrate ratio. Explain.

Answer and reasoning. Initial rate depends on number of carriers and kinetics. The limiting ratio follows ΔGcycle = 0 and depends on stoichiometry and driving force. More carriers may improve actual leaky steady accumulation but not the ideal equilibrium limit.

15. In an intestinal epithelium SGLT is intact, GLUT2 intact, but basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase is blocked. What happens immediately and later?

Answer and reasoning. SGLT can initially operate while the sodium gradient persists. Over time sodium accumulates, reducing favourable sodium entry and sugar uptake. Basolateral export and metabolic consumption affect actual glucose concentration. It is indirect inhibition of secondary transport.

16. A 12-subunit F0 rotor uses an electrochemical gradient of 120 mV magnitude per proton. Is synthesis costing 50 kJ/mol ATP feasible at ideal coupling?

Answer and reasoning. Energy per proton = F × 0.120 = 11.58 kJ/mol. Four protons/ATP give 46.31 kJ/mol, less than 50. Not feasible under stated conditions. Minimum voltage magnitude = 50/(4 × 96.485) = 0.1296 V, ignoring losses.

23. Consolidation: follow the energy and the charge

For any unfamiliar transporter, begin by drawing its two compartments and assigning a direction to each moved species. Write the chemical and electrical contribution to free energy for each species, and multiply by the number moved per completed cycle. Add any ATP, redox or other chemical reaction that is directly coupled. If the entire cycle has a negative free-energy change, the chosen direction is thermodynamically favourable. If it is positive, a tightly coupled reversible cycle cannot sustain that direction under the stated conditions. Then examine the actual protein's topology, kinetic mechanism and regulation to decide whether it can operate at the predicted rate. Finally, count net charges to determine whether direct current is expected. These three operations—energy accounting, mechanistic classification and charge accounting—are independent but complementary.

The central biological lesson is that membranes are not static partitions. Pumps establish gradients, leaks dissipate them, carriers couple them to other processes, and metabolism continually restores the chemical energy invested. The result is a regulated non-equilibrium state. Active transport links the molecular architecture of the membrane to the later study of membrane potential, electrical excitability, organelle identity and cell signalling.
