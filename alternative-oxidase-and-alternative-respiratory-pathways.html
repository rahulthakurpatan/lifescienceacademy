Alternative Oxidase and Alternative Respiratory Pathways

How plants redistribute respiratory electrons to balance energy conservation, carbon metabolism and redox status.

1. Why plants need respiratory flexibility

Consider a leaf in bright sunlight after a cool night. Carbon metabolism supplies reducing equivalents to mitochondria, but the demand for ATP need not increase at the same rate. If every respiratory electron must pass through energy-conserving complexes, the proton gradient may become sufficiently large to oppose further proton translocation. Electron carriers become highly reduced and the regeneration of NAD⁺ may become limiting for continued carbon metabolism. The cell faces a problem of balancing electron supply with the rate at which it can use conserved energy.

Respiration therefore performs two related but distinct services. It conserves energy as ATP, and it oxidizes reduced carriers so that other metabolic reactions can continue. Plants possess an enzyme called alternative oxidase (AOX) that makes the relationship between these services flexible. AOX provides another outlet for respiratory electrons to reach oxygen, but extracts less usable gradient energy from them than the conventional route. The apparently wasteful pathway makes sense when continued redox turnover matters more than maximizing ATP yield from each electron pair.

In the preceding chapters, we followed carbon oxidation through the citric acid cycle, transfer of electrons to oxygen and conversion of the resulting gradient into ATP. Here we examine the branch points that allow plants to modify this coupling. The relevant principle is that photosynthetic and nonphotosynthetic tissues experience different and changing relationships among carbohydrate supply, biosynthetic activity and ATP demand. A root actively accumulating mineral nutrients may require strongly coupled respiration, whereas a leaf temporarily receiving excess reductant may benefit from additional capacity for electron removal. Neither tissue is restricted to a single route, and the balance can change during development or stress.

The alternative pathway is not a separate organelle, a form of fermentation or a replacement for all mitochondrial respiration. It is integrated with the same inner mitochondrial membrane and the same electron-carrier pool as the cytochrome pathway. This structural connection provides the starting point for understanding the mechanism, calculations and experimental evidence.

2. Ubiquinone connects two terminal pathways

Ubiquinone (Q) is a lipid-soluble carrier of the inner mitochondrial membrane. In its fully reduced form, ubiquinol (QH₂), it carries two electrons and two hydrogen equivalents. Matrix NADH can donate electrons through Complex I, while succinate oxidation through Complex II transfers electrons through enzyme-bound flavin and iron–sulfur centres. Both processes reduce Q. Other dehydrogenases can also feed electrons into this shared pool. Because Q diffuses within the membrane, input through one enzyme does not determine a unique terminal route.

In the conventional respiratory chain, Complex III oxidizes QH₂ and passes electrons individually to cytochrome c. Complex IV accepts electrons from cytochrome c and reduces molecular oxygen to water. These two complexes contribute to the proton motive force across the inner membrane. AOX instead receives electrons directly from QH₂ and reduces oxygen to water without using Complex III, cytochrome c or Complex IV. The two routes may operate simultaneously, competing for the reduced form of Q.

Notice that the pathway branches after Q. Saying that AOX bypasses all proton pumping would be incorrect: electrons can still enter through Complex I and conserve energy before reaching AOX. The part that is necessarily bypassed is the cytochrome branch downstream of Q. Conversely, electrons can enter through a non-pumping enzyme yet exit through the cytochrome pathway, conserving energy at Complexes III and IV.

The redox state of the Q pool depends on the difference between its reduction by input enzymes and its oxidation by terminal pathways. At steady state the two rates balance, though a large fraction of Q may remain reduced. An increase in AOX flux can increase Q oxidation and permit upstream electron transfer to continue. This is the causal basis of the redox flexibility, not a claim that AOX directly oxidizes every metabolic NADH molecule.

3. The catalytic chemistry of AOX

AOX is an inner mitochondrial membrane-associated terminal quinol oxidase containing a non-haem diiron catalytic centre. Plant AOX is generally nuclear encoded and imported into mitochondria. Its donor is QH₂ and its acceptor is molecular oxygen. The oxidation half reaction can be written QH₂ → Q + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻. Complete reduction of oxygen requires four electrons: O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O. Multiplying the first equation by two and adding cancels the electrons and protons, giving the net reaction:

2 QH₂ + O₂ → 2 Q + 2 H₂O.

One electron pair corresponds to one QH₂ oxidized and half an oxygen molecule reduced.

There is no vectorial proton pumping by AOX. Chemical hydrogen equivalents used in the reaction must not be confused with the transfer of protons from matrix to intermembrane space that produces an electrochemical gradient. The overall chemical reaction remains energetically favorable, but the decrease in free energy along this terminal route is not captured through the usual proton-translocating reactions. The energy not conserved is dissipated, largely as heat.

Complex IV also reduces oxygen to water, but its substrate is reduced cytochrome c and it belongs to an energy-conserving terminal route. Thus the two enzymes share an ultimate electron acceptor but differ in electron donor, pathway position and energetic consequence. AOX is not an ATP synthase, a proton channel, or an enzyme that uses superoxide as its normal substrate. It lowers ATP yield by altering the route of respiratory electrons rather than by hydrolyzing ATP after its synthesis.

4. Alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases

Plants also contain type II NAD(P)H dehydrogenases that transfer electrons to Q without pumping protons. Their catalytic sites can face the matrix or the outer side of the inner membrane, and individual enzymes differ in their preference for NADH or NADPH. Internal forms can access matrix reducing equivalents. External forms can access reducing equivalents outside the inner membrane, including cytosolic NADH supplied across the relatively permeable outer membrane. The inner membrane itself is not freely permeable to NADH, so the orientation of the enzymes is biologically meaningful.

Complex I ordinarily oxidizes matrix NADH and translocates about four protons per pair of electrons. If an internal alternative dehydrogenase transfers the same electrons to Q, this energetic contribution is lost. The next part of the route, however, remains open: the resulting QH₂ may be oxidized by Complex III or by AOX. Alternative entry and alternative exit are independent choices at different locations in the respiratory network.

Complex II also reduces Q without pumping protons, but it is succinate dehydrogenase, not an alternative NADH dehydrogenase. Its role connects the citric acid cycle to electron transport. A common examination error is to assign the same proton yield to all electron donors because all ultimately reduce oxygen. The energy conservation depends on the route rather than only on the final chemical product.

Rotenone inhibits electron transfer through classical Complex I. If relevant alternative dehydrogenases are active, rotenone-resistant NADH oxidation may remain. Succinate may also support respiration independently of Complex I. Residual oxygen uptake after a Complex I block therefore cannot alone identify a specific alternative dehydrogenase: the available substrate and its compartment must be considered. In contrast, AOX can account for an alternative terminal route after the Q pool, not for direct replacement of Complex I.

5. Quantitative energy accounting and ATP yield

To compare routes, adopt the conventional textbook model of proton translocation per electron pair: Complex I contributes 4 H⁺, Complex III contributes effectively 4 H⁺ through the Q cycle, and Complex IV contributes 2 pumped H⁺. Complex II, type II dehydrogenases and AOX contribute zero pumped protons. The chemical protons used by Complex IV to form water are not added again to the pumped-proton count. These conventions allow the energetic contributions of successive complexes to be added without double counting.

Let h denote the effective cost in proton equivalents of producing one ATP and making it available for cellular use. We use h ≈ 4 for the following idealized examples, incorporating transport costs approximately. This value is not a fixed property of all plant mitochondria: ATP synthase rotor stoichiometry, metabolite transport, membrane leak and other processes affect the actual yield. The calculation concerns oxidative phosphorylation only, not substrate-level ATP formation by glycolysis or the citric acid cycle.

EntryExitH⁺ equivalents per 2e⁻ATP estimate
Complex IIII and IV4 + 4 + 2 = 102.5
Complex IAOX4 + 0 = 41.0
II or type II NDIII and IV0 + 4 + 2 = 61.5
II or type II NDAOX00

A P/O ratio is conventionally ATP synthesized per oxygen atom reduced, equivalent to half an O₂ molecule. The entries in the table are estimated P/O values. If a problem instead asks for ATP per entire O₂ molecule, multiply by two. For example, classical Complex-I-linked respiration gives about five ATP per O₂, while Complex-I-to-AOX flow gives about two. A fully non-pumping entry and exit combination gives no directly coupled ATP under the assumed model; this does not imply that the entire mitochondrion or cell lacks ATP or a gradient generated through other processes.

For mixed pathways let N be the total number of electron pairs, f the fraction entering via Complex I and a the fraction exiting via AOX. The entry flux contributes 4fN proton equivalents. The conventional exit carries (1−a)N pairs and contributes 6(1−a)N equivalents. Dividing their sum by N gives proton equivalents per pair, then dividing by h gives the idealized ATP yield:

P/O ≈ [4f + 6(1−a)]/h.

Assumptions: all remaining entry is non-pumping; all non-AOX exit is via III and IV; proton leak and other sources of gradient are neglected. Both fractions range from zero to one.

Suppose 80 electron pairs reach Q, 60 via Complex I, and half of the total exit through AOX. The input contributes 60 × 4 = 240 proton equivalents. The 40 pairs leaving through III and IV contribute another 40 × 6 = 240. Total 480, which at h = 4 corresponds to 120 ATP. Since 80 pairs consume 40 O₂ molecules, P/O = 120/80 = 1.5 and ATP/O₂ = 3. The calculation does not require knowing which individual entry event was paired with which exit because energetic contributions add at the shared Q pool.

At fixed total flux and entry fraction, raising the AOX fraction reduces the model yield by 6/h for each electron pair switched from the conventional exit. But the assumption of fixed flux must be made explicit. In a living plant, the lower energetic yield may be accompanied by a higher rate of oxidation, or changes in substrate availability, proton leak and ATP demand. Lower ATP per electron pair is not the same claim as lower total ATP synthesis per unit time.

Consider a second comparison: a tissue requires 60 arbitrary ATP units from respiration. At 2.5 ATP per pair, the conventional NADH route would require 24 electron pairs, or 12 O₂ molecules. At one ATP per pair, the Complex-I-to-AOX route would require 60 pairs or 30 O₂ molecules. That is 2.5 times the electron flux for the same modeled ATP production. This illustrates the possible carbon cost of alternative respiration, but it assumes enough substrate and oxygen are available and ignores changes in other ATP-producing reactions.

6. Regulation: abundance, activation and engagement

Respiratory demand can change rapidly, so AOX must be regulated in more than one way. In well-studied plant AOX isoforms, the protein forms a dimer and a regulatory disulfide bond can link its subunits. The oxidized, disulfide-linked form is generally less active; reduction exposes regulatory thiols. Alpha-keto acids such as pyruvate activate many reduced plant AOX isoforms through interactions involving regulatory cysteine residues. The exact response varies among isoforms, some of which respond to other organic acids or differ in redox regulation.

Pyruvate provides an informative link to carbon metabolism. As carbon enters respiration, pyruvate can be oxidized to acetyl-CoA and supply reducing equivalents through mitochondrial metabolism. Activation of an alternative outlet in response to particular organic acids can help match electron-disposal capacity to metabolic conditions. This is an explanation of why metabolite regulation is useful, not evidence that a rise in pyruvate must increase AOX flux in every tissue.

Even an activated enzyme requires substrate. If most Q is oxidized, little QH₂ is available to AOX. If Complex III efficiently competes for QH₂, the alternative branch may carry only a small fraction of electrons despite abundant AOX protein. Conversely, changes in Q reduction or enzyme activation can increase alternative flux without increasing the total amount of protein. Distinguish four quantities: transcript abundance, protein abundance, maximum capacity under a specified test condition, and the actual electron flux under normal conditions. None can automatically substitute for another.

On longer time scales, mitochondrial metabolic and redox changes can influence expression of nuclear AOX genes, an instance of retrograde signaling. Stress-induced AOX expression therefore reveals a change in regulatory state. It does not by itself demonstrate improved photosynthesis, reduced oxidative damage or greater alternative pathway engagement. An investigator seeking those conclusions must measure the corresponding physiological variables. This distinction between molecular expression and pathway activity is a recurring principle of experimental biology.

7. Cyanide-resistant respiration and experimental reasoning

Classical observations showed that certain plant tissues continued consuming oxygen after inhibition of the cytochrome oxidase pathway. This challenged the assumption that oxygen reduction must always proceed through Complex IV. AOX can account for such a result because it is comparatively insensitive to cyanide under the usual respiratory assay conditions. Electrons that reach Q can still be transferred to oxygen even when the normal cytochrome terminal oxidase is inhibited.

Salicylhydroxamic acid (SHAM) and n-propyl gallate are traditional AOX inhibitors in plant respiratory physiology. Suppression of the cyanide-resistant component by an AOX inhibitor provides evidence for alternative pathway capacity. These inhibitors are not perfectly specific and tissues can contain additional oxygen-consuming processes. Interpretation must therefore consider residual background oxygen uptake, matched controls and changes in electron distribution. The following hypothetical rates illustrate the logic; they are not a laboratory protocol or published data.

The untreated rate is 100, the cytochrome-inhibited rate is 45 and the residual after both pathways are blocked is 5. In a simple background correction, 45−5 = 40 units is the AOX-sensitive capacity measured in the absence of the competing terminal branch. Corrected untreated respiration is 95 units. The tempting calculation 40/95 ≈ 42% does not establish the original proportion of flux through AOX. Inhibiting the conventional outlet can increase Q reduction and redirect electrons to AOX, changing the state we intended to measure.

The same difficulty appears if the order of inhibition is reversed. Suppose blocking AOX barely changes total oxygen uptake. One explanation is that little AOX was originally active. Another is that the conventional branch had enough spare capacity to take over the alternative flux. These interpretations cannot be separated using total oxygen uptake alone. If the conventional branch were operating close to its capacity, the effect of blocking AOX might be much larger. A single inhibitor result must be interpreted in terms of the whole network.

Oxygen isotope discrimination offers a more direct approach to partitioning respiratory flux. AOX and cytochrome c oxidase discriminate differently against heavier oxygen isotopes during oxygen reduction. Suitable calibration and measurement of discrimination can estimate the relative use of the two terminal routes without first forcing all electrons into one branch. For a simplified linear mixing example, assume endpoint discrimination values of 20 and 30 per mille and an observed value of 24 per mille. The alternative fraction is (24−20)/(30−20) = 0.4. These values are illustrative, not universal constants. Actual analysis requires calibrated endpoint values and appropriate assumptions. The important conclusion is that flux-specific evidence answers a different question from protein abundance or inhibitor-defined capacity.

8. Redox balance and continuation of carbon metabolism

Several citric acid cycle reactions generate NADH by reducing NAD⁺. Continued operation requires regeneration of oxidized cofactors. If the respiratory chain cannot accept electrons rapidly enough, reduced carriers accumulate. This can restrict further oxidation of carbon intermediates and affect the supply of carbon skeletons for biosynthesis. By providing another exit from QH₂, AOX can relieve some of this constraint. When upstream electron transfer remains functional, oxidizing more QH₂ helps regenerate oxidized Q and can allow NADH oxidation to proceed.

Think of the quinone pool as a junction in a connected reaction network. A highly energy-conserving outlet is useful, but if its operation is limited by the gradient or some other condition, traffic can accumulate at the junction. Another outlet may have a lower energy return while permitting turnover. The molecular explanation lies in availability of oxidized carriers, not in the analogy itself. The process requires sufficient oxygen and active upstream enzymes; AOX cannot maintain NADH oxidation when all paths to Q are blocked or oxygen is absent.

The advantage of redox flexibility becomes clearer when we consider respiration as a supplier of biosynthetic intermediates as well as ATP. Carbon skeletons from central metabolism are used in nitrogen assimilation and synthesis of organic compounds. A high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio can constrain some dehydrogenase reactions even when ATP is not immediately scarce. In such a setting, continued oxidation can support carbon metabolism at a cost in ATP yield. This is a trade-off, not a general rule that all carbon should be directed through the less efficient route.

Do not confuse oxygen consumption with carbon dioxide production. The electron transport chain consumes oxygen; CO₂ is released in other reactions, such as decarboxylation of carbon intermediates. The respiratory quotient, CO₂ released divided by O₂ consumed, depends on substrate and metabolic reactions. Changing the terminal oxidase alone does not fix the respiratory quotient or tell us the exact rate of carbon assimilation.

9. Reactive oxygen species and the limits of protection

Electron transfer is not always perfectly insulated from molecular oxygen. Under some conditions an electron leaks to oxygen, generating superoxide. Complexes I and III are important mitochondrial sites of this process. Superoxide dismutase can convert superoxide to hydrogen peroxide; other enzymes and redox systems remove or use peroxide. Controlled reactive oxygen species (ROS) also participate in signaling, whereas excessive production relative to removal can damage cellular constituents.

High carrier reduction and a high proton motive force can promote electron leakage under particular conditions. AOX can oxidize excess QH₂ and allow electrons to leave the chain without additional proton translocation at III and IV. In suitable circumstances, this lowers the redox pressure that favors ROS production. AOX is therefore not an antioxidant enzyme in the same direct mechanistic sense as superoxide dismutase or catalase. Its normal substrate is QH₂, and it changes the circumstances under which ROS are generated rather than directly scavenging all ROS.

Imagine two comparable leaves facing a transient mitochondrial respiratory bottleneck. One has sufficient active AOX to provide an alternative outlet, whereas the other has low AOX capacity. The latter might develop a more reduced Q pool and show higher mitochondrial ROS production. The prediction is conditional: substrate supply, antioxidant systems, oxygen availability and the type of stress can all change the result. Total cellular peroxide can originate from chloroplasts, peroxisomes and other processes too, so a rise in whole-cell peroxide does not automatically locate its source in the respiratory chain.

Some ROS signals are biologically useful for acclimation. The aim is homeostasis, not removal of every reactive molecule. In comparing a mutant and wild type, it is important to examine pathway flux, redox markers and tissue performance together before asserting that AOX caused a particular stress phenotype.

10. Stress, thermogenesis and coordination with photosynthesis

Cold can alter the rates of metabolic processes unequally, changing the relationship between respiratory electron supply and ATP demand. Drought or salinity can restrict carbon assimilation while changing redox balance, and pathogen responses can alter metabolic activity and signaling. Changes in AOX expression or engagement have been observed in many such situations. The usefulness of the pathway is context dependent: neither induction nor inhibition necessarily leads to the same outcome in every organ, species or stage of development.

Thermogenesis in certain flowers and inflorescences illustrates energy dissipation on a larger scale. Some arum relatives produce substantial heat at particular reproductive stages. High alternative respiratory flux can contribute to heat production and can help the release of volatile signals. Respiration still requires substrate and oxygen; AOX does not create energy independently of oxidation. Other mechanisms of dissipation can participate depending on species. Most plants do not show conspicuous heating merely because their mitochondria contain AOX.

In photosynthetic tissue, chloroplasts and mitochondria cooperate through cellular metabolism. Chloroplast light reactions generate ATP and reducing power, while mitochondria can oxidize reducing equivalents supplied indirectly through metabolite exchange. When photosynthetic reductant production exceeds the capacity of available sinks, mitochondrial respiration can contribute to cellular redox balancing. AOX is one component of this coordination. It does not directly accept electrons from photosystem I, nor does it constitute chloroplast cyclic electron flow. Effects on net carbon assimilation depend on the entire metabolic situation.

The next chapter concerns photorespiration, which begins with Rubisco oxygenation. Glycine conversion in mitochondria produces NADH that must be reoxidized or redistributed. The respiratory electron transport system, including AOX where active, can participate in the balance of reducing equivalents. However, AOX does not catalyse glycine decarboxylation, Rubisco oxygenation or the recycling of glycolate carbon. Photorespiration and alternative respiration are distinct pathways that interact through shared cellular metabolism.

11. AOX versus proton leakage and oxygen limitation

AOX can reduce the coupling of oxidation to ATP formation, but it does so differently from a protonophore or an uncoupling protein. A leak dissipates a gradient that has already been generated by respiratory complexes; AOX bypasses complexes that would otherwise generate part of that gradient. One changes the rate of gradient dissipation and the other changes its generation. Similar changes in measured ATP/O₂ ratios or membrane potential therefore need not indicate identical mechanisms.

Oligomycin inhibits ATP synthase. In coupled mitochondria this often reduces classical electron flow because protons cannot return through ATP synthase readily and further pumping becomes energetically difficult. AOX can permit some electron transfer despite this restriction, depending on the availability of QH₂ and the entry route. It does not reactivate inhibited ATP synthase. A protonophore may instead relieve the opposing gradient and stimulate conventional oxygen uptake while lowering ATP production. These contrasting responses help discriminate respiratory mechanisms when several variables are measured together.

Cyanide resistance must not be confused with oxygen independence. AOX is itself an oxygen-reducing enzyme. Under complete anoxia neither terminal oxidase can sustain aerobic electron transport. Fermentation provides a different way of regenerating NAD⁺ by transferring electrons to organic acceptors, allowing glycolysis to continue. In plants, reduction of acetaldehyde to ethanol is one such reaction. It is not a branch of mitochondrial electron transfer to oxygen.

For any unfamiliar inhibitor problem, locate the affected step first, trace which donors can still reduce Q, identify which terminal routes remain available and then assess gradient conservation and ATP synthase function. If Complex III is blocked, for example, AOX can still accept electrons from Q and Complex I may still contribute to the gradient. If Complex I is also blocked, respiration depends on an accessible alternative input. This causal sequence is more reliable than memorizing a list of inhibitors without knowing their targets.

12. Examination practice

1. MCQ: substrate and acceptor

Which correctly describes AOX? A. NADH → cytochrome c; B. QH₂ → O₂ without pumping; C. ATP → ADP to oxidize Q; D. QH₂ → NAD⁺.

Answer and explanation

B. Ubiquinol is the donor, oxygen the acceptor. A confuses entry and the cytochrome route, C confuses AOX with ATP hydrolysis and D reverses the relevant electron flow.

2. MCQ: retained proton conservation

Which route conserves about four H⁺ per electron pair? A. I → AOX; B. II → AOX; C. I → III → IV; D. Type II ND → III → IV.

Answer and explanation

A: I contributes four and AOX zero. B gives zero, C ten and D six under the specified model. Bypassing III and IV does not bypass I.

3. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Plant respiration may continue despite inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase. Reason: AOX accepts electrons directly from reduced Q and transfers them to oxygen.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion when AOX, substrate and oxygen are available. This does not prove that every residual oxygen-consuming reaction is AOX.

4. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Increasing AOX expression always increases plant growth. Reason: AOX can help maintain Q redox turnover.

Answer and explanation

The assertion is false and the reason is conditionally true. Expression does not establish engagement, and alternative flux has a cost in ATP conservation. Benefit depends on which process limits growth.

5. Numerical: mixed respiratory flux

Sixty percent of electron pairs enter through I and 25% exit through AOX. Assume h = 4 and no leakage. Find P/O and ATP per O₂.

Answer and explanation

4(0.6)+6(0.75)=6.9 proton equivalents per pair. P/O=6.9/4=1.725 and ATP/O₂=3.45. The fractional values are averages, not fractional individual ATP molecules.

6. Numerical: equal ATP output

Two idealized systems generate 50 oxidative ATP units, using either classical NADH respiration or I → AOX. Determine electron pairs and oxygen molecules required.

Answer and explanation

Classical: 50/2.5=20 pairs, 10 O₂. Alternative: 50/1=50 pairs, 25 O₂. The latter requires 2.5 times the electron flux under these assumptions.

7. Data interpretation

Untreated oxygen uptake is 80 nmol min⁻¹; after cytochrome pathway inhibition it is 35, and after blocking both routes 5. Estimate corrected alternative capacity. Is this native AOX flux?

Answer and explanation

35−5=30 nmol O₂ min⁻¹. Corrected initial uptake is 75. The ratio 30/75 is not necessarily the native fraction because inhibition redirects electrons to the remaining route.

8. Experimental inference

A plant has twice as much AOX protein as a control, but calibrated isotope measurements show unchanged alternative fraction. Can absolute AOX flux have changed?

Answer and explanation

Yes. Absolute flux is the alternative fraction multiplied by total respiratory flux. A change in total flux changes absolute AOX flux even if the fraction remains constant. Protein abundance alone is not a flux measurement.

9. Conceptual comparison

Why can both a protonophore and AOX decrease ATP/O₂ even though they act differently?

Answer and explanation

A protonophore dissipates an existing gradient; AOX prevents some gradient generation by bypassing III and IV. They alter different terms in the balance of proton motive force.

10. Analytical reasoning

Why does AOX not allow sustained mitochondrial electron transport in anoxia?

Answer and explanation

AOX still requires oxygen as the terminal acceptor. Fermentation can regenerate NAD⁺ through organic acceptors but is not AOX-mediated aerobic respiration.

13. Revision and active recall

Redraw the respiratory network with Q at the centre. Label the classical and alternative entry routes on one side and the cytochrome branch and AOX on the other. Annotate proton contributions before calculating ATP. Explain why Complex I can continue to conserve some energy even when AOX is the only terminal branch. Derive the mixed-pathway P/O expression without looking at the formula. Then explain why inhibitor-defined capacity differs from actual flux, why an increase in protein amount is not proof of engagement, and why redox relief can be valuable despite the lower ATP yield.

AOX accepts electrons from QH₂ and reduces oxygen to water without vectorial proton pumping. Alternative dehydrogenases bypass Complex I independently of AOX. Pathway choice is regulated by substrate, enzyme activation, expression and the competing cytochrome route. Redox turnover, stress-related flexibility and thermogenesis may benefit, but the energetic cost remains and physiological outcomes are conditional.
