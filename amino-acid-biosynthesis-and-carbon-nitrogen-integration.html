1. From inorganic nitrogen to a living plant

A nitrate ion entering a root cannot become part of a leaf protein simply by binding to a carbon compound. Its nitrogen is in a highly oxidized state and must first be reduced. The preceding chapters followed nitrate to nitrite and ammonium, and then the assimilation of ammonium by glutamine synthetase (GS) and glutamate synthase (GOGAT). These reactions solve the problem of incorporating inorganic nitrogen into organic molecules. They do not yet explain how a single nitrogen input can produce the twenty common proteinogenic amino acids. A growing cell needs different carbon backbones, different side chains and a coordinated supply of nitrogen for every one of these products.

Consider two genetically identical seedlings supplied with the same nitrate concentration. One receives enough light and carbon dioxide for sustained photosynthesis; the other has very little available carbohydrate. Even if both absorb nitrate, their ability to convert it into amino acids can differ. Assimilation requires carbon skeletons, reducing equivalents and energy. The nitrogen-rich nutrient solution is therefore not by itself proof that the second seedling can sustain protein synthesis. Nor does a high concentration of soluble amino acids automatically indicate rapid growth: if translation or export slows, amino acids may accumulate despite weak synthesis.

Amino acids have three related but distinct functions in this chapter. They supply residues for proteins, they form pools of mobile organic nitrogen, and they act as precursors of other compounds. Glutamate, for example, contributes nitrogen to aspartate and alanine, and its carbon skeleton supports the synthesis of proline and arginine. Glutamine supplies amide nitrogen to a variety of biosynthetic reactions. Aspartate and asparagine connect central metabolism to long-distance nitrogen distribution. The pathways are not isolated straight lines; they form a network in which the same substrates can have competing fates.

To make sense of this network, separate the fate of nitrogen from the fate of carbon. If glutamate donates its amino nitrogen to oxaloacetate, the newly formed aspartate contains the carbon skeleton of oxaloacetate, not the five-carbon backbone of glutamate. The latter reappears as 2-oxoglutarate. This observation is the central accounting principle for the chapter. At every reaction, ask which molecule carries the carbon atoms, which carries the transferred nitrogen, and whether ATP or reducing power is required. With that discipline, even unfamiliar amino-acid reactions become understandable.

Amino acid biosynthesis must also be distinguished from translation. Biosynthesis produces free amino acids from metabolic precursors. During translation, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases activate amino acids and attach them to corresponding tRNAs; ribosomes incorporate the residues into a polypeptide. A defect in amino acid biosynthesis can starve the translation machinery of its substrates, but a defect in translation can cause amino acid accumulation without increasing biosynthetic flux. The detailed molecular machinery of protein synthesis belongs to Unit 03.

Why a common nitrogen entry does not produce a common amino acid

Imagine a germinating seed supplied with nitrate and sugar. The seed must make structural proteins, metabolic enzymes and membrane transport proteins. Those proteins require an assortment of amino acids, not simply a large quantity of glutamate. The amino acid sequence of a protein fixes the relative demand for different amino acids. Increasing the availability of one residue cannot substitute for the absence of another in the genetic code. This creates a coordination problem: pathways must share the newly assimilated nitrogen while providing very different carbon structures at appropriate rates. A pathway that produces lysine efficiently but leaves methionine deficient cannot support unrestricted protein synthesis. Free lysine might accumulate even as growth slows.

The use of family names is a way to solve this conceptual complexity. Instead of memorizing twenty unrelated pathways, begin with central carbon metabolism and identify a small number of branch points. A four-carbon oxaloacetate molecule can accept nitrogen to make aspartate. Subsequent reactions preserve, rearrange or add carbon atoms. A three-carbon pyruvate molecule can accept nitrogen directly to produce alanine, but valine synthesis requires a condensation and carbon loss before the final amino group is introduced. In these cases the precursor is not identical to the eventual nitrogen acceptor. A family describes a biosynthetic ancestry; it does not prescribe a single reaction type.

The pool of an amino acid represents the amount of that compound accessible in a measured tissue at a particular time. A flux describes the amount formed, consumed or transported per unit time. If a leaf contains 10 µmol of alanine and forms and consumes 4 µmol every hour, its pool can remain constant while a substantial flux passes through it. By contrast a pool of 20 µmol in a poorly growing leaf may have a formation rate of only 1 µmol per hour. The concentration measured by a chemical assay would be higher in the second leaf, yet its synthesis rate could be smaller. This distinction underlies nearly every experimental interpretation in this chapter.

The distinction also explains why the nitrogen requirement of a growing organ cannot be inferred simply from its concentration of free amino acids. A developing seed may rapidly withdraw amino acids into storage proteins. A mature leaf may have a smaller demand for new proteins but a considerable need to replace damaged enzymes. In each organ the amounts present are determined by the balance of formation, import, export and use. The same pathway can therefore participate in growth in one tissue and nitrogen redistribution in another. The aim is to connect chemical mechanism to these different physiological circumstances rather than assigning each compound a single fixed purpose.

2. Glutamate and glutamine: two chemically different nitrogen donors

Glutamate has five carbons and one α-amino nitrogen. Glutamine also has five carbons, but its side-chain carboxyl group has been converted to a carboxamide, giving it a second nitrogen. It is useful to label the two nitrogen positions of glutamine separately: the α-amino N is associated with the central carbon, whereas the side-chain amide N is associated with the carbonyl carbon. Neither is a free ammonium ion, and the two positions are not interchangeable without reactions that move or exchange nitrogen.

GS converts glutamate and ammonium into glutamine using ATP. In a simplified biochemical equation, Glu + NH₄⁺ + ATP → Gln + ADP + Pi. GOGAT then uses glutamine, 2-oxoglutarate (2-OG) and reducing equivalents to form two glutamates. One glutamate retains the original glutamine carbon skeleton and α-amino N; the other has the carbon skeleton of 2-OG and nitrogen derived from the side-chain amide. The first product can return to GS, while the additional product can support the synthesis of other organic compounds. Thus GOGAT provides a means by which freshly captured amide nitrogen becomes α-amino nitrogen.

Glu + Gln are not merely a reservoir that is emptied once. Consider a continuous stream of ammonium entering a cell. If nitrogen is exported from glutamate to alanine, the resulting 2-OG can be used again in the GS–GOGAT system. The size of the glutamate pool can remain almost constant even when the rate of nitrogen transfer is large. This is why a pool measurement and a flux measurement answer different questions. A pool is an amount at a moment; flux measures amount transferred per unit time.

In transamination, glutamate commonly donates the α-amino group to an α-keto acid. In glutamine-dependent amidotransfer reactions, the side-chain amide nitrogen of glutamine is transferred to a chemically suitable acceptor. In both cases, nitrogen is incorporated into a product without requiring each receiving pathway to carry out its own primary ammonium assimilation. The glutamate and glutamine pools are consequently metabolic junctions rather than the final destinations of all assimilated nitrogen.

One example outside the immediate amino acid network is glutamine-dependent nitrogen donation to nucleotide synthesis. Another is the role of glutamate as a precursor in tetrapyrrole synthesis in plants. These examples show why disrupting GS or GOGAT affects more than proteins. This chapter concentrates on the routes into amino acids and organic nitrogen transport; other nitrogenous compounds are examined where their own pathways belong.

If a plant receives a short pulse of ¹⁵NH₄⁺, newly formed glutamine can carry ¹⁵N in its amide position before much label appears in other amino acids. Later, GOGAT can place this nitrogen in the amino group of glutamate, and aminotransferases can distribute it. The timing is a prediction for a suitable pulse experiment rather than a universal order visible at every sampling resolution. Rapid exchange, pre-existing pools, tissue type and alternative inputs can obscure the sequence.

Follow the two nitrogen positions separately

For bookkeeping, call the nitrogen that was originally attached to glutamate N₁ and the incoming ammonium nitrogen N₂. GS gives glutamine containing both N₁ and N₂. In GOGAT, the side-chain nitrogen N₂ is released within the enzyme and used to aminate the five-carbon acceptor 2-oxoglutarate. The glutamine carbon framework becomes glutamate carrying N₁; the second carbon framework becomes glutamate carrying N₂. The enzyme has transformed an amide nitrogen into an α-amino nitrogen. This is more informative than saying that glutamine merely becomes two glutamates, because it explains which nitrogen can subsequently be transferred by a conventional aminotransferase. Notice that a five-carbon glutamine molecule cannot produce ten carbon atoms on its own.

If a fresh ammonium ion is to become the nitrogen of asparagine, two alternative bookkeeping routes can be followed. It might enter the glutamine amide and then be transferred directly by asparagine synthetase to the side chain of pre-existing aspartate. Alternatively, it could move through GOGAT to glutamate and then through aspartate aminotransferase to the α-amino group of aspartate. Subsequent amidation of that aspartate could produce asparagine bearing the new label at its α position. With a sufficiently long pulse, both positions might become labeled. An observed labeled asparagine molecule does not by itself identify the sequence of reactions that labeled it.

This difference matters for a mass spectrometric measurement. A glutamine molecule with one additional mass unit may carry the heavy isotope at either nitrogen position. Total molecular mass distinguishes zero, one and two heavy nitrogen atoms but does not necessarily locate a single label. Fragment information or a position-sensitive chemical measurement is needed to establish which position is enriched. In a conceptual examination problem, the statement that amide nitrogen was measured separately is therefore a significant piece of experimental information. Without it, a claimed ordering of individual nitrogen-transfer reactions may go beyond the data.

An intact plant contains several spatially separated glutamate and glutamine pools. Chloroplast GS2 and ferredoxin-dependent GOGAT are important in photosynthetic nitrogen assimilation and reassimilation of photorespiratory ammonium; cytosolic GS isoforms and NADH-dependent GOGAT are especially important in various nonphotosynthetic or remobilizing tissues. These associations are not exclusive rules for every species or organ. A whole-leaf measurement combines metabolites from different cell types and compartments. A high bulk glutamine concentration need not establish that the glutamine concentration inside a particular plastid is high enough to sustain every amide-requiring enzyme.

The same chemistry is reused in several biosynthetic reactions because a glutamine amide can supply ammonia at an enzyme's active site without requiring a large free ammonium pool throughout the cell. In the simplest picture an amidotransferase hydrolyzes the donor amide while a second part of the reaction incorporates the released nitrogen into an activated acceptor. The regenerated glutamate can return to GS or enter an aminotransferase reaction. This coupling of donor regeneration with acceptor transformation provides a chemical explanation for the central position of glutamine and glutamate in the nitrogen network. It is not a claim that every nitrogen-containing molecule is produced by one identical enzyme mechanism.

3. PLP-dependent transamination and nitrogen bookkeeping

An α-keto acid and its corresponding α-amino acid differ in the chemistry at their α-carbon. Pyruvate is the keto-acid counterpart of alanine, oxaloacetate corresponds to aspartate, and 2-oxoglutarate corresponds to glutamate. An aminotransferase interconverts an amino acid and a keto acid in a reaction that conserves nitrogen. For alanine aminotransferase (AlaAT), the simplified reaction is Glu + pyruvate ⇌ 2-OG + Ala. For aspartate aminotransferase (AspAT), it is Glu + oxaloacetate ⇌ 2-OG + Asp. Both are reversible and neither directly consumes ATP or free ammonium.

The cofactor pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), derived from vitamin B₆, enables the transfer. PLP is commonly held in the active site by a Schiff base with an enzyme lysine residue. When the amino-acid substrate binds, its amino group replaces that enzyme linkage to form an external aldimine. The conjugated PLP system stabilizes otherwise difficult changes in electron distribution around the substrate α-carbon. After rearrangement and hydrolysis, the amino acid is released as its corresponding keto acid, and the cofactor is left in the aminated form, pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP).

The second half of the reaction begins when an acceptor keto acid binds to the aminated enzyme. Reversal of the chemical steps generates the corresponding amino acid and regenerates PLP. The donor and acceptor need not be present in the active site at the same time; the cofactor temporarily carries the nitrogen. This is a useful example of a ping-pong reaction mechanism. The enzyme does not permanently consume PLP or PMP. It cycles between these two forms as the catalytic reaction proceeds.

The specificity of an aminotransferase depends on the architecture of its active site. PLP alone is not enough to make every amino acid from every keto acid. Different substrates have distinct side chains and require appropriate binding orientations. Some aminotransferases accept more than one substrate pair; others are much more selective. Plant AspAT isoenzymes operate in different compartments, including plastids and cytosol. Consequently, it is inappropriate to assign all aspartate formation to one universally specified compartment. A plant may need transport of metabolites across an organelle membrane to connect enzymes that are not co-located.

The forward and reverse rates are determined by substrate and product availability and enzyme properties. For the AlaAT equation, a high pyruvate concentration together with sufficient glutamate can favor alanine production. If alanine is abundant and the cell requires pyruvate, the reverse reaction can contribute to carbon recovery. The actual net direction depends on conditions, not on the arrow selected in a textbook. For a reaction close to equilibrium, a change in concentration ratio may reverse the net flux even without a change in enzyme abundance.

Write the reaction quotient for ideal dilute behavior as Q = [2-OG][Ala]/([Glu][pyruvate]), using concentrations relative to their standard state. The relationship ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q explains why an increase in pyruvate alone decreases Q and makes the forward direction more favorable, holding everything else fixed. It does not establish that the reaction has an arbitrarily large forward rate. Reaction thermodynamics constrain direction; enzyme activity and access to substrates constrain kinetics. In a cell, concentrations in different compartments and metabolite removal also influence the outcome.

Transamination is not de novo incorporation of inorganic nitrogen. Suppose 4 μmol of glutamate reacts with 4 μmol of pyruvate in a closed idealized assay. Complete forward conversion makes 4 μmol of alanine and 4 μmol of 2-OG; the number of organic nitrogen atoms is unchanged. To sustain this conversion without exhausting the original glutamate pool, another pathway must supply new nitrogen or regenerate glutamate using previously assimilated nitrogen. This is precisely the network-level role of GS–GOGAT.

A simple experiment distinguishes these routes. Supply purified AlaAT with unlabeled pyruvate and α-¹⁵N-glutamate. If the enzyme and cofactor function and the reaction proceeds forward, the alanine product should contain ¹⁵N. If ¹³C₃-pyruvate is used simultaneously, alanine may contain three labeled carbon atoms as well. The two isotope labels identify two different molecular origins. Free ¹⁵NH₄⁺ is not an equivalent substitute for α-¹⁵N-glutamate in an isolated AlaAT assay.

Why PLP allows amino-group exchange without net oxidation

A primary amino group on an amino acid is not easily detached in solution while retaining the rest of the molecule intact. The active site of a typical aminotransferase provides a route in which PLP forms a reversible covalent imine linkage with the substrate. In many enzymes the resting cofactor is associated with an active-site lysine. The incoming amino acid replaces that linkage to form an external aldimine. Rearrangement of the covalently bound intermediate facilitates transfer of the amino group to the cofactor, and the original carbon skeleton leaves as its keto acid. The conjugated structure of PLP helps stabilize otherwise unfavorable intermediates. The enzyme then uses PMP in the second half-reaction and regenerates PLP when the new amino acid leaves.

This explains why the overall exchange can proceed without using NADH to reduce an independently generated carbonyl and without requiring ATP for each transfer. One substrate is converted from amino acid to keto acid, while the other undergoes the reciprocal change. The two half-reactions are coupled through the enzyme-bound cofactor. A student should not infer that the first donor releases a freely diffusing ammonium ion into the cytosol before the second amino acid is formed. The nitrogen is carried transiently by the cofactor. This is different from the GS reaction, which introduces ammonium using ATP-dependent activation.

A useful carbon accounting exercise is to combine glutamate carrying five carbon atoms and pyruvate carrying three. The products are five-carbon 2-oxoglutarate and three-carbon alanine. Both sides contain eight carbon atoms and one nitrogen. If only the carbon of glutamate is labeled with ¹³C, the immediate 2-oxoglutarate product carries the label, whereas alanine receives carbon from pyruvate. If instead only its nitrogen is labeled with ¹⁵N, the alanine formed by the forward reaction carries that nitrogen. Over a longer interval the carbon label may reach other metabolites through central metabolism, so these predictions concern the immediate reaction and not the eventual labeling of a whole plant.

Concentrations, equilibrium and enzyme capacity

Suppose a simplified compartment has 4 mM glutamate, 2 mM pyruvate, 1 mM 2-oxoglutarate and 2 mM alanine. For the written forward AlaAT reaction the concentration quotient is (1 × 2)/(4 × 2) = 0.25. If the equilibrium constant at the chosen conditions is 1, then Q is below K and the forward direction is thermodynamically favored. Addition of alanine to 10 mM raises Q to 1.25, provided the other concentrations are initially held constant. Under that assumption the reverse direction is favored. The enzyme accelerates approach toward equilibrium but does not change K. No actual plant AlaAT equilibrium constant is asserted by this illustrative example.

A reversible enzyme may be important even if its net flux is small. Imagine forward and reverse rates of 8 and 7 µmol per hour. The net production of alanine is only 1 µmol per hour, but an isotope can rapidly exchange between amino acid pools. In an inhibitor treatment both gross rates might fall to 2 and 1 µmol per hour, leaving the same net flux but much slower label equilibration. Labeling measurements are therefore sensitive to exchange as well as net synthesis. A directional arrow in a pathway figure generally summarizes a physiological route of interest; it is not necessarily a statement that all molecular events occur in that direction.

To test enzyme specificity, use an assay with the same amino donor and several alternative keto acids under comparable conditions. Formation of alanine with pyruvate and aspartate with oxaloacetate can be monitored by suitable product assays. If an enzyme preparation yields a larger signal with pyruvate, it has greater measured activity with that substrate under those conditions; one cannot immediately conclude that the protein never accepts any other substrate. Substrate concentrations may be far from saturation, or more than one enzyme may be present. A purified enzyme and systematic substrate-concentration series provide stronger evidence for specificity than a crude extract tested at one concentration.

An organism with reduced plastidic aspartate aminotransferase activity may show altered aspartate-family pools even though a whole-cell aminotransferase assay retains activity from other isoforms. This illustrates the importance of metabolic localization. Carbon skeletons and amino acids have to cross particular membranes before they can participate in reactions elsewhere. A cytosolic isoform may not completely replace plastid activity if the exchange of substrates or products across the plastid envelope is limiting. Thus, loss of a compartment-specific enzyme can generate a phenotype that is more specific than the change in total activity suggests.

4. Aspartate and asparagine: a carbon-efficient route for organic nitrogen

Aspartate carries four carbon atoms and one amino nitrogen. Its precursor oxaloacetate participates in central carbon metabolism and can be generated through several routes. AspAT transfers α-amino nitrogen from glutamate to oxaloacetate, yielding aspartate and 2-OG. This connects the glutamate-centered nitrogen network to a four-carbon skeleton. Aspartate itself participates in protein synthesis and is the starting material for several longer biosynthetic pathways.

Asparagine differs from aspartate because the terminal side-chain carboxyl group is replaced by a carboxamide group. Asparagine synthetase (AS) commonly transfers the amide nitrogen from glutamine to aspartate in higher plants. The overall simplified reaction is Asp + Gln + ATP → Asn + Glu + AMP + PPi. The aspartate carbon skeleton and its α-amino nitrogen are retained in asparagine. The second nitrogen comes from the amide group of glutamine. The remaining glutamine skeleton becomes glutamate.

Activation of the aspartate side-chain carboxyl group by ATP is important. The carboxyl group would otherwise be an unfavorable target for direct amide formation under cellular conditions. AS generates an activated intermediate through ATP cleavage to AMP and pyrophosphate. Ammonia produced from glutamine hydrolysis is transferred to this intermediate. Subsequent hydrolysis of PPi by pyrophosphatase favors the overall process. A molecule of ATP cleaved to AMP and PPi costs approximately two high-energy phosphate equivalents when ATP is regenerated; this is not the same as consuming two ATP molecules in the written AS equation.

Compare the nitrogen-transport properties of glutamine and asparagine. Each carries two N atoms per molecule, but glutamine carries five carbon atoms and asparagine carries four. Their molar N:C ratios are 2/5 = 0.40 and 2/4 = 0.50, respectively. Asparagine therefore carries more nitrogen per carbon atom. Conversely, per mole of N transported, it carries two moles of carbon atoms rather than 2.5. This difference explains why asparagine is an efficient vehicle for organic nitrogen when carbon is relatively limited or when nitrogen must be moved to developing tissues. The ratio alone does not predict the dominant transport compound in all plants: enzyme regulation, transporter availability, tissue metabolism and species identity matter.

Some plants show increased expression of particular AS genes under darkness or conditions of relatively high nitrogen compared with carbohydrate availability. The advantage is not that asparagine synthesis requires no carbon or energy; it requires both. Instead, the molecule packages two nitrogen atoms in a shorter carbon skeleton than glutamine. In a prolonged dark treatment, absolute biosynthesis may still decline as energy and carbon skeleton availability decline. A rise in asparagine relative to other amino acids is not equivalent to an increase in total N assimilation.

When transported asparagine reaches a growing sink, its nitrogen need not remain locked in that molecule. Asparaginase can hydrolyze asparagine to aspartate and ammonium. The ammonium may then re-enter GS–GOGAT, while aspartate can enter proteins or other metabolic pathways. In addition, amino acids can be exchanged and metabolized through reactions suited to the particular tissue. Thus transport forms must be understood together with both their synthesis at sources and their metabolism at sinks.

If 12 μmol aspartate and 9 μmol glutamine are available to AS, at most 9 μmol of asparagine can form in a simplified closed system with excess ATP. The products carry 18 μmol of nitrogen atoms in asparagine, 9 μmol of nitrogen atoms in glutamate, and three μmol of unreacted aspartate nitrogen. Before the reaction the substrates contained 12 + 18 = 30 μmol N; afterward the accounted total is 18 + 9 + 3 = 30 μmol N. The reaction consumes 9 μmol ATP molecules, corresponding to about 18 μmol high-energy phosphate equivalents after pyrophosphate hydrolysis. This type of bookkeeping is more reliable than treating all ATP-consuming reactions as energetically identical.

Asparagine synthesis, hydrolysis and the economics of allocation

ATP activation creates a reactive aspartyl intermediate that makes formation of a side-chain amide chemically favorable. The nitrogen supplied by glutamine can then attack the activated carbonyl; AMP and pyrophosphate are produced. Hydrolysis of pyrophosphate in the cellular context helps drive the process forward. Counting one ATP molecule consumed is correct as a molecular stoichiometry, but the energetic expenditure is commonly counted as two phosphoanhydride-equivalent steps when ATP is converted to AMP and pyrophosphate and the latter is hydrolyzed. These are different conventions, so a calculation must state which is being used. The nitrogen on the α-carbon of aspartate remains present in the product.

A plant transporting 40 µmol of nitrogen as asparagine requires 20 µmol of the molecules because each has two nitrogen atoms. They contain 80 µmol of carbon atoms. The same quantity of nitrogen carried exclusively as glutamine would require 20 µmol of that carrier but contain 100 µmol of carbon atoms. The saving is 20 µmol carbon atoms relative to the glutamine-only example. If nitrogen were carried as aspartate instead, 40 µmol molecules would be necessary and their carbon content would be 160 µmol. These computations compare the elemental compositions of transport forms; they do not prove that asparagine always has the lowest total energetic cost once synthesis, loading and conversion in the receiving tissue are included.

Asparaginase provides a route for recovering the amide nitrogen from transported asparagine. Hydrolysis produces aspartate and ammonium. The aspartate carbon skeleton can be used directly or undergo transamination. The released ammonium can re-enter GS–GOGAT provided an appropriate assimilation capacity exists. This means that a sink receiving asparagine receives usable carbon as well as nitrogen and can redistribute both according to its own metabolic demands. The relative importance of hydrolysis and other metabolic conversions varies with tissue and species. An inability to mobilize the transported pool would restrict sink growth even when the vascular sap contains abundant nitrogen.

Consider two plants with the same total leaf nitrogen, but one displays greater asparagine accumulation after dark treatment. There are several plausible explanations: AS synthesis may have increased, asparagine breakdown may have decreased, or transport to the sink may have slowed. A higher ratio of Asn to Gln is consistent with altered nitrogen allocation, but it cannot alone identify which of these rates changed. If AS transcript abundance also rises, the case for regulatory induction is strengthened, but the actual enzyme flux is still unmeasured. Combining the transcript, enzyme activity, precursor pools and time-resolved label transfer gives a more convincing causal account.

An important exception to a simple asparagine economy argument is a tissue experiencing severe energy depletion. It may receive carbon-poor nitrogen input yet lack ATP to support the very biosynthetic reactions that make a compact nitrogen carrier. The expression of an AS gene can be favored by a relative excess of nitrogen without the resulting flux being unlimited. Likewise a seed that has a strong requirement for a particular amino acid may benefit from direct import of that amino acid rather than from conversion of all incoming nitrogen through asparagine. Plant allocation is a constrained network, not a competition in which the molecule with the highest nitrogen-to-carbon ratio must always win.

5. The aspartate family: branch points and feedback

An amino-acid family groups products by the carbon precursor from which the pathway begins. It does not imply that every member is formed in a single step or that all its nitrogen atoms come directly from the first precursor. The aspartate family provides a particularly clear example of this distinction. Lysine, methionine, threonine and isoleucine are connected to the aspartate pathway in plants. Isoleucine is additionally grouped with the branched-chain amino acids because its final synthesis shares enzymes and reactions with valine and leucine.

Aspartate kinase (AK) uses ATP to phosphorylate aspartate. Aspartate semialdehyde dehydrogenase then forms aspartate semialdehyde with reducing power. This intermediate is an important junction: part of the flux enters the lysine route, and another part enters the homoserine route. The chemical logic is that converting a relatively unreactive carboxyl group into a reduced aldehyde creates an intermediate that can be extended or modified to make new side chains. These reactions involve more than moving amino groups; they alter the carbon skeleton's functional groups.

In the lysine branch, dihydrodipicolinate synthase (DHDPS) catalyzes condensation of aspartate semialdehyde with pyruvate. Further steps in the diaminopimelate pathway produce lysine. Lysine has an additional amino group in its side chain, and its carbon skeleton has six carbons rather than the four of aspartate. Thus the simple name 'aspartate-derived' does not mean that all six carbons or both N atoms arise from a single aspartate molecule. Recognizing the additional carbon input avoids a frequent isotope-tracing mistake. The pathway is found in plants and many microorganisms, whereas animals generally obtain lysine from their diet.

In the other major branch, homoserine dehydrogenase converts aspartate semialdehyde to homoserine with reducing equivalents. Homoserine can be activated by phosphorylation to O-phosphohomoserine. Threonine synthase catalyzes the formation of threonine. Methionine biosynthesis also uses O-phosphohomoserine in plants, but it requires sulfur input from cysteine through cystathionine γ-synthase. Cystathionine is subsequently converted to homocysteine, and methionine synthase transfers a methyl group to homocysteine. The methyl donor is linked to folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism. In this way, methionine synthesis couples the aspartate family to sulfur assimilation and methyl-group metabolism.

Threonine can be used directly in protein synthesis or converted by threonine deaminase to 2-oxobutyrate with release of ammonium. 2-Oxobutyrate then enters the branched-chain pathway with pyruvate and eventually yields isoleucine. An aminotransferase introduces nitrogen at a late step. This example shows why tracing carbon ancestry and nitrogen ancestry independently is essential. Although isoleucine is classified as an aspartate-family member, threonine's original amino nitrogen need not be retained in the isoleucine product.

Branch regulation helps distribute flux according to demand. If lysine is already abundant, feedback inhibition of lysine-sensitive aspartate kinase isoforms and DHDPS can restrict further synthesis. Threonine-sensitive AK isoforms and regulation at later branch enzymes contribute additional control. In the isoleucine route, isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase in many plant systems. Feedback inhibition acts on enzymes already present and can adjust reaction rates rapidly. Changes in enzyme expression can act over longer intervals. Neither process is identical to shutting down an entire metabolic family.

Imagine a plant with a feedback-insensitive DHDPS but normal upstream enzymes. When lysine accumulates, DHDPS can continue to consume aspartate semialdehyde more readily than the normal enzyme. This can increase lysine formation, but the effect is not unlimited: common precursor supply, aspartate kinase control, other enzymes, lysine degradation and transport can all constrain accumulation. Other aspartate-family products might be affected because they compete for the shared intermediate. An experiment must therefore measure multiple metabolites rather than lysine alone before claiming that the modification has no other consequences.

Plant research on feedback-insensitive enzymes and amino acid accumulation provides an experimental basis for the branch model. The relevant evidence is not simply that an enzyme is inhibited in a test tube; changing a regulatory property can alter the distribution of amino acid pools in a living plant. However, measured pool changes reflect synthesis and breakdown together. A direct test of branch flux requires a tracer time course or an independently constrained flux calculation. The distinction is useful when evaluating Part C questions involving engineered plants.

Reconstruct the aspartate family from the first branch

A useful way to draw this family is to start with oxaloacetate, which gains an amino group to yield aspartate. Aspartate kinase then activates its side-chain carboxyl group by phosphorylation. Aspartate semialdehyde dehydrogenase uses reducing power to produce the semialdehyde. At this stage the four-carbon skeleton can follow at least two major routes. The lysine route condenses the semialdehyde with pyruvate, while the homoserine route reduces the semialdehyde. The products of the latter route share homoserine as a precursor. The separation of common steps and dedicated steps is the central reason why inhibition of different enzymes generates different patterns of deficiency.

In the lysine route, condensation of a four-carbon semialdehyde with a three-carbon pyruvate produces a seven-carbon intermediate; the pathway ultimately reaches diaminopimelate, which loses a carboxyl group to produce six-carbon lysine. The additional amino group of lysine is introduced within the pathway, rather than by attaching an amino group directly to oxaloacetate and completing the synthesis in one step. Plants use a diaminopimelate pathway distinct from the α-aminoadipate route characteristic of fungi. For this chapter the important features are the shared aspartate entry, the condensation with pyruvate, the two nitrogen atoms in lysine and the dedicated feedback-sensitive entry enzyme DHDPS.

The homoserine branch provides the common carbon framework for threonine and methionine. Homoserine kinase yields O-phosphohomoserine, which can be converted by threonine synthase to threonine. Alternatively, plant cystathionine γ-synthase combines the activated homoserine derivative with reduced sulfur supplied by cysteine; cystathionine is subsequently converted to homocysteine, whose methylation produces methionine. The methyl group is provided through folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism. Thus methionine has five carbons even though the homoserine framework has four. Its sulfur is not obtained from nitrate, and a methionine deficiency can persist despite abundant inorganic nitrogen if reduced sulfur is limiting.

Methionine also supplies the precursor for S-adenosylmethionine, which has roles in methyl transfer and ethylene biosynthesis. This provides a physiological connection to the hormone chapter without changing the topic of the current lesson. The demand for methionine reflects both its incorporation into protein and the turnover of SAM-dependent reactions. In plants SAM can activate threonine synthase, providing a regulatory link between methionine status and the competing threonine branch. The detailed regulatory responses vary between isoforms and species, but the branch logic is robust: changes in one end-product can influence flux to another product through shared precursors.

Isoleucine illustrates an overlap between families. Threonine deaminase converts four-carbon threonine into 2-oxobutyrate, releasing its nitrogen as ammonium. The carbon skeleton enters a condensation with pyruvate, followed by transformations similar to those used in valine biosynthesis. A final transamination introduces amino nitrogen. Thus the isoleucine carbon backbone has an aspartate-derived contribution, even though its late reactions belong to the branched-chain amino acid pathway. The nitrogen removed from threonine need not be the nitrogen eventually attached to isoleucine. This is exactly why a carbon-family diagram cannot be read as a map of nitrogen transfer without further information.

Feedback at common and dedicated enzymes

A shared upstream enzyme such as aspartate kinase can respond to pathway end-products and reduce the total inflow into the network. A dedicated enzyme such as DHDPS provides additional control over lysine production without directly shutting all other branches. If lysine is abundant, inhibition of the dedicated step can reduce lysine synthesis while leaving substrate available for threonine and methionine synthesis. Conversely, a feedback-insensitive DHDPS can pull more carbon through the lysine branch if the upstream supply and downstream reactions permit it. A simple drawing showing two inhibitory arrows is not a guarantee that the feedback coefficients are equal: distinct plant AK isoenzymes show different sensitivities to lysine and threonine.

Consider a hypothetical plastid receiving 30 µmol aspartate semialdehyde per hour. Initially 12 enter the lysine branch and 18 the homoserine branch. Suppose a mutation raises the lysine draw to 20 while total input remains fixed and there are no other drains or accumulation. Homoserine flux must fall to 10 µmol per hour. The decline is 8, or about 44% of its original 18. If instead the total inflow rises to 38, the homoserine flux could remain at 18. The conclusion of competition therefore depends explicitly on the fixed-inflow condition. It is possible for a regulatory perturbation to change the total throughput as well as the partitioning between branches.

Classical manipulation of lysine feedback and degradation in Arabidopsis seeds demonstrates that formation and removal both control accumulation. Increasing the synthetic capacity through a feedback-insensitive DHDPS can elevate lysine; reducing its catabolism can further increase the free pool. These two interventions address different terms in a mass balance, so a combined effect does not imply that they act on the same biochemical reaction. It also does not guarantee a proportional increase in seed protein-bound lysine or normal seed viability. Nutritional analysis must distinguish free amino acid, total amino acid after protein hydrolysis, seed mass and overall yield.

An important examination distinction is between removing feedback and deleting an enzyme. Deletion of the sole active dedicated lysine enzyme in a simplified model blocks formation through that branch. A mutation specifically removing product inhibition leaves the catalytic reaction possible and may raise its rate under conditions where the wild-type enzyme is inhibited. The effects are opposite even though both mutations concern the same gene. In real plants isoenzyme redundancy and imported lysine may modify the phenotype. Predict the biochemical effect first, then consider compensation at the tissue and whole-plant levels.

6. Pyruvate, branched-chain amino acids and the glutamate family

Pyruvate is a three-carbon product of glycolytic carbon metabolism. Direct transamination by AlaAT gives alanine without changing the number of carbons. Alanine can serve as a reversible link between carbohydrate and nitrogen metabolism. In some plant tissues under oxygen limitation, changes in pyruvate metabolism favor alanine accumulation. The nitrogen is transferred from an existing organic donor, and the carbon skeleton is recovered if the reaction runs in reverse. The extent of accumulation depends on the tissue and physiological state.

Valine biosynthesis differs from direct alanine formation because its five-carbon skeleton must first be constructed. Acetohydroxyacid synthase (AHAS), also known as acetolactate synthase, catalyzes a reaction using two pyruvate molecules, releasing carbon dioxide and forming a five-carbon intermediate. Subsequent reduction and dehydration generate a branched keto acid. A branched-chain aminotransferase then transfers nitrogen to form valine. The carbon count is informative: six carbons entered the initial condensation and one was lost as carbon dioxide. The final amino group is added after construction of the branched skeleton.

Leucine synthesis branches from a valine-pathway keto acid. Isopropylmalate synthase condenses the five-carbon keto acid with acetyl-CoA, followed by rearrangement and oxidative decarboxylation to form a six-carbon keto acid. Transamination produces leucine. Count the carbon atoms: five plus two minus one gives six. This relationship is why a defect in one of the common branched-chain pathway enzymes can affect both valine and leucine, whereas a defect in a leucine-specific branch enzyme is expected to have a more direct effect on leucine.

Isoleucine also has six carbons but enters the shared pathway through 2-oxobutyrate, which derives from threonine. AHAS combines a four-carbon substrate with a three-carbon pyruvate and releases carbon dioxide, giving a six-carbon intermediate. The downstream reactions resemble the valine pathway. Valine, leucine and isoleucine are therefore chemically related and share biosynthetic steps, yet their precise carbon origins differ. The final transamination introduces organic amino nitrogen in all three routes.

The common enzyme AHAS is inhibited by certain herbicides. The physiological problem is not that these compounds remove free amino acids instantly; the inhibited enzyme restricts production of branched-chain carbon skeletons. Growing meristems have a continuing need for protein synthesis and are consequently sensitive to disrupted supply. Supplementation experiments with the relevant products can help identify which pathway is affected under suitable conditions, although uptake and transport may limit the rescue. This is a conceptual example of how enzyme specificity and plant growth connect.

The 2-OG-derived family includes glutamate, glutamine, proline and arginine. Glutamate and glutamine are made by the assimilation reactions already considered. Proline biosynthesis from glutamate includes ATP-dependent phosphorylation and reductive reactions that form glutamate semialdehyde, which can cyclize to pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C). P5C reductase forms proline. The product retains the five-carbon skeleton, but the amino nitrogen becomes part of a ring. Proline formation thus requires chemical rearrangement and reduction rather than a single direct transamination.

Proline often accumulates in plants exposed to drought or salinity, contributing to osmotic adjustment and protective functions. Its abundance reflects both synthesis and degradation. During recovery, proline can be oxidized and its carbon and nitrogen recovered through glutamate metabolism. Measuring a higher concentration is not sufficient to prove that a plant has greater stress tolerance or that all proline was synthesized during the stress treatment. The stress physiology of proline is developed in 6.H; the central lesson here is that a nitrogen-containing compound can serve as a reversible metabolic resource.

Arginine biosynthesis begins from glutamate and proceeds through acetylated intermediates to ornithine. Acetylation helps organize the pathway and prevents unwanted side reactions during the formation of the semialdehyde intermediates. Ornithine combines with carbamoyl phosphate to form citrulline; further reactions involving aspartate form argininosuccinate and then arginine with release of fumarate. Nitrogen is incorporated at multiple steps. Arginine has six carbon atoms and four nitrogen atoms, a high N:C ratio of 4/6. It can therefore be important in nitrogen storage, especially in some seeds. This does not mean it is always the dominant long-distance nitrogen form.

Regulatory connections help balance this branch with the rest of the cell. Arginine can restrict its own synthesis by feedback control at earlier steps, and plastid metabolism responds to the availability of nitrogen and energy. The broad principle is more important than memorizing a separate regulatory protein for every species. Compare a plastid supplied with abundant glutamate but insufficient ATP to one supplied with both. The second has greater potential to support energy-requiring steps, although other substrates and feedback may still constrain the pathway.

Constructing the branched carbon skeletons

Valine has five carbon atoms, so direct amination of three-carbon pyruvate cannot produce valine. The initial acetohydroxyacid synthase reaction joins two pyruvate-derived contributions and releases carbon dioxide, producing a five-carbon acetolactate framework. Ketol-acid reductoisomerase rearranges and reduces the intermediate; dihydroxyacid dehydratase produces a branched keto acid, 2-oxoisovalerate. A branched-chain aminotransferase supplies its amino nitrogen to give valine. The carbon-building reactions therefore precede the final aminotransferase step. This sequence provides a reason why a defect in AHAS can alter several amino acids at once, whereas a defect limited to the final alanine aminotransferase has a different direct target.

In leucine synthesis, the valine-pathway keto acid 2-oxoisovalerate is the branch substrate. Isopropylmalate synthase condenses it with acetyl-CoA, and subsequent isomerization and oxidative decarboxylation extend the carbon framework by one net carbon atom. The final keto acid has six carbons and is converted into leucine by transamination. Leucine is therefore related to valine through a common keto-acid intermediate, not simply by adding a methyl group to an already completed valine molecule. The regulation of isopropylmalate synthase provides a dedicated point at which the leucine branch can respond to its own product.

For isoleucine, combine four-carbon 2-oxobutyrate with the contribution from pyruvate after decarboxylation. This produces a six-carbon intermediate that follows the common branched-chain reaction sequence. Valine uses two pyruvates and loses one carbon overall, while isoleucine uses 2-oxobutyrate plus pyruvate and likewise loses one carbon in the initial condensation. The carbon budgets are therefore 3 + 3 − 1 = 5 and 4 + 3 − 1 = 6, respectively. These simple balances allow the student to predict product size even if the names of the intervening keto acids have been forgotten.

A typical plant AHAS inhibitor reduces the input into this shared synthetic network. A rescue experiment that supplies valine alone may not restore growth because leucine and isoleucine can remain limiting. Even supplementation with all three cannot guarantee complete rescue if uptake is inadequate or plastid pools are inaccessible. If all three amino acids rescue substantially more effectively than valine alone, however, that pattern supports a shared biosynthetic bottleneck. The inference is stronger if the inhibitor directly reduces AHAS activity in a comparable enzyme assay. A second unrelated stressor may also reduce growth but would not be expected to show the same specific substrate-rescue pattern.

Proline and arginine as contrasting glutamate-family outputs

In the proline pathway, glutamate is activated and reduced by the bifunctional enzyme P5CS, forming glutamate semialdehyde that can cyclize to pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C). P5C reductase then yields proline. The five-carbon skeleton and nitrogen can be retained while the side-chain chemistry changes to form the cyclic amino acid. This route consumes ATP and reducing equivalents. During recovery, proline can be oxidized through mitochondrial enzymes back toward glutamate, releasing reducing power. Accumulation of proline under stress can therefore reflect increased synthesis, reduced degradation, altered transport or a combination of these. The pool alone does not quantify the rate of osmotic adjustment or establish that it causes all of the observed tolerance.

A comparison of two salt-treated plants illustrates the issue. Plant A accumulates 20 µmol proline per gram fresh mass and survives, whereas plant B accumulates 35 and grows poorly. It would be wrong to conclude that the larger proline pool necessarily confers greater tolerance. Plant B may be experiencing more severe stress or slower degradation. Fresh mass itself can vary with water content, altering the apparent concentration. Measurements per dry mass, water status, growth and direct perturbation of proline metabolism can help distinguish adaptive function from a marker of injury. This reasoning stays within plant nitrogen metabolism while preparing for more complete stress physiology in 6.H.

Arginine is particularly nitrogen-rich: six carbons and four nitrogens per molecule. Its synthesis via ornithine, citrulline and argininosuccinate uses additional nitrogen donors and energy. Carbamoyl phosphate introduces a carbon and nitrogen contribution; aspartate provides another nitrogen during argininosuccinate formation and the residual aspartate-derived carbon leaves as fumarate. The arginine molecule's total nitrogen content cannot be explained by a single transfer from glutamate. This illustrates how multiple central nitrogen entry points are coupled within a pathway. Its high nitrogen content also makes arginine a useful form for storing nitrogen in some seeds or storage tissues, though transport composition differs among species.

Breakdown of arginine through arginase produces ornithine and urea, and urease can release ammonium from urea for reassimilation. Urease requires nickel, so a deficiency in that micronutrient can interfere with reuse of urea-derived nitrogen. This links mineral nutrition to organic nitrogen mobilization. The example does not mean that nickel is a nitrogen donor or that all arginine nitrogen passes through urea in every tissue; it identifies one route through which a storage compound can contribute to the supply of reusable nitrogen during development. A seedling relying on stored protein before effective photosynthesis requires coordinated nitrogen release and enough energy to use it.

7. Aromatic amino acids, serine, glycine and sulfur incorporation

Not every amino-acid family begins with a TCA-cycle intermediate or pyruvate. Plants synthesize aromatic amino acids through the shikimate pathway, which connects phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) from glycolytic metabolism with erythrose 4-phosphate from the pentose phosphate network. These two carbon precursors contribute to an initial seven-carbon compound, and subsequent reactions produce shikimate-derived intermediates leading to chorismate. Chorismate is a branch point for the biosynthesis of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan, among other compounds.

Phenylalanine and tyrosine in many plants are formed largely through plastidial prephenate/arogenate routes. Amination can occur at the prephenate stage to produce arogenate, followed by reactions that yield the aromatic amino acids. Other routes can operate in particular species or compartments. The important contrast with simple pyruvate transamination is that the aromatic carbon skeleton must first be constructed and aromatized. A nitrogen donor supplies the amino group at an appropriate stage of the pathway rather than creating the aromatic ring itself.

Tryptophan takes a different branch from chorismate. Anthranilate synthase uses glutamine-derived nitrogen in the conversion of chorismate to anthranilate. Further steps lead to indole-containing intermediates, and tryptophan synthase combines indole with serine to form tryptophan. The final molecule has two nitrogen atoms, one in its indole ring and one in the amino group. Their origins need not be identical. A pulse of amide-labeled glutamine could supply label to the indole nitrogen; serine can supply the α-amino nitrogen. This is another example in which the nitrogen positions must be distinguished.

Aromatic amino acids also feed the synthesis of numerous specialized products. Phenylalanine is a substrate for phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, the entry enzyme for phenylpropanoid formation. Tryptophan is linked to indole-derived compounds, including an important route of auxin biosynthesis. These products explain why the shikimate pathway is physiologically important beyond protein synthesis. Detailed phenylpropanoid and hormone pathways belong to their dedicated chapters. Here the focus is on the formation and supply of the amino acid precursors.

Serine has multiple routes of synthesis in plants. One starts from 3-phosphoglycerate, a photosynthetic and glycolytic intermediate. Oxidation produces a phosphorylated keto acid, transamination introduces nitrogen, and dephosphorylation gives serine. In photorespiring leaves, glycine and serine are extensively interconverted in mitochondria during the recycling of carbon from Rubisco oxygenation. These pathways have different physiological roles, and their relative contributions depend on the tissue and conditions. Glycine is also connected to one-carbon metabolism through serine hydroxymethyltransferase. Thus serine and glycine link amino acid production to photosynthesis, carbon salvage and biosynthetic one-carbon units.

Cysteine biosynthesis couples nitrogen-containing carbon compounds to sulfur assimilation. Serine acetyltransferase converts serine and acetyl-CoA to O-acetylserine. O-acetylserine(thiol)lyase uses sulfide and O-acetylserine to form cysteine and acetate. The serine amino group is retained while sulfur replaces the activated side-chain group. Sulfide is generated through plant sulfate assimilation. If sulfate reduction is limited, supplying extra nitrate may increase some nitrogenous pools but does not necessarily restore cysteine or methionine synthesis. Sulfur and nitrogen nutrition are thus interdependent rather than independently substitutable.

Cysteine participates in glutathione synthesis and provides reduced sulfur for the methionine pathway. Glutathione contains glutamate, cysteine and glycine and has important antioxidant functions. The network therefore connects three families of amino acids to cellular redox maintenance. Nevertheless, glutathione should not be counted as a free amino acid; it is a tripeptide. Its abundance depends on ATP-consuming synthesis, substrate availability and turnover, not just the amount of cysteine in isolation.

This diversity of pathways gives an important general rule: never assume that every amino acid is synthesized simply by transferring glutamate's amino group to a familiar central-metabolic keto acid. Some require multiple carbon additions or reductions; some use glutamine amide nitrogen; others incorporate a nitrogen-bearing precursor or sulfur. The common metabolic logic is conservation of atoms combined with enzyme-catalyzed conversions and resource constraints.

Distinct routes into aromatic amino acids

The shikimate pathway begins when phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and erythrose 4-phosphate contribute to a seven-carbon intermediate. After transformations including reduction, phosphorylation and a later PEP-dependent step, chorismate is produced. Chorismate is a branch point rather than an amino acid. In the phenylalanine and tyrosine routes it is rearranged to prephenate. In many plants, prephenate undergoes transamination to form arogenate, after which distinct reactions form phenylalanine or tyrosine. Arogenate dehydratase and arogenate dehydrogenase are useful representative branch enzymes. Alternative routes exist, and the predominance of one route in a model plant should not be made an absolute statement for all plants.

Tryptophan uses chorismate differently. Anthranilate synthase incorporates nitrogen supplied by glutamine into anthranilate; subsequent transformations introduce additional carbon framework from phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate and eventually produce indole. Tryptophan synthase combines indole with serine. The serine contributes the amino-bearing portion of tryptophan, while the indole nitrogen originated earlier in the pathway. Tryptophan therefore contains two nitrogens, whose origins must be considered separately in a tracer experiment. The distinction between glutamine amide donation and amino-acid incorporation is especially clear in this pathway.

Feedback-sensitive anthranilate synthase provides a dedicated control point for tryptophan synthesis. Imagine a plant with enhanced phenylalanine consumption in cell-wall phenolic production. A larger withdrawal of chorismate-derived precursors may change flux toward tryptophan if the shared pathway cannot supply the extra demand. Nevertheless, increased input into the common pathway could compensate. Aromatic amino acids connect primary metabolism to specialized metabolites, but the full phenylpropanoid pathway is taught in 6.G. Here the question is how nitrogen enters and how the carbon-family branches compete for a common intermediate.

Serine, glycine and reduced sulfur

Serine is produced through a phosphorylated pathway from 3-phosphoglycerate, especially important in nonphotosynthetic tissues and in some developmental contexts. The first reaction oxidizes the three-carbon precursor to phosphohydroxypyruvate. Phosphoserine aminotransferase introduces nitrogen from glutamate, and phosphoserine phosphatase removes the phosphate to yield serine. This sequence demonstrates that transamination may act on an intermediate rather than the final free keto acid associated with a familiar glycolytic metabolite. Phosphorylation and dephosphorylation can organize metabolic routes within specific compartments. A mutant with impaired phosphoserine synthesis may not be fully rescued by the presence of alternative photorespiratory serine production if the relevant tissue cannot access it.

In illuminated C3 leaves, photorespiration supplies another substantial source of serine. Two glycine molecules can yield one serine with release of carbon dioxide and ammonium through the mitochondrial glycine decarboxylase/serine hydroxymethyltransferase system. The nitrogen released must be reassimilated, linking photorespiration to GS–GOGAT. Counting atoms provides a useful check: two glycines supply four carbon atoms and two nitrogens, while serine carries three carbons and one nitrogen, carbon dioxide carries the remaining carbon, and ammonium carries the remaining nitrogen. This is a nitrogen recycling problem even if the plant has not taken up any new nitrate during the interval.

The relationship between sulfur and amino acid synthesis is most direct in cysteine. Serine acetyltransferase forms O-acetylserine from serine and acetyl-CoA. O-acetylserine(thiol)lyase combines O-acetylserine with sulfide, releasing acetate. Sulfide derives from reduced sulfur assimilation and is not the same substrate as sulfate. A nitrate-rich but sulfate-deprived plant can synthesize many nitrogenous intermediates while lacking enough reduced sulfur for cysteine, methionine and glutathione production. In sulfur deficiency O-acetylserine can accumulate, reflecting an imbalance between carbon/nitrogen precursor availability and reduced sulfur supply. Its accumulation can also participate in sulfur-responsive regulation, but it is not by itself a unique diagnostic of the rate of sulfate uptake.

Histidine belongs to a different carbon-family category: its synthesis begins with phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate and ATP. It shares metabolic resources with nucleotide metabolism but is not merely a product of nucleotide degradation. Histidine provides a useful reminder that grouping the standard amino acids by carbon origin requires more than the major glycolytic and TCA-derived families. For NET-level purposes it is enough here to identify its distinct PRPP-associated origin and the existence of pathway feedback at ATP phosphoribosyltransferase. Detailed enumeration of all intermediates adds little to the nitrogen-integration argument.

8. Carbon skeleton supply, energy and nitrogen assimilation

The assimilation network continually consumes carbon skeletons. 2-OG is required when GOGAT turns glutamine into glutamate; oxaloacetate is the immediate acceptor in AspAT; pyruvate supplies alanine and branched-chain pathways; 3-phosphoglycerate supplies serine; and PEP together with erythrose 4-phosphate supplies aromatic compounds. These precursor pools are not independent of one another. Their production and consumption link amino acid synthesis to photosynthesis, glycolysis and the TCA network.

Photosynthesis in mature leaves supplies carbohydrates and reducing power. The fixed carbon can be converted into triose phosphates, sucrose, starch or other intermediates. Glycolytic and respiratory metabolism in both green and non-green tissues supplies ATP and carbon skeletons. Isocitrate dehydrogenase produces 2-OG through oxidative decarboxylation of isocitrate, and multiple cellular compartments contribute to the availability of 2-OG for nitrogen assimilation. Saying simply that GOGAT uses a TCA intermediate does not require that the enzyme itself be located in the mitochondrial matrix.

A continuing withdrawal of oxaloacetate or 2-OG for biosynthesis creates a need for replenishment. Anaplerotic reactions replenish intermediates drained from central metabolism. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) adds bicarbonate to PEP, yielding oxaloacetate and inorganic phosphate. In a simplified equation, PEP + HCO₃⁻ → oxaloacetate + Pi. This reaction can supply carbon skeletons for nitrogen assimilation in addition to the well-known role of PEPC in C₄ photosynthesis. Do not confuse anaplerotic CO₂ fixation in a C₃ plant with the entire C₄ carbon-concentrating pathway.

Consider the combination of GS–GOGAT with AspAT. The simplified net GS–GOGAT reaction consumes ammonium, 2-OG, ATP and reducing equivalents to give an additional glutamate. AspAT transfers that glutamate nitrogen to oxaloacetate, regenerating 2-OG. In the combined idealized reaction, glutamate and 2-OG can cancel as internal intermediates. The net result is incorporation of ammonium-derived nitrogen into the carbon skeleton of oxaloacetate, with an ATP and reducing-power requirement inherited from assimilation. This is a net accounting model, not a claim that the reactions occur in one compartment or that the internal pools are never used for other purposes.

For each 1 mol of new nitrogen entering the glutamate pool through GS–GOGAT, the idealized cost includes one ATP cleaved to ADP and two electron equivalents for GOGAT. If nitrate is the starting source, nitrate-to-ammonium reduction also requires eight electron equivalents. For example, assimilating 5 μmol nitrate-derived N into alanine via NR, NiR, GS, GOGAT and AlaAT requires at least 5 μmol ATP at GS and 50 μmol electron equivalents for reduction plus GOGAT, under these explicitly simplified assumptions. It also requires 5 μmol pyruvate as final carbon acceptor. Energy costs for uptake, carbon-skeleton production, transport, maintenance and subsequent protein synthesis are not counted in that minimum.

Nitrogen and carbon availability interact through both metabolic and regulatory mechanisms. If nitrate is abundant and carbon supply is reduced, organic acid availability may become limiting. If carbon is plentiful but nitrogen is scarce, plants may accumulate carbohydrate while protein synthesis and growth remain nitrogen limited. Nitrogen assimilation capacity also responds to nitrate, sugars, organic acids and amino acid status through changes in enzyme activity and gene expression. These responses help match the metabolic investment to the plant's nutrient state rather than relying solely on substrate concentrations.

A nitrogen supply experiment should therefore include a carbon dimension. In a simple factorial design, compare high and low nitrogen at high and low light, keeping other nutrients and temperature constant. Measuring dry mass, nitrate, organic acids, soluble amino acids and total nitrogen can distinguish several possible patterns. If a low-light, high-nitrate group accumulates nitrate but makes little new protein, a carbon or energy limitation is plausible. Yet light also affects gene expression, nitrate reductase activity, respiration and development. To establish a causal mechanism, additional measurements of assimilation rates and carbon availability are needed.

The network is a useful example of co-limitation: relieving one resource constraint can reveal another. Adding nitrogen to a carbon-limited plant may not increase biomass, and adding carbon to a nitrogen-starved plant cannot create nitrogen atoms. A balanced supply of mineral nutrients, light, water and carbon dioxide is needed for sustained growth. The relevant question is not which nutrient is always most important, but which metabolic process limits flux under the stated conditions.

Derive the cost of supplying an amino group

The simplified net GS–GOGAT reaction uses ammonium, 2-oxoglutarate, one ATP and two reducing equivalents to produce one net glutamate, alongside ADP and inorganic phosphate. Combining it with AspAT cancels both glutamate and 2-oxoglutarate from the net substrate-product list: the overall net carbon acceptor is oxaloacetate, which becomes aspartate. The intermediate 2-oxoglutarate is regenerated through the transfer step, although metabolic pools must be maintained to allow the reactions to run. The resulting aspartate contains newly assimilated nitrogen and the oxaloacetate carbon framework. It would be incorrect to charge a second ATP directly to AspAT simply because the overall process requires energy.

When nitrate supplies the initial nitrogen, nitrate reductase transfers two electrons to reduce nitrate to nitrite and nitrite reductase uses six more electrons to yield ammonium. GS–GOGAT adds two reducing equivalents at the assimilation stage. Thus, for one newly assimilated nitrate-N directed into alanine in this idealized accounting, the nitrate-to-ammonium reduction requires eight electrons and the glutamate synthase step two, totaling ten. At least one ATP molecule is cleaved by GS. This omits costs of uptake, precursor production, maintenance, transport and subsequent protein incorporation. Reporting the calculation as the full cost of building plant protein would substantially underestimate the energetic requirement.

Suppose photosynthesis can provide the equivalent of 60 µmol electrons per hour specifically for the combined reduction and assimilation steps. If nitrate is the only N source, the upper bound for new amino-N formation under the above assumptions is 60/10 = 6 µmol per hour. With ammonium supplied directly, the corresponding electron-only upper bound is 60/2 = 30 µmol per hour, but that does not mean ammonium-fed plants will necessarily grow five times faster. ATP, carbon skeleton availability, toxicity, pH regulation and enzyme capacity may all be limiting. The calculation illustrates a stoichiometric bound, not a prediction of whole-plant yield.

Replenishment versus withdrawal

PEP carboxylase adds bicarbonate carbon to three-carbon phosphoenolpyruvate, yielding four-carbon oxaloacetate and inorganic phosphate. This is an anaplerotic reaction when it replenishes intermediates withdrawn for biosynthesis. Oxaloacetate can be converted to malate, used for aspartate synthesis or contribute to the maintenance of organic-acid pools. PEP carboxylase is not a replacement for Rubisco in ordinary C3 photosynthetic carbon fixation. Its metabolic replenishment function is relevant even in nonphotosynthetic cells. A plant using central-metabolic intermediates for amino acid production must maintain the carbon pathways that sustain the available acceptor pools.

Consider a tissue with an oxaloacetate production rate of 15 µmol per hour from replenishing reactions and an initial total withdrawal of 15, partitioned as 9 for aspartate synthesis and 6 for other metabolism. Increasing the aspartate withdrawal to 12 without altering supply creates a net loss of 3 µmol per hour. If the initial accessible pool is 12 µmol, extrapolation at that constant loss would reach zero in four hours. In reality the rates would change as substrate concentration declined; the linear extrapolation is an initial mass-balance illustration rather than a biologically plausible prediction of complete depletion. Increased replenishment or reduced competing withdrawal would be needed to establish a new steady state.

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio is useful only when the numerator and denominator are specified. An elemental C:N ratio for a dry leaf measures total elemental contents. A ratio of soluble sugars to amino acids instead describes selected metabolic pools. Both can respond to nutrient supply, but they are not numerically interchangeable. If dry matter contains 40% carbon and 2% nitrogen by mass, the mass ratio is 20:1. The molar ratio is (40/12)/(2/14), approximately 23.3:1, because carbon and nitrogen atoms have different molar masses. Reporting either ratio without its basis can create apparent disagreement between otherwise consistent measurements.

At the whole-plant level, carbon availability also acts through regulation. Sugars and metabolites provide information about energy and carbon status, while nitrate, amino acids and related signals reflect nitrogen availability. A plant exposed to high nitrate and low light can adjust uptake, storage and enzyme expression rather than attempting unrestricted assimilation of all incoming nitrate. Conversely, under high light and low nitrogen, fixed carbon can accumulate in carbohydrates because growth requires nitrogen-containing cellular material. These contrasting conditions explain why carbon fixation rate, leaf nitrate and final biomass need not change in parallel. Nutrient responses should be interpreted as interacting constraints rather than separate one-factor laws.

9. Sources, sinks, seed filling and nitrogen remobilization

Nitrogen assimilation in a plant is spatially distributed. Roots can acquire inorganic nitrogen and assimilate part of it locally; nitrate can also move toward leaves through the xylem. Mature photosynthetic leaves can supply carbohydrates and export organic nitrogen through the phloem. Developing leaves, root tips, meristems and seeds are sinks that require nitrogen for protein and nucleic acid production. Source and sink are functional terms: a tissue can change its role during development.

Glutamine and asparagine are common organic nitrogen carriers. Aspartate, glutamate and other amino acids may also be present in vascular sap. Some nitrogen-fixing legumes export substantial nitrogen in ureides such as allantoin and allantoate, while others predominantly transport amides. This variation is especially important when interpreting comparisons between species. The presence of a ureide in a vascular sample is not proof of a universal plant nitrogen-transport pathway. Nor does the detection of amino acids prove that nitrate is absent from the same transport system.

The seed-filling stage illustrates nitrogen allocation. A developing seed accumulates storage proteins and other nitrogen-containing compounds. It obtains amino acids and other organic nitrogen forms from maternal tissues, and its own metabolism can interconvert those inputs to match the amino-acid composition needed for protein synthesis. The amount of incoming nitrogen matters, but so do transport, biosynthesis, sink metabolism and the rate of protein deposition. A high concentration of an amino acid in the seed may result from high import, local synthesis or low utilization; these possibilities cannot be separated by concentration alone.

Senescing leaves demonstrate the opposite direction of change. Chloroplast proteins and other nitrogen-containing molecules can be degraded, releasing amino acids and ammonium through downstream metabolism. Plants can reassimilate released ammonium and convert nitrogen into forms suitable for export to young tissues or seeds. This recycling conserves nitrogen already present in the plant; it does not add newly fixed nitrogen. Cytosolic GS isoforms, AS and other enzymes can participate in nitrogen remobilization, with isoform contributions varying across organs and species.

A source-leaf labeling experiment can show remobilization. Supply a plant with ¹⁵N during vegetative growth, then switch to unlabeled nutrient during seed filling. If ¹⁵N appears in seeds while old labeled leaves lose ¹⁵N, the result is consistent with transfer of previously assimilated nitrogen. One must still account for nitrogen from stems, roots and the medium. Measuring whole-plant isotope recovery and tissue masses is more informative than comparing isotope percentage in one leaf and one seed alone.

As a simple nitrogen budget, suppose a leaf contains 3.0 mmol N at the start of a chase period and 1.2 mmol N at the end. Its net loss is 1.8 mmol N. If developing seeds gain 1.5 mmol N and no other information is supplied, it is not valid to conclude that every atom of seed nitrogen came from that particular leaf. Roots may continue assimilating nitrogen, and other tissues may act as sources or sinks. If ¹⁵N specifically labels the leaf and all label is recovered, a tracer budget can constrain the transfer more directly. This example separates net mass balance from source attribution.

Nitrogen use efficiency is not a single biochemical enzyme property. At the plant scale it may refer to biomass or yield relative to nitrogen supplied or taken up; definitions differ according to the question. Increased assimilation at one step does not necessarily improve whole-plant efficiency if carbon supply, transport or sink utilization remains limiting. A plant that efficiently remobilizes nitrogen to seeds may have a different performance measure from one selected for vegetative leaf yield. The definition and experimental context must be stated before interpreting a numerical comparison.

Source–sink nitrogen is a dynamic system

A root supplied with mineral nitrogen can assimilate part of it locally and send nitrate or organic nitrogen upward. A leaf can reduce nitrate, synthesize amino acids and export organic nitrogen through the phloem. Developing fruits, root tips, young leaves and seeds are typical sinks, but their demand and import capacity change with development. An organ may be a sink during early expansion and later become a source. Nitrogen compounds in xylem sap are often dominated by a subset of amino acids, whereas phloem sap can carry a broader mixture. Transport measurements must therefore specify which vascular stream, species and developmental stage were sampled.

An amino acid transporter changes movement across a cell membrane; it does not add nitrogen to the molecule being transported. This elementary distinction helps classify mutants. If phloem amino acid loading is impaired, source leaves may accumulate soluble amino acids while seeds receive less nitrogen. The initial assimilation enzymes could remain normal. Conversely, a synthesis-deficient source may export less amino acid simply because the available pool is smaller even though the transporter functions normally. A targeted isotope label delivered directly to a source leaf, coupled with measurements of export and assimilation, can distinguish the alternatives more effectively than a single endpoint measurement in seeds.

In certain nodulated legumes, fixed nitrogen is exported substantially in ureides such as allantoin and allantoate; other legumes commonly export amides. This variation is a useful counterexample to treating glutamine and asparagine as universal obligatory transport compounds. Ureides have high nitrogen content and their degradation releases nitrogen for use in receiving tissues. Their presence is associated with specific metabolic capabilities and cannot be assumed merely from the fact that a plant belongs to Fabaceae. The detailed nitrogen fixation and nodule processes are developed in 6.C.05 and 6.C.06; the current point is that long-distance nitrogen form is a physiological choice shaped by species and metabolism.

Seed storage versus free amino acid accumulation

A developing seed combines carbon imported as sugars with nitrogen imported largely in organic forms. It may convert incoming compounds into other amino acids and then incorporate them into storage proteins. The composition of the storage proteins and the amount of protein per seed influence the overall nitrogen requirement. For example, suppose two seed genotypes each contain 20 mg protein, but one has a higher lysine residue fraction. It requires greater lysine supply for that protein mass, even if total nitrogen content is similar. An increase in free lysine cannot by itself establish that the storage protein composition has changed. Separate measurements of free pools and hydrolyzed protein are needed to answer the nutritional question.

After germination, stored protein is hydrolyzed and amino acids become available for new protein synthesis, interconversion and respiration. This creates a temporal link between nitrogen deposited during seed filling and nitrogen used before the seedling has acquired an effective external supply. A mutation affecting seed protein mobilization could leave a large reserve of total nitrogen while the growing embryonic axis experiences a shortage of soluble amino acids. The total seed nitrogen assay would not identify this bottleneck. Likewise, total protein depletion is not direct proof that the released nitrogen has reached the growing axis; transport and assimilation must also be functional.

A whole-plant nitrogen balance includes inputs from roots or associated fixation, net losses, and redistribution among organs. If total plant nitrogen is unchanged during a short chase but older leaves lose nitrogen and seeds gain it, redistribution is a plausible explanation. If the plant also absorbs new unlabeled nitrogen during the chase, the same organ may show an increase in nitrogen amount and a decrease in fractional label. The fraction is diluted by new input. To establish how much old nitrogen was transferred, use excess-label amount rather than the fractional label alone. This distinction connects the source–sink story directly to the tracer methods in the next section.

10. Evidence and exam-level interpretation of isotope and enzyme experiments

Stable-isotope tracing follows atoms rather than relying on visual resemblance between substrates and products. ¹⁵N is a stable isotope of nitrogen that can be distinguished from the more abundant ¹⁴N. In a pulse-chase experiment, a labeled nitrogen source is provided briefly and then replaced with unlabeled nitrogen. Sampling multiple time points can reveal movement of nitrogen into glutamine, glutamate and downstream products. The pulse reveals early incorporation; the chase helps reveal transfer, dilution and turnover.

Consider an illustrative experiment with isolated, metabolically active plant tissue supplied with ¹⁵NH₄⁺. At 15 seconds, glutamine amide-N enrichment is higher than glutamate amino-N enrichment. At one minute, glutamate enrichment increases, followed by aspartate and alanine. This ordering is consistent with GS–GOGAT and subsequent transamination. It does not by itself establish that every molecule of glutamate was formed by GOGAT, because parallel pathways can contribute and pre-existing pools may dilute the tracer. An appropriate control uses an unlabeled source to establish natural isotope abundance and background signals.

The difference between fractional enrichment and amount of labeled nitrogen is especially important. Suppose a glutamine pool contains 10 μmol molecules and a glutamate pool contains 40 μmol molecules. If the glutamine amide position has 20 atom-percent excess ¹⁵N, it contains 2 μmol excess label at that position. If glutamate amino nitrogen has 10 atom-percent excess, it contains 4 μmol excess label. Although glutamine has the larger fractional enrichment, glutamate contains more excess labeled nitrogen in this example. Total nitrogen counting in glutamine would also have to account for its α-amino position separately.

To calculate excess label for a pool with one N per molecule, multiply the pool amount by the atom-fraction excess. For a compound with multiple nitrogen positions, sum the contribution from each position, or multiply the number of N atoms by the average atom-fraction excess if the average is defined across all N atoms. The formula assumes the pool amount and enrichment refer to the same sample and time. The isotope amount provides information about label recovery, but estimating net metabolic flux also requires time and assumptions about exchange and precursor enrichment.

Aminotransferase function can be studied with substrate-specific enzyme assays. One design for AspAT supplies glutamate and oxaloacetate and detects the formation of aspartate or 2-OG. A coupled assay may use another enzyme to convert a product while monitoring a cofactor. In such a design, the auxiliary enzyme must not be rate limiting, and the measured optical signal must be correctly related to the target reaction. Controls without the target enzyme or an essential substrate help identify background reactions. A reaction rate measured in an extract represents catalytic capacity under the assay conditions, not necessarily the in vivo flux.

An enzyme-deficiency experiment provides a different type of evidence. If plastidic AspAT activity is reduced and certain aspartate-derived amino acids decline, the phenotype supports a contribution of plastid aspartate production to the pathway. However, cytosolic isoforms and metabolite transport can provide partial compensation. The enzyme-deficiency phenotype is therefore interpreted in the context of compartmentation and tissue type. A genetic complementation test can strengthen causal attribution, while a tracer test can address the changes in carbon and nitrogen movement.

Use a substrate-rescue experiment to locate a metabolic block. If a seedling with a defect in the threonine-to-isoleucine branch responds to isoleucine supplementation but not threonine supplementation, the observations are consistent with a block downstream of threonine. The experiment assumes the supplemented amino acids reach the relevant cells in usable form. If uptake differs between treatments, a failure to rescue may reflect transport rather than pathway position. The most convincing interpretation combines the phenotype with enzyme activity, product measurements and knowledge of the biochemical route.

Finally, an increase in transcript abundance does not prove an increase in flux. An AS transcript may increase during carbon starvation, but the actual rate of asparagine formation may be constrained by ATP, glutamine, aspartate, enzyme abundance or degradation. Transcript measurements establish a regulatory response; protein abundance and enzyme activity establish different levels of potential capacity; isotope and mass-balance measurements can address rates. CSIR-NET analytical questions often test the ability to distinguish these evidential levels rather than to recall enzyme names.

A pulse-chase experiment with explicit atom budgets

Suppose a root tissue receives a short pulse of enriched ammonium followed by unlabeled ammonium. In three samples taken during the pulse, glutamine amide excess enrichment is 6%, 20% and 26%, whereas glutamate α-amino enrichment is 1%, 7% and 15%. Aspartate α-amino enrichment is 0.5%, 3% and 10%. The order is compatible with the expected GS–GOGAT–AspAT pathway. It does not establish that the pathway is unique or prove every intermediate compartment, because different pool sizes and exchange rates affect enrichment. The stronger inference arises when the temporal pattern is combined with biochemical knowledge of the reactions and a perturbation that blocks one of them.

For a molecule with two nitrogen atoms, report whether enrichment is defined over all its nitrogen positions or just one specified position. If a sample contains 10 µmol asparagine at a mean atom-fraction excess of 0.15 across both positions, it contains 10 × 2 × 0.15 = 3 µmol excess heavy nitrogen atoms. If 0.15 instead refers only to the amide position and the α position is at natural abundance, the excess is 10 × 0.15 = 1.5 µmol. The numerical difference results from the denominator, not a different isotope chemistry. Natural-abundance correction is necessary when working with excess enrichment rather than the raw heavy-isotope fraction.

A plant with 12 µmol total glutamate at 25% atom-fraction excess contains 3 µmol excess label. Another has 40 µmol glutamate at 10% excess and thus contains 4 µmol excess label. The first sample is more enriched, yet the second contains a greater absolute amount of tracer. Without both concentration and enrichment, the direction of a difference in label amount cannot always be determined. Even with both measurements, a single time point does not give a flux unless one knows the elapsed time and how much labeled precursor was available over that interval. This is why a well-designed tracer experiment includes repeated sampling and measurements of input enrichment.

Consider an idealized two-hour pulse in which the label amount in a product rises from zero to 6 µmol while the donor nitrogen is maintained at a uniform excess fraction of 0.30. If the product is initially unlabeled, the only input is this donor and labeled product loss is negligible, formation of total nitrogen into the product is estimated as 6/0.30 = 20 µmol over two hours, or 10 µmol per hour. If labeled product is degraded or exported during the interval, the same measured accumulation understates input. If donor enrichment changes substantially, the calculation must use its time course rather than an endpoint concentration. Always write the assumptions before interpreting the rate.

Discriminating synthesis, transport and turnover

A useful experiment compares a wild-type plant, an AS-deficient mutant and a complemented line expressing functional AS. Provide the same labeled nitrogen input and measure precursor enrichment, asparagine concentration, enzyme activity and nitrogen exported to the sink. If the mutant shows reduced formation of labeled asparagine despite comparable labeled glutamine and aspartate supply, and complementation restores that formation, the evidence supports a direct role for AS. A low asparagine concentration without a tracer result could also reflect increased removal; the combination of measurements narrows the alternative explanations.

A second experiment can separate a biosynthetic defect from a transport defect. Apply a short pulse of labeled asparagine directly to the source tissue of both genotypes. If formation from inorganic nitrogen is defective but the import/export machinery is intact, a labeled asparagine pulse may still reach the receiving organ. If export itself is impaired, supplying the finished compound may not restore its transfer. Differences in uptake at the application site must be controlled, and labeled products formed by asparagine breakdown should be distinguished from intact asparagine. The aim is to select a treatment that bypasses one hypothesized step without bypassing all the others.

An enzyme activity measurement provides a potential capacity at the selected substrate and assay conditions. For example, an extract from a mutant may have half the maximal DHDPS activity of wild type under saturating substrates. This does not automatically imply half the lysine synthesis rate inside living cells. Substrate availability, feedback inhibition, other isoforms and turnover can make the relation nonlinear. A complementary experiment varying lysine concentration can show whether product inhibition differs between extracts. If the mutant retains substantial activity at a lysine concentration that inhibits wild type, one has evidence for altered feedback sensitivity rather than simply altered enzyme abundance.

A clean examination dataset might report lysine synthesis proxy values of 100, 40 and 20 units for wild-type extracts at zero, intermediate and high lysine concentrations, and 90, 85 and 80 for a mutant. At zero lysine the mutant has slightly lower capacity; at high lysine it retains almost 89% of its own initial activity while wild type retains 20%. The data support reduced product sensitivity rather than increased maximal activity. They do not establish that the mutation is located in the catalytic site or identify its nucleotide change. Direct genetic or structural evidence is needed to make that further inference.

Finally, consider the controls for a nutrient interaction experiment. To determine whether carbon supply modifies the response to nitrogen, arrange a factorial comparison of low and high carbon supply with low and high nitrogen supply. Use comparable plant age, light history and tissue sampling; where carbon is altered through light, consider other light-dependent effects as potential confounders. Measure biomass together with nitrate, amino acid and sugar pools. If the nitrogen effect on biomass is larger under high carbon than under low carbon, the data support an interaction on the chosen biomass scale. They do not identify a particular signaling molecule without additional targeted evidence. These experimental distinctions are the practical foundation for answering analytical examination questions about the integrated network.

Examination traps and misconceptions

Every nitrogen atom in glutamine comes from new ammonium.

GS adds one amide nitrogen; the original glutamate amino nitrogen remains.

Aminotransferases create nitrogen atoms.

They exchange existing amino nitrogen between carbon skeletons; GS–GOGAT provides net assimilation.

Glutamate carbon becomes alanine carbon in AlaAT.

Alanine carbon comes from pyruvate. Glutamate becomes 2-OG.

PLP is consumed stoichiometrically.

Its PLP/PMP forms cycle and the cofactor is regenerated.

An aminotransferase determines equilibrium position.

It accelerates both directions; Q relative to K determines thermodynamic tendency.

Asparagine synthesis costs two ATP molecules per molecule.

One ATP is cleaved to AMP and PPi; this is often counted as two high-energy bond equivalents.

Asparagine always dominates transport.

Transport composition depends on species, vascular stream and developmental stage; some legumes export ureides.

All aspartate-family products have four carbons.

Additional carbon is incorporated or lost in branches. Lysine and isoleucine contain six carbons and methionine five.

Isoleucine cannot belong to two families.

Its carbon ancestry includes threonine; late biosynthetic steps overlap the branched-chain pathway.

Feedback-insensitive DHDPS is inactive DHDPS.

It retains activity but is less inhibited by lysine; a null mutation has a different effect.

Valine is simply aminated pyruvate.

Its five-carbon skeleton is constructed before transamination; direct pyruvate amination gives alanine.

Nitrate alone supplies sulfur to cysteine.

Reduced sulfur is needed for O-acetylserine conversion; nitrate supplies nitrogen.

All serine in plants originates from photorespiration.

The phosphorylated route also operates, including in nonphotosynthetic tissues.

Higher amino acid content proves faster synthesis.

Pool size reflects formation, breakdown, transport and utilization.

A higher isotope percentage always means more label.

Multiply atom-fraction excess by the amount of measured nitrogen.

Every labeled asparagine molecule proves direct amide transfer.

Label can also enter its α-amino group via GOGAT and AspAT; position matters.

Increased free seed lysine proves improved protein-bound lysine.

Separate free pools, hydrolyzed protein composition and total seed yield.

A rise in proline proves stronger stress tolerance.

The increase can reflect stress severity, altered degradation or water loss as well as adaptive synthesis.

Part B — Conceptual foundations

Question 1.

Which atom of glutamine is supplied directly by GS from ammonium?

  1. Side-chain amide N
  2. α-amino N
  3. Carboxyl carbon
  4. Both N atoms
Answer and explanation

A. GS amidates the side-chain carboxyl group of existing glutamate. The original α-amino group is retained. Carbon is not supplied by ammonium, and only one new N is incorporated.

Question 2.

What happens to glutamate carbon during forward AlaAT?

  1. It becomes alanine carbon
  2. It becomes 2-oxoglutarate
  3. It becomes CO₂ quantitatively
  4. It becomes pyruvate
Answer and explanation

B. AlaAT transfers amino N, not the glutamate carbon backbone. Pyruvate carbon becomes alanine; glutamate carbon remains in 2-OG. No decarboxylation is required.

Question 3.

Which cofactor cycles between an aldehyde and an aminated form during transamination?

  1. FAD
  2. Biotin
  3. PLP/PMP
  4. Coenzyme A
Answer and explanation

C. PLP and PMP temporarily accept and donate amino nitrogen. FAD participates in redox, biotin in carboxylation, and CoA in acyl transfer.

Question 4.

Which statement about asparagine synthetase is correct?

  1. Consumes NADH as the direct amide donor
  2. Forms asparagine from pyruvate
  3. Releases two ATP molecules
  4. Typically converts ATP to AMP and PPi
Answer and explanation

D. AS activates aspartate using ATP cleavage to AMP and PPi. Glutamine donates amide N. Pyruvate is not the acceptor, and ATP is consumed rather than released.

Question 5.

Which branch enzyme connects threonine to isoleucine carbon skeleton synthesis?

  1. Aspartate aminotransferase
  2. Threonine deaminase
  3. Glutamine synthetase
  4. Asparaginase
Answer and explanation

B. Threonine deaminase forms 2-oxobutyrate, which enters the branched-chain pathway. The other enzymes act on Asp/Glu, Gln or Asn.

Question 6.

Which carbon precursors initiate the shikimate pathway?

  1. Glu and Gln
  2. Oxaloacetate and ammonium
  3. Acetyl-CoA and sulfate
  4. PEP and erythrose 4-phosphate
Answer and explanation

D. The initial condensation combines PEP with erythrose 4-phosphate. The other pairs do not build the shikimate precursor skeleton.

Question 7.

Why may nitrate supplementation fail to restore cysteine synthesis in a sulfur-deficient plant?

  1. Reduced sulfur is also required
  2. Cysteine has no nitrogen
  3. Nitrate substitutes for sulfide
  4. Cysteine is synthesized only in animals
Answer and explanation

A. Cysteine formation requires sulfide and O-acetylserine. Nitrate provides N, not S; cysteine contains both and is synthesized by plants.

Question 8.

Which reaction directly replenishes oxaloacetate from PEP in an anaplerotic route?

  1. AlaAT
  2. GS
  3. DHDPS
  4. PEP carboxylase
Answer and explanation

D. PEPC adds bicarbonate to PEP to form oxaloacetate. The other enzymes carry out nitrogen transfer, amide formation or a lysine-pathway reaction.

Question 9.

An inhibitor of AHAS should most directly restrict production of which group?

  1. Asp and Asn
  2. Val, Leu and Ile
  3. Phe, Tyr and Trp
  4. Glu and Gln
Answer and explanation

B. AHAS initiates shared steps of branched-chain amino acid synthesis. The other groups originate from different major pathways.

Question 10.

Which statement best explains the role of glutamate in new alanine biosynthesis?

  1. Its five-carbon skeleton becomes three-carbon alanine
  2. It supplies free ATP to AlaAT
  3. It transfers amino N to pyruvate and becomes 2-OG
  4. It is translated directly into alanine
Answer and explanation

C. Transamination conserves carbon skeletons and transfers N. AlaAT does not consume ATP or involve translation.

Question 11.

Which immediate precursor undergoes the lysine-branch condensation with pyruvate?

  1. Aspartate semialdehyde
  2. Homoserine
  3. Glutamine
  4. Chorismate
Answer and explanation

A. DHDPS uses aspartate semialdehyde and pyruvate. Homoserine is on the competing branch; glutamine donates nitrogen elsewhere, and chorismate feeds aromatic pathways.

Question 12.

Which plant enzyme forms threonine from O-phosphohomoserine?

  1. Threonine deaminase
  2. Threonine synthase
  3. Aspartate kinase
  4. Cystathionine γ-synthase
Answer and explanation

B. Threonine synthase makes threonine; deaminase removes nitrogen from threonine, AK activates aspartate upstream, and cystathionine γ-synthase directs the substrate toward methionine.

Question 13.

Which intermediate is shared by the valine and leucine pathways?

  1. Chorismate
  2. Oxaloacetate
  3. 2-oxoisovalerate
  4. Asparagine
Answer and explanation

C. 2-Oxoisovalerate may be aminated to valine or extended through the leucine branch. The other compounds are precursors or products of distinct pathways.

Question 14.

Which precursor provides sulfur to cystathionine synthesis in plants?

  1. Nitrate
  2. Glutamate
  3. Methionine alone
  4. Cysteine
Answer and explanation

D. Cysteine carries reduced sulfur into the methionine pathway. Nitrate and glutamate supply nitrogen-related substrates, not reduced sulfur. Methionine is downstream.

Question 15.

Which enzyme forms O-acetylserine from serine?

  1. Serine acetyltransferase
  2. O-acetylserine(thiol)lyase
  3. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase
  4. GOGAT
Answer and explanation

A. Serine acetyltransferase uses acetyl-CoA to activate serine; the lyase uses the resulting O-acetylserine with sulfide. SHMT exchanges one-carbon units and GOGAT assimilates nitrogen.

Question 16.

Which is a characteristic starting substrate of histidine biosynthesis?

  1. 2-Oxoglutarate only
  2. PRPP
  3. Chorismate
  4. Homoserine
Answer and explanation

B. Histidine has a distinct PRPP- and ATP-associated biosynthetic origin; the other compounds are associated with glutamate, aromatic or aspartate-family metabolism.

Question 17.

What is the main immediate carbon precursor of the phosphorylated serine pathway?

  1. Acetyl-CoA
  2. Ribose 5-phosphate only
  3. 3-Phosphoglycerate
  4. Aspartate
Answer and explanation

C. The phosphorylated route starts with 3-phosphoglycerate, followed by oxidation, transamination and dephosphorylation. The other precursors do not initiate this route.

Question 18.

Which product of arginine breakdown can release ammonium through urease?

  1. Ornithine
  2. Fumarate
  3. Citrate
  4. Urea
Answer and explanation

D. Arginase yields urea and ornithine; urease hydrolyzes urea. Ornithine retains nitrogen but is not the urease substrate, and fumarate and citrate are organic acids.

Question 19.

Which metabolite contributes the α-amino-bearing portion of tryptophan in its final synthesis step?

  1. Serine
  2. Aspartate
  3. Glycine alone
  4. Glutamine amide alone
Answer and explanation

A. Tryptophan synthase joins indole and serine. The indole nitrogen derives earlier from glutamine; that earlier donation is not the final incorporation of the α-amino-bearing part.

Question 20.

Which statement correctly distinguishes feedback inhibition from loss of enzyme?

  1. Both remove the enzyme's coding sequence
  2. Feedback inhibition can be reversible while enzyme deletion removes its catalytic contribution
  3. Feedback inhibition always increases product formation
  4. Both always change equilibrium constants
Answer and explanation

B. Feedback modulates activity of present enzyme; loss removes catalytic capacity from that gene. Neither necessarily changes the reaction equilibrium constant.

Question 21.

Which compound is a characteristic ureide nitrogen transport form in some legumes?

  1. Pyruvate
  2. Malate
  3. Allantoin
  4. Ethylene
Answer and explanation

C. Allantoin and allantoate can carry fixed N in ureide-exporting legumes. Pyruvate and malate are organic carbon metabolites, and ethylene contains no nitrogen.

Question 22.

In a typical illuminated C3 leaf, glycine-to-serine photorespiratory conversion releases which nitrogen compound?

  1. Nitrate
  2. Nitrite
  3. N₂
  4. Ammonium
Answer and explanation

D. Photorespiratory glycine decarboxylation releases ammonium, which is reassimilated via GS–GOGAT. Nitrate and nitrite are upstream inorganic N forms, and this is not N₂ fixation.

Question 23.

Which statement about plant amino acid transporters is correct?

  1. They move amino acids across membranes without creating new nitrogen atoms
  2. They reduce atmospheric nitrogen directly
  3. They catalyze peptide bond formation
  4. They convert all imported amino acids into nitrate
Answer and explanation

A. Transport changes location, not elemental identity. Nitrogen fixation, translation and nitrate metabolism are distinct biochemical processes.

Question 24.

Which statement identifies a direct control point for leucine synthesis?

  1. GS alone
  2. Isopropylmalate synthase
  3. Nitrite reductase
  4. Phenylalanine ammonia lyase
Answer and explanation

B. Isopropylmalate synthase initiates extension of the valine-pathway keto acid toward leucine. GS and nitrite reductase act in nitrogen entry; PAL consumes phenylalanine.

Question 25.

What directly supplies the methyl group in plant methionine synthesis from homocysteine?

  1. ATP phosphate
  2. Nitrate oxygen
  3. A folate-associated one-carbon donor
  4. Carbon dioxide alone
Answer and explanation

C. Methionine synthase transfers a methyl group from methylated tetrahydrofolate. ATP can activate other metabolites but is not the immediate methyl donor; nitrate and free CO₂ do not provide this group directly.

Part C — Analytical and numerical reasoning

Question 26.

A plant needs to transport 20 μmol N exclusively as asparagine. How many μmol carbon atoms accompany it?

  1. 40
  2. 50
  3. 80
  4. 10
Answer and explanation

A. Asn carries 2 N and 4 C. Ten μmol molecules carry 20 μmol N and 40 μmol C. 50 would correspond to glutamine; 80 and 10 confuse molecules with atoms.

Question 27.

If DHDPS loses feedback inhibition, what is the most defensible prediction?

  1. All amino acids increase equally
  2. Lysine must decrease
  3. Lysine branch flux may increase, subject to other limitations
  4. Aspartate kinase becomes unnecessary
Answer and explanation

C. DHDPS directs flux into the lysine branch. Feedback resistance may increase that flux, but precursor availability, other enzymes and catabolism remain relevant. AK is still upstream.

Question 28.

A 20 μmol glutamate pool has 15 atom-percent excess label. How much excess ¹⁵N is present?

  1. 15 μmol
  2. 3 μmol
  3. 30 μmol
  4. 0.3 μmol
Answer and explanation

B. Glu has one N. Multiply 20 by 0.15 = 3 μmol. Percent is converted to a fraction and does not equal amount by itself.

Question 29.

A low-light treatment causes a higher Asn:Gln ratio but lower biomass. Which inference is warranted?

  1. Total N assimilation necessarily increased
  2. Asn requires no energy
  3. Nitrogen allocation changed; net assimilation requires additional evidence
  4. Carbon is irrelevant
Answer and explanation

C. A ratio describes composition, not net flux. Asn synthesis requires ATP and carbon. Low biomass may reflect multiple constraints.

Question 30.

A sample contains 8 μmol Gln with 25% excess label only at its amide position. What is the excess label amount?

  1. 2 μmol
  2. 4 μmol
  3. 8 μmol
  4. 16 μmol
Answer and explanation

A. Only one of the two positions is labeled above background; 8 × 0.25 = 2 μmol. Multiplying by two would be incorrect for position-specific enrichment.

Question 31.

An AS mutant has less asparagine but unchanged total N. What can be inferred?

  1. All N assimilation ceased
  2. No plant nitrogen is transported
  3. Glutamine cannot be made
  4. Organic nitrogen partitioning may have changed
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced Asn can coexist with redistribution to other N pools. Total N alone cannot establish flux, transport or GS activity.

Question 32.

In an idealized experiment, 7 μmol nitrate-N becomes alanine via GS–GOGAT and AlaAT. Minimum electron equivalents from nitrate reduction and GOGAT?

  1. 14 μmol
  2. 56 μmol
  3. 70 μmol
  4. 7 μmol
Answer and explanation

C. Nitrate reduction needs eight electrons per N and GOGAT two more. 7 × 10 = 70 μmol electrons. ATP at GS is an additional cost.

Question 33.

A labeled source leaf loses 1 mmol excess ¹⁵N while seeds gain 0.7 mmol. Which interpretation is valid?

  1. Exactly all leaf label entered seeds
  2. Seeds made 0.7 mmol atmospheric nitrogen
  3. No label can have moved elsewhere
  4. 0.7 mmol is recovered in seeds; other pools/losses require accounting
Answer and explanation

D. A tracer budget must include all tissues and losses. The observed change supports transfer but does not establish complete recovery.

Question 34.

A plant has 16 µmol asparagine with 10% mean excess ¹⁵N across both nitrogen atoms. How much excess label is present?

  1. 1.6 µmol
  2. 3.2 µmol
  3. 16 µmol
  4. 32 µmol
Answer and explanation

B. There are 32 µmol nitrogen atoms, and 0.10 × 32 = 3.2 µmol excess ¹⁵N. A counts only one position. C and D ignore enrichment.

Question 35.

An idealized pool contains 5 mM glutamate, 4 mM pyruvate, 2 mM 2-OG and 5 mM alanine. For forward AlaAT, Q equals?

  1. 2
  2. 0.2
  3. 0.5
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

C. Q = [2-OG][Ala]/([Glu][pyruvate]) = 10/20 = 0.5. A inverts Q, B and D use incorrect concentration combinations. Direction also requires K.

Question 36.

At fixed semialdehyde input of 40 µmol/h, lysine branch flux rises from 14 to 24 µmol/h. No other drain or accumulation exists. New homoserine flux?

  1. 26
  2. 24
  3. 10
  4. 16 µmol/h
Answer and explanation

D. Mass balance gives 40 − 24 = 16. The prior flux was 26; 10 is the amount of reduction. 24 is the new lysine flux. The fixed input assumption is essential.

Question 37.

Wild-type DHDPS has activity 80 without lysine and 20 with lysine. Mutant activity is 70 and 63. Which is best supported?

  1. Mutant has reduced relative sensitivity to lysine
  2. Mutant has greater uninhibited maximal activity
  3. Mutant has no DHDPS activity
  4. Lysine stimulates mutant activity
Answer and explanation

A. Wild type retains 25%, mutant 90% of its own starting activity. The mutant's uninhibited value is lower, not higher, and its activity still falls slightly. Data support altered feedback.

Question 38.

A donor is held at 25% atom-fraction excess. In 3 hours a product accumulates 6 µmol excess N, with no labeled loss and no other donor. Estimated total input rate?

  1. 2
  2. 8 µmol N/h
  3. 24 µmol N/h
  4. 1.5 µmol N/h
Answer and explanation

B. Total input is 6/0.25 = 24 µmol over 3 h, or 8 µmol/h. 2 is tracer accumulation per hour only; 24 is the total amount rather than a rate.

Question 39.

Equal tissue masses contain 10 µmol glutamate at 30% excess and 40 µmol at 10% excess. Which contains more label?

  1. First, by 2 µmol
  2. Equal
  3. Second, by 1 µmol
  4. Second, by 20 µmol
Answer and explanation

C. The first contains 3 µmol excess label and the second 4. Fractional enrichment is higher in the first, but amount is higher in the second.

Question 40.

A tissue has 80 µmol electron equivalents available for nitrate reduction plus GS–GOGAT assimilation. What is the electron-only upper bound for new nitrate-derived amino N?

  1. 40
  2. 10
  3. 16
  4. 8 µmol
Answer and explanation

D. Nitrate to ammonium costs 8 electrons and GOGAT 2; 80/10 = 8 µmol. The estimate omits energy and carbon constraints. The other answers omit or miscount reduction.

Question 41.

If 15 µmol N is transported as glutamine and 25 µmol N as asparagine, how many µmol carbon atoms are carried?

  1. 87.5
  2. 80
  3. 100
  4. 40
Answer and explanation

A. Glutamine: 15/2 × 5 = 37.5 C; asparagine: 25/2 × 4 = 50 C; total 87.5. The other choices treat the mixture as a single carrier or confuse N with C.

Question 42.

A leaf's AS transcript doubles but its ATP falls markedly and asparagine remains unchanged. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. AS flux doubled
  2. Transcription changed; flux is not established
  3. ATP is unnecessary for AS
  4. Asparagine export must be zero
Answer and explanation

B. Transcript, ATP and concentration are different measurements. A steady pool can arise from matched formation/removal. AS uses ATP, and no export measurement was supplied.

Question 43.

A mutant shows normal ¹⁵N incorporation into glutamine but low labeling of asparagine amide. Supplying matched labeled glutamine does not rescue, whereas AS complementation does. Most likely direct defect?

  1. Nitrate uptake only
  2. Glycine decarboxylase
  3. AS-dependent amide transfer
  4. Rubisco activation only
Answer and explanation

C. Normal precursor labeling and genetic complementation locate the defect at AS-dependent conversion, not initial inorganic uptake. Photorespiration or Rubisco cannot explain the specific complementation on their own.

Question 44.

An AHAS inhibitor suppresses growth; valine alone barely helps, while Val + Leu + Ile restores much more growth. What does the result support?

  1. All three arise directly from a single pyruvate transamination
  2. The inhibitor primarily blocks GS
  3. The plant cannot absorb valine at all
  4. A common BCAA biosynthetic bottleneck
Answer and explanation

D. AHAS feeds the shared branched-chain network. All three products can be needed for protein synthesis. A bypass pattern supports the bottleneck but direct enzyme evidence is still valuable.

Question 45.

A sample of dry leaf tissue has 36% carbon and 3% nitrogen by mass. Approximate molar C:N?

  1. 14:1
  2. 12:1
  3. 10.3:1
  4. 36:1
Answer and explanation

A. Per 100 g, carbon is 36/12 = 3 mol and N is 3/14 ≈ 0.214 mol. Ratio ≈14. 12 is the mass ratio. The other choices use incorrect conversion.

Question 46.

Replenishment of oxaloacetate is 18 µmol/h, and total withdrawal is 22. With an initial accessible pool of 20 µmol, what is the initial linear depletion timescale?

  1. 1 h
  2. 5 h
  3. 4 h
  4. 11 h
Answer and explanation

B. Net loss 4 µmol/h, so 20/4 = 5 h if rates remain constant. Real rates change near depletion. 4 is the rate rather than the time.

Question 47.

After labeled ammonium input, labeled asparagine is detected. What extra information best distinguishes direct amide donation from labeling via AspAT?

  1. Total leaf fresh weight
  2. Presence of chlorophyll
  3. Position-specific label enrichment in α-amino and amide N
  4. Total number of stomata
Answer and explanation

C. The two routes initially place label in different nitrogen positions. Bulk fresh weight, chlorophyll and stomata do not discriminate nitrogen atom histories.

Question 48.

A source leaf has normal tracer incorporation into amino acids but reduced labeled N in seeds; the source soluble pool rises. Which hypothesis is most directly supported?

  1. Nitrogen fixation necessarily increased
  2. A complete loss of GS
  3. Nitrate reduction must be absent
  4. Impaired source-to-sink export or sink use
Answer and explanation

D. Normal source incorporation argues against a primary complete assimilation block. The source accumulation plus reduced delivery points toward allocation or utilization, which need additional transport assays.

Assertion and reason

Question 49.

Assertion: Asparagine carries more N per C than glutamine. Reason: Both carry two N, but Asn has four C and Gln has five.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false
Answer and explanation

A. The ratios are 0.50 and 0.40 N/C; the reason directly explains the difference.

Question 50.

Assertion: Aminotransferases directly assimilate free ammonium. Reason: Their catalytic cofactor can carry amino nitrogen.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion false; reason true
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

B. The cofactor carries an amino group derived from an organic substrate. Primary ammonium incorporation is a distinct reaction.

Revision and active recall

  • GS places inorganic nitrogen in glutamine amide; GOGAT transfers this nitrogen to glutamate.
  • PLP-dependent transamination transfers an amino group while conserving donor and acceptor carbon skeletons.
  • AS uses glutamine amide N and aspartate to form asparagine, with ATP → AMP + PPi.
  • Family names identify carbon ancestry, not necessarily the origin of every nitrogen atom.
  • Branch feedback coordinates competing pathways; pool size is not a measurement of flux.
  • Carbon, ATP, reductant, sulfur and transport jointly constrain amino acid supply and growth.
