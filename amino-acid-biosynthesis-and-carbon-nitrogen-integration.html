1. From inorganic nitrogen to organic diversity

A germinating seedling can absorb nitrate from its environment without acquiring a single intact amino acid from that nitrate. Its new leaves nevertheless require proteins containing many different amino acids. This observation establishes the distinction between nitrogen uptake and nitrogen metabolism. Uptake changes the location of an available nitrogen compound; assimilation changes its chemical form; biosynthesis distributes assimilated nitrogen among products. The first two processes establish a pool of organic nitrogen, but they do not automatically supply the particular molecular mixture required for construction of chloroplasts, membranes and dividing cells. Amino acid biosynthesis is the set of reactions that solves this distribution problem.

A plant must solve two accounting problems at once. Nitrogen has to enter covalent bonds with carbon, and the carbon frameworks must be available in the correct molecular forms. Fixation of carbon dioxide supplies carbon to central metabolism. Reduction of nitrate, or acquisition of ammonium from other sources, supplies reduced nitrogen. These flows converge but are not interchangeable. A nitrate-fed seedling with inadequate photosynthetic carbon supply may have abundant nitrogen in the medium and still grow poorly. Conversely a well illuminated seedling lacking usable nitrogen cannot convert all of its assimilated carbon into new protein. Growth responds to whichever input constrains the relevant biosynthetic processes, and sometimes to several interacting limitations.

A free amino acid pool is not the same thing as protein. Before translation can assemble a chain, the appropriate amino acids must be available for aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases. The ribosome then selects aminoacyl-tRNAs according to the message. It does not synthesize the carbon framework of lysine or reduce nitrate into the nitrogen of alanine. A defect in lysine biosynthesis and a defect in charging lysyl-tRNA may both impair protein production, but they are defects in different stages. Exogenous lysine might bypass the first defect if it can enter the relevant cells and compartment; it does not generally repair a defective charging enzyme.

Most higher plants can synthesize the standard protein amino acids, including the amino acids humans must obtain in adequate amounts through diet. The human nutritional term essential is therefore not a statement that the plant cannot synthesize that amino acid. In plants the relevant issue is whether the metabolic pathway and substrate supply can satisfy the demand of tissues at a particular developmental stage. Distinguishing dietary essentiality from biochemical indispensability prevents a common error when reading about crop nutritional quality.

2. Glutamate and glutamine: two chemically distinct donors

Glutamate contains an alpha-amino group and a side-chain carboxyl group. Glutamine retains the alpha-amino group but carries a side-chain carboxamide in place of the distal carboxyl group. Consequently glutamate contains one nitrogen atom whereas glutamine contains two. This distinction is mechanistically important. Enzymes transferring an alpha-amino group commonly use glutamate as donor. Enzymes that need ammonia at an activated acceptor commonly use glutamine as an amide nitrogen donor. A molecule of glutamine therefore does not have a single chemically equivalent pair of nitrogen atoms.

The glutamine synthetase reaction consumes ATP to add ammonium to glutamate, producing glutamine. Glutamate synthase uses glutamine, 2-oxoglutarate and reducing equivalents to make two glutamates. The first of these glutamates derives its carbon skeleton from glutamine; the second derives from 2-oxoglutarate. One glutamate may replenish the starting pool and another may donate nitrogen to a downstream product. Thus the cycle creates a net entry point for ammonium into a carbon skeleton without requiring net consumption of an entire pre-existing glutamate molecule on every turn.

Once this initial incorporation occurs, many reactions do not need to reduce inorganic nitrogen again. Transfer of the amino group of glutamate to oxaloacetate gives aspartate and regenerates 2-oxoglutarate. Transfer to pyruvate gives alanine and the same oxoacid. Glutamate therefore serves as a metabolic junction between central carbon metabolism and the families of nitrogen-containing compounds. It is not a fuel that permanently disappears each time nitrogen leaves it: regeneration of 2-oxoglutarate helps reconnect these reactions with the GS–GOGAT system.

A glutamine-dependent amidotransferase instead hydrolyses the side-chain amide, leaving glutamate, and channels the released ammonia toward a chemically prepared acceptor. The free ammonia concentration of the whole cell need not increase in proportion to this transfer because the enzyme can couple generation and utilization locally. One should not infer that all such reactions use ATP directly; whether ATP is required depends on the acceptor reaction. Asparagine synthetase is an important ATP-dependent example, whereas anthranilate synthase uses a different acceptor chemistry.

Nitrogen labeling can distinguish these two donor roles. A short pulse of labeled ammonium may label the glutamine amide first as a result of glutamine synthetase activity. Subsequent GOGAT activity can transfer label into the amino nitrogen of newly synthesized glutamate; aminotransferases can distribute it to other amino acids. Exact timing varies with tissues, pool sizes and rates, so this sequence is a mechanistic prediction rather than a universal measurement timetable.

3. PLP-dependent transamination and reaction direction

An aminotransferase interconverts an amino acid and an alpha-keto acid while conserving their carbon skeletons in the corresponding products. The overall reaction exchanges an amino group and a carbonyl group. In the aspartate aminotransferase reaction glutamate plus oxaloacetate yields 2-oxoglutarate plus aspartate. In the alanine aminotransferase reaction glutamate plus pyruvate yields 2-oxoglutarate plus alanine. Both reactions are reversible. They do not use ATP directly, do not require nitrate as a substrate and do not release free ammonium as an obligatory net product.

The usual cofactor is pyridoxal phosphate, abbreviated PLP, a vitamin B6 derivative. An enzyme-bound PLP initially forms a Schiff base, an imine linkage, with an active-site lysine. The substrate amino group replaces this linkage to form an external aldimine. PLP stabilizes intermediates during proton rearrangements, and the carbon skeleton of the donor is released as its corresponding keto acid. At that point the cofactor has accepted nitrogen and exists as pyridoxamine phosphate, PMP. Binding a suitable acceptor keto acid allows the reverse chemical sequence, forming the new amino acid and regenerating PLP. The two half-reactions explain why both substrates need not occupy the active site simultaneously. PLP is recycled, not a net nitrogen source.

Recognition of a carbon skeleton is also a central part of catalysis. The active site of aspartate aminotransferase accommodates different chemical features from the site of an aminotransferase acting on hydrophobic branched-chain keto acids. Therefore a shared cofactor does not imply that every enzyme can catalyse every transfer. Plants contain multiple aminotransferase isoenzymes, sometimes operating in different compartments. Inferences about the effect of removing one isoenzyme require information about alternative enzymes and transport of metabolites between compartments.

A reversible equation does not state a fixed physiological direction. If aspartate is continuously removed into other reactions, the forward direction may be favored. If aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate are supplied and glutamate or oxaloacetate is consumed, the reverse direction may dominate. The reaction quotient expresses the relative activities of products and reactants. For glutamate plus oxaloacetate forming aspartate plus 2-oxoglutarate, Q equals the product of aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate activities divided by the product of glutamate and oxaloacetate activities. At equilibrium Q equals the equilibrium constant. The enzyme accelerates approach toward equilibrium without changing that constant. A measurement of high enzyme activity in an extract cannot by itself determine the sign of the net flux in a living cell.

A transamination is different from oxidative deamination. The latter can remove nitrogen from an amino acid as ammonium and often couples the reaction to a redox cofactor. Aminotransferase chemistry need not produce free ammonium or a net reduced redox cofactor. Equally, it is distinct from a direct amidation such as glutamine synthetase. In examination problems identify the chemical group being transferred before choosing the enzyme class. Look for paired amino acid and oxoacid substrates in a transamination; look for ATP-dependent activation plus a nitrogen source in an amidation.

4. Aspartate, asparagine and nitrogen economy

Aspartate is produced when the amino group of glutamate is transferred to oxaloacetate. Its carbon skeleton has four carbon atoms. Aspartate can serve as a substrate for protein synthesis, as a precursor for other amino acids and as a nitrogen-bearing starting material for nucleotide pathways. One fate of particular physiological importance is amidation of its side chain to form asparagine. The latter retains its four carbon atoms but now has two nitrogen atoms. Asparagine is consequently a compact carrier of organic nitrogen.

The common plant glutamine-dependent asparagine synthetase catalyses the reaction aspartate plus glutamine plus ATP yielding asparagine plus glutamate plus AMP plus pyrophosphate. Water and proton terms depend on the convention used for balancing biochemical ionic species. ATP activates the side-chain carboxyl group of aspartate through an adenylated intermediate. Ammonia derived from glutamine then attacks that activated group, displacing AMP and forming the side-chain amide. Pyrophosphate hydrolysis can help drive the process in vivo. Because ATP is converted to AMP and pyrophosphate, restoring the ATP pool typically costs two high-energy phosphoryl-transfer equivalents rather than one. This is distinct from saying that two ATP molecules are directly bound and hydrolysed in the synthetase reaction.

Compare the elemental costs of carrying nitrogen in asparagine and glutamine. Both molecules carry two nitrogen atoms. Asparagine has four carbons and glutamine five. Their nitrogen-to-carbon atom ratios are therefore 2/4 and 2/5, or 0.50 and 0.40. Carrying a fixed amount of nitrogen entirely as asparagine uses twenty percent fewer carbon atoms than carrying the same amount entirely as glutamine. This calculation describes molecular composition, not the total energetic expense of biosynthesis or membrane transport. Glutamine remains indispensable as a donor and an important transport form despite the ratio advantage of asparagine.

Carbon availability can influence nitrogen partitioning. A plant experiencing darkness cannot maintain light-driven carbon fixation, although respiration of reserves and mobilization of stored carbon may continue. Under certain conditions an increased relative abundance of organic nitrogen favors asparagine formation and storage. This is not an instruction to assume that every plant in darkness increases every asparagine synthetase isoform. Lam, Peng and Coruzzi characterized Arabidopsis ASN1 regulation in relation to light and metabolic status. Their 1994 study provides evidence that ASN1 expression responds to carbon and nitrogen conditions. Expression evidence must be interpreted at its measured level: an increase in mRNA is consistent with altered enzyme production but does not alone measure pathway flux.

A seedling can redistribute nitrogen even when net external uptake is negligible. During mobilization of seed reserves, proteins are degraded, and amino acids may be transported, metabolized or reassimilated. The nitrogen atoms in a new leaf need not be newly acquired from the soil. Asparagine and glutamine can carry organic nitrogen between source and sink tissues. The predominant transport form depends on species, nutritional conditions and developmental stage. In particular, some nitrogen-fixing legumes transport substantial nitrogen as ureides rather than relying exclusively on amides. This variation rules out a single universal nitrogen transport molecule.

5. Amino acid families and carbon precursors

Grouping amino acids by precursor carbon skeleton helps explain why nitrogen metabolism cannot be studied independently of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle. Oxaloacetate gives rise to aspartate. 2-oxoglutarate provides the skeleton of glutamate. Pyruvate supplies alanine and enters branched-chain pathways. Three-phosphoglycerate provides a route to serine, and phosphoenolpyruvate together with erythrose 4-phosphate initiates aromatic amino acid synthesis through the shikimate pathway. These families share precursors but individual members may require additional carbon inputs or specialized nitrogen transfer steps.

The aspartate family begins with phosphorylation of aspartate by aspartate kinase and subsequent reduction to aspartate semialdehyde. This intermediate divides the pathway. The lysine branch condenses an aspartate-derived intermediate with pyruvate through dihydrodipicolinate synthase, followed by reactions leading to diaminopimelate and lysine. A different branch reduces aspartate semialdehyde to homoserine. Homoserine supplies intermediates for methionine and threonine biosynthesis, and threonine can be deaminated to 2-oxobutyrate for isoleucine synthesis. Isoleucine therefore belongs to the aspartate-derived family by its threonine precursor and to the branched-chain family by the chemistry of later steps. These classification schemes do not contradict each other.

Branching creates a problem of allocation. When one product is abundant, feedback inhibition can reduce the demand for intermediates entering its branch and leave more precursor available to other branches. Plant aspartate kinase isoforms and dihydrodipicolinate synthase participate in such regulation. An altered enzyme insensitive to feedback may increase flux into its branch, yet the steady-state amount of its product can still be limited by catabolism or consumption. Galili and colleagues have reviewed how combining feedback-insensitive lysine synthesis with reduced lysine degradation can increase seed lysine accumulation. The example demonstrates why a biosynthetic modification need not have a simple one-to-one effect on the final concentration.

Alanine represents a particularly short pathway from pyruvate because an amino group can be transferred directly. Valine and leucine require construction of branched keto acid skeletons before their final transamination. Valine synthesis begins with condensation involving two pyruvate molecules, while the isoleucine pathway begins with 2-oxobutyrate and pyruvate. Acetohydroxyacid synthase catalyses these initial carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions. Later, branched-chain aminotransferases supply nitrogen to suitable keto acids. Leucine biosynthesis uses a valine-pathway keto acid and an acetyl-CoA-derived carbon contribution through a distinct extension pathway. A final transamination does not mean that the full biosynthesis has consisted only of transamination.

Glutamate is itself a member of the 2-oxoglutarate family. Glutamine arises from amidation of glutamate. Proline formation from glutamate involves ATP-dependent activation, reduction to glutamate semialdehyde, cyclization and subsequent reduction of pyrroline-5-carboxylate. Arginine synthesis involves the ornithine pathway followed by addition of nitrogen through citrulline and argininosuccinate metabolism. Arginine contains four nitrogen atoms, illustrating how an amino acid can be useful for nitrogen storage even if not every nitrogen atom is an alpha-amino group. The extent and compartmentation of these pathways vary among tissues and species.

Aromatic amino acids originate from central carbon metabolites that enter the shikimate pathway. Chorismate provides a branch point for phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan formation. Tryptophan synthesis includes glutamine-dependent nitrogen donation during formation of anthranilate, and later incorporates serine-derived carbon. Phenylalanine and tyrosine pathways in many plants proceed via arogenate, although alternative routes exist. Cysteine synthesis links reduced sulfur to an activated serine-derived carbon skeleton: O-acetylserine combines with sulfide through O-acetylserine(thiol)lyase. Nitrogen alone cannot replace a deficiency of sulfur required for this reaction and for sulfur-containing downstream compounds including methionine.

6. Carbon, energy and growing sinks

When an aminotransferase consumes oxaloacetate or pyruvate, it draws on intermediates shared with many other pathways. Sustained net removal demands replenishment. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase can contribute to anaplerotic carbon metabolism by forming oxaloacetate from phosphoenolpyruvate and bicarbonate. Anaplerosis means replenishing intermediates removed from a metabolic cycle or network. It is particularly important when organic acids supply carbon skeletons for net biosynthesis rather than returning quantitatively through a cycle. The reaction does not replace the entire photosynthetic carbon fixation machinery, and its role in a C3 plant should not be confused with its specialized function in C4 photosynthesis.

The cost of building an amino acid depends on the source of nitrogen and the chemical transformations needed. Starting with nitrate incurs electron costs for nitrate and nitrite reduction before nitrogen reaches ammonium. The GS–GOGAT pathway then uses ATP and reducing power. Subsequent biosynthesis may require additional ATP or reducing equivalents, depending on the family. A transamination alone is not directly ATP-consuming, but the production of its substrates can be energetically costly. An examination question about net costs must define the starting metabolites and system boundary; otherwise more than one accounting convention may be defensible.

Photosynthetic leaves are both sources of carbon and active nitrogen-metabolizing tissues. Roots, young leaves and developing seeds can act as sinks, importing carbon compounds and organic nitrogen. A developing seed has high demand for storage protein and therefore for a balanced mixture of amino acids. Raising the amount of one free amino acid may not raise total protein synthesis if another amino acid, energy supply or storage protein expression becomes limiting. Likewise, a free metabolite pool can expand during slowed consumption without any increase in its synthesis rate.

Consider two seedlings supplied with the same amount of ammonium. One receives sufficient light and carbon dioxide; the other experiences prolonged carbon limitation. Other variables being comparable, the second may struggle to provide 2-oxoglutarate, ATP and reducing power for net organic nitrogen production. Ammonium may accumulate or uptake may be downregulated. However it would be incorrect to assert that carbon-limited plants always accumulate ammonium, since the outcome depends on regulation, existing reserves and alternative metabolic fates. The useful prediction is that nitrogen incorporation cannot continue indefinitely without carbon skeletons and energy.

7. Experimental reasoning and isotope tracing

A nitrogen isotope pulse is a way to follow atoms rather than merely compare concentrations. Nitrogen-15 is a stable isotope. If roots receive labeled ammonium for a short time and metabolites are sampled later, mass spectrometry can measure the fraction of nitrogen atoms labeled in particular amino acids. An unlabeled control establishes natural isotope abundance and analytical background. A time series is crucial because a late sample alone may show label in many products without revealing the route taken. Positional information, when experimentally available, can distinguish label in glutamine's amide nitrogen from its alpha-amino nitrogen.

A large amount of labeled glutamine shortly after feeding supports rapid entry through glutamine synthetase, but the labeling pattern alone does not prove that every molecule of ammonium in every tissue enters via that route. Transport, turnover and isotopic dilution can change observed enrichment. Suppose an illustrative pool contains 40 micromoles of nitrogen atoms and has 25 percent enrichment above a simplified zero-label baseline. It contains 10 micromoles of labeled nitrogen atoms. A second pool containing 100 micromoles of nitrogen atoms with 10 percent enrichment also contains 10 micromoles of labeled nitrogen. Percent enrichment by itself cannot rank total labeled nitrogen accumulation between the two pools.

Enzyme inhibition provides a complementary strategy. If a particular aspartate aminotransferase isoenzyme contributes to label transfer from glutamate into aspartate, inhibiting it may reduce the initial rate of labeled aspartate appearance. An appropriate vehicle control, measurement of substrate pools and assessment of viability are essential. If the plant has redundant isoenzymes, the effect may be small despite a substantial contribution from the targeted enzyme under other conditions. If the inhibitor affects unrelated processes, an altered label pattern may reflect indirect consequences rather than the specific catalytic step.

Interpret abundance using a pool balance: change in amount per unit time equals input flux minus output flux. At steady state the two fluxes balance but may both be large. A small pool with rapid turnover can carry substantial metabolic traffic. For example, an aspartate pool of 5 micromoles turning over at 10 micromoles per hour has a nominal turnover time of half an hour under a well-mixed constant-pool approximation. A 50 micromole pool carrying the same flux has a five-hour turnover time. This reasoning explains why labeling dynamics can differ strongly even for pathways with similar net rates.

Gene expression measurements supply another kind of evidence. A comparison of ASN1 transcript abundance under dark and sucrose-supplied conditions can test whether the response is associated with carbon availability rather than light alone. To infer that altered expression changes nitrogen allocation, one would also want enzyme activity and metabolite or isotope measurements. An association between an expression change and a concentration change is informative but not necessarily sufficient to establish direction of causality. Distinguishing observations from inferred mechanisms is central to Part C experimental questions.

8. Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which donor usually transfers an alpha-amino group to oxaloacetate?
  1. Glutamine amide nitrogen
  2. Glutamate
  3. Nitrate
  4. Molecular nitrogen

Answer: B. Aspartate aminotransferase exchanges the alpha-amino group of glutamate with the carbonyl group of oxaloacetate. Nitrate and N₂ require other metabolic entry processes.

2. Part B — Which cofactor is directly involved in typical aminotransferase chemistry?
  1. PLP
  2. Biotin
  3. FAD only
  4. Heme

Answer: A. PLP cycles between pyridoxal phosphate and pyridoxamine phosphate forms. Biotin typically transfers activated carbon dioxide, not alpha-amino groups.

3. Part B — Which products are formed by glutamine-dependent asparagine synthetase?
  1. Asparagine, glutamate, AMP and PPi
  2. Asparagine and nitrate
  3. Glutamine and ADP only
  4. Aspartate and ammonia only

Answer: A. Glutamine supplies its amide nitrogen; ATP activation of aspartate yields AMP and PPi, and glutamine becomes glutamate.

4. Part B — Which intermediate is a major branch point in the plant aspartate family?
  1. Acetyl-CoA alone
  2. Aspartate semialdehyde
  3. Glucose 6-phosphate
  4. Ribulose bisphosphate

Answer: B. Aspartate semialdehyde feeds lysine synthesis and homoserine-dependent branches. The other intermediates are not this branch point.

5. Part B — Which amino acid is produced by direct transamination of pyruvate?
  1. Alanine
  2. Lysine
  3. Tryptophan
  4. Arginine

Answer: A. Pyruvate is the corresponding keto acid of alanine. The other three products require more complex biosynthetic pathways.

6. Part B — Which pathway provides chorismate?
  1. Shikimate pathway
  2. GS–GOGAT
  3. Photorespiratory glycine cleavage
  4. Nitrate reduction

Answer: A. The shikimate pathway forms chorismate for aromatic amino acid synthesis. GS–GOGAT assimilates ammonium.

7. Part C — Two pools contain 60 and 30 μmol of nitrogen atoms, enriched 10% and 20% above a zero-label teaching baseline. Which contains more labeled nitrogen?
  1. First
  2. Second
  3. Equal
  4. Cannot calculate

Answer: C. The products of pool size and enrichment are 6 and 6 μmol. The larger fractional enrichment does not mean a larger labeled amount.

8. Part C — Feedback-insensitive lysine synthesis increases pathway entry but seed lysine stays unchanged. What additional measurement best tests increased degradation?
  1. Chlorophyll color alone
  2. Lysine catabolic flux or products
  3. Ribosome number alone
  4. Soil nitrate alone

Answer: B. Steady-state lysine concentration reflects both input and output. Measuring degradation can distinguish enhanced turnover from unchanged synthesis.

9. Part C — A mutation reduces one aspartate aminotransferase isoenzyme, but total aspartate labeling is only modestly affected. What interpretation is justified?
  1. Transamination never occurs
  2. All PLP enzymes are absent
  3. Alternative isoenzymes or transport may compensate
  4. Aspartate contains no nitrogen

Answer: C. Compensation can preserve total flux. A whole-tissue result cannot automatically resolve each isoenzyme's contribution.

10. Part C — If 10 μmol of nitrogen atoms are transported entirely as asparagine or entirely as glutamine, how many μmol of carbon atoms are carried?
  1. 20 and 25
  2. 40 and 50
  3. 25 and 20
  4. 10 and 10

Answer: A. Both molecules contain two N atoms, so 5 μmol of molecules are needed. Asparagine contains four carbons, glutamine five.

11. Part C — A dark-treated seedling shows higher ASN1 transcript abundance. Which conclusion is supported directly?
  1. Asparagine synthesis flux must double
  2. ASN1 mRNA abundance increased
  3. Protein synthesis has stopped
  4. All asparagine synthetase isoforms increased

Answer: B. Transcript abundance is the measured variable. Flux and protein abundance require additional measurements.

12. Part C — A metabolite pool remains constant while label rapidly enters and leaves. What is the best explanation?
  1. Zero flux
  2. Balanced nonzero input and output flux
  3. No chemical reaction occurs
  4. The isotope cannot enter molecules

Answer: B. A steady concentration requires balanced inputs and outputs, not zero turnover. Isotope kinetics can reveal traffic through a stable pool.

9. Revision and active recall

Track carbon skeletons and nitrogen positions separately. Distinguish transamination from amidation, and pool size from flux.

  1. Why does glutamine contain two nitrogen atoms?
  2. What is regenerated when glutamate donates its amino group?
  3. Why does PLP cycle through PMP?
  4. Which substrate provides the carbon skeleton of aspartate?
  5. How does asparagine synthetase activate aspartate?
  6. Why can isoleucine belong to two families?
  7. What is the carbon cost of transporting two nitrogen atoms in asparagine versus glutamine?
  8. Why does an increased transcript not establish increased flux?
  9. How can a constant pool have rapid turnover?
  10. Why does carbon limitation constrain ammonium assimilation?
