1. From genetic information to protein chemistry

Consider two protein molecules that differ at a single position in a chain of hundreds of residues. One may remain soluble while the other associates with neighbouring chains; one enzyme may bind a substrate while the other does not. How can such a small difference matter? The answer begins with the chemical identity of the residue. Replacing an acidic carboxylate with a nonpolar side chain removes charge and changes hydration. Replacing a small glycine with a bulky residue changes the accessible conformations. A protein is therefore not a string of interchangeable building blocks: its sequence encodes a detailed pattern of chemical possibilities.

Earlier chapters established atomic structure, hydrogen bonding, water, acid–base equilibria and nucleic acids. The genetic information stored in nucleic acids becomes functional in part through ordered amino acid chains. This chapter follows the logic from the individual amino acid to its ionisation in water, then to the amide linkage that assembles residues. The geometric consequences of that linkage become the starting point for the next chapter on protein backbone conformation.

We will separate three questions that are easily confused in an examination. Which atoms and bonds define an amino acid? What fraction of each functional group is protonated at a given pH? Which of those groups remain chemically available after chain formation? Answering them in order makes peptide charge, isoelectric point, mass calculations and laboratory evidence much easier to interpret.

Learning objectives

Recognise and classify all twenty standard residues; assign stereochemistry; derive fractional charge and pI; interpret diprotic and triprotic titration curves; explain peptide formation, amide resonance and hydrolysis; calculate peptide charge, mass and bond counts; connect chemistry with assays, chromatography, electrophoresis and sequence analysis.

2. The shared α-amino acid architecture

Most ribosomally incorporated amino acids are α-amino acids. The α-carbon lies next to the carboxyl carbon and carries an amino group, a hydrogen and a variable side chain R. The amino and carboxyl groups provide handles for head-to-tail chain formation; R determines the distinctive chemical behaviour of each residue. Number the carboxyl carbon C1, the next carbon C2 or Cα, then label successive side-chain carbon positions β, γ, δ and ε as appropriate.

The uncharged formula H₂N–CH(R)–COOH is useful for showing connectivity, but is usually not the dominant form near neutral aqueous pH. The amino group accepts a proton while the carboxyl group loses one. This creates a molecule with internal ionic groups and a large dipole. The ionic lattice of crystalline amino acids often gives high melting or decomposition temperatures. Their solubility depends on the side chain, pH, ionic strength and crystal packing; not all amino acids dissolve equally well.

A residue is the portion of an amino acid incorporated into a chain. Formal peptide condensation removes the elements of water per linkage. A free glycine has an average molar mass near 75.07 g mol⁻¹, whereas a glycyl residue contributes about 57.05 Da to an ordinary peptide. The difference is useful for mass accounting, but do not imagine that the same free-amino-acid protonation states persist unchanged in the interior of a peptide.

Amino acids also exist outside the α-amino acid family: β-alanine has amino nitrogen at Cβ, while γ-aminobutyric acid has it at Cγ. Both are biological molecules, but neither is one of the standard twenty residues directly assigned by the ordinary genetic code. Proline belongs to the standard α-amino acids despite its cyclic secondary amine. The term amino acid describes chemical functional groups, whereas proteinogenic identifies participation in protein biosynthesis.

4. D/L and R/S stereochemistry

A tetrahedral carbon with four different substituents is stereogenic. At the α-carbon of alanine the four groups are H, CH₃, amino and carboxyl, so two mirror-image configurations are possible. Glycine has two hydrogen substituents and is achiral. In an achiral environment, enantiomers have equal ordinary equilibrium properties except opposite optical rotation and their interactions with chiral environments. Enzymes and ribosomes are themselves chiral, so the same amino acid in its opposite configuration can have very different biological recognition.

D/L nomenclature describes relative configuration with reference to glyceraldehyde. In the conventional Fischer projection of an α-amino acid with COOH up and R down, an amino group on the left is L, on the right D. Horizontal bonds project towards the viewer and vertical bonds away. A 180° in-plane rotation preserves a Fischer configuration; a 90° rotation interpreted as a new Fischer projection does not. D/L does not predict the sign of optical rotation, which is measured and denoted (+) or (−).

R/S describes absolute configuration according to Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priorities. For alanine, nitrogen has higher atomic number than carbon; of the two carbon groups, the carboxyl carbon outranks methyl because its attached atoms include oxygens. Hydrogen is lowest. Orient the lowest group away, then clockwise 1→2→3 is R and counterclockwise S. If the lowest group points towards you, reverse the apparent result. Most standard L-amino acids are S at Cα, but L-cysteine is R because the side-chain carbon is attached to sulfur, which outranks the oxygens used in the comparison of the carboxyl group. Glycine is neither R nor S.

Isoleucine and threonine possess an additional stereocentre at Cβ. Each has four stereoisomers (2²), comprising two enantiomer pairs with no meso form. Natural L-isoleucine is (2S,3S) and L-threonine (2S,3R). Their allo forms retain the α-configuration and invert the β-centre, making them diastereomers rather than mirror images. A racemate contains equal enantiomers and is optically inactive in an achiral medium even though its individual molecules are chiral.

D-amino acids occur in nature: D-alanine and D-glutamate in bacterial peptidoglycan, D-serine in signalling, and D-residues in several nonribosomal peptides. Racemases can interconvert enantiomers through transient removal and re-addition of the α-proton, often using a planar intermediate and sometimes pyridoxal phosphate. The preference for L-residues in ribosomal protein synthesis reflects biological specificity, not a prohibition of D-amino acid chemistry.

5. Nonpolar residues, branching and steric effects

Replacing glycine's hydrogen with alanine's methyl adds nonpolar surface and changes the steric options at Cα. Hydrophobic groups do not form a special covalent bond with one another. Rather, exposing their surface to water changes the free energy of the combined solute–solvent system. Burial in a folded protein can reduce the exposed nonpolar area. This principle explains why hydrophobic residues often cluster in soluble protein interiors and why hydrophobic sequences can be compatible with lipid membranes, without implying that every nonpolar residue must be buried.

Valine, leucine and isoleucine are branched-chain amino acids. Val and Ile branch at Cβ, while Leu branches at Cγ. This alters packing and the steric constraints on adjacent backbone and side-chain rotations. Leu and Ile are constitutional isomers: their formula and residue masses are identical, but their atom connectivity differs. Ordinary intact mass measurement cannot distinguish them. Protein packing, chromatography and specialised analytical methods can be sensitive to the structural difference.

Glycine has very little side-chain steric restriction. It can adopt backbone conformations less accessible to many other amino acids and is important in the repeating Gly–X–Y architecture of collagen. Proline lies at the other extreme: its side chain closes to its α-nitrogen, constraining backbone φ. The free amino acid has a secondary amine, historically called an imino acid, though it is not an ordinary imine with a C=N bond. In an internal peptide position, its nitrogen is a tertiary amide without an N–H hydrogen-bond donor. Proline also increases the relative cis population of the preceding peptide bond, an important consideration in folding kinetics.

Methionine's sulfur is part of a thioether, not a thiol. It can undergo oxidation to methionine sulfoxide and further to sulfone but does not ordinarily form cystine-type disulfides. It is the precursor of S-adenosylmethionine, an activated methyl donor, and is used in translation initiation, although the initiating methionine can be removed during maturation. Hydropathy scales quantify preferences for specified environments rather than universal intrinsic constants; a hydrophobic sequence is a clue to possible membrane association, not conclusive structural evidence.

6. Hydroxyl, amide and aromatic side chains

Serine carries a primary alcohol and threonine a secondary alcohol. Both can donate and accept hydrogen bonds. They are largely uncharged near neutral pH, but enzyme active sites can facilitate their nucleophilic reactions. The hydroxyl groups can be phosphorylated, adding a titratable negatively charged group that changes binding and electrostatics. Ser and Thr also provide sites for certain forms of O-glycosylation. A covalent modification can therefore change protein function without changing the encoded sequence.

Asparagine and glutamine contain neutral side-chain carboxamides, differing by one methylene group. The carbonyl oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond; amide N–H groups can donate. The amide nitrogen is a poor hydrogen-bond acceptor because its lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl. Do not confuse Asn and Gln with Asp and Glu, which carry side-chain carboxylate charges. Deamidation of Asn or Gln can create acidic products; Asn deamidation can also produce isoaspartate via a succinimide intermediate, changing both charge and local backbone connectivity. Asn is a site of canonical N-glycosylation in an appropriate protein context.

Phenylalanine has a hydrophobic benzyl side chain. Tyrosine has a phenolic hydroxyl and therefore combines aromatic surface with polarity; at high pH its phenol can lose a proton to yield phenolate. Tryptophan has an indole ring whose N–H can donate a hydrogen bond. Its nitrogen lone pair contributes to aromaticity and is not an ordinary basic amine. Aromatic rings can participate in π–π contacts and cation–π interactions with ammonium or guanidinium groups. Their strength depends on geometry and environment rather than the presence of an aromatic ring alone.

Trp generally contributes most strongly per residue to absorbance near 280 nm, Tyr also contributes significantly, and Phe is much weaker there. Cystine contributes modestly. Tryptophan fluorescence is sensitive to local polarity and quenching, so changes may report altered protein environments. A fluorescence signal from several Trp residues is a mixture; it cannot automatically identify one unique fold. These optical properties link the molecular structure of amino acids to experimental protein chemistry.

7. Acidic, basic and sulfur-containing residues

Aspartate and glutamate contain side-chain carboxyl groups. At pH near 7, their free side chains are predominantly COO⁻. Asp has one methylene between Cα and the side-chain carboxyl; Glu has two. The extra length alters how the charge reaches a partner. Both can participate in salt bridges, coordinate metals and transfer protons in catalysis, depending on the local environment. Stabilisation of the carboxylate or of the neutral acid changes its pKa.

Lysine has a flexible chain ending in a primary ε-amino group. At typical physiological pH its protonated ammonium is strongly favoured, but the deprotonated group can be a nucleophile. Acetylation of that nitrogen creates an amide and removes its ordinary positive charge. Methylation changes steric and recognition properties and can preserve positive charge; trimethyllysine is a quaternary ammonium species. Lysine can also form isopeptide linkages through its side-chain nitrogen, as in many ubiquitin conjugates.

Arginine contains a guanidino group. Protonation produces guanidinium, whose charge is resonance-delocalised over the nitrogen-containing group. It remains strongly positive at physiological pH because of its high pKa. Guanidinium can contact several oxygens, for example in carboxylates or nucleic-acid phosphates. Lys and Arg are therefore common in DNA-binding proteins, but overall charge does not alone determine sequence-specific recognition.

Histidine's imidazole group has a representative free side-chain pKa near 6. The protonated fraction at pH 7 is about 9.1%, not 100%. Neutral imidazole can occur in two tautomeric forms, differing in which nitrogen carries hydrogen. The capacity to accept and donate protons, together with local shifts in pKa, makes histidine particularly useful in enzyme catalysis. A pKa near the reaction pH enables both protonation states to be populated.

Cysteine contains a thiol, R–SH. Deprotonation gives the more nucleophilic thiolate R–S⁻. Oxidation of two thiols gives a disulfide: 2 R–SH ⇌ R–S–S–R + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. The product from two free cysteines is cystine. Protein disulfides can connect two segments within one chain or two separate chains. They are covalent side-chain crosslinks, not backbone peptide bonds.

Many secretory and extracellular environments support disulfide formation; the cytosol is usually more reducing, although there are exceptions. Protein disulfide isomerase helps form and rearrange disulfides in suitable contexts. DTT, β-mercaptoethanol and TCEP are laboratory reducing agents. Methionine, despite containing sulfur, is chemically distinct from cysteine. The ability to form disulfides depends on thiol chemistry, not the simple presence of sulfur.

8. Nonstandard amino acids, modifications and nutritional classification

Selenocysteine resembles cysteine with selenium replacing sulfur. It is genetically encoded in specialised systems through selected recoding of UGA, requiring dedicated tRNA and additional machinery; not every UGA becomes selenocysteine. Selenium changes acidity and redox reactivity. Pyrrolysine is incorporated at selected UAG codons in certain archaea and bacteria using specialised machinery. These are often called the twenty-first and twenty-second genetically encoded amino acids. They do not replace the familiar standard set of twenty.

Hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine occur through post-translational modification, notably in collagen. Prolyl hydroxylation involves oxygen, iron and 2-oxoglutarate, with ascorbate supporting the appropriate iron state. Gamma-carboxyglutamate is formed by vitamin K-dependent chemistry and provides additional carboxylate groups for calcium binding. Phosphoserine, phosphothreonine and phosphotyrosine illustrate how chemical modifications can control charge and signalling. Citrullination of arginine converts the basic guanidinium group to a neutral ureido group. These modified residues generally arise after synthesis rather than receiving an independent standard codon.

Ornithine and citrulline are urea-cycle intermediates; homocysteine is involved in methionine metabolism; β-alanine contributes to pantothenate and coenzyme A. GABA derives from glutamate decarboxylation. Glycine contributes to porphyrin, purine and glutathione synthesis, while tryptophan and tyrosine can act as precursors to signalling molecules. Distinguish chemical amino acids, genetically encoded residues and metabolites that merely contain amino acid-like functional groups.

For adult human nutrition the nine conventionally essential amino acids are His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp and Val. Essential means dietary supply is necessary to meet needs, not that other amino acids are unimportant. Arginine and others may be conditionally essential under particular developmental or metabolic circumstances. Glucogenic and ketogenic categories describe catabolic products rather than nutrition or charge. Leu and Lys are conventionally exclusively ketogenic; Ile, Phe, Thr, Trp and Tyr can have both glucogenic and ketogenic fates in standard teaching classifications. Detailed metabolic pathways belong to later nodes.

9. Amphoteric chemistry and zwitterion formation

An amino acid can donate and accept protons. In Brønsted–Lowry terms it is amphoteric. Consider glycine at very low pH: the amino group is NH₃⁺ and the carboxyl is COOH, so the net charge is +1. Raise the pH through the carboxyl pKa, and the dominant species becomes NH₃⁺–CH₂–COO⁻ with net zero. Raise it through the ammonium pKa and NH₃⁺ becomes NH₂, yielding a net −1 species. The three states are joined by reversible proton-transfer equilibria.

A zwitterion has both positive and negative formal charges but zero net charge. It is not a molecule with every atom uncharged. An equilibrium solution contains a distribution of protonation states; the population average charge can be fractional even though the formal charge of each state is an integer. The isoelectric point is a zero mean charge condition, which usually favours the zwitterionic state for ordinary free amino acids.

The carboxyl proton is removed before the ammonium proton because the resulting carboxylate is resonance stabilised and the two functional groups have different acid–base energetics. The nearby ammonium also stabilises carboxylate electrostatically. A pKa is a property of a particular equilibrium in an environment, not simply a number permanently attached to an atom. For amino acids with additional ionisable side chains, start by drawing the fully protonated state, then remove one proton per successive step. Each deprotonation lowers formal charge by one, whether a positive group becomes neutral or a neutral group becomes negative.

10. Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and fractional charge

For an acid HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Kₐ = a(H⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA), where a denotes activity. Taking negative logarithms and approximating activity ratios by concentration ratios in dilute solution gives pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]). For a basic group, use the conjugate acid BH⁺ ⇌ B + H⁺ and write pH = pKa + log₁₀([B]/[BH⁺]). The numerator is always the deprotonated species. At pH = pKa, their ratio is one and the populations are equal.

Define an acidic group's deprotonated fraction f₋ = 1/(1 + 10pKa−pH). Its mean charge contribution is −f₋. For a basic group the protonated fraction f₊ = 1/(1 + 10pH−pKa) and its charge contribution is +f₊. These formulas follow from the ratio in the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation by dividing the relevant species concentration by the sum of the two forms. At one pH unit above pKa, the deprotonated fraction is about 90.9%; two units above, approximately 99.0%. The opposite applies below pKa.

The independent-group formula works well when effective site pKa values are provided and interactions between sites can be ignored. For a polyprotic molecule, sequential macroscopic pKa values instead describe overall successive proton loss. Microscopic site equilibria can be coupled and must not automatically be identified with independent site constants. The next section uses the exact sequential model for species distributions.

Exam principle

pKa describes a protonation equilibrium; pI describes an ensemble's mean net charge. A group buffers most effectively around its pKa. A molecule is not necessarily an effective buffer at its pI.

11. Diprotic and triprotic species distributions

For an ideal diprotic amino acid, denote H₂A⁺ ⇌ HA⁰ ⇌ A⁻ with sequential constants K₁ and K₂. Let h = [H⁺]. From the equilibrium expressions [HA⁰]/[H₂A⁺] = K₁/h and [A⁻]/[H₂A⁺] = K₁K₂/h². Normalising these ratios gives denominator D = h² + K₁h + K₁K₂. The mole fractions are α₊ = h²/D, α₀ = K₁h/D and α₋ = K₁K₂/D. Their sum equals one. Mean charge is α₊ − α₋. This form automatically handles all three states, even if their populations overlap.

For glycine, at pH 2.34 the cation and neutral zwitterion are almost equally abundant because h = K₁ and the anion population is negligible. At zero mean charge α₊ = α₋, hence h² = K₁K₂. Taking −log₁₀ gives pI = (pK₁+pK₂)/2. With 2.34 and 9.60, pI = 5.97. The neutral state strongly dominates because the two pKa values are far apart. This is a derivation of the pI relation, not an arbitrary averaging rule.

For a triprotic amino acid, let z₀ be the fully protonated charge and K₁, K₂, K₃ be successive macroscopic dissociation constants. The denominator is D = h³ + K₁h² + K₁K₂h + K₁K₂K₃. Each term divided by D is the fraction after zero, one, two or three proton losses respectively. The four charges are z₀, z₀−1, z₀−2 and z₀−3. Multiply each by its mole fraction and sum to find average charge. The exact pI solves that sum equal to zero. This is more reliable than a two-state approximation when several ionisation steps are close together.

For Asp, fully protonated charge is +1, so the ladder is +1, 0, −1, −2. For Lys it is +2, +1, 0, −1. At pI, the two charged neighbours around the neutral form largely cancel when nonadjacent states are negligible. This is why the two-pKa averaging method works well for many exam exercises; it is an approximation for a general triprotic case, not always mathematically exact. pH is formally based on activity; concentration expressions assume an ideal approximation, and high ionic strength can introduce differences.

12. Isoelectric point and the charge-ladder method

The isoelectric point is the pH where the average net electric charge is zero. To calculate a free amino acid pI, determine its fully protonated charge, sort its sequential pKa values, and reduce the charge by one at each step. Select the two constants that bracket the neutral state. Do not average all three constants or always select the α-amino and α-carboxyl values. For multiple closely spaced groups, solve the full charge-balance equation.

Cysteine is a useful exception to a simplistic classification rule: its thiol pKa is below its α-ammonium pKa in this tabulation, so the neutral species lies between the α-carboxyl and thiol steps. Tyr's phenolic pKa is above its α-ammonium pKa, so the neutral state is bounded by the first two steps. Always use the actual given pKa order. Histidine is classified as basic but has a substantially lower pI than Arg or Lys because its imidazole group loses a proton at relatively low pH.

Below pI, an ordinary ampholyte has positive mean net charge; above pI, negative. At pI, internal positive and negative charges may coexist. Electrophoretic migration is near zero in the ideal model, but diffusion and other physical transport processes remain. Many proteins have reduced solubility near pI because electrostatic repulsion between molecules decreases, which can promote aggregation. This is a tendency, not a universal solubility minimum: salts, folding, hydrophobicity and specific interactions all matter.

Distinguish pI from the actual pH of an amino acid solution. The latter depends on concentration, other ions and buffers. The isoelectric point of a peptide cannot generally be obtained by averaging the pI values of its component amino acids, because chain formation removes most free α-amino and α-carboxyl groups from titration.

13. Titration curves and buffer capacity

Imagine slowly adding strong base to a fully protonated glycine solution. Define base equivalents as moles of OH⁻ per mole of glycine, after accounting for any excess strong acid used to prepare the starting solution. At half an equivalent, cation and zwitterion are approximately equimolar and pH ≈ pKa₁. At one equivalent, the zwitterion dominates and pH is close to pI. At one and a half equivalents, zwitterion and anion are approximately equimolar, giving pH ≈ pKa₂. Beyond the principal steps, excess base and water equilibria determine the pH.

Buffer regions have shallow slope because an appreciable acid/conjugate-base pair can absorb added H⁺ or OH⁻. For a monoprotic ideal system of analytical concentration C, buffer capacity excluding water is β = 2.303 C Kₐh/(Kₐ+h)². It is maximal at h = Kₐ, giving β ≈ 0.576C mol L⁻¹ per pH unit. Near an equivalence point between well-separated pKa values the buffering capacity can be low and pH can change steeply. The exact curve depends on concentration, dilution, ionic strength and water ionisation.

A triprotic amino acid can have three distinguishable buffering regions near 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5 equivalents if its pKa values are sufficiently separated. Asp starts at +1 and reaches zero after one proton loss. Lys starts +2 and reaches zero after two losses, so its isoelectric point lies near the second equivalence point. Histidine likewise starts +2 but has a different pKa pattern. Overlapping ionisation steps can obscure separate plateaus. Count equivalents and determine the charge ladder instead of memorising a generic curve.

Extra added strong acid would require additional titrant. 14. How the protein environment changes pKa

Ionisation depends on the free-energy difference between protonated and deprotonated forms. If the deprotonated state is stabilised, pKa decreases; if the protonated state is stabilised, pKa increases. This is true for acidic and basic groups alike, provided their two states are identified correctly. Free amino acid pKa tables are reference values, not universal constants for side chains buried in proteins.

A carboxylate in a poorly solvating nonpolar environment may be less favourable than neutral COOH, raising its pKa. A buried lysine ammonium may instead be destabilised relative to neutral NH₂, lowering its pKa. Opposite charges, hydrogen bonds, solvent access and metal coordination can alter or reverse these simple tendencies. For a catalytic cysteine, local stabilisation of thiolate increases its nucleophilic population; a histidine pKa shifted near reaction pH allows both proton donor and acceptor forms to participate.

Suppose a catalytic carboxyl group has pKa 6.5 rather than 4.0. At pH 5.5 its deprotonated fraction is about 9.1%, whereas an ordinary pKa 4.0 group would be about 96.9% deprotonated. The altered environment can keep a normally acidic group protonated so it can donate a proton at the required step. This is a mechanistic reason enzymes use organised microenvironments rather than isolated functional groups in water.

Ionisation and folding are coupled: changing pH may disrupt salt bridges or hydrogen bonds and alter repulsion; changing conformation alters hydration and ionisation energetics. A protein that unfolds on acidification does not necessarily hydrolyse peptide bonds. Noncovalent structural changes and covalent backbone cleavage are separate phenomena. Later enzyme and folding nodes develop these ideas further.

15. Peptide formation: chemical linkage and directionality

Join the α-carboxyl carbon of amino acid 1 to the α-amino nitrogen of amino acid 2. The resulting –CO–NH– linkage is an amide called a peptide bond. The conventional formal reaction is H₂N–CH(R₁)–COOH + H₂N–CH(R₂)–COOH → H₂N–CH(R₁)–CO–NH–CH(R₂)–COOH + H₂O. One molecule of water is lost in the net composition accounting for each ordinary linkage. The first free α-amino group is the N-terminus and the last free α-carboxyl group is the C-terminus unless blocked or otherwise modified.

Peptide sequences are written N to C. Gly–Ala and Ala–Gly have the same composition and mass but are distinct molecules because their residue order and terminal identities differ. Dipeptide means two residues, tripeptide three; oligopeptide and polypeptide have no universally fixed length boundary. A protein is a functional biological macromolecule comprising one or more polypeptides, often with associated groups or modifications. Do not equate the number of residues with the number of backbone bonds.

The formal condensation is not an efficient reaction merely by mixing free amino acids in water. A carboxylate is a poor electrophile and a protonated amino group is a poor nucleophile. Formation of the amide needs activation of the acyl group and control of nucleophilic attack. In cells aminoacyl-tRNA provides an activated ester; chemical synthesis uses activated carboxyl components and protecting groups. This connects the thermodynamic accounting of water loss with the actual kinetic and mechanistic requirements.

Isopeptide linkages can use side-chain amino or carboxyl groups, as in Lys-linked ubiquitin conjugates and the γ-glutamyl bond of glutathione. A head-to-tail cyclic peptide consumes both normal terminal groups; a disulfide-cyclised peptide may retain both backbone termini. A depsipeptide has at least one ester in place of an amide. Always identify the actual chemical linkage before applying charge or bond-count formulas.

16. Resonance, amide planarity and cis/trans geometry

The nitrogen lone pair in a peptide amide is delocalised into the carbonyl system. Draw O=C–N and ⁻O–C=N⁺ as resonance contributors; the real molecule is a resonance hybrid rather than two separate rapidly interconverting structures. The C–N bond acquires partial double-bond character, making it shorter than a typical C–N single bond (often around 1.32–1.33 Å) and substantially restricting rotation. The carbonyl and amide groups also create a polar linkage with local dipole, even when there is no usual net formal charge.

A typical peptide plane includes Cα of residue i, carbonyl C and O, N and H of residue i+1, and Cα of residue i+1. For proline the corresponding N substituent belongs to its ring rather than an N–H. The plane is approximate; proteins can show small distortions. The torsion angle ω along Cαᵢ–C′ᵢ–Nᵢ₊₁–Cαᵢ₊₁ is near 180° in trans and near 0° in cis. Trans is usually strongly preferred because it reduces steric contacts between adjacent α-carbon substituents. X–Pro bonds have a higher relative cis population than most other bonds, but cis is not invariably dominant.

Amide cis/trans interconversion has an activation barrier and can limit folding kinetics. Peptidyl-prolyl isomerases catalyse interconversion without changing the equilibrium ratio for unchanged species. Rotation around the N–Cα bond defines φ; rotation around Cα–C′ defines ψ; side-chain rotations are χ angles. These variables, with the restricted ω bond, will be developed in node 1.C.02. At this stage understand that conformational rotation does not change sequence or invert the stereochemical configuration at Cα.

Hydrogen-bond implications

Peptide carbonyl oxygen is an acceptor. Most peptide N–H groups are donors, but amide nitrogen is not ordinarily an acceptor. Internal proline lacks its own N–H donor. These features explain backbone hydrogen-bond patterns in secondary structure.

17. Biological peptide assembly: aminoacyl-tRNA and peptidyl transfer

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases select amino acids and their appropriate tRNAs. First the amino acid carboxyl reacts with ATP to form aminoacyl-AMP with PPi release. Then the activated aminoacyl group transfers to an OH of the terminal adenosine of tRNA, producing an ester and releasing AMP. Depending on the synthetase, transfer may initially occur to the 2′ or 3′ hydroxyl; the group can move between them. Hydrolysis of PPi favours charging, with ATP-to-AMP corresponding to two high-energy phosphate equivalents in conventional accounting.

The net charging reaction is amino acid + ATP + tRNA → aminoacyl-tRNA + AMP + PPi. Many synthetases have editing mechanisms that reject near-cognate amino acids. Selection and proofreading are important because the ribosome primarily checks tRNA–mRNA recognition, not every amino acid independently. Indirect charging pathways also exist, so not every organism has precisely one independent direct synthetase for each standard amino acid.

The ribosome holds the growing peptide on P-site tRNA and the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA at the A site. Its free α-amino group attacks the ester carbonyl of the peptidyl-tRNA. The acyl-transfer reaction passes through tetrahedral geometry and transfers the growing chain to the incoming amino acid. The P-site tRNA loses its acyl group. Translocation moves the tRNAs and mRNA for the next elongation cycle, so ribosomal growth proceeds from N to C.

The peptidyl transferase centre is formed primarily by large-subunit rRNA; ribosomal peptide synthesis is thus an RNA-catalysed reaction. Substrate positioning and the terminal tRNA 2′-OH assist the chemistry. ATP is not directly hydrolysed at the instant of every peptide bond formation: energy has been stored in the activated ester by charging. GTP-dependent steps of delivery and translocation are separate energetic costs. In idealised accounting, a chain of n residues needs n charging events and n−1 ordinary peptide linkages. Questions asking about ATP molecules, phosphate equivalents and total translation cost require different counts.

18. Hydrolysis, chemical synthesis and peptide diversity

Peptide hydrolysis adds water across the amide to generate carboxyl and amino groups. It is often thermodynamically favourable in water, yet uncatalysed hydrolysis near neutral pH is slow. The amide resonance stabilises the reactant and the nitrogen group is a poor leaving group without suitable proton transfer. Proteases catalyse hydrolysis by activating water or other nucleophiles, positioning the substrate and stabilising reaction intermediates. Denaturation by heat or urea usually disrupts noncovalent structure, not the ordinary peptide backbone.

Endopeptidases cleave internal linkages; exopeptidases act near termini. Trypsin typically cleaves after Lys or Arg, with reduced cleavage when Pro follows and other context-dependent effects. Chymotrypsin preferentially cleaves after bulky aromatic residues, with specificity dependent on conditions. Cleavage fragments can reveal sequence constraints, but a predicted site is not proof of cleavage in a folded or modified substrate.

Strong acid hydrolysis of a protein permits residue composition analysis but destroys order. Asn and Gln are deamidated to Asp and Glu, Trp may be destroyed, and Cys, Met, Ser and Thr can require correction or special treatment. A composition report may combine Asp/Asn as Asx and Glu/Gln as Glx. Do not infer that an original peptide lacks Asn just because an acid hydrolysate reports Asp. Independent sequence data or appropriate analytical treatment is needed.

Solid-phase peptide synthesis commonly starts with the intended C-terminal residue attached to a resin. Remove its temporary N-protecting group, couple an activated protected amino acid, and repeat; the chain grows from C to N, opposite to ribosomal growth. Fmoc and Boc are common protection strategies. Side-chain protecting groups prevent undesired reactions. Final deprotection, cleavage from support and purification are needed to eliminate incomplete-coupling products. Nonribosomal peptide synthetases instead use modular enzymes and activated carrier-protein intermediates and can incorporate unusual residues, D-amino acids and cyclic structures. Peptide assembly is therefore a broader chemical process than ribosomal translation.

19. Calculating the charge and pI of peptides

When a residue is in an ordinary peptide interior, its α-amino and α-carboxyl groups are now amides and do not behave like free groups. Mark the free N-terminus, free C-terminus and ionisable side chains. At pH near 7, the usual integer approximation is N +1, C −1, D/E −1, K/R +1, His partly positive, Cys/Tyr mostly neutral. Modify this if termini are capped or residues chemically modified. For precise answers, use effective pKa values and fractional charges.

In the independent-site approximation, mean charge z(pH) = Σbasic 1/(1+10pH−pKa) − Σacidic 1/(1+10pKa−pH). Add any fixed charges separately. The peptide pI is the root z = 0. The bounding-pKa average works approximately for a simple, widely separated charge ladder; general peptides can require numerical root finding. Do not average the individual free amino acid pI values.

Worked example: Asp–Ala–Lys

Assume C-terminal pKa 3.1, Asp 3.9, N-terminal 8.0 and Lys 10.5. The fully protonated chain is +2, then +1, 0, −1 and −2 as the four groups lose protons. Approximate pI = (3.9+8.0)/2 = 5.95. At pH 7, N contributes +0.909 and Lys +0.9997; C contributes −0.9999 and Asp −0.9992. Total ≈ −0.090. Integer counting suggests zero, whereas the fractional model correctly gives a weak negative charge above pI.

Worked example: Gly–His–Glu

Use C 3.1, Glu 4.2, His 6.0 and N 8.0. The initial +2 becomes +1 after C-terminal deprotonation, zero after Glu, −1 after His, and −2 after N. Approximate pI = (4.2+6.0)/2 = 5.10. At pH 7, acidic groups contribute nearly −2, while His contributes +0.091 and N +0.909, giving approximately −1. This is why counting every basic amino acid as +1 is unreliable.

Worked example: terminal blocking and modification

N-acetylation converts a free terminal amino group to an amide, removing its usual positive contribution. C-terminal amidation replaces a carboxyl group, removing its negative contribution. Both can alter the pI despite retaining the same number of residues and ordinary backbone bonds. Lys ε-acetylation similarly eliminates its ordinary positive charge; phosphorylation adds a negatively charged group. A head-to-tail cyclic peptide has neither normal free terminus; disulfide cyclisation alone does not remove them.

20. Residue count, molecular mass and peptide topology

A single ordinary linear chain of n residues has n−1 peptide bonds. Given average free amino acid masses Mᵢ, the average peptide mass is ΣMᵢ − 18.015(n−1) Da numerically. If the supplied masses are already residue masses, add one water to their sum for an unmodified linear peptide instead. Do not subtract water twice. For a set of c separate linear chains containing n total residues, there are n−c backbone peptide bonds. A head-to-tail cyclic chain has n bonds and its mass is the sum of residue masses without terminal water.

Two separate chains of 40 and 60 residues joined by a disulfide have (40−1)+(60−1)=98 backbone peptide bonds. Reduction of the disulfide separates the covalently connected chains without hydrolysing any of those amides. The fully reduced combined chain mass is about 2.016 Da higher per disulfide reduced, due to addition of two H atoms. An intrachain disulfide in a single 100-residue chain instead leaves one chain with 99 backbone bonds after reduction.

A rough average residue mass of 110 Da permits quick protein-size estimates: a 33 kDa unmodified chain is roughly 300 residues. Actual composition and modifications change the result. Sequence diversity is combinatorial: twenty choices at each of n positions produce 20ⁿ distinct ordered sequences with repetition allowed. If composition is specified, the count is n!/(n₁!n₂!⋯). Two Gly, one Ala and one Lys permit 4!/2! = 12 distinct linear sequences. Equal composition and mass do not establish residue order. Cyclic sequence counts may additionally require rotational symmetry considerations.

21. Natural peptides as chemical case studies

Glutathione is γ-glutamyl–cysteinyl–glycine. The first bond uses glutamate's side-chain γ-carboxyl rather than the normal α-carboxyl. The Cys thiol participates in redox chemistry: two GSH molecules form glutathione disulfide (GSSG), and glutathione reductase uses NADPH-derived reducing power to regenerate GSH. This reaction changes side-chain connectivity, not peptide-bond count. The unusual γ-glutamyl linkage also distinguishes it from an ordinary α-Glu–Cys–Gly peptide of identical residue composition.

Oxytocin and vasopressin are nonapeptide hormones containing an intramolecular disulfide between Cys1 and Cys6 and a C-terminal amide. Their disulfide ring does not consume the N-terminal α-amino group. Their sequence differences alter receptor recognition. This example distinguishes a backbone cyclic peptide from a peptide with a side-chain crosslink. Neither the ring alone nor the residue count establishes terminal charge.

Mature human insulin has an A chain of 21 residues and a B chain of 30 residues, joined by two intermolecular disulfides, plus one intrachain disulfide in the A chain. The total is 51 residues and 49 ordinary backbone peptide bonds. It is formed through processing of a larger translated precursor. Reducing its disulfides can separate A and B but does not regenerate the precursor. Carnosine is β-alanyl-L-histidine; its imidazole contributes to buffering. These examples show why actual connectivity and post-translational processing are essential in peptide reasoning.

22. Chemical tests and optical measurement

Ninhydrin reacts with many free α-amino acids through oxidative deamination and decarboxylation, yielding a characteristic purple product for many primary amino acids. Proline and hydroxyproline give a distinct, usually yellow response because their free amino nitrogen is secondary. Other available amino groups, including peptide termini and Lys side chains, can react, so the test is not a unique identifier. The biuret reaction involves Cu²⁺ coordination with multiple peptide bonds in alkaline conditions and produces a violet complex. An ordinary dipeptide generally lacks the two peptide bonds needed for the characteristic reaction, whereas a conventional tripeptide can respond.

Beer–Lambert law A = εlc links absorbance A, molar extinction coefficient ε (M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), optical path l (cm) and concentration c (M). Peptide bonds absorb strongly in the far UV, approximately 190–220 nm, so peptides lacking aromatic residues can still be detected using appropriate wavelengths or other assays. Buffers, solvent impurities, scattering and contaminants affect signals. A good experiment uses blanks, a calibration range and an appropriate reference. Neither weak A280 nor lack of one colour reaction proves absence of peptides. Different techniques interrogate different chemical groups.

23. Electrophoresis, ion exchange and chromatography

A positive ion moves towards the cathode (negative electrode), a negative ion towards the anode (positive electrode) in a conventional electrophoresis setup. At pH below its pI an amino acid tends to be positive; at pH above its pI negative. A species near pI has small net electrophoretic mobility in an ideal system, though diffusion, electroosmosis and matrix effects remain. Size and shape affect friction and therefore mobility in addition to charge.

A cation exchanger carries negative fixed groups and retains cations; an anion exchanger carries positive fixed groups and retains anions. The name describes the exchanged ion, not the charge of the matrix. At pH 6, Lys is a candidate for retention on a cation exchanger and Asp on an anion exchanger. Elution may use increasing ionic strength, which competes for ionic sites, or a pH change that weakens solute charge. Exact behaviour depends on resin, buffer and other solutes.

Isoelectric focusing uses a pH gradient. A molecule in a region below its pI is positive and migrates towards higher pH, while above its pI it is negative and migrates towards lower pH. At pI it focuses because movement away changes the sign of its charge. Two-dimensional gels often combine isoelectric focusing with SDS-PAGE. SDS confers negative charge approximately proportional to size, so standard SDS-PAGE separates denatured proteins mainly by molecular mass rather than native pI. Reducing conditions can reveal chains connected by disulfides.

In thin-layer or paper chromatography, Rf = distance travelled by solute from origin / distance travelled by solvent front. It depends on the stationary phase, mobile phase and conditions, not merely the amino acid name. Reversed-phase HPLC commonly separates peptides via hydrophobic interactions, also influenced by charge and conformation. Size-exclusion chromatography separates by hydrodynamic size: larger species generally access fewer pores and elute earlier. Combining separations based on distinct properties is more informative than assuming one migration distance identifies a sequence.

24. Determining sequence and interpreting mass spectra

Complete hydrolysis gives composition but destroys order. Sanger's FDNB reagent labels available amino groups; after hydrolysis the derivative of a free N-terminal residue can identify the terminus, although Lys side-chain labelling must be distinguished. Dansyl chloride offers fluorescent labelling. Edman degradation uses phenyl isothiocyanate to derivatise the N-terminal amino group and selectively remove the first residue as an identifiable derivative, leaving a shortened chain for another cycle. A blocked N-terminus or a mixture of chains limits interpretation, and cumulative losses restrict useful read length. Carboxypeptidases provide complementary C-terminal evidence, but their release rates and specificity vary.

Overlapping fragments reconstruct order. For instance, trypsin yields Ala–Lys and Gly–Phe from a four-residue peptide, while a second cleavage or sequencing experiment supplies the overlapping Lys–Gly segment. The consistent ordering is Ala–Lys–Gly–Phe. Without the overlap or terminal identity the two tryptic fragments alone need not uniquely establish ordering. Real experiments also account for missed cleavages, modifications and mixtures.

In positive-ion mass spectrometry a protonated ion with charge z has m/z ≈ (M+z×1.007276)/z, where M is the neutral monoisotopic mass in Da. If [M+2H]²⁺ appears at m/z 501.0073, then M ≈ 2×501.0073−2×1.007276 = 1000.000 Da. Do not apply the proton-adduct relation unchanged to sodium adducts, negative ions or misassigned isotopes. Mass alone does not determine the complete sequence.

Tandem MS selects an ion and fragments it. Common collision-based fragmentation gives b-series ions containing the N-terminal part and y-series ions containing the C-terminal part. Differences of successive peaks in a series can identify residue masses, e.g. Gly 57.0215, Ala 71.0371, Val 99.0684 Da (monoisotopic residue increments). Leu and Ile have identical residue masses and ordinary mass differences cannot distinguish them. Multiple methods, specialised fragmentation or database information can help. Reduced and nonreduced digests can help map disulfides. DNA-derived protein sequence predicts the encoded residues but not every mature modification or processing event.

25. Integrated numerical and reasoning problems

1. Protonation at two pH values

A basic group with pKa 9.5 is 1/(1+0.1)=90.9% protonated at pH 8.5 and 1/(1+10)=9.09% at pH 10.5. Mean positive charge decreases tenfold; the ratio of deprotonated to protonated forms increases one hundredfold. Ratios and fractions are not the same quantity.

2. Infer pI from charge ladder

Three constants 2.0, 6.2 and 9.4 with initial charge +2 give +2→+1→0→−1. Approximate pI = (6.2+9.4)/2 = 7.8. At pH 5 the molecule is positive and tends towards cathode; at pH 10 negative and tends towards anode. The first two pKa values bracket +1, not zero.

3. Mixed side chains

A free peptide with 2 Lys, 1 Arg, 3 Glu, 1 Asp and 1 His has approximate terminal charges cancelling near pH 7. Its basic side chains supply +3+0.091, acidic −4, so net about −0.91. A more exact model uses all effective pKa values including termini. The sign predicts migration towards the anode, not a precise mobility.

4. Multiple chains and disulfides

Three linear chains have 25, 35 and 40 residues plus four disulfide links. There are 100−3=97 ordinary backbone peptide bonds, not 100 or 101. Four disulfides remove eight H atoms from the reduced form, approximately 8.06 Da. Reduction changes crosslinks without changing peptide-bond count.

5. Sequence and function

Replacing a surface Glu with Val removes a negatively charged side chain near neutral pH and adds hydrophobic surface. In haemoglobin S, the β-chain Glu6Val substitution promotes a hydrophobic interaction between deoxygenated haemoglobin molecules, contributing to polymerisation. The number of peptide bonds is unchanged. This bridges amino acid chemistry to higher-order association and shows why one substitution can have substantial functional consequences.

6. Experimental inference

A protein preparation has two bands of 20 and 30 kDa after reducing SDS-PAGE and one band near 50 kDa without reduction. A reasonable hypothesis is a disulfide-linked heterodimer, but not a unique proof: electrophoretic mobility is approximate, and other covalent links or unusual migration can affect the result. Confirm with intact mass and disulfide mapping. The critical distinction is evidence consistent with a model versus evidence uniquely establishing it.

  4. 6.3
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. The second and third constants bracket zero.

4. Basic group pKa 6.0 at pH 7.0: mean charge?

  1. +0.909
  2. +0.5
  3. +0.091
  4. −0.091
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. 1/(1+10)=0.091 protonated.

5. Acidic thiol pKa 8.0 at pH 9.0: mean charge?

  1. −0.091
  2. −0.909
  3. +0.909
  4. 0
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. 10/11 deprotonated with −1 charge.

6. Lowering a thiol pKa at fixed pH usually:

  1. Reduces thiolate
  2. Raises thiolate
  3. Changes L to D
  4. Breaks peptide backbone
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. The deprotonated population increases.

7. At first half-equivalence for a well-separated diprotic amino acid, pH is approximately:

  1. pI
  2. pKa₂
  3. pKa₁
  4. 14
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. The first acid–base pair is equimolar.

8. A 30-residue ordinary linear chain has how many peptide bonds?

  1. 30
  2. 29
  3. 31
  4. 15
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. n−1 = 29.

9. Three linear chains of 15, 20 and 25 residues joined by disulfides have how many backbone peptide bonds?

  1. 60
  2. 59
  3. 57
  4. 54
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. n−c=60−3=57; disulfides are distinct.

10. Four Gly of free mass 75.07 form a linear tetrapeptide. Mass?

  1. 300.28
  2. 228.22
  3. 246.24
  4. 264.25 Da
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. Subtract three waters: 300.28−54.045.

11. A head-to-tail cyclic peptide of eight residues has how many backbone bonds?

  1. 7
  2. 8
  3. 9
  4. 0
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. Cyclisation adds one linkage to the linear chain.

12. Free Ala–Lys–Asp at pH 7 has approximate integer charge:

  1. +2
  2. −2
  3. +1
  4. 0
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: D. Terminal charges cancel, Lys and Asp cancel.

13. A peptide is negative at pH 7. Its pI is ordinarily:

  1. Above 7
  2. Below 7
  3. Exactly 7
  4. Unrelated
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. The net charge decreases with increasing pH.

14. Asp, Gly and Lys at pH 6 on a negative cation-exchange resin: strongest expected retention?

  1. Asp
  2. Gly
  3. Lys
  4. All equal
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. Lys carries positive mean charge.

15. 2 Trp and 1 Tyr, no cystine, give ε280 approximately:

  1. 6990
  2. 12490
  3. 13980
  4. 5500 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. 2×5500+1490=12490.

16. A=0.25, l=1 cm, ε=10000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Concentration?

  1. 2.5 µM
  2. 25 µM
  3. 250 µM
  4. 2500 µM
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. A/(εl)=25×10⁻⁶ M.

17. A peptide fails Edman sequencing but is detected by MS. Plausible reason?

  1. Blocked N-terminus
  2. No peptide bonds necessarily
  3. All glycine
  4. Zero mass
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A. Ordinary Edman requires an accessible free amino terminus.

18. Two tryptic fragments have uncertain order. Most useful evidence?

  1. Same composition
  2. Overlapping fragment from second cleavage
  3. A280
  4. Same pH
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. Overlap establishes adjacency.

19. [M+2H]²⁺ m/z 401.0073. Neutral mass?

  1. 400
  2. 402
  3. 800
  4. 802 Da
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. 2×401.0073−2×1.007276≈800.

20. Reduction of one disulfide in a two-chain protein:

  1. Breaks peptide bond
  2. Adds about 2.016 Da combined mass
  3. Removes residues
  4. Adds water per residue
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. Two H atoms are restored to the thiols.

21. Lys acetylation reduces positive charge because:

  1. Ammonium becomes an amide
  2. Asp changes to Arg
  3. Disulfide formation
  4. Cα becomes achiral
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A. The ε-amino group becomes a largely nonbasic amide.

22. The nucleophile in ribosomal peptidyl transfer is:

  1. A-site aminoacyl α-amino group
  2. Anticodon base
  3. P-site free carboxylate
  4. rRNA phosphate
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A. It attacks the activated P-site ester carbonyl.

23. Ideal synthesis of 12-residue chain requires charging ATP molecules and bonds respectively:

  1. 11,12
  2. 12,11
  3. 24,12
  4. 12,12
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. 12 charging events, 11 linkages; ATP→AMP counts differently in energy equivalents.

24. Acid hydrolysis reports Asp and Glu but no Asn or Gln. Justified conclusion?

  1. Original may have had Asn/Gln
  2. Original necessarily lacked them
  3. Sequence is known
  4. All residues D
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A. Asn and Gln deamidate during acid hydrolysis.

25. X–Pro trans→cis changes:

  1. Sequence
  2. All α stereocentres
  3. ω torsion without backbone cleavage
  4. Residue count
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. Geometric isomerisation preserves connectivity.

26. Two Gly and two Ala in a linear tetrapeptide permit how many distinct sequences?

  1. 4
  2. 6
  3. 8
  4. 16
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. 4!/(2!2!)=6.

27. pI about 7.6 with a side-chain buffering region near pH 6 suggests:

  1. Asp
  2. Leu
  3. His
  4. Lys
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C. Imidazole pKa near 6 and His pI near 7.6.

28. A peptide without aromatic residues has little A280. Best conclusion?

  1. It cannot have peptide bonds
  2. A280 is a poor concentration measure here
  3. It is carbohydrate
  4. Zero mass
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B. Near-UV absorption depends on aromatic groups.
