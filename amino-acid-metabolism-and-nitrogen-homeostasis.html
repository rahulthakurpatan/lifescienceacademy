1. The nitrogen problem: why amino acids have two metabolic destinies

After a meal, amino acids enter the circulation from digested protein. Several hours later, even without new dietary protein, cells still need amino acids to replace damaged enzymes, build transporters and maintain tissues. The supply is maintained by a small, constantly renewed free amino acid pool. Dietary input, protein turnover and synthesis of dispensable amino acids feed this pool; protein synthesis, formation of nitrogen-containing compounds and degradation drain it. Unlike glycogen or triacylglycerol, the body has no specialised, inert storage polymer for surplus amino acids. Muscle protein can be mobilised, but this sacrifices functional tissue rather than emptying a dedicated fuel depot.

Every ordinary protein amino acid has a carbon framework carrying an α-amino group and a carboxyl group, although proline has a secondary amino group incorporated in a ring. At physiological pH the α-amino group is predominantly protonated. The side chain defines how the carbon framework will be processed and may itself contain additional nitrogen, as in glutamine, asparagine, lysine, arginine, histidine and tryptophan. Carbon and nitrogen therefore require separate accounting. Removing the α-amino group from alanine gives a three-carbon framework equivalent to pyruvate, which is already familiar from glycolysis. The nitrogen, however, cannot enter a carbon oxidation pathway or disappear when the carbon becomes carbon dioxide.

Consider an animal eating more protein than needed for synthesis. The amino groups are collected and converted into excretable nitrogen, while carbon skeletons can be oxidised, used for gluconeogenesis or converted into lipid. During fasting, the same logic operates on amino acids released from tissue protein, but glucose production becomes particularly important. The rate of protein breakdown is not identical to the rate of amino acid oxidation because many released amino acids are recycled. Net balance depends on the rates of both protein synthesis and breakdown, not simply dietary intake.

Nitrogen balance is nitrogen intake minus nitrogen loss over a stated interval. Loss includes urinary urea, ammonium and other nitrogen-containing compounds as well as faecal and smaller integumentary losses. A positive balance is expected during net tissue growth; a negative balance indicates net body nitrogen loss, as can occur during severe starvation. Approximate protein nitrogen content is 16% by mass, so dividing protein grams by 6.25 estimates grams of nitrogen. This convention is useful but not universal: different proteins have different amino acid compositions and hence nitrogen contents.

For example, if a person consumes 75 g of protein and total measured nitrogen loss is 14 g per day, estimated intake is 75/6.25 = 12 g N per day and balance is −2 g N per day. This is roughly equivalent to 12.5 g of net protein loss under the conversion assumption, not 2 g of protein loss. A urine-only measurement that ignores other losses overestimates balance. The balance equation describes the whole organism; it says nothing by itself about which tissue supplied the lost nitrogen.

The central problem is now clear: transfer nitrogen without unnecessarily releasing toxic free ammonia, deliver surplus nitrogen to organs able to dispose of it, and route the carbon into useful metabolism. These three operations explain the organisation of this chapter.

2. Transamination: collecting nitrogen without releasing ammonia

Cells do not need a different ammonia-releasing enzyme for every amino acid. Instead, many amino acids give their amino group to a common acceptor, α-ketoglutarate (2-oxoglutarate), producing glutamate. The donor becomes its corresponding α-keto acid. This reversible exchange is called transamination. The general reaction is amino acid₁ + α-keto acid₂ ⇌ α-keto acid₁ + amino acid₂. The carbon framework of each participant stays with its own molecule; the nitrogen is transferred between frameworks. There is no net consumption of ATP, no oxidation by NAD⁺ and no net free ammonium production in this reaction.

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT, also GPT) catalyses alanine + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ pyruvate + glutamate. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST, also GOT) catalyses aspartate + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ oxaloacetate + glutamate. In muscle, abundant pyruvate may favour the reverse ALT direction, incorporating glutamate nitrogen into alanine. In liver, alanine can be reconverted to pyruvate. AST similarly supplies aspartate nitrogen to the urea cycle when glutamate donates its amino group to oxaloacetate. The enzyme name does not specify the direction of physiological flux.

Why is glutamate such an effective collection point? Its carbon skeleton, α-ketoglutarate, is a major TCA cycle intermediate. It can be regenerated after nitrogen removal and accept another amino group. Thus, transamination couples many apparently unrelated amino acids to a limited number of familiar carbon pathways. Glutamate's central position is not evidence that all nitrogen must pass through it: glutamine and asparagine side-chain amide nitrogen can be released by hydrolysis, and not all α-amino acids undergo ordinary transamination as their first catabolic reaction.

Transamination is generally close enough to equilibrium that changing substrate and product concentrations can reverse its direction. For ALT, the reaction quotient is Q = [pyruvate][glutamate]/([alanine][α-ketoglutarate]), using activities approximated by concentrations relative to a standard state. If Q is smaller than the equilibrium constant, the written forward reaction is thermodynamically favoured; if Q is larger, the reverse is favoured. A catalyst accelerates approach to equilibrium without changing its position. Consumption of pyruvate in gluconeogenesis or consumption of glutamate in another reaction can therefore pull nitrogen transfer forward.

For an illustrative ALT system with K′ = 1, concentrations of alanine, α-ketoglutarate, pyruvate and glutamate of 4, 2, 1 and 2 mM give Q = (1 × 2)/(4 × 2) = 0.25. At 310 K, ΔG = RT ln(Q/K′) = 8.314 × 310 × ln(0.25) ≈ −3.57 kJ mol⁻¹. If pyruvate rises to 8 mM with other concentrations momentarily unchanged, Q becomes 2 and ΔG becomes +1.79 kJ mol⁻¹. This simple example explains how an identical enzyme can support opposite physiological roles without a change in catalytic identity. The assumed K′ is illustrative, not a measured ALT equilibrium constant.

Many amino acids use this strategy, but lysine and threonine are standard exceptions to the generalisation that most amino acids start degradation by transamination. Proline is first oxidised to a cyclic imine and ultimately glutamate. Serine and threonine can undergo eliminative deamination. Consequently, identifying glutamate as the main nitrogen hub must not become a claim that all amino acid pathways have identical first steps.

3. How pyridoxal phosphate makes amino-group transfer possible

The α-carbon of an amino acid is not ordinarily reactive enough to exchange its amino group at a useful rate. Aminotransferases solve this chemical problem with pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), the active aldehyde-containing derivative of vitamin B₆. A conjugated pyridine ring and an aldehyde allow PLP to stabilise reaction intermediates. The enzyme holds PLP in a specific geometry so that an α-carbon bond can be broken and rearranged without random reactions occurring throughout the cytosol.

In the resting aminotransferase, the PLP aldehyde is usually covalently attached to an enzyme lysine by a Schiff base (an imine), called the internal aldimine. When the amino acid binds, its α-amino group replaces the lysine nitrogen to form the external aldimine. The catalytic base removes the α-proton from the substrate. The resulting negative charge is delocalised through the conjugated PLP system; the quinonoid intermediate is therefore much easier to form than an unsupported carbanion. This is the basis of describing PLP as an electron sink.

Protonation at the cofactor side of the conjugated system yields a ketimine. Hydrolysis of that intermediate releases the donor's α-keto acid and leaves the cofactor in its aminated form, pyridoxamine 5′-phosphate (PMP). Notice what happened: the amino nitrogen has not been discharged into solution as ammonia. It is temporarily carried by the enzyme-bound cofactor. This separation of substrate binding and product release is a chemical safeguard as well as a catalytic mechanism.

In the second half-reaction an acceptor α-keto acid binds, reacting with PMP to form a ketimine. The steps reverse: rearrangement, proton transfer and transaldimination yield a new amino acid and restore the enzyme–PLP internal aldimine. The cofactor is recycled; one molecule of PLP can participate in many cycles. A nitrogen label on the original donor appears in the new amino acid, not permanently in PLP. No net redox cofactor is consumed because one carbon framework loses amino nitrogen while the other gains it.

This mechanism is a classical ping-pong (double-displacement) reaction. The first product leaves before the second substrate binds to the modified enzyme form. Under ideal steady-state kinetics, plots of 1/v versus 1/[A] at several fixed concentrations of B are parallel rather than intersecting. The ideal initial-rate equation is v = Vmax[A][B]/(KB[A] + KA[B] + [A][B]). Dividing by Vmax[A][B] gives 1/v = (1/Vmax)(1 + KA/[A] + KB/[B]). At fixed B, the slope is KA/Vmax, independent of B, while the intercept changes. This connects a molecular mechanism to the enzyme kinetics of Subunit 1.D; real inhibition, product accumulation or nonideal mechanisms may change the pattern.

PLP participates in other reactions, including amino acid decarboxylation, racemisation and β-elimination. Which bond is broken depends largely on how the enzyme orients the external aldimine and positions catalytic groups. A vitamin B₆ deficiency can thus affect several pathways, not just ALT. Conversely, adding PLP cannot replace a missing aminotransferase protein. In a coupled ALT assay, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate by added lactate dehydrogenase while NADH is oxidised. The absorbance decrease at 340 nm measures ALT-linked flux only if the coupling reaction is fast enough and the appropriate substrates are present.

4. Oxidative deamination and the glutamate hub

Transamination solves the collection problem but does not remove nitrogen from the body. In mammalian liver mitochondria, glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) connects the glutamate pool to free ammonium. Its oxidative direction is L-glutamate + NAD(P)⁺ + H₂O ⇌ α-ketoglutarate + NH₄⁺ + NAD(P)H + H⁺. The exact written proton balance depends on the ionic convention; the important stoichiometry is one carbon skeleton regenerated, one nitrogen released and one reduced nicotinamide cofactor formed per glutamate. Unlike aminotransferases, GDH catalyses an oxidation coupled to hydrolysis.

The enzyme first removes reducing equivalents from the α-carbon and forms an imino intermediate. Water then hydrolyses the imine to the keto acid and ammonium. This is why the reaction is called oxidative deamination rather than hydrolytic deamination alone. GDH can also catalyse reductive amination: ammonium, α-ketoglutarate and NAD(P)H combine to produce glutamate. The direction depends on concentrations, redox state and regulation. In mammalian hepatic nitrogen disposal, the oxidative direction is particularly important; it must not be assumed to be irreversible or universally dominant in all organisms.

Combine a transamination with GDH and cancel glutamate and α-ketoglutarate from the two equations. The overall transformation is amino acid + NAD(P)⁺ + H₂O → corresponding α-keto acid + NH₄⁺ + NAD(P)H + H⁺. This coupled collection and release is termed transdeamination. A small catalytic pool of α-ketoglutarate can accept many amino groups as long as regeneration continues. The resulting NADH may support oxidative phosphorylation, but free ammonia disposal will require energy later. Counting the reduced cofactor as energy without accounting for nitrogen disposal is therefore incomplete.

Mammalian GDH activity is sensitive to metabolic state. ADP and leucine can activate it, while GTP and, in many contexts, ATP inhibit it. An energy-poor cell is favoured to oxidise a carbon skeleton, while a high-energy signal can restrain the flux. The regulation is allosteric: it changes enzyme behaviour rather than changing the chemical equilibrium. The redox ratio also matters; a high mitochondrial NADH/NAD⁺ ratio opposes reactions that produce more NADH. It is therefore wrong to conclude that high ADP alone guarantees a given rate regardless of substrate availability.

Other enzymes release nitrogen by different chemistry. Glutaminase hydrolyses glutamine to glutamate and ammonium; asparaginase hydrolyses asparagine to aspartate and ammonium. The α-amino group is retained in both products; it is the side-chain amide that is removed. PLP-dependent serine dehydratase converts serine to pyruvate plus ammonium via elimination and subsequent hydrolysis of an imine intermediate. These reactions explain why nitrogen from two-nitrogen amino acids need not follow a single route. Amino acid oxidases also exist, using flavin and oxygen, but are not the main general hepatic nitrogen-collection mechanism.

For a tracing experiment, provide alanine labelled only at its amino nitrogen with ¹⁵N, along with unlabelled α-ketoglutarate. ALT produces labelled glutamate and unlabelled pyruvate. Addition of oxidative GDH can produce labelled ammonium. If instead oxaloacetate and AST are supplied, labelled aspartate can form without free labelled ammonium ever being released by those transfer reactions. These alternatives become the two entry routes for nitrogen into the urea molecule.

5. Ammonia, acid–base chemistry and the need for transport

Ammonia is produced continuously by amino acid degradation, amide hydrolysis, intestinal microbial metabolism and breakdown of other nitrogenous compounds. In water it exists as NH₃ and its conjugate acid NH₄⁺. At physiological pH most of the total is ammonium, but a small fraction is uncharged NH₃. The uncharged species can cross some membranes relatively readily; ammonium can also interact with specific transport systems and substitute for potassium in some transport processes. Neither species can be treated as completely harmless simply because the other predominates.

The acid dissociation equation is NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺. For an approximate pKₐ of 9.25, Henderson–Hasselbalch gives [NH₃]/[NH₄⁺] = 10^(pH − 9.25). At pH 7.40 this ratio is about 0.0141; the uncharged fraction is 0.0141/(1 + 0.0141) ≈ 0.0139, or 1.39%. At pH 8.0 the ratio is about 0.0562 and the fraction about 5.32%. These are approximations using a stated pKₐ; temperature and ionic environment affect the value. The calculation shows that a relatively modest pH change can markedly change the diffusible fraction even while ammonium remains the dominant species.

High total ammonia is especially damaging to the central nervous system. Astrocytes normally use glutamine synthetase to incorporate ammonium into glutamine. When nitrogen delivery exceeds the capacity for safe disposal, glutamine can accumulate and contribute to osmotic stress and swelling. Ammonia also disrupts neurotransmitter balance, ion transport and mitochondrial metabolism. Diversion of carbon intermediates and disturbance of glutamate homeostasis are part of this problem, but a single simplistic claim that ammonia toxicity is always caused only by α-ketoglutarate depletion is insufficient. The effects depend on tissue, concentration and duration.

Organisms employ different excretory strategies. Many aquatic animals can release ammonia directly into abundant surrounding water (ammonotelism). Mammals convert much excess nitrogen to urea, which is less toxic and can be carried in blood before renal excretion (ureotelism). Birds and many reptiles excrete much nitrogen as relatively insoluble uric acid, reducing water loss at substantial metabolic cost (uricotelism). These categories describe predominant products; they are not claims that a given organism excretes only one nitrogen compound. The relevant constraint is the balance between energy, water availability and toxicity.

The mammalian liver performs much nitrogen disposal, but amino acid breakdown occurs in tissues far from the liver. Rather than continuously releasing free ammonia into blood, extrahepatic tissues commonly package nitrogen as glutamine or alanine. These are chemically distinct solutions: glutamine stores an additional nitrogen in an amide, while alanine carries one amino nitrogen on a pyruvate-derived skeleton.

6. Glutamine: a two-nitrogen carrier and metabolic donor

Glutamine synthetase (GS) catalyses glutamate + NH₄⁺ + ATP → glutamine + ADP + Pi, with proton terms depending on the ionic convention. Glutamate already contains one α-amino nitrogen. The incoming nitrogen is attached to the side-chain carboxyl group, forming an amide. ATP first activates the γ-carboxyl group as a γ-glutamyl phosphate intermediate; ammonia attacks, phosphate leaves and the amide bond forms. This explains the energetic requirement: direct formation of an amide from a carboxylate and ammonia is not favourable under ordinary cellular conditions without activation.

Glutamine is a particularly effective soluble nitrogen carrier because it has two nitrogen atoms and does not release the side-chain nitrogen spontaneously at physiological conditions. Skeletal muscle and other extrahepatic tissues can use GS to collect ammonia, and the bloodstream can transport glutamine to the liver, kidney, intestine or other consuming tissues. In liver mitochondria, glutaminase catalyses glutamine + H₂O → glutamate + NH₄⁺. Glutamate can subsequently transfer its α-amino nitrogen to aspartate or undergo GDH-mediated oxidative deamination. The two nitrogen atoms of a transported glutamine may therefore have different immediate fates.

It is essential to distinguish glutamine synthetase from glutaminase. GS spends ATP and incorporates ammonium; glutaminase uses water and releases it. They are different enzymes, not a reversible reaction catalysed by the same protein. If both ran at high rates in the same compartment with no useful nitrogen transfer, their net reaction would waste ATP. Tissue-specific expression, compartmentation and regulation help convert what might be a futile cycle into a functional transport system.

The liver provides an elegant example of complementary nitrogen-handling capacity. Periportal hepatocytes receive blood arriving from the portal circulation and are rich in urea cycle enzymes; they remove a large nitrogen load. A small population of perivenous hepatocytes, rich in GS, captures residual ammonium before blood leaves the liver. Urea synthesis has high capacity, whereas the GS system provides high-affinity scavenging. These two systems are not interchangeable: blocking hepatic GS may permit ammonia escape despite an intact urea cycle, and loss of ureagenesis cannot be fully compensated by storing all nitrogen indefinitely as glutamine.

The kidney also metabolises glutamine, particularly during sustained metabolic acidosis. Glutaminase and GDH can release two ammonium equivalents from one glutamine, while its carbon skeleton supports metabolic pathways that generate bicarbonate for the blood. Excretion of NH₄⁺ in urine represents net acid removal under these conditions. In an idealised accounting where both glutamine nitrogens are excreted as ammonium and the skeleton is metabolised appropriately, one glutamine contributes two ammonium and two new bicarbonate equivalents. Urea excretion is therefore not the only physiologically important route of nitrogen loss.

Glutamine also serves constructive metabolism. Many amidotransferases hydrolyse its amide and channel the nitrogen to a biosynthetic acceptor, for example in purine and pyrimidine synthesis. The remaining molecule is glutamate. These enzymes may couple a glutaminase domain to a synthase reaction, reducing escape of reactive ammonia. This dual role as nitrogen carrier and donor makes glutamine central to rapidly growing cells. The next nucleotide chapter will use the difference between amide and α-amino nitrogen repeatedly.

A position-specific tracer distinguishes these roles. With [amide-¹⁵N]glutamine, glutaminase first produces ¹⁵NH₄⁺ and unlabelled glutamate; with [α-¹⁵N]glutamine, the first ammonium is unlabelled and glutamate retains the isotope. If the glutamate later undergoes oxidative deamination or AST-mediated transfer, the second label appears in ammonium or aspartate respectively. Simply recording that a substrate is labelled glutamine without specifying the labelled position is insufficient for a unique prediction.

7. The glucose–alanine cycle: exporting carbon and nitrogen together

During exercise or fasting, muscle glycolysis produces pyruvate, while amino acid metabolism produces glutamate. ALT can transfer the amino nitrogen from glutamate to pyruvate, generating alanine and regenerating α-ketoglutarate. Alanine enters the bloodstream and travels to the liver. There hepatic ALT reverses the exchange, producing pyruvate and glutamate from alanine and α-ketoglutarate. The two products have distinct fates: pyruvate can enter gluconeogenesis and glutamate transfers nitrogen toward the urea cycle.

The liver exports newly made glucose, which muscle can use again. Thus alanine transport is not merely a way of moving amino nitrogen: it also moves a three-carbon skeleton in a form that can be returned as carbohydrate. During exercise, this route works alongside the Cori cycle, which transfers lactate to liver. Both cycles transfer three-carbon carbon skeletons, but lactate transports no nitrogen. ALT itself neither produces NADH nor consumes it. The lactate dehydrogenase reaction of the Cori cycle is explicitly redox coupled.

Two alanines supply the six carbons required for one glucose, assuming no diversion or isotope exchange. The liver's gluconeogenic conversion of two pyruvates to glucose requires four ATP, two GTP and two NADH, as derived in 1.E.05. Two alanines also deliver two nitrogen atoms. If these provide the two nitrogens of one urea, the urea pathway uses three ATP molecules representing four high-energy phosphate equivalents. The combined gross phosphate cost is therefore ten ATP equivalents per glucose-plus-urea pair (six for glucose and four for urea), plus the gluconeogenic reducing-equivalent requirement. This is not a net whole-body energy yield; the redox balance, fumarate recovery and glycolytic ATP in muscle must be specified separately.

One nitrogen can enter via GDH as free ammonium, producing NADH if NAD⁺ is used, and the other can enter via AST as aspartate. The fate of reducing equivalents depends on compartmentation. If an exam problem credits one NADH from GDH and one from fumarate conversion to oxaloacetate, it must also decide whether these reducing equivalents can meet the cytosolic gluconeogenic demand or be oxidised in the respiratory chain. They cannot be counted both as reducing equivalents for glucose formation and as ATP generated by oxidative phosphorylation.

The energy burden is largely shifted to liver, where fatty acid oxidation in fasting can provide ATP and reducing power. Muscle does not synthesise urea at the same high capacity as liver and lacks the complete hepatic glucose-export machinery. The division of labour is thus not a mere transport coincidence. It matches each tissue's enzyme complement and energy requirements.

8. Urea cycle: the origin of each atom and the mitochondrial reactions

In 1932 Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit studied urea production in liver tissue and identified the cyclic role of ornithine and related intermediates. The key conceptual observation was that a relatively small supply of an intermediate could support production of much more urea than could be obtained by its stoichiometric destruction. The intermediate had to be regenerated. The urea cycle was recognised before the citric acid cycle and exemplifies the power of tracing catalytic intermediates rather than merely listing substrates and products. In the classical liver-slice experiments, one mole of added ornithine could support formation of roughly twenty moles of urea, according to the Royal College of Physicians historical account. This result argues against ornithine being a stoichiometric nitrogen source: a nonregenerated molecule could not account for that many product molecules. It supports cyclic regeneration, although the complete modern enzyme sequence required further biochemical work.

Urea, CO(NH₂)₂, contains two nitrogen atoms and one carbonyl carbon. One nitrogen originates from free ammonia (physiologically the ammonium/ammonia pool); the other originates from the α-amino group of aspartate. The carbonyl carbon comes from bicarbonate. Ornithine acts as a regenerated carrier and contributes no net carbon or nitrogen to urea in a completed cycle. The two urea nitrogens are chemically equivalent in the final product, but their biochemical origins can be distinguished by labelling the precursors separately.

The first two reactions occur in the mitochondrial matrix of hepatocytes. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I) activates bicarbonate using ATP. Bicarbonate is phosphorylated to an activated carboxyphosphate intermediate, then nitrogen from ammonia is incorporated to form carbamate; a second ATP phosphorylates carbamate to carbamoyl phosphate. The overall substrate accounting is bicarbonate + ammonium + 2 ATP → carbamoyl phosphate + 2 ADP + Pi, with water and proton terms dependent on ionic notation. The enzyme requires N-acetylglutamate as an obligatory allosteric activator. The energy investment produces an activated carbamoyl donor and provides directionality.

Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II must not be confused with CPS I. In mammalian cells, CPS II is cytosolic, part of the multifunctional CAD enzyme and uses glutamine amide nitrogen for pyrimidine synthesis. It is regulated according to nucleotide requirements, not by the obligatory N-acetylglutamate activation characteristic of CPS I. The name carbamoyl phosphate is the same, but the nitrogen donor, cellular compartment and physiological purpose differ.

Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) transfers the carbamoyl group from carbamoyl phosphate to the terminal amino group of ornithine, producing citrulline and Pi. Ornithine has a five-carbon framework, and the newly attached carbamoyl group adds one carbon. Citrulline therefore has six carbons. The nitrogen from free ammonia now resides in the ureido portion of citrulline. No extra ATP is consumed by OTC because the carbamoyl phosphate donor has already been activated. This is a general example of using stored chemical energy in group transfer.

Citrulline leaves the matrix for the cytosol, while ornithine returns to the matrix through a mitochondrial carrier system. The distinction between mitochondrial and cytosolic reactions has mechanistic consequences. An intact set of soluble enzymes cannot sustain normal flux if transport of intermediates across the inner membrane is disrupted. Conversely, measuring enzyme activity in a homogenate does not establish that a pathway functions normally in intact cells. The remaining reactions must now attach the second nitrogen and regenerate ornithine.

9. Completing the cycle: aspartate, fumarate and arginine

In the cytosol, argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS1) combines citrulline with aspartate. This is the second nitrogen-entry step. ATP first activates citrulline through an adenylated intermediate; the amino group of aspartate attacks and AMP is released. The net reaction is citrulline + aspartate + ATP → argininosuccinate + AMP + PPi. Hydrolysis of PPi by cellular pyrophosphatase helps pull this otherwise energetically demanding ligation forward. Although only one ATP molecule appears here, its conversion to AMP and PPi costs two high-energy phosphate equivalents when ATP is regenerated.

The product argininosuccinate contains ten carbons: six from citrulline and four from aspartate. Argininosuccinate lyase (ASL) cleaves it into arginine (six carbons) and fumarate (four carbons). Importantly, the aspartate nitrogen remains in the guanidino group of arginine, while its four-carbon framework is released as fumarate. The lyase does not release the nitrogen as ammonia, and it does not require an extra ATP. This separates nitrogen transfer from carbon recycling in an especially elegant way.

Arginase 1 hydrolyses arginine to ornithine and urea. The guanidino carbon becomes the carbonyl carbon of urea; the two nitrogens introduced through carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate become the nitrogen atoms of urea. Ornithine returns to the mitochondrion to accept another carbamoyl group. Urea enters the circulation and is ultimately eliminated principally by the kidneys. Arginase is a hydrolytic enzyme, not an oxidase. Its reaction consumes water and is dependent on manganese in the enzyme's active site.

Tracing carbon throughout a turn demonstrates the cycle's logic. Five ornithine carbons enter, combine with one bicarbonate carbon to make six-carbon citrulline, then receive four aspartate carbons to make ten-carbon argininosuccinate. Four leave in fumarate, six remain in arginine, one leaves in urea, and five return as ornithine. At steady state there is no net consumption of ornithine. A cycle intermediate can accumulate or be depleted during perturbation, but the ideal complete turnover regenerates it.

Aspartate is commonly supplied by AST: oxaloacetate + glutamate ⇌ aspartate + α-ketoglutarate. Thus glutamate nitrogen can reach both urea entry points. When one glutamate is oxidatively deaminated and another supplies aspartate, two different reactions remove two amino groups without requiring both nitrogens to enter as free ammonia. In isotope reasoning, introducing labelled bicarbonate predicts labelled urea carbon; introducing labelled aspartate amino nitrogen predicts labelled urea nitrogen; labelling aspartate carbons predicts labelled fumarate initially rather than labelled urea carbon. Over longer times, central metabolic recycling may redistribute labels, so short-term direct tracing and long-term isotopic equilibrium must be distinguished.

10. Energetic accounting and the aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt

Adding the five urea cycle reactions cancels carbamoyl phosphate, citrulline, argininosuccinate, arginine and ornithine. The useful net skeleton and energy accounting is NH₄⁺ + HCO₃⁻ + aspartate + 3 ATP → urea + fumarate + 2 ADP + AMP + 2 Pi + PPi, with water and proton coefficients dependent on the chemical species convention. Subsequent PPi hydrolysis yields two additional Pi. This bookkeeping preserves the two nitrogen inputs, one bicarbonate carbon and four-carbon fumarate output; it should not be mistaken for a fully specified ionic equation without defining all protonation states.

The distinction between molecules and energy equivalents is frequently examined. CPS I converts two ATP to two ADP, costing two high-energy bonds. ASS1 converts a third ATP to AMP and PPi; restoring AMP to ATP requires two phosphorylation equivalents. Adenylate kinase can catalyse AMP + ATP ⇌ 2 ADP, after which both ADP require phosphorylation. Thus a complete urea cycle consumes three ATP molecules directly but four ATP equivalents. OTC, ASL and arginase do not directly consume ATP.

For 5 mmol urea formed, the direct ATP consumption is 15 mmol, while regeneration requires 20 mmol high-energy phosphate equivalents. The same production disposes of 10 mmol nitrogen atoms, or approximately 140 mg nitrogen because 1 mmol of N atoms has a mass of 14 mg. The urea formed has a mass near 300 mg because urea has a molar mass near 60 g mol⁻¹. If a problem asks for the nitrogen fraction in urea, two N atoms contribute 28 g per 60 g of urea, or 46.7% by mass. ATP cost per nitrogen atom is two ATP equivalents under this gross accounting.

Fumarate is not necessarily a waste product. Cytosolic fumarase hydrates fumarate to malate; malate dehydrogenase oxidises malate to oxaloacetate and reduces NAD⁺ to NADH. AST can then use oxaloacetate as the acceptor for nitrogen from glutamate, regenerating aspartate. This collection of reactions is the aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt, sometimes described as part of the Krebs bicycle linking the urea and citric acid cycles. The connection conserves a four-carbon skeleton and couples nitrogen disposal to central carbon and redox metabolism. It does not mean the urea cycle is itself a complete TCA cycle.

If one NADH from this shunt can be oxidised via a malate–aspartate shuttle and mitochondrial respiratory chain with an effective P/O ratio of 2.5, its potential ATP value is 2.5. The simplified net energetic cost would then be 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 ATP equivalents per urea. This is a conditional estimate, not a universally fixed physiological cost. If cytosolic NADH reducing equivalents reach the respiratory chain through a glycerol phosphate shuttle with an effective yield of 1.5, the corresponding estimate is 2.5. If NADH instead supports gluconeogenesis, subtracting respiratory ATP from the urea cost is inappropriate. The gross cost remains four regardless of how the products are used.

The shunt also illustrates why metabolic integration must be evaluated through net reactions. If fumarate is diverted elsewhere rather than returned to aspartate, the urea cycle requires continued aspartate supply from other sources. The cell may use anaplerotic reactions to replenish TCA intermediates. The amount of energy recovered depends on the actual flux network, not just the number of arrows in a diagram. When comparing amino acid oxidation yields, specify whether the accounting starts at the amino acid or its carbon skeleton and whether nitrogen disposal is included.

11. Regulation and pathway disruptions: predicting metabolites

Urea synthesis must increase when amino acid degradation increases, but it should not consume ATP unnecessarily when little nitrogen needs disposal. CPS I provides a key control point through N-acetylglutamate (NAG), made from glutamate and acetyl-CoA by N-acetylglutamate synthase (NAGS). NAG is required for substantial CPS I activity; it is an allosteric activator rather than a substrate nitrogen donor. Arginine activates mammalian NAGS, creating a link between amino acid abundance, urea cycle intermediates and the capacity to make carbamoyl phosphate. Increasing NAG can activate existing enzyme rapidly without synthesising more protein.

Over longer intervals, dietary protein content and hormonal state influence the expression of urea cycle enzymes. A high-protein diet increases nitrogen delivery and generally induces capacity for disposal. Early fasting can increase amino acid delivery from muscle, while prolonged fasting often reduces protein use as other tissues adapt to ketone bodies. Nitrogen excretion can therefore fall during prolonged fasting relative to its earlier phase. These are whole-body trends, not claims that every enzyme or tissue responds identically. Flux also requires ATP, bicarbonate, aspartate, mitochondrial transport and functioning downstream reactions.

Genetic enzyme defects are natural perturbations that reveal the order of the cycle. Loss of CPS I or NAGS limits carbamoyl phosphate production, yielding high ammonia and low citrulline. OTC deficiency also gives low citrulline, but carbamoyl phosphate can accumulate in mitochondria; excess carbamoyl phosphate can reach cytosolic pyrimidine metabolism, increasing orotic acid formation. In CPS I or NAGS deficiency this overflow is typically absent because carbamoyl phosphate formation is restricted. This is a powerful distinction between defects that superficially share the same upstream and downstream clinical signs.

ASS1 deficiency causes prominent citrulline accumulation because citrulline cannot be efficiently combined with aspartate. ASL deficiency leads to argininosuccinate accumulation; arginase 1 deficiency characteristically raises arginine. These predictions follow the principle that a block reduces formation of downstream products and may cause the upstream substrate to accumulate. Actual plasma concentrations depend on diet, residual activity, alternative pathways and tissue transport, so they are patterns rather than universal absolute values. Arginase deficiency can present differently from early-cycle defects; not every block produces the same degree of hyperammonemia.

Imagine two hepatocyte preparations given equal ammonium and bicarbonate. Preparation X produces little citrulline and little orotate; Y produces little citrulline but abundant orotate. If all other assumptions hold, X suggests deficient carbamoyl phosphate formation, while Y suggests impaired use by OTC. Adding NAG restores urea formation in a preparation lacking NAGS but retaining functional CPS I; it cannot restore activity of absent CPS I or overcome a downstream missing OTC. This logic distinguishes an enzyme from its obligatory activator without relying on memorised disease names.

It is useful to compare these inborn disturbances with loss of functional liver tissue or portal blood bypassing hepatic detoxification. Either can reduce ammonia clearance without any single defective urea cycle enzyme. An increase in ammonia is therefore a consequence, not a unique diagnosis. Here the disorders are biochemical tests of pathway organisation rather than a guide to clinical management.

12. Carbon skeletons: the seven entry points and the meaning of glucogenic

After nitrogen is removed, the carbon skeleton of an amino acid is still a valuable metabolite. The diverse side chains of the twenty standard amino acids are funnelled into seven standard central metabolic entry products: pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate, oxaloacetate, acetyl-CoA and acetoacetate (or acetoacetyl-CoA). Some amino acids contribute to more than one entry product because different parts of their carbon framework follow different pathways. Others have alternative routes that depend on species and tissue. Classifying amino acids requires knowledge of their products, not simply how many carbons they possess.

Glucogenic amino acids yield carbon that can contribute to net glucose synthesis. Pyruvate can be carboxylated to oxaloacetate by pyruvate carboxylase; oxaloacetate becomes phosphoenolpyruvate through PEP carboxykinase. Carbon entering at α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA or fumarate can move along the appropriate segment of the TCA cycle toward oxaloacetate. Aspartate and asparagine supply oxaloacetate more directly. These inputs can replenish the cycle as intermediates are withdrawn to support gluconeogenesis.

Ketogenic amino acids yield acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate equivalents. These products can support ketone body formation, lipid synthesis or oxidation. In mammals, acetyl-CoA cannot provide net glucose because pyruvate dehydrogenase is effectively irreversible and the conventional TCA cycle loses two carbons as CO₂ per acetyl-CoA entering. Oxaloacetate is regenerated, not generated in a net amount by acetyl-CoA alone. This does not mean an individual labelled acetyl carbon can never appear in glucose; isotope exchange and simultaneous anaplerotic flux can redistribute labels. The impossibility concerns net carbon conversion, not the possible history of every atom.

Leucine and lysine are the standard exclusively ketogenic amino acids. Isoleucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan and, in the conventional examination classification, threonine have both glucogenic and ketogenic potential. The remaining standard amino acids are glucogenic. The classification of threonine deserves care: it can provide α-ketobutyrate and then propionyl-CoA via dehydratase; acetyl-CoA-producing routes differ in importance between species and humans lack functional threonine dehydrogenase. Thus the textbook dual classification describes biochemical possibilities, not an assertion that all routes operate at equal rates in human tissues.

A useful thought experiment is to replace all dietary carbohydrate with an equal carbon amount of leucine or alanine while assuming glucose production is required. Alanine carbon can supply pyruvate and hence net glucose; leucine carbon alone cannot. Both can provide energy, and leucine oxidation may indirectly support gluconeogenesis from another substrate. The words ketogenic and glucogenic therefore describe net carbon fate, not whether an amino acid always forms ketone bodies or glucose in every cell. Hormones and substrate demands determine actual flux.

13. Degradation mechanisms: from amino acid families to central metabolites

The simplest carbon entry points follow directly from transamination. Alanine gives pyruvate, and aspartate gives oxaloacetate. Asparagine can first undergo amide hydrolysis to aspartate. Serine can yield pyruvate through dehydratase; cysteine can be degraded through several routes to pyruvate, with sulfur removed and ultimately oxidised or otherwise metabolised. Glycine and serine interconvert via PLP-dependent serine hydroxymethyltransferase, which transfers a one-carbon unit to or from tetrahydrofolate. Glycine can also be cleaved to CO₂, ammonium and 5,10-methylene-THF by the glycine cleavage system, so not every glycine carbon necessarily appears in pyruvate.

Glutamine becomes glutamate by glutaminase; proline is oxidised via pyrroline-5-carboxylate and glutamate semialdehyde; histidine degradation yields formiminoglutamate (FIGLU), which transfers a formimino group to tetrahydrofolate and produces glutamate. Arginine can yield ornithine through arginase and then glutamate semialdehyde through ornithine aminotransferase. These routes converge on glutamate, which becomes α-ketoglutarate by GDH or aminotransferase chemistry. Folate deficiency can impede FIGLU utilisation, a classical example of one-carbon metabolism affecting amino acid catabolism.

Valine, isoleucine, methionine and threonine can yield propionyl-CoA, a three-carbon thioester. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase uses biotin, bicarbonate and ATP to produce D-methylmalonyl-CoA. A racemase/epimerase converts this to L-methylmalonyl-CoA, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase uses adenosylcobalamin (vitamin B₁₂) to rearrange it into succinyl-CoA. The added bicarbonate carbon explains the conversion of a three-carbon substrate to a four-carbon product. This route is also encountered in odd-chain fatty acid oxidation, illustrating shared metabolic machinery. A block of the mutase or deficiency of the required cofactor can lead to methylmalonic acid accumulation.

The branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) leucine, isoleucine and valine share two initial steps. Branched-chain aminotransferase (BCAT) transfers the amino group to α-ketoglutarate, forming a branched-chain α-keto acid. Branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) then catalyses oxidative decarboxylation with CoA and NAD⁺, forming a branched acyl-CoA, CO₂ and NADH. The multienzyme chemistry resembles pyruvate dehydrogenase, employing thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoamide, CoA, FAD and NAD⁺. BCKDH phosphorylation inhibits activity, while dephosphorylation activates it. Unlike many amino acids, BCAAs undergo substantial initial metabolism in skeletal muscle because hepatic BCAT activity is low.

Downstream BCAA routes diverge. Valine yields propionyl-CoA and hence succinyl-CoA, making it glucogenic. Isoleucine yields acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA, giving it dual potential. Leucine forms HMG-CoA through its own mitochondrial degradation pathway, then acetyl-CoA and acetoacetate, making it exclusively ketogenic. These differences explain why the same shared first enzyme does not imply identical final metabolic products. BCKDH deficiency, associated with maple syrup urine disease, causes accumulation of all three branched-chain amino acids and their corresponding keto acids. Blocking a common step gives a multi-substrate metabolic pattern.

Phenylalanine hydroxylase converts phenylalanine to tyrosine using tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) and O₂. This reaction adds a hydroxyl group to the aromatic ring; the other oxygen atom is reduced. BH₄ must be regenerated. Tyrosine then undergoes transamination to p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, oxidative rearrangement to homogentisate, ring cleavage to maleylacetoacetate, isomerisation to fumarylacetoacetate and hydrolysis to fumarate and acetoacetate. The six-carbon aromatic ring is thus opened and its products enter both glucogenic and ketogenic metabolism. The initial nine-carbon tyrosine framework loses one carbon as CO₂ before the two four-carbon products are formed.

Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency disrupts conversion to tyrosine, producing excess phenylalanine and alternative products and making tyrosine conditionally essential. Homogentisate dioxygenase deficiency causes homogentisate accumulation (alkaptonuria), while fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase deficiency blocks the last step of tyrosine degradation (tyrosinemia type I). These examples locate the block within a carbon degradation sequence. They do not imply that all aromatic amino acid disorders disrupt urea formation directly.

Tryptophan can give alanine-derived pyruvate and ketogenic products through the kynurenine pathway; it also supplies precursors for NAD⁺ synthesis. Lysine undergoes degradation through routes including the saccharopine pathway, ultimately yielding acetoacetyl-CoA; its carbon fate is exclusively ketogenic in the standard classification. These routes involve several specialised intermediates, but the examination-relevant logic is the carbon endpoint, the distinction from ordinary transamination, and the connection to cofactor and precursor metabolism rather than memorisation of every minor intermediate.

14. Building amino acids: central metabolism supplies carbon frameworks

Catabolism explains how carbon skeletons enter central metabolism; biosynthesis asks the complementary question of how cells obtain carbon skeletons for amino acids. The answer is not twenty independent carbon pathways. Central carbohydrate metabolism generates a few precursor families. Glycolysis provides pyruvate and 3-phosphoglycerate, the pentose phosphate pathway provides ribose-5-phosphate and erythrose-4-phosphate, and the TCA cycle provides α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate. Different organisms possess different enzymes for elaborating these skeletons, which is why the same family map can describe bacterial and plant synthesis without implying that animals perform every pathway.

Glutamate is the principal α-amino donor in many biosynthetic transaminations. α-Ketoglutarate can acquire nitrogen through reductive amination by GDH, or through transfer reactions from other amino acids. In many microorganisms, at low ammonium availability, glutamine synthetase works together with glutamate synthase (GOGAT): glutamine + α-ketoglutarate + reducing equivalents → two glutamates. Combining GS with GOGAT cancels one glutamate and glutamine and yields net incorporation of ammonium into α-ketoglutarate, with ATP and reducing power consumed. The high-affinity pathway is useful under nitrogen limitation despite its energy cost. Detailed plant nitrate and ammonium assimilation belongs to Unit 6; the essential connection here is how inorganic nitrogen becomes organic donor nitrogen.

The α-ketoglutarate family comprises glutamate, glutamine, proline and arginine. Glutamine synthesis uses GS as already discussed. Proline synthesis starts with ATP-dependent activation of glutamate's side-chain carboxyl group followed by reduction to glutamate semialdehyde. This spontaneously cyclises to pyrroline-5-carboxylate, which is reduced to proline. The cyclic structure of proline therefore reflects the chemical organisation of its precursor, not an unrelated carbon pathway. Arginine synthesis uses ornithine and citrulline intermediates; animals can make some arginine through tissue-specific reactions, but the capacity may be inadequate during growth or illness.

The oxaloacetate family starts with AST transferring glutamate nitrogen to oxaloacetate to make aspartate. In mammals, asparagine synthetase uses glutamine amide nitrogen and ATP to convert aspartate to asparagine, producing glutamate, AMP and PPi. The enzyme activates the side-chain carboxyl group of aspartate before nitrogen incorporation. Aspartate also feeds purine and pyrimidine synthesis and the urea cycle. In plants and many microorganisms, the aspartate family extends through aspartate semialdehyde to lysine and homoserine-derived threonine and methionine; threonine can also supply α-ketobutyrate for isoleucine synthesis. Mammals lack the pathways needed to make these essential end products de novo.

The pyruvate family begins with ALT-mediated amination to alanine. In bacteria and plants, condensation of pyruvate-derived substrates via acetolactate synthase and subsequent rearrangement, reduction, dehydration and transamination produces valine and related branched-chain amino acids. Leucine is made by elongating the valine precursor α-ketoisovalerate with acetyl-CoA before further reactions. Isoleucine uses α-ketobutyrate derived from threonine in a common route. These are anabolic pathways with direction-specific activation and regulation, not the mammalian degradative BCKDH pathway run backwards.

The 3-phosphoglycerate family illustrates carbon oxidation during biosynthesis. Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase oxidises 3-phosphoglycerate to 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate while producing NADH. Phosphoserine aminotransferase transfers an amino group from glutamate, and phosphoserine phosphatase removes phosphate to form serine. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase converts serine to glycine while transferring a one-carbon unit to THF. In mammals, cysteine can be made from serine carbon and sulfur originating from methionine via homocysteine and cystathionine, using PLP-dependent enzymes. The dependence on an essential sulfur amino acid explains why cysteine synthesis cannot make the dietary sulfur requirement disappear.

The aromatic family in plants and many microorganisms begins with phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate. The shikimate pathway yields chorismate, a branch point for phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. Animals lack this pathway. Humans can nevertheless make tyrosine from phenylalanine through hydroxylation, so tyrosine is ordinarily dispensable provided phenylalanine and the relevant enzymes and cofactors are adequate. Histidine belongs to the ribose-5-phosphate family: activated ribose phosphate, PRPP, and ATP contribute to its construction in microorganisms and plants. Humans cannot synthesise histidine de novo. PRPP also appears in nucleotide biosynthesis, demonstrating why pentose phosphate metabolism is a precursor-producing pathway as well as a source of NADPH.

Biosynthetic families should be interpreted as origins of carbon and other precursor atoms rather than one-step reactions or universally present pathways. The family name aspartate does not imply that aspartate itself must be consumed to synthesise every related amino acid in every organism. The useful map connects shared intermediates to branched routes and predicts where an enzyme block or a deficiency of precursor carbon will have downstream effects.

15. Essential amino acids, feedback and metabolic specialisation

In human nutrition an essential (indispensable) amino acid is one that cannot be synthesised in sufficient quantities to meet physiological requirements and must be supplied through diet. The nine conventionally essential amino acids are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. This is a statement about human metabolic capacity, not about the biological importance of the molecule. Alanine is no less essential to many proteins than lysine, but humans can make alanine from pyruvate.

Conditionally essential amino acids are normally synthesised but can become limiting when demand exceeds capacity or a precursor pathway is impaired. Arginine can be conditionally essential during growth, and glutamine, cysteine, tyrosine, glycine and proline may become conditionally limiting in particular physiological contexts. Tyrosine depends on phenylalanine and cysteine depends partly on methionine as the sulfur donor. Removing dietary phenylalanine cannot generally be compensated merely by feeding tyrosine because the hydroxylation reaction does not reverse to produce phenylalanine. The same precursor–product asymmetry applies to sulfur transfer from methionine to cysteine.

Protein synthesis requires all amino acids specified by the protein sequence. If one essential amino acid is limiting, supplying larger amounts of other amino acids cannot substitute for it. Their excess may instead be oxidised and their nitrogen excreted. This principle explains why protein quality depends partly on indispensable amino acid composition, not merely on total protein mass. It also distinguishes nitrogen balance from amino acid adequacy: a high nitrogen intake does not necessarily supply the correct pattern of precursors for protein synthesis.

Microorganisms capable of synthesising all required metabolites from a suitable minimal medium are prototrophic for those nutrients. A mutant lacking a biosynthetic step may become auxotrophic and require the end product or a downstream intermediate. Imagine a linear pathway precursor → A → B → amino acid C. A mutant blocked in A → B can grow when supplied B or C if they enter the cell, but not when supplied A. A double mutant in B → C cannot be rescued by B. This is the logic of nutritional mutant experiments, famously used by Beadle and Tatum to connect genes with enzymes in Neurospora. The inference assumes transport, regulatory feedback and side pathways do not confound rescue.

Feedback inhibition prevents unnecessary consumption of carbon, nitrogen, ATP and reducing equivalents. A final amino acid often inhibits an early committed step of its own biosynthetic pathway. For instance, bacterial isoleucine can inhibit threonine deaminase, limiting conversion of threonine to α-ketobutyrate. In branched pathways, control must allow one product to be made even if a sibling product is abundant. Distinct isoenzymes or cumulative feedback at branch points can achieve this; aspartokinase regulation in bacteria is a classic family-dependent example. Inhibition of a committed enzyme changes flux, not the underlying equilibrium of the reaction.

Amino acids also serve as precursors beyond proteins. Glutamate is decarboxylated to GABA, histidine to histamine, and tyrosine supplies catecholamine precursors. Glycine and succinyl-CoA initiate heme synthesis, while glycine, glutamate and cysteine form glutathione. Methionine becomes S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), an activated methyl donor; serine and glycine contribute to the folate one-carbon pool. These are meaningful links to cellular functions. The mechanisms of neurotransmission and detailed vitamin metabolism will be treated in their respective nodes rather than repeated here.

16. Integrated nitrogen and carbon homeostasis: evidence and limitations

After a protein-containing meal, absorbed amino acids support protein synthesis and formation of specialised metabolites. Insulin can favour amino acid uptake and protein synthesis, whereas the amino acid supply itself can stimulate both insulin and glucagon, helping maintain glucose availability. Excess amino acids are not simply stored intact. Their nitrogen is disposed of and their carbon skeletons enter pathways according to energy and hormonal state. In a fed organism, carbon may contribute to lipid synthesis or oxidation; in fasting, glucogenic amino acids can sustain hepatic glucose production.

Early fasting increases the need to maintain blood glucose for tissues with obligatory or substantial glucose requirements, such as red blood cells. Muscle provides alanine and glutamine, liver performs gluconeogenesis and ureagenesis, and kidney contributes to nitrogen and acid–base handling. As fasting continues and the brain uses more ketone bodies, glucose requirement falls and protein can be spared relative to early fasting. Fatty acid oxidation supplies much of the energy needed for hepatic metabolism. This explains the relationship with 1.F.01 and 1.F.02 without treating amino acids as merely another fat-like fuel.

Consider three simultaneous measurements in an illustrative perfused liver experiment. With alanine alone the liver consumes alanine and produces some urea and glucose; with alanine plus a fatty acid substrate it produces more glucose if energy supply was limiting; with an ETC inhibitor it produces less ATP and may reduce both glucose and urea formation. Such an experiment demonstrates dependence on energy supply, but the data alone do not prove that fatty acid carbon became glucose. To test carbon origin one needs position-specific isotope tracing and appropriate controls. In mammalian metabolism, even-chain fatty acid acetyl-CoA cannot supply net glucose, although it can power glucose synthesis from alanine.

In a short tracer experiment, administer [U-¹³C₃,¹⁵N]alanine. Transamination separates the labels: ¹³C₃ pyruvate retains alanine carbon and ¹⁵N glutamate receives its nitrogen. If two fully labelled pyruvate molecules become glucose without isotope dilution or exchange, the glucose is ¹³C₆. If only one is labelled, the idealised product is ¹³C₃ glucose. The nitrogen label can appear in urea through either ammonia or aspartate, depending on further reactions. A ¹⁵N-labelled urea molecule alone does not identify which entry route was used; position-specific precursor labelling and measurements of intermediates strengthen inference.

A pathway map is a statement of biochemical capacity, not a measurement of rate. Pools may remain constant while flux rises, and a metabolite may accumulate because synthesis increased or consumption decreased. Measuring a high glutamine concentration does not alone distinguish increased GS activity from decreased glutaminase activity. A good Part C answer therefore combines reaction stoichiometry, compartment, direction of flux, enzyme regulation and experimental assumptions before reaching a conclusion. This reasoning is transferable to nucleotide pathways in the next node.

17. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason questions

1. Which reaction releases no free ammonium?

A. Glutaminase; B. ALT; C. GDH oxidative direction; D. Asparaginase.

Answer and reasoning: B. ALT transfers the α-amino group to α-ketoglutarate. A and D hydrolyse an amide; C oxidatively releases the α-amino nitrogen.

2. Which cofactor form retains donor nitrogen after the first aminotransferase half-reaction?

A. NADH; B. PLP internal aldimine; C. PMP; D. FADH₂.

Answer and reasoning: C. PMP is the aminated cofactor. PLP is restored after the second half-reaction; NADH and FADH₂ are redox carriers rather than the amino carrier.

3. Which is the obligatory allosteric activator of hepatic CPS I?

A. PRPP; B. NAG; C. UTP; D. Citrulline.

Answer and reasoning: B. NAG activates CPS I. PRPP and UTP are relevant to nucleotide pathway regulation; citrulline is produced downstream.

4. The second nitrogen of urea is supplied directly by which molecule?

A. Ornithine; B. Aspartate; C. Fumarate; D. Bicarbonate.

Answer and reasoning: B. ASS1 incorporates aspartate amino nitrogen. Ornithine is regenerated; fumarate is a carbon product; bicarbonate provides the carbonyl carbon.

5. Which pair is exclusively ketogenic?

A. Leucine and lysine; B. Leucine and valine; C. Isoleucine and lysine; D. Phe and Tyr.

Answer and reasoning: A. Leu and Lys yield only ketogenic entry products. Val is glucogenic, Ile and both aromatic amino acids listed are dual.

6. Which cofactor is required for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase?

A. Biotin; B. PLP; C. Adenosylcobalamin; D. BH₄.

Answer and reasoning: C. B₁₂ coenzyme enables rearrangement. Biotin is used in the preceding carboxylation; PLP in transfer chemistry; BH₄ in aromatic hydroxylation.

7. Which change is expected after a common BCKDH block?

A. Only leucine accumulates; B. All three BCAA keto acids accumulate; C. Only aspartate accumulates; D. Urea carbon is lost.

Answer and reasoning: B. The enzyme is shared by Leu, Ile and Val keto acids; the other answers confuse downstream divergence or unrelated pathways.

8. Which amino acid is essential in humans but derives from the ribose-phosphate family in biosynthetic organisms?

A. Histidine; B. Alanine; C. Glycine; D. Glutamate.

Answer and reasoning: A. His uses PRPP and ATP in organisms possessing the pathway. The other three are synthesised from central intermediates in humans.

9. Assertion: ALT can support alanine formation in muscle and consumption in liver. Reason: the net reaction is reversible and local concentrations affect direction.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, reason does not explain; C. Assertion true only; D. Reason true only.

Answer and reasoning: A. Different metabolite demands change reaction quotient and pull the same reversible reaction in different directions.

10. Assertion: Urea formation directly consumes four ATP molecules. Reason: conversion of ATP to AMP costs two phosphate equivalents.

A. Both true; B. Both false; C. Assertion true only; D. Reason true only.

Answer and reasoning: D. Three ATP molecules are used directly; four is the high-energy phosphate equivalent count. The reason is true.

11. Assertion: Tyrosine can become conditionally essential in phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency. Reason: normal tyrosine synthesis can use phenylalanine.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion only; D. Reason only.

Answer and reasoning: A. Loss of conversion removes endogenous supply of tyrosine through that route, making the dietary requirement conditional.

18. Part C: quantitative and analytical problems

1. A tissue oxidises 8 mmol alanine and transfers all its nitrogen to urea. What minimum number of urea molecules in mmol and gross phosphate-equivalent cost result?

Assume all nitrogen is disposed of as urea, with no change in intermediate pools.

Answer and reasoning: Each alanine supplies one N atom. Two N per urea gives 4 mmol urea. Direct ATP = 12 mmol; equivalent cost = 16 mmol. Counting eight urea molecules would double nitrogen output.

2. At pH 7.25 and pKₐ 9.25, calculate the fraction of total ammonia present as NH₃. Compare pH 8.25.

Assume the Henderson–Hasselbalch approximation applies.

Answer and reasoning: Ratios are 10⁻² = 0.01 and 10⁻¹ = 0.1. Fractions are 0.01/1.01 = 0.990% and 0.1/1.1 = 9.09%. A tenfold ratio increase is not exactly a tenfold fraction increase.

3. A person consumes 90 g protein per day, excretes 13 g urinary N and loses another 2 g N daily. Estimate balance.

Use 6.25 g protein per g N.

Answer and reasoning: Intake 14.4 g N; total loss 15 g; balance −0.6 g N/day. Equivalent protein loss ≈3.75 g/day if the conversion applies. Ignoring nonurinary loss incorrectly suggests positive balance.

4. A sample produces 2.5 mmol urea, and fumarate-derived NADH is oxidised with P/O = 2.5. Calculate gross and conditional net ATP equivalents.

Assume one recoverable NADH per urea and no other energy adjustments.

Answer and reasoning: Gross 2.5 × 4 = 10 mmol equivalents; recovery 2.5 × 2.5 = 6.25 mmol; conditional net 3.75 mmol equivalents. This does not change direct consumption of 7.5 mmol ATP.

5. A liver receives two alanines per glucose formed and eliminates both nitrogen atoms as urea. Compute gross energetic requirements.

Ignore shuttle costs and redox recovery; state NADH separately.

Answer and reasoning: Two pyruvates need 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH; urea requires 3 ATP molecules, one to AMP. Total direct nucleotide triphosphates = 9; total high-energy equivalents = 10, plus 2 NADH for gluconeogenesis.

6. A preparation receives [amide-¹⁵N]glutamine, unlabelled aspartate and functional glutaminase, CPS I and downstream enzymes. Which immediate urea atom may be labelled?

Assume no subsequent exchange before incorporation.

Answer and reasoning: Glutaminase releases ¹⁵NH₄⁺; CPS I introduces that nitrogen into carbamoyl phosphate and ultimately urea. The α-amino nitrogen on glutamate is initially unlabelled. The label is not a carbon label.

7. Two cell lines with impaired ureagenesis show low citrulline. X has high orotate; Y does not. Which defect is more likely in X?

Assume equal nitrogen delivery and normal downstream pyrimidine enzymes.

Answer and reasoning: OTC loss in X: carbamoyl phosphate forms but is not efficiently consumed, enabling pyrimidine overflow. Y is compatible with CPS I or NAGS impairment; the measurements alone cannot discriminate those two.

8. An auxotroph grows on downstream metabolite B or product C but not precursor A, in pathway A → B → C. Locate the defect.

Assume normal uptake and a single complete block.

Answer and reasoning: A → B is blocked. B bypasses that step and C bypasses the entire pathway. A B → C defect would not be rescued by B. Failure of uptake would make the inference less secure.

9. An ALT experiment has Q = 4, K′ = 1 at 310 K. Compute ΔG and predict direction.

Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Answer and reasoning: ΔG = RT ln(Q/K′) = 8.314 × 310 × 1.386 = 3.57 kJ/mol. The forward reaction is unfavourable at that composition; the reverse is favoured. Enzyme concentration changes rate, not ΔG.

10. With ideal ping-pong kinetics, fixed [B] rises while [A] varies. What happens to Lineweaver–Burk slope and intercept?

Use the equation derived in the PLP section.

Answer and reasoning: Slope KA/Vmax stays constant; intercept (1 + KB/[B])/Vmax decreases as B rises. Parallel lines are consistent with a modified enzyme intermediate, unlike the usual intersecting sequential pattern.

11. A liver is supplied only ¹³C-bicarbonate and unlabelled aspartate and ammonia. A short pulse produces labelled urea. Where is the label?

Ignore later redistribution.

Answer and reasoning: The only urea carbon comes from bicarbonate via CPS I, so the carbonyl carbon is labelled. Aspartate carbon initially leaves as fumarate. There is no labelled nitrogen in the supplied substrates.

12. A mutant lacks propionyl-CoA carboxylase. Compare valine, leucine and isoleucine carbon utilisation.

Assume other steps remain intact.

Answer and reasoning: Valine's succinyl-CoA route is impaired. Isoleucine's propionyl branch is impaired while its acetyl-CoA branch may remain available. Leucine is exclusively ketogenic and does not depend on this propionyl route.

13. A tissue has high glutamine concentration but constant extracellular ammonia. Does this prove GS flux has increased?

Give at least two alternatives.

Answer and reasoning: No. Glutamine may accumulate from increased synthesis, decreased glutaminase, reduced export or increased uptake. Steady ammonia can reflect balanced inputs/outputs. Tracer incorporation or flux measurements with controls are required.

14. A fasting liver oxidises even-chain fatty acids and converts labelled alanine carbon to glucose. A student concludes fat supplies net glucose carbon. Evaluate.

Distinguish energy source from carbon source.

Answer and reasoning: The conclusion is unsupported. Fat oxidation supplies ATP and reducing power; alanine supplies gluconeogenic carbon. Acetyl-CoA does not yield net glucose in mammals. Label transfer from acetyl-CoA under exchange does not demonstrate net carbon production.

15. A GS inhibitor is added to a tissue exposed to ammonium. Predict glutamine formation and possible alternative fate.

Assume no compensatory enzyme induction during the observation.

Answer and reasoning: GS-dependent incorporation falls; free ammonium may accumulate or be transferred to another tissue. Glutaminase is not the reverse GS enzyme and cannot simply synthesise glutamine. Other nitrogen pathways may change the size of the effect.

19. Consolidation: follow the atoms, not just the names

Begin a revision map with the free amino acid pool, then split each substrate into its nitrogen and carbon destinies. Draw glutamate in the middle of the nitrogen map and connect ALT, AST and GDH. Add glutamine synthetase and glutaminase with arrows in opposite tissue contexts, and place alanine between muscle and liver. Draw the mitochondrial boundary across the urea cycle: CPS I and OTC on one side; ASS1, ASL and arginase on the other. Mark one nitrogen entering as ammonia, one as aspartate and one carbon from bicarbonate. Regenerate ornithine and return fumarate carbon through malate and oxaloacetate.

On the carbon side, connect seven entry products to central metabolism. Check why leucine and lysine cannot supply net glucose, why propionyl-CoA can, and how an amino acid can provide both ketogenic and glucogenic products. Finally reverse your attention from catabolism to construction: locate the six precursor families and distinguish microbial and plant capacities from human dietary requirements. This completes a connected picture rather than a memorised catalogue.
