1. Recognition is not the same as an immune response

Imagine a protein on the surface of a bacterium that has entered a vertebrate host. The protein is an object that can be recognized in several different ways. An innate receptor might detect a chemical pattern shared among many bacteria. A B lymphocyte might bind a small surface feature of this particular protein, whereas a conventional T lymphocyte might recognize a fragment derived from the protein only after another cell has displayed that fragment in association with a major histocompatibility complex molecule. All three recognition events concern the same biological object, but the information available to each receptor is different. The adaptive system exploits these differences so that a cell can select a target, generate a response and direct appropriate effector mechanisms against that target.

An antigen is a molecular structure that is specifically recognized by an adaptive immune receptor. For an antibody or B-cell receptor, recognition involves direct interaction with a chemical surface on a molecule or molecular assembly. For most conventional alpha-beta T-cell receptors, the recognized surface is a complex formed by an antigen-derived peptide and a major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule. The definition therefore does not require the antigen to be a whole pathogen, a foreign protein or even a molecule capable of inducing an immune response by itself. One bacterium carries numerous molecular antigens, and a single protein antigen can provide multiple distinct sites of recognition. Conversely, a small chemical group can provide a single recognizable structure even when it has little ability to stimulate antibody production independently.

Antigenicity describes the capacity for specific recognition or binding by the relevant receptor or antibody. Immunogenicity describes the capacity to induce an adaptive immune response in a specified host under specified conditions. These are logically related but experimentally different properties. To demonstrate antigenicity one might expose an existing antibody to a candidate molecule and measure the amount of complex formed. To demonstrate immunogenicity one must establish that exposure has stimulated an appropriate adaptive response, for example by comparing antigen-specific antibody levels before and after exposure while including appropriate controls. The second observation depends on many cellular events that are absent from the first assay. A positive binding reaction therefore does not establish that the bound molecule can independently activate naive lymphocytes.

The distinction also applies to the host's own molecules. An antibody raised in one species against a conserved cellular protein can recognize a similar protein in a second species. The ability to bind the protein is antigenicity. Whether the protein stimulates a new response in an intact animal depends on the history and state of its lymphocyte population. The immune system ordinarily limits potentially harmful responses to self components through tolerance, not because self proteins lack chemically recognizable surfaces. A self molecule may be an autoantigen if it is recognized by an autoreactive lymphocyte or autoantibody. The existence of such recognition and the occurrence of pathological autoimmunity are separate questions, because the biological response depends on access, activation, regulation and the downstream effector consequences.

It is useful to separate recognition, activation and execution as three levels of analysis. Recognition is a molecular interaction. Activation is a regulated change in the behavior of a cell, including signaling, changes in gene expression and, where appropriate, proliferation or differentiation. Execution is the resulting biological action, such as blocking a toxin, facilitating uptake of bacteria by phagocytes or initiating a complement reaction. A soluble antibody can perform recognition without undergoing cellular activation itself. It can nevertheless recruit cells that execute a response through receptors for its constant region. This separation explains the modular architecture that we will examine after learning the nature of the antigen.

2. Why some antigens induce responses more readily than others

Immunogenicity is not a single fixed number printed on a molecule. It is an outcome that reflects both the antigen and the responding organism. Two animals exposed to the same purified protein may generate different responses if their MHC alleles favor presentation of different peptides or their prior exposures have left different populations of responsive lymphocytes. The same individual may respond differently to a protein encountered through a damaged barrier than to the protein encountered repeatedly in a non-inflammatory setting. Thus a description of an immunogenic material is always implicitly tied to a dose, route, host and set of biological conditions.

Foreignness is a useful predictor because adaptive lymphocytes capable of attacking normal self components are normally removed, rendered less responsive or restrained. Nevertheless foreignness is not sufficient. A foreign molecule may be physically inaccessible, unstable or incapable of providing the signals needed to activate the relevant cells. Nor is foreignness necessary in every case; immune responses against self determinants occur when protective tolerance mechanisms fail. The relevant variable is not simply chemical difference from a reference host but the availability and activation potential of the corresponding lymphocyte population. A highly conserved foreign protein may contain only a few surfaces or peptides sufficiently different from self to provide effective targets in one host.

Molecular size often correlates with the probability that a material can display multiple B-cell epitopes and provide peptides for T-cell help. Large proteins can contain numerous folded surfaces and processed peptide sequences. Many small soluble chemicals contain no peptide sequence at all and do not supply conventional helper T-cell epitopes. However, size is a trend rather than an absolute boundary. A large homogeneous synthetic polymer may offer limited chemical variety or be poorly processed, whereas a short peptide appropriately presented in an inflammatory context can induce a response. A numerical molecular weight threshold cannot distinguish all immunogens from all non-immunogens. The relevant questions concern accessible recognition sites, processing and accompanying activation signals.

Chemical complexity affects the diversity of potential interactions. A folded protein provides polar, charged and nonpolar groups in a spatial arrangement shaped by its amino acid sequence. A complex carbohydrate can display repeated sugar linkages and substituents that are absent from host glycans. These structures are recognized differently even when their masses are similar. Repetitive bacterial capsular polysaccharides are important because their regularly spaced features can engage multiple B-cell receptors. Some can stimulate antibody responses without the classical peptide-specific helper T-cell interaction, although the quality and persistence of the response differ from those commonly elicited by protein antigens. We will return to the cellular mechanisms in the chapter devoted to B-cell activation.

Processing is another determinant. Conventional helper T-cell responses generally require that a protein yield fragments that bind the host's MHC class II molecules. A chemically stable protein may contain numerous candidate peptide sequences but release very few appropriate fragments during uptake and degradation. Alternatively, its fragments may be abundant but unsuitable for the particular MHC alleles of the host. In that case B-cell binding to the native protein may still occur while efficient T-cell cooperation is limited. The distinction makes it possible to predict different results from assays that measure native antigen binding and assays that measure cellular responses to processed antigen.

The route by which an antigen is encountered changes the biological surroundings of recognition. A material that remains in the gut lumen may never reach the same populations of antigen-presenting cells as a material entering a tissue through a damaged surface. A healthy mucosal environment can support non-inflammatory immune regulation in response to many harmless substances. Inflammation can instead favor antigen-presenting cell activation, costimulation and subsequent lymphocyte responses. This does not mean that every orally encountered antigen induces tolerance or that every injected antigen induces immunity. Local tissue conditions, the nature of the material, its physical distribution and the state of the responding cells all contribute to the result.

An adjuvant is a component used to enhance or shape an immune response to an accompanying antigen. Some adjuvants promote innate recognition or inflammatory signals; others change the localization or physical presentation of the antigen. These effects can increase the likelihood that the antigen is encountered by suitably activated antigen-presenting cells. It is incorrect to imagine that an adjuvant simply increases the intrinsic affinity of all antibodies for an antigen. Affinity is a property of an individual binding interaction. An adjuvant can alter which cells become activated and how responses develop, but it does not chemically transform an existing antibody into a higher-affinity molecule merely by being present during an assay.

Dose is not a guarantee of a monotonic adaptive response. At a very low dose there may be insufficient antigen available to the appropriate cells. At other doses, responsive clones can be stimulated effectively. In some experimental settings prolonged or very large antigen exposure without appropriate signals can promote reduced responsiveness rather than stronger immunity. Clearance, persistence and tissue injury may also vary with dose. Therefore a graph of the amount of antigen administered against antibody production cannot be interpreted as a simple binding curve: it contains several biological processes, each of which may have its own concentration and time dependence. The shape must be established experimentally rather than assumed.

Immunodominance is the unequal contribution of different antigenic determinants to an immune response. If a protein contains ten distinguishable surfaces, a polyclonal antiserum may bind strongly to only a few. Such dominance depends on accessibility, the frequencies and binding properties of available clones, competition and regulatory conditions. For T cells, processing and peptide binding to MHC molecules also affect which determinants become prominent. An immunodominant epitope is not necessarily the most protective one. A pathogen may expose abundant variable structures that attract many antibodies while retaining a less accessible but functionally constrained site. This distinction is important when considering whether a measured response reflects effective interference with the biological activity of an antigen.

3. Haptens, carriers and linked recognition

Small chemical determinants supplied a particularly clear way to distinguish antigenicity from immunogenicity. A hapten is a small molecule that can be specifically recognized by an antibody but ordinarily fails to elicit an effective antibody response on its own. In classic experimental systems the hapten is attached to a larger immunogenic carrier, often a protein. The carrier contributes properties that the free chemical group lacks, including the ability to be processed into peptides recognized by conventional helper T lymphocytes. The conjugate presents the hapten as a B-cell target while simultaneously making carrier-derived peptide information available for cellular cooperation.

Karl Landsteiner's investigations with chemically modified proteins and small chemical determinants helped establish that antibody recognition depends on fine features of molecular structure. Related compounds could differ in their capacity to inhibit a binding reaction, even though they shared a substantial part of their chemical framework. The experimental principle was to generate antibodies against a determinant in a larger immunogenic context and then examine their ability to interact with related structures. Small changes in the position or chemical identity of a substituent could change the strength of recognition. The conceptual advance was that immune specificity could be investigated as molecular chemistry instead of treating the response to a foreign substance as an indivisible property.

Consider a B lymphocyte carrying a receptor that recognizes hapten H. If H is covalently coupled to carrier protein P, binding of the receptor to H can promote uptake of the entire conjugate. Within the cell, protein P can be degraded into peptides, and some of those peptides can be displayed by class II MHC molecules. A previously activated helper T cell specific for a peptide from P can then interact with the B cell and deliver signals that support its response. The B cell retains its specificity for H even though the cooperating T cell recognizes information derived from P. Their targets do not have to be the same epitope; the two recognition events are connected by uptake of the same antigenic material.

This arrangement is called linked recognition. The linkage matters because it gives a B cell that has recognized a surface structure access to the peptide determinants of the associated carrier. If the free hapten and an unrelated protein are merely mixed, a hapten-specific B cell that internalizes hapten does not automatically acquire enough of the protein to display the appropriate helper determinant. The mixture is therefore not equivalent to a covalent conjugate for a classical hapten-carrier experiment. There are other routes of antigen uptake and physical associations other than covalent bonds, so the strongest general statement concerns linkage within the material internalized and processed by the B cell rather than an absolute requirement for one particular chemical bond.

A carrier-specific helper response can be important even when the desired antibodies recognize only the attached carbohydrate or chemical group. This explains the broad rationale of conjugate vaccines against some encapsulated bacteria. A bacterial polysaccharide linked to a carrier protein can permit carbohydrate-specific B cells to obtain help from T cells that recognize protein-derived peptides. The desired antibody can still bind the polysaccharide on the bacterium. The carrier has not replaced the original determinant, but has altered the cellular circumstances under which the determinant is recognized and responded to. The detailed comparison of immunization types belongs later in this subunit.

A simple experiment separates anti-hapten and anti-carrier recognition. After immunization with H coupled to P, the resulting serum can be tested against H linked to a different carrier Q, against unmodified P and against unmodified Q. Binding to H–Q that is absent with Q alone supports the presence of anti-hapten antibodies or antibodies against a shared linkage-dependent determinant. Binding to P alone supports an anti-carrier component. If a soluble free H molecule inhibits binding to H–Q, this further supports recognition of H itself. It remains important to compare the density and accessibility of H on the different conjugates, because equal total protein concentration does not guarantee equal numbers of available hapten sites.

Free hapten is often useful as a competitive inhibitor in a recognition assay because it can occupy the binding site of an anti-hapten antibody without forming a large network. A monovalent hapten occupies only one binding site and cannot by itself connect separate antibody molecules into an extended lattice. Hence a decrease in precipitation after adding the hapten need not indicate chemical destruction of antibody or denaturation of the conjugated antigen. It can result from ordinary reversible competition. This gives an experimental way to study binding strength without assuming that precipitation is itself the first molecular event.

There are also boundaries to the classical hapten model. The way a small molecule is attached to a protein can produce a newly recognizable surface composed partly of the attachment site and neighboring protein residues. An antibody to that composite structure may bind the conjugated form strongly and the free molecule weakly. Small molecules can modify host proteins and create novel determinants in biological settings, but not every small molecule acts through the same pathway. In particular, some unconventional T-cell recognition mechanisms involve nonpeptide ligands or interactions not adequately described by the standard peptide-carrier model. Such exceptions do not alter the fundamental distinction between recognition and immunogenicity, and they should not be used to replace the classical linked recognition mechanism in a question that explicitly describes it.

4. B-cell and T-cell epitopes represent different molecular information

The part of an antigen that contributes directly to receptor recognition is called an epitope or antigenic determinant. Its complementary surface on an antibody is called the paratope. For a folded protein, an epitope often occupies only a small fraction of the molecular surface. Many additional regions may be exposed, and each can be recognized by a different antibody population. It is consequently more precise to describe an antibody as specific for a determinant on a protein than as recognizing every part of that protein. When two antibodies compete for binding, their epitopes may overlap, or the two antibodies may interfere sterically even if the exact contacted residues differ.

A linear or continuous B-cell epitope contains a contiguous sequence of amino acid residues in the primary structure. An antibody against such a region may recognize a synthetic peptide that reproduces that sequence. Yet linear does not mean that three-dimensional conformation is irrelevant. A peptide must adopt a shape and orientation compatible with the antibody binding site, and the matching sequence must be accessible in the intact protein if the antibody is to recognize the native state. A sequence buried inside a tightly folded protein may become available only after unfolding, cleavage or another structural change. Thus the ability to bind a peptide does not automatically predict access to the same sequence in a living cell or intact virus.

A discontinuous or conformational B-cell epitope is formed by residues that may be widely separated in the primary sequence but close together after folding. These residues collectively produce the molecular surface contacted by an antibody. For example, residues 15, 62 and 114 of a hypothetical protein might be brought together by its tertiary structure. A short peptide containing residues 10–20 would reproduce only part of that surface. Denaturation that separates the residues may abolish recognition even though every peptide bond remains intact. Some conformational epitopes also include contributions from separate subunits of an assembled protein, making the quaternary structure important to binding.

These categories are informative but not perfectly exclusive. A contiguous stretch can form a conformation-dependent epitope if the antibody requires its native secondary structure. A discontinuous epitope can occasionally contain one dominant contiguous region that accounts for a significant proportion of binding. A result from a peptide-binding assay must therefore be interpreted with evidence from intact antigen. The experimental question is not merely whether the recognized residues occur consecutively, but whether the antibody recognizes the same accessible spatial arrangement in the tested material.

Changes in glycosylation offer a further example. An antibody can recognize a carbohydrate determinant on a glycoprotein directly, recognize a surface that contains both carbohydrate and polypeptide features, or bind a nearby protein region whose accessibility is altered by the glycan. Removing the glycan can therefore reduce, increase or leave binding unchanged, depending on the interaction. If a glycosidase-treated preparation loses recognition, the researcher must consider whether the glycan was itself part of the epitope or whether the treatment indirectly changed the protein structure. A separate probe for overall folding or an independent antibody against a distant region can help distinguish these possibilities.

The most familiar conventional T-cell epitope is a peptide that is recognized in the context of an MHC molecule. A T-cell receptor usually contacts both exposed residues of the peptide and part of the MHC surface. The peptide alone is not generally sufficient to reproduce the recognized ligand. Some peptide residues act principally as anchors that stabilize association with the MHC groove, while others contribute to the surface contacted by the receptor. A substitution at an anchor can reduce T-cell activation by preventing presentation even if the substituted residue was never directly contacted by the receptor. A substitution at a receptor-facing residue may leave peptide binding to MHC largely intact yet impair recognition.

A B-cell epitope is not required to match a T-cell peptide in the same antigen. A B cell can bind a folded outer surface of a protein, internalize it and present a peptide from a buried region that was never accessible to the B-cell receptor. Conversely, a surface determinant made predominantly of carbohydrate may be recognized by B cells but cannot be assumed to yield a conventional peptide epitope for T cells. The cell that processes the associated protein produces a different kind of molecular signal. This is precisely the distinction exploited by linked recognition.

MHC polymorphism helps explain why T-cell epitope recognition can vary across individuals who encounter the same pathogen. A given peptide may be effectively presented by one MHC allele but not another. In a teaching experiment, two antigen-presenting cell populations can be supplied with the same peptide while expressing different MHC molecules. If a T-cell clone responds only to one population, the result is compatible with MHC-restricted recognition, provided that peptide loading and cell viability are controlled. It does not establish that the antigen cannot be recognized by any T cell in the other individual; different MHC alleles may present other peptides and recruit other clones.

Class I MHC-associated peptides often come from proteins synthesized within cells, whereas class II MHC-associated peptides commonly originate from material taken up from outside cells. These are useful organizing principles for the next chapters, but neither statement is an absolute rule. Dendritic cells can present some extracellular antigens through class I by cross-presentation. Some intracellular constituents can also enter class II processing pathways. Unconventional T-cell subsets recognize nonclassical presenting molecules or other ligands, including lipid-associated recognition by CD1-restricted T cells. The phrase conventional peptide–MHC recognition is therefore a deliberate qualification, not a claim about every T lymphocyte.

To investigate the behavior of a particular antibody experimentally, compare a native folded protein, the same protein after verified unfolding, and a set of peptides spanning its sequence. Strong native binding together with weak binding to both the unfolded protein and the individual peptides supports a conformational determinant, although altered adsorption or nonspecific loss of antigen must be excluded. Binding to one peptide and the unfolded protein suggests a continuous determinant, but additional testing is needed to determine whether the same sequence is available in the native structure. In contrast, to examine conventional T-cell recognition, one should compare presentation of the relevant peptide with suitable MHC and presentation controls, not simply test binding of a T-cell receptor to the native folded protein.

5. How biochemical experiments revealed antibody architecture

The recognition of antigens by circulating proteins was understood before the chemical structure of those proteins was established. Early measurements of antiserum activity showed that a soluble blood component could bind a target specifically. But the binding behavior did not reveal how many chains formed the active molecule, whether its recognition sites were identical or how one protein could both recognize an antigen and recruit other defense mechanisms. Researchers therefore needed to separate biological activity into measurable molecular components. The experiments of Rodney Porter and Gerald Edelman provided complementary answers to these problems and earned them the 1972 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Porter's 1959 study of rabbit gamma globulin used papain to fragment antibody molecules. An enzyme that cuts a polypeptide at susceptible positions is a useful probe of molecular organization because the resulting fragments can be separated and tested independently. The important observation was that two similar fragments retained antigen-binding capacity, whereas another fragment behaved differently and could crystallize. The two binding fragments became known as Fab fragments, and the third as the Fc fragment. The result established a physical separation between the regions responsible for recognizing antigen and the region that supplies many other antibody functions. The separated binding fragments were approximately fifty kilodaltons each, consistent with the approximate mass of a typical intact IgG molecule being about one hundred and fifty kilodaltons.

In an independent approach, Edelman investigated the polypeptide constituents of immunoglobulins by disrupting the disulfide bonds connecting chains. Reduction breaks covalent links between cysteine residues, which can allow subunits to separate under suitable denaturing conditions. The appearance of larger and smaller polypeptides supported the existence of heavy and light chains. This strategy addressed a question different from the proteolytic fragmentation experiment. Papain showed that different molecular regions retained distinct functional properties, whereas chemical reduction showed that the intact protein consisted of multiple polypeptide chains connected by disulfide bonds. Combining these observations permitted a model in which two identical heavy and two identical light chains assemble into a symmetric molecule.

Pepsin digestion provides a useful contrasting probe. Under suitable conditions it cuts IgG below the hinge disulfide bonds, so that the two antigen-binding arms remain connected in an F(ab′)₂ fragment, while much of the Fc region is degraded. The F(ab′)₂ fragment retains bivalency and can cross-link antigen, whereas an individual Fab is monovalent. If a multivalent antigen precipitates with intact IgG and F(ab′)₂ but not with Fab, the simplest explanation is the loss of antibody cross-linking capacity in Fab rather than a loss of specificity. Actual proteolysis products depend on enzyme conditions and antibody subclass; the standard textbook comparison assumes complete generation of the characteristic fragments.

Structural analysis by X-ray crystallography extended the chain and fragment model. A three-dimensional structure can reveal which amino acid loops approach the antigen, how domains pack together and which surfaces are exposed for interaction with other proteins. In a complex with antigen, the contacts provide structural explanations for binding specificity. A static crystal structure does not by itself report the rates of association and dissociation or the full distribution of flexible antibody conformations in solution. These require other types of evidence. The power of the structural model comes from agreement among independent observations, including chain composition, fragmentation patterns, binding experiments and structures of isolated domains or complexes.

A teaching version of the fragmentation experiment can be organized around three preparations made from the same antibody: intact IgG, Fab and F(ab′)₂. Each preparation is tested for binding to a soluble monovalent determinant, bridging two copies of a determinant and interacting with an Fc-binding receptor. If the fragments are pure and properly folded, each retains a binding site, only the intact molecule and F(ab′)₂ possess two sites, and only the intact preparation has the normal Fc region. The three measurements are not redundant: they distinguish molecular recognition, multivalency and communication with effector systems. A negative Fc-receptor result with Fab is thus expected, not evidence that the antibody has lost antigen specificity.

6. The four-chain immunoglobulin molecule

A conventional IgG molecule is built from four polypeptide chains. Two heavy chains are identical to one another in the usual single-specificity antibody, and two light chains are similarly identical. Each heavy chain has an approximate mass of fifty kilodaltons and each light chain approximately twenty-five kilodaltons, although exact masses differ with amino acid sequence, subclass and attached carbohydrates. The total mass is therefore about one hundred and fifty kilodaltons. A molecular mass estimate describes the mass of a single assembled molecular species in daltons; molar mass expresses the corresponding amount in grams per mole. Confusing these units with a concentration in grams per liter can lead to large errors when comparing the number of antibodies in different samples.

The term heavy chain refers to the larger of the two types of polypeptide and does not mean the chain occupies only the lower portion of a Y-shaped diagram. Each heavy chain runs continuously from the tip of an arm into the stem. Each light chain runs beside the amino-terminal region of a heavy chain and ends before the stem. Thus each arm contains contributions from one heavy and one light chain. A covalent disulfide bond ordinarily joins the light chain to its associated heavy chain, and further disulfide bonds connect the heavy chains, particularly in the hinge region of IgG. Numerous noncovalent interactions also stabilize the assembly. Breaking a particular covalent bond does not necessarily immediately dissociate all domains under native conditions because the noncovalent interactions can persist.

Each light chain has a variable domain (VL) and a constant domain (CL). A typical IgG heavy chain has one variable domain (VH) and three constant domains, CH1, CH2 and CH3, with a hinge between CH1 and CH2. A domain is a structural unit within a polypeptide that has a characteristic folded organization. Domain and chain are not interchangeable terms: the heavy chain is one continuous polypeptide containing several domains. In a standard IgG the four chains collectively contain twelve immunoglobulin domains, eight from the two heavy chains and four from the two light chains. Four of those twelve domains are variable domains. The number of antigen-binding sites is nevertheless two, because each site is formed by the cooperation of one VH and one VL domain.

The variable regions differ substantially in sequence among antibodies with different binding properties. Constant regions are comparatively conserved within a particular immunoglobulin class or subclass and provide interaction surfaces and structural properties characteristic of that class. Constant does not mean absolutely invariant in all individuals or across all species. Genetic polymorphism and differences between isotypes are important examples of variation. Nor does the term variable region imply that every amino acid in that region changes between antibodies. Most of the folded domain requires a reasonably conserved framework, with greater diversity concentrated in particular loops that form the antigen-recognition surface.

The two light-chain types in humans are kappa and lambda. A conventional antibody normally contains either two kappa chains or two lambda chains, not one of each. Both can be associated with heavy chains of any of the five major human immunoglobulin classes. It is the heavy-chain constant region, rather than the light-chain type, that defines whether an antibody is called IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE or IgD. A question that describes a light-chain replacement must therefore be considered in terms of potential changes in assembly or recognition, not an automatic change in heavy-chain isotype. There are specialized antibodies with unusual architectures in some vertebrates, so the two-identical-light-chain statement describes the conventional human molecule.

The standard immunoglobulin fold is based on two beta sheets packed against one another. Conserved hydrophobic residues help stabilize the internal core, and an intradomain disulfide bond provides additional structural support in typical domains. The edges of the beta strands are connected by loops. In variable domains some of these loops have a direct role in recognition. The architecture demonstrates a general principle of protein evolution: a conserved scaffold can tolerate variation at selected surface positions without losing the overall folding pattern. Changes in buried framework residues may destabilize the domain, whereas some changes in exposed loops can alter binding specificity while preserving the fold.

Disulfide bonds operate at two structural levels. Intrachain disulfides form between two cysteine residues within a single polypeptide and stabilize the folded domains. Interchain disulfides join separate heavy or light chains. A reducing reagent that reaches the bonds may disrupt both categories, depending on conditions. Under reducing and denaturing electrophoresis an ordinary IgG yields heavy-chain and light-chain bands because the intact covalent assembly is broken. Under nonreducing denaturing conditions, many antibodies retain their interchain disulfides and can migrate largely as intact covalent molecules. A gel band is not a direct count of all domains; migration reports the size and behavior of the polypeptides or covalently associated assemblies under the selected conditions.

Glycosylation also contributes to the properties of the assembled molecule. Human IgG normally bears conserved N-linked carbohydrate at an asparagine in each CH2 domain. These glycans influence Fc architecture and the ability to interact with certain Fc receptors. Altering their composition can affect effector activity without changing the amino acid sequence of the antigen-binding site. Glycosylation elsewhere, including in variable regions of some antibodies, can also influence accessibility or binding. Consequently a mutation or processing change that affects glycosylation should not automatically be interpreted as a modification of the antigen epitope: the change may occur on the antibody itself and affect recognition, effector action or both.

The familiar Y-shaped representation is an average conceptual diagram, not a rigid three-dimensional object. The hinge and other flexible domain junctions permit movements of the arms relative to the stem and to one another. This flexibility allows the two sites to accommodate antigenic determinants at different orientations and separations. At the same time there are limits imposed by chain length, steric obstruction and the energetic costs of adopting particular configurations. A repeated epitope can be present at a density that is high in chemical terms but still poorly arranged for simultaneous binding by both arms. The difference between antigen density and usable geometry is essential to understanding avidity.

7. From variable-domain sequence to a specific paratope

A binding site is generated where the VH and VL domains come together. Each variable domain contains three especially variable loop regions known as complementarity-determining regions, commonly abbreviated CDR1, CDR2 and CDR3. Together the six principal loops contribute to the antigen-recognition surface of one conventional antibody arm. The surrounding framework supports their positions and shapes. An intact conventional IgG therefore contains two copies of this composite recognition surface, one at each arm, rather than six or twelve independent sites. The distinction between a loop contributing to a site and an entire binding site is particularly important in structural-labeling questions.

Different numbering schemes define the precise boundaries of CDRs in somewhat different ways. For NET and SET reasoning the essential point is that hypervariable surface loops are enriched in residues important for binding. It is too strong to claim that only residues formally assigned to CDRs can influence antigen recognition. Framework residues can affect loop positioning, domain stability or, in some cases, contact antigen directly. Likewise, not every residue within a CDR necessarily contributes equally to binding energy. Some side chains make important contact, others help position neighboring side chains, and some may be largely solvent exposed. Functional importance must be established by perturbation or structural evidence rather than inferred solely from sequence variability.

A small molecule may bind within a relatively deep pocket between the variable domains. A peptide can associate with a groove or a more open surface, and an antibody against a protein often recognizes a broad irregular patch on that protein. These are useful geometric patterns, not an absolute classification of paratope shape. The epitope can include charges, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, nonpolar surfaces and protruding groups. Complementarity involves a three-dimensional arrangement of these features. Two objects can be geometrically similar while having incompatible electrostatic or hydration properties, just as two chemically similar groups may fail to bind if their spatial orientation differs.

The fact that both heavy and light variable domains contribute explains why replacing one light chain can alter antigen recognition even when the heavy-chain sequence is unchanged. The new VL can change the shape of the interface, remove a contacting residue or alter the orientation of the VH loops. A resulting antibody may recognize the same antigen with reduced affinity, acquire a different cross-reactivity or fail to fold properly. None of these possibilities can be decided from heavy-chain class alone. The generation of natural antibody diversity through selection and rearrangement of these sequences will be considered in the next chapter. Here we are concerned with the structural consequence of combining two variable domains.

CDR3 of the heavy chain often contributes strongly to the center of the binding surface and can vary extensively in sequence and length. This property helps explain its importance in the diversity of recognition. However, ranking CDR3 as always the sole determinant of specificity would be misleading. Some antibodies depend heavily on contacts made by the other heavy-chain CDRs or by the light-chain loops. Antigens differ in size, shape and chemistry, and individual antibodies use distinct combinations of contacts. The structure of a complex and the effects of mutations are more informative than a universal rule about one loop.

An antibody and its antigen need not behave as perfectly rigid pieces. Prior to binding, each molecular partner samples a set of conformations determined by its internal motions and interactions with the solvent. Binding can favor a conformation already present or induce small adjustments after initial contact. The observed complex reflects a balance between favorable intermolecular interactions and the cost of adopting that arrangement. This is why a static lock-and-key drawing is useful for introducing specificity but inadequate as a complete physical explanation. Flexibility makes recognition possible for molecules that are not perfectly complementary in a single unbound snapshot, while energetic and steric constraints still limit the range of accepted structures.

An instructive prediction follows when two antibodies recognize overlapping but distinct epitopes. If antibody A binds a region shared with antibody B, simultaneous occupation may be impossible because the contacting surfaces overlap. Even if the residues contacted by the antibodies are not identical, the molecular bodies may collide. Competition between antibodies thus establishes interference under the tested conditions; it does not alone map every contact residue. Conversely, two antibodies that bind the same protein simultaneously may recognize sufficiently separated regions, or the antigen may be multimeric and offer separate copies of the same region. The topology of the antigen must be known before the competition pattern can be used to assign epitope relationships.

8. Fab, Fc and the division of immune labor

The antigen-binding region of one arm of IgG includes the entire associated light chain and the amino-terminal VH and CH1 portions of the heavy chain. This is the Fab region. The stem of the IgG diagram is formed by the remaining carboxy-terminal heavy-chain domains, primarily CH2 and CH3, and constitutes the major Fc region. The hinge connects the recognition and effector portions, allowing the arms to orient toward the antigen while the stem remains available to other molecules. This architecture separates the identification of a target from the means of acting against it, enabling different antibodies to employ related effector mechanisms despite recognizing entirely different antigens.

Fc receptors on immune cells bind characteristic surfaces of antibody constant regions. An antibody attached to a bacterial surface through Fab exposes Fc regions that can be recognized by receptors on phagocytic cells. Multiple simultaneous interactions can cluster activating Fc receptors and promote uptake. The antibody has therefore converted a molecular pattern on a bacterium into a signal that can be read by a host cell without requiring that cell to have a specific receptor for every microbial antigen. This is a form of opsonization. Opsonization facilitates recognition and uptake; it does not imply that the antibody itself physically engulfs the bacterium or catalyzes all subsequent killing reactions.

Not all Fc receptors have the same effects. Some participate in activation, others can contribute to inhibitory feedback, and specialized receptors help transport or preserve immunoglobulins. For example, the inhibitory Fc gamma receptor FcγRIIB can dampen B-cell responses when appropriately co-engaged with the B-cell antigen receptor. Fc-mediated interactions therefore provide both recruitment of defense functions and regulation. A mutation that disrupts one Fc receptor interaction may diminish a specific effector process while leaving other processes intact. The consequences depend on the receptor, immunoglobulin subclass, cell type and surrounding signals.

Complement provides a second major example. A complement component such as C1q can recognize appropriately clustered Fc regions on certain antigen-bound antibodies. IgM and several IgG subclasses are particularly important in classical complement activation. The arrangement of antigen-bound antibodies matters because efficient triggering requires compatible geometry for the initiation complex. Merely adding soluble unbound IgG does not normally activate the pathway strongly. The resulting complement cascade can produce deposition of opsonins, inflammatory signals and membrane attack in suitable targets, as described in the preceding chapter. Fab supplies target recognition; Fc supplies a connection to the complement pathway.

Neutralization differs because in many circumstances antigen binding itself can directly block a biological interaction. A toxin may need to recognize a cell-surface receptor before entering a susceptible cell. An antibody attached to the toxin at the relevant surface can interfere with that binding. A Fab fragment may retain this direct neutralization capacity even in the absence of Fc. Other neutralization situations benefit from bivalency, cross-linking or Fc-mediated clearance, so the presence of a functional Fab does not establish that the fragment will be as effective as the intact antibody in every context. The critical experiment compares equal accessible binding-site concentrations and observes the particular biological process that is being inhibited.

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, or ADCC, illustrates a further separation of recognition from execution. An NK cell can express FcγRIIIa, also known as CD16a, which binds Fc regions of certain IgG antibodies on a target cell. Engagement and clustering of the receptor can stimulate cytotoxic activity against the coated cell. The NK cell does not need a clonally rearranged antigen receptor specific for the exact molecular epitope recognized by the antibody. Antibody recognition supplies targeting, while the NK cell contributes the machinery for cytotoxicity. This should be distinguished from the conventional cytotoxic T-cell receptor recognition of a peptide–MHC complex.

Membrane-bound immunoglobulin and secreted immunoglobulin share the same fundamental recognition architecture but differ in their cellular context. Membrane immunoglobulin contains a membrane-anchoring region and associates with the signaling proteins Igα and Igβ, also designated CD79a and CD79b. Together these form the B-cell receptor complex. Secreted antibody lacks the membrane anchor characteristic of the receptor form and can act at sites remote from the cell that produced it. The membrane form recognizes antigen and participates in initiating a cellular response, whereas the soluble form can directly bind extracellular targets and recruit other immune mechanisms. The same specificity can therefore be deployed as a sensor on a lymphocyte or as an effector molecule released by its differentiated descendants.

It is useful to distinguish isotype, allotype and idiotype before examining individual antibody classes. Isotype refers to class and subclass differences shared as part of the immunoglobulin repertoire of a species, especially the differences in heavy-chain constant regions. Allotype refers to inherited allelic differences between individuals within a species in immunoglobulin sequences, often in constant regions. Idiotype refers to the collection of distinctive antigenic features associated with the variable region of a particular immunoglobulin, termed idiotopes individually. An idiotope may lie in or near the antigen-binding site, but idiotype is not simply another word for the antigen epitope. One antibody molecule can be an antigen for another antibody directed against its immunoglobulin features.

An anti-isotype reagent directed against a human IgG constant-region feature can recognize many different human IgG molecules regardless of the original antigens they bind. An anti-allotype reagent can distinguish an inherited immunoglobulin variant under suitable conditions. An anti-idiotypic reagent may distinguish antibodies with particular variable-region features even when their heavy-chain class is the same. These differences are conceptually useful for interpreting the results of secondary detection. For example, detection of bound primary antibody by an anti-IgG reagent reveals the class of the primary antibody being detected, not the identity of the original antigen by itself. Appropriate negative controls are required to demonstrate that the primary antibody, rather than the secondary detection reagent, establishes antigen specificity.

9. IgM and IgG: multivalent recognition and systemic defense

The immunoglobulin classes are named according to the heavy-chain constant regions. Human IgM contains mu heavy chains, IgG gamma chains, IgA alpha chains, IgE epsilon chains and IgD delta chains. In a conventional antibody the corresponding variable domains provide antigen recognition in each class. Variation among constant regions determines domain number, polymerization tendencies, receptor interactions and tissue distribution. This allows the immune system to express related recognition information in molecular formats that behave differently. The genetic mechanism by which activated B cells can change heavy-chain constant-region usage is addressed in the next node; the present chapter emphasizes what those molecular formats accomplish.

Membrane IgM is a monomeric immunoglobulin unit associated with signaling partners on the B cell. It is bivalent in the conventional form, possessing two antigen-binding sites. Secreted IgM is commonly a pentamer containing five immunoglobulin units and a joining chain, or J chain, which helps organize polymer assembly. Each unit contributes two potential binding sites, giving a typical pentamer ten sites. Secreted hexameric IgM lacking J chain can also occur. The phrase ten sites describes the theoretical number of recognition sites in the conventional pentamer; it does not guarantee that ten separate epitopes on a particular antigen are simultaneously occupied.

IgM heavy chains have four constant domains rather than the three found in conventional IgG. IgM lacks the classic IgG hinge but retains other structural flexibility. Its large polymeric form is particularly useful for binding repetitive determinants such as those displayed on some bacterial surfaces. If one site detaches, other occupied sites may retain the molecule nearby, favoring rebinding. This can result in a stable overall association despite moderate or relatively low affinity of the individual interactions. The property is especially useful early in a humoral response before selection has produced populations of highly refined binding sites, though high-affinity IgM and later IgM responses also exist.

Secreted IgM is efficient at initiating classical complement under appropriate antigen-bound conditions. Binding to a surface can arrange the polymer so that C1q can engage exposed Fc features productively. In contrast, ordinary soluble pentameric IgM circulating without appropriate target engagement is not simply an uncontrolled initiator of complement. The combination of polymeric architecture and conditional recognition protects against indiscriminate activation while providing effective recruitment when repeated antigen is encountered. A single properly engaged IgM pentamer may provide an activation geometry that generally requires several nearby antigen-bound IgG molecules, but this comparison should not be interpreted as a guarantee independent of surface organization and subclass.

Because of its large size, IgM is concentrated primarily within the vascular compartment relative to smaller antibody classes, although it is also present at other sites and can be transported into secretions in appropriate polymeric forms. The size advantage for multivalent recognition thus entails a tradeoff: the same molecular assembly that provides many sites can influence movement through tissue spaces. A high serum concentration is not equivalent to high exposure at every anatomical site. Biological effectiveness depends on the distribution of the antibody, the location of the target and the effector molecules available there.

IgG is typically a monomer with two binding sites and is the major immunoglobulin class in normal human serum by mass. Its comparatively small size facilitates distribution into extracellular fluids. Humans possess four IgG subclasses, IgG1 through IgG4, differing in hinge properties and constant-region features. They do not all activate complement or bind Fc receptors with equal effectiveness. IgG3 and IgG1 are generally effective activators of classical complement, whereas IgG4 is generally ineffective through this route. IgG2 can activate complement under some conditions, especially when antigen arrangement is favorable. Subclass differences illustrate why a broad statement about IgG function may require qualification when an experimental question identifies a particular subclass.

The neonatal Fc receptor, FcRn, contributes to the persistence of IgG. IgG taken into acidic intracellular compartments can bind FcRn and be protected from degradation, then be returned to extracellular conditions where the interaction weakens and IgG is released. This pH-dependent binding cycle helps account for the prolonged persistence of many IgG molecules relative to several other antibody classes. FcRn also contributes to maternal IgG transfer across the human placenta. These activities involve interaction with Fc rather than recognition of the foreign antigen. A change that selectively disrupts FcRn binding can therefore alter the distribution or persistence of an antibody even if its Fab affinity remains normal.

Maternal transfer of IgG illustrates passive immunity. The recipient obtains preformed recognition molecules without necessarily having generated the corresponding adaptive lymphocyte response. Protection may persist while the transferred antibodies remain sufficiently abundant, but their presence is not evidence that the recipient has established antigen-specific memory. In a developmental or physiological comparison, maternal antibody transfer also differs among mammalian species. Human placental transfer is a useful representative example, not a universal rule that every species acquires maternal antibodies through the same tissue or route.

A conventional IgG4 antibody can undergo a phenomenon called Fab-arm exchange, generating molecules with two different antigen-binding arms under physiological conditions. This is a significant exception to the simple statement that all IgG molecules always carry two identical paratopes. For standard examination problems, an unspecified conventional single-specificity IgG is assumed to have two equivalent recognition sites unless the question states otherwise. The exception demonstrates why biological models are best treated as well-defined reference cases. A feature of one subclass should not be generalized to all antibodies, just as the properties of a pentameric secreted IgM should not be assigned to membrane IgM.

10. IgA, IgE and IgD: defense in distinct biological environments

The properties of IgA illustrate the importance of the location at which antibody action is needed. Mucosal epithelia separate internal tissues from the contents of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and other exposed environments. A protective response here should intercept many potential pathogens without necessarily producing intense inflammation throughout the epithelial barrier. Antibody that can be delivered to the luminal side of the epithelium is particularly useful. IgA has evolved molecular forms and transport relationships that support this type of defense.

Human serum IgA is predominantly monomeric, whereas secretory IgA is commonly formed from two immunoglobulin units associated with a J chain and a component derived from an epithelial transport receptor. The dimer has four theoretical antigen-binding sites. Polymerized IgA produced by plasma cells near a mucosal surface can associate with the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor, pIgR, on the tissue-facing side of epithelial cells. The receptor-bound complex enters a vesicular transport route across the epithelium. At the luminal surface, cleavage of the receptor releases antibody associated with an extracellular part of pIgR known as the secretory component. This is a classic example of transcytosis, the transfer of material across a polarized cell through intracellular compartments.

The J chain and the secretory component must not be confused. The J chain is a small polypeptide produced by the antibody-producing cell that contributes to the assembly and receptor recognition of polymeric IgA and IgM. The secretory component originates from the epithelial pIgR. Their distinct origins explain why an epithelial receptor defect can reduce the appearance of secretory IgA at a mucosal surface even when local plasma cells produce polymeric IgA normally. Conversely, a defect in appropriate polymer assembly can interfere with the receptor-mediated transport step despite intact epithelial pIgR. A measurement of IgA in blood alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

Secretory IgA can bind microbial surfaces or soluble products and limit their access to the epithelium, a process often referred to as immune exclusion. By blocking attachment or facilitating retention in mucus, antibody can reduce the chance that a microbe or toxin encounters susceptible epithelial cells. The associated secretory component contributes to stability in the protease-rich environment of some secretions. The protective action does not require the antibody to enter every microorganism or directly kill it. Restricting access to a vulnerable tissue can be a successful immune outcome in its own right.

IgA is not simply an antibody that lacks effector functions. In appropriate tissue contexts, IgA-containing complexes can interact with Fc alpha receptors on myeloid cells, including human FcαRI, and influence cellular responses. These interactions differ from the classical complement recruitment typical of appropriately arranged IgM and some IgG subclasses. In general, IgA is not an efficient classical-pathway activator. Its physiological behavior depends on molecular form, anatomical location, receptor expression and the configuration of the antigen–antibody complex. The mucosal protection model should not be stretched into a claim that every IgA molecule is anti-inflammatory in every situation.

Human IgA has two subclasses, IgA1 and IgA2. Differences in their hinge regions influence properties such as susceptibility to certain bacterial proteases. A protease that cleaves the IgA1 hinge can separate recognition regions from other parts of the antibody or interfere with multivalent binding, reducing selected protective effects at mucosal surfaces. IgA2 has a shorter hinge and is resistant to some proteases that cleave IgA1. This illustrates a biologically meaningful structure–function relationship rather than a need to memorize every species-specific protease. The presence of a microbial cleavage mechanism also demonstrates that pathogens can disrupt antibody effectiveness without changing the original antigenic determinant.

IgE has a different specialization. Its heavy chains contain four constant domains, as in IgM, but ordinary secreted IgE is monomeric and bivalent. A characteristic feature is its strong interaction through Fc with the high-affinity receptor FcεRI, which is expressed on mast cells and basophils, among other cell types in appropriate contexts. The receptor can bind monomeric IgE without antigen being present. A mast cell can therefore carry a population of antigen-specific IgE molecules on its surface before encountering the antigen. This arrangement is sometimes described as sensitization, but receptor occupancy alone must be distinguished from the activation that can follow cross-linking.

When an antigen engages suitably positioned receptor-bound IgE molecules and brings FcεRI complexes together, intracellular signaling can lead to release of stored mediators and production of additional mediators. A monovalent ligand that occupies one Fab site without bringing neighboring receptor complexes together may bind the IgE but fail to trigger the same response. A multivalent allergen is more likely to produce the required cross-linking geometry, provided the relevant IgE specificities are present. The physical organization of antigen recognition therefore links the chemistry of the binding site to cellular signal initiation, a principle that also appears in B-cell receptor activation.

IgE participates in immune responses associated with parasitic infections, including helminths, and is central to many immediate allergic reactions. In these settings the consequences of recognition involve multiple cell types, cytokines and effector mechanisms, not a general ability of soluble IgE to kill a parasite directly. A large parasite may be impossible for an individual phagocyte to engulf, making interactions involving extracellular mediators and specialized cells biologically relevant. The same capacity for rapid mediator release can be harmful when directed against otherwise innocuous environmental antigens. The molecular basis of IgE binding does not intrinsically distinguish a protective anti-parasite response from a harmful allergy; the biological setting determines the consequences. The detailed hypersensitivity mechanisms are reserved for their own node.

IgD is found predominantly as a membrane immunoglobulin on many mature naive B cells, often coexpressed with IgM. The two forms can use the same rearranged variable region through alternative RNA processing, allowing the cell to display related recognition specificity with two distinct heavy-chain constant regions. A cell carrying IgM and IgD is not therefore necessarily a cell with two unrelated antigen specificities. IgD is also present in soluble form at relatively low concentrations in human serum. Its precise physiological roles are more context-dependent than the standard functions assigned to IgG, IgA or IgE. The safe textbook conclusion is that IgD has an important role in the repertoire of B-cell surface immunoglobulins, not that it is absent from all secretions or has no possible biological activity.

Across vertebrates, this comparison must be made with attention to evolutionary differences. Birds possess IgY, an important systemic antibody class with functional similarities to mammalian IgG but distinct molecular features. Camelids possess conventional antibodies and naturally occurring heavy-chain-only antibodies, in which an unpaired variable domain can support antigen binding. These examples show that the four-chain human immunoglobulin is a representative solution rather than the only possible architecture for adaptive humoral recognition. The conserved theme is a molecular recognition surface coupled to mechanisms that distribute or act on the recognized material. The detailed design of engineered single-domain antibody formats belongs to the next chapter.

11. The chemistry of reversible antigen–antibody association

When an antigen approaches an antibody, the interaction does not normally involve forming a permanent covalent bond between the two molecules. Instead, complementary surfaces associate through a combination of noncovalent interactions. Such binding is reversible: molecules can form complexes and later separate. Reversibility is not a sign that the interaction is biologically weak. Many individual noncovalent contacts can act together, and their combination can produce a complex that remains associated long enough to interfere with a target or recruit another component of the immune system. To understand the observed binding strength, one must consider both the contacts formed and the molecular states that are replaced when the partners associate.

Hydrogen bonds involve favorable arrangements of a hydrogen-bond donor and an acceptor. Their geometry is important because the ability to form a hydrogen bond depends on the relative orientation of chemical groups. In aqueous solution, groups that participate in a hydrogen bond across the interface may previously have formed interactions with water. Consequently, the presence of a hydrogen bond in an antibody–antigen structure does not guarantee a large net favorable contribution to association. The energetic benefit depends partly on how the new interaction compares with the interactions lost on desolvation. A buried polar group that cannot establish a compensating contact may instead impose a cost.

Electrostatic interactions arise from charges and electric dipoles. A negatively charged antigen group can interact favorably with a positively charged antibody residue when geometry and local conditions permit. Like charges may oppose close association, although the influence of surrounding residues and solvent can complicate a simple charge-pair picture. Dissolved ions screen electric interactions in solution. Increasing ionic strength can therefore diminish some long-range electrostatic contributions to binding, but it may also affect other features of the protein and solvent. A change in binding after salt addition suggests sensitivity to the solution environment; it is not sufficient to calculate the number of salt bridges at the molecular interface.

The pH of a solution influences protonation states of ionizable groups. If an antigen contains a residue that gains or loses a proton near the experimental pH, changing pH can alter the local charge pattern. Similar changes can occur in antibody residues. This can modify a favorable interaction or remove an unfavorable one. At extreme pH, protein folding and aggregation can also change, creating effects that are not restricted to a single contact. To interpret a pH-dependent binding curve, the researcher should distinguish a reversible shift in recognition from irreversible loss of structural integrity. Returning the material to its original pH and assessing its binding again provides one way of probing this distinction.

Hydrophobic association can contribute when nonpolar surfaces become buried as the antigen and antibody come together. In water, exposed nonpolar groups influence how neighboring water molecules organize. Bringing compatible nonpolar surfaces together changes this arrangement and can favor association, depending on the accompanying enthalpic and entropic changes. Hydrophobic interactions are not a special covalent force between two nonpolar side chains. They reflect the thermodynamics of the entire system, including solvent. This is why the magnitude of the contribution can depend on temperature, molecular size and the precise chemical environment of the interface.

Van der Waals interactions become important when atoms approach at suitable distances. Many weak contacts across a complementary molecular surface can contribute substantially to a stable complex. If two surfaces approach too closely, repulsive interactions rise sharply because their electron distributions overlap unfavorably. Therefore a mutation that replaces a small side chain by a larger one can decrease affinity through a steric clash even when the new side chain has chemical properties that appear attractive. The geometry of a binding surface governs whether potential favorable contacts can actually form. A simple list of amino acid types at an interface does not replace a three-dimensional understanding of their positions.

Binding also changes the freedom of motion available to both partners. Separate molecules can translate and rotate independently in solution. When they associate, the relative freedom of translation and rotation is restricted. Flexible loops or peptide segments may become more ordered. These changes often impose entropic costs, whereas solvent release or other rearrangements can provide compensating benefits. In standard thermodynamic notation, the free-energy change for association is related to enthalpy and entropy by ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. Here T is absolute temperature, ΔH° is the standard enthalpy change and ΔS° is the standard entropy change. The standard free-energy change reflects the net balance, not the strength of a single force.

An antibody mutation can affect both enthalpy and entropy. Suppose a flexible loop is constrained in a conformation close to the bound state. If the constraint does not interfere with antigen contact, the loop may lose less conformational freedom during association. On the other hand, it may become unable to adapt to small variations in the antigen, reducing binding to related structures. A favorable effect on one determinant can thus accompany a loss of cross-reactivity with another. Predicting the result from chemical intuition alone is difficult because structural changes, hydration and conformational energy are coupled. A reliable experimental conclusion requires comparisons under matched solution conditions.

The thermodynamic analysis also clarifies why antigen recognition does not normally require ATP hydrolysis by the antibody. Once an antibody and a suitable antigen encounter each other, formation of a reversible complex is governed by their interaction energetics and concentrations. The organism used energy and biosynthetic machinery to produce and secrete the antibody, and cells that respond to antibody-coated targets use energy for signaling and movement. These are different processes from the binding event itself. The association of an antibody with an antigen in a test tube can proceed in the absence of living cells and added ATP if conditions preserve the relevant molecular structures.

12. Affinity, equilibrium occupancy and the kinetics of binding

Affinity refers to the strength of interaction between a single binding site and a single compatible determinant. It can be described quantitatively by an equilibrium model for a simple one-to-one interaction. Let B represent a free antibody binding site, A a free monovalent antigen, and BA the complex containing one site and one antigen. The reversible reaction is B + A ⇌ BA. At equilibrium the rate of complex formation equals the rate of dissociation. This is a dynamic condition: individual molecules continue to associate and separate, but the average concentrations do not change with time.

The equilibrium dissociation constant Kd is defined by Kd = [B][A]/[BA], where square brackets indicate equilibrium molar concentrations. Because the numerator is the product of two concentrations and the denominator is one concentration, the concentration-form Kd has units of molarity. A smaller Kd indicates that a smaller free antigen concentration is required to achieve a particular occupancy. For the same concentrations of free reactants, a smaller Kd favors a larger equilibrium concentration of complex. The equilibrium association constant Ka is the reciprocal, Ka = 1/Kd, with units of inverse molarity in this concentration convention. These definitions make clear why high affinity corresponds to a small dissociation constant and a large association constant.

Suppose the total concentration of antibody binding sites is Bt. Under the one-site model, Bt = [B] + [BA]. The fraction of sites occupied is θ = [BA]/Bt. Rearranging the expression for Kd gives [B] = Kd[BA]/[A]. Substitution into the total site equation gives Bt = [BA](1 + Kd/[A]). Dividing the complex concentration by Bt yields θ = [A]/(Kd + [A]). This relationship predicts a saturating curve when occupancy is plotted against free antigen concentration. As free antigen becomes much larger than Kd, the occupancy approaches one. At a free concentration equal to Kd, occupancy is one-half. At a free concentration nine times Kd, occupancy is nine-tenths.

The derivation used total binding-site concentration rather than total antibody-molecule concentration. A conventional IgG molecule has two potential binding sites, so one nanomolar of intact IgG corresponds to two nanomolar of sites in the ideal model. If soluble monovalent ligand binds independently to those two sites, each site can be treated with the same one-site equation. The fraction of occupied sites is not necessarily equal to the fraction of antibody molecules bearing at least one ligand. If each site has occupancy θ and the two are independent, the probability that both are empty is (1 − θ)². The probability that a molecule has at least one ligand is therefore 1 − (1 − θ)². At θ = 0.5, three quarters of the molecules are predicted to bear at least one ligand even though only half of the sites are occupied.

Free antigen concentration is an important assumption. In an experiment containing a very small concentration of antibody sites relative to added ligand, the free ligand may be close to the total amount added. If the concentrations of sites and ligand are comparable, binding can deplete the free ligand substantially. Substituting total ligand into the occupancy expression in that situation can overestimate binding. The more general mass-balance model defines At = [A] + [BA] and Bt = [B] + [BA]. Let x = [BA]. Then Kd = (Bt − x)(At − x)/x. Rearrangement gives x² − (Bt + At + Kd)x + BtAt = 0. The physically meaningful solution is the smaller root, because x cannot exceed either total concentration. This model still assumes independent sites and a single monovalent ligand; it is not a general solution to multivalent immune-complex formation.

To see why the correction matters, consider a hypothetical solution with ten nanomolar total ligand, ten nanomolar total sites and a Kd of five nanomolar. Substituting total ligand as though it were free would predict occupancy of about two thirds. In the mass-balance equation the sum Bt + At + Kd equals twenty-five nanomolar. The discriminant is 625 − 400 = 225 square nanomolar, with square root fifteen nanomolar. The smaller root is (25 − 15)/2 = five nanomolar. Thus five nanomolar of complex forms and only half the sites are occupied. The remaining free antigen is five nanomolar, exactly equal to Kd, as required by the one-site occupancy relation. This consistency check is more informative than applying a memorized expression without examining its assumptions.

The binding process also has a time dimension. For an elementary reversible association, the forward rate is kon[B][A] and the reverse rate is koff[BA]. The association rate constant kon has units M⁻¹ s⁻¹, while the dissociation rate constant koff has units s⁻¹. The concentration of complex therefore changes according to d[BA]/dt = kon[B][A] − koff[BA]. At equilibrium the two terms are equal, giving Kd = koff/kon. This relationship is useful for distinguishing equilibrium affinity from binding kinetics. A low Kd can arise through rapid association, slow dissociation or a combination of the two.

Two antibodies can have identical equilibrium affinity but different kinetic behavior. Suppose antibody X has kon = 10⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 0.01 s⁻¹, while antibody Y has kon = 10⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 0.001 s⁻¹. Both have Kd = 10⁻⁸ M. Antibody X associates and dissociates ten times faster than Y in the ideal model. If equilibrium is reached at the same antigen concentration, their site occupancies are the same. Yet during a short exposure X may capture antigen more rapidly, while after free antigen is removed Y may retain complexes longer. A measurement made after a fixed incubation and wash can therefore differ even when the two true equilibrium constants are identical.

During a dissociation experiment in which free antigen is removed and rebinding is negligible, the complex concentration decays according to d[BA]/dt = −koff[BA]. Integrating yields [BA]t = [BA]0 exp(−koff t). The dissociation half-life is ln(2)/koff. With koff = 0.01 s⁻¹, the half-life is approximately 69.3 seconds; with koff = 0.001 s⁻¹, it is approximately 693 seconds. The half-life is not the time for every complex to break; it is the time at which the average population of complexes has fallen to half its starting value under the stated conditions. On an antigen-coated surface where rebinding occurs, the observed decay may depart from this simple equation.

Under conditions in which free antigen is maintained approximately constant during association, the approach to equilibrium also has a simple time constant. Since [B] = Bt − [BA], the rate equation becomes d[BA]/dt = kon[A]Bt − (kon[A] + koff)[BA]. The relaxation rate is kon[A] + koff. Beginning with empty sites, the occupied fraction approaches its equilibrium value as θ(t) = θeq(1 − exp(−(kon[A] + koff)t)). At higher ligand concentration the association phase can become faster as well as reach a higher occupancy. This conclusion requires that free ligand remain approximately constant and the binding mechanism not contain additional slow conformational steps. A curved or multiphasic experimental response may signal a departure from these assumptions.

The standard free energy of association provides another representation of affinity. To use a logarithm correctly, its argument must be dimensionless. With a standard concentration c° of one molar, the dimensionless association constant for the ideal dilute model is Ka c°, and ΔG° = −RT ln(Ka c°) = RT ln(Kd/c°). R is the gas constant, approximately 8.314 joules per mole per kelvin, and T is absolute temperature. At 298 kelvin, a Kd of ten nanomolar gives a standard association free energy near −45.6 kilojoules per mole. A tenfold decrease in Kd changes ΔG° by −RT ln 10, about −5.71 kilojoules per mole at this temperature. The small free-energy difference explains how relatively modest changes in a molecular interface can produce a substantial change in equilibrium affinity.

None of these equations establishes that a high-affinity antibody is necessarily protective. The equations describe a defined molecular recognition interaction. Biological efficacy additionally depends on how much antigen is accessible, whether the bound site has a relevant function, how the antibody is distributed and which effector mechanisms can be engaged. A very high-affinity antibody against an internal protein inaccessible on an intact pathogen may be excellent for detecting disrupted material yet ineffective at blocking entry. A moderate-affinity antibody against a repetitive exposed surface may achieve strong overall attachment through avidity. Quantitative reasoning becomes most valuable when the physical model is connected to the biological mechanism rather than treated as an isolated numerical exercise.

13. Avidity, geometry and cooperative attachment without allostery

Avidity describes the overall functional strength of a multivalent interaction. When an antibody with two or more binding sites encounters an antigen or surface carrying multiple compatible epitopes, more than one site can participate in attachment. Each individual site has its own intrinsic affinity. Once one arm of a bivalent antibody has attached, the other arm is physically constrained near the antigen, and the probability of encountering a compatible nearby epitope may become much greater than for an arm freely diffusing through solution. A second interaction can stabilize the association without any need to change the amino acid sequence or intrinsic affinity of the first binding site.

The key distinction is between the chemical strength of a single site and the probability of complete molecular separation. In a bivalently attached IgG, one bond may break while the other remains intact. The released arm is then still held in the vicinity of the target and may attach again. Complete escape requires that all remaining connections be lost before effective rebinding occurs. It is not necessary for both bonds to break at precisely the same instant; they may break successively during an interval in which no new attachment forms. This mechanism can markedly reduce the apparent dissociation of the whole antibody relative to a monovalent fragment containing the same binding surface.

A simple conceptual measure of this proximity effect is effective local concentration. After one antibody arm binds, an appropriately placed second epitope is confined relative to the second binding site. Its effective concentration in the accessible search volume can be much higher than the bulk concentration of soluble antigen. In an idealized model, the equilibrium tendency for the second attachment scales with effective concentration divided by the intrinsic Kd of one site. A large ratio favors the doubly bound state. This is not an expression for a universal overall Kd of intact IgG, because the initial binding statistics, entropy of tethering and geometry differ between systems. It provides a physical explanation for why the second binding event is often easier than capture of a second independent freely diffusing molecule.

Geometry can eliminate the advantage even if an antigen bears many chemical copies of a determinant. Two epitopes can be too close together for both antibody arms to approach without collision. They can be too far apart for the hinge and flexible regions to span. They may also face in directions that make simultaneous occupation difficult. In a densely packed surface, the number of accessible positions may be much smaller than the total number of repeated chemical groups. Thus a calculation based on the nominal valence of the antibody and total epitope number is an upper-bound argument, not a guarantee of the configuration that will form.

Compare a conventional IgG and a Fab fragment prepared from that IgG. Both contain the same antigen-recognition sequence in their corresponding binding sites. Against a freely soluble monovalent hapten, the intrinsic site affinity may be essentially the same. Against a surface that displays appropriately spaced copies of the hapten, intact IgG can bind through both arms and exhibit slower apparent dissociation. The Fab cannot do this because it has only one binding site. A finding that the intact antibody remains on the surface during a wash therefore does not imply that cleavage weakened the noncovalent interactions at the paratope. It may simply have removed the ability to sustain multivalent attachment.

The same principle is amplified in secreted pentameric IgM. Multiple antigen-binding arms can contribute to attachment to a suitable repetitive structure. An IgM with moderate monovalent affinity may show very strong overall binding to a bacterium displaying many accessible copies of its determinant. However, an IgM should not always be ranked above an IgG in avidity irrespective of the target. A high-affinity IgG directed against a sparse or geometrically favorable epitope could attach more stably than an IgM whose binding sites have weaker affinity or cannot reach the available determinants. Avidity is an interaction property involving both partners, not an absolute constant assigned solely on the basis of antibody class.

In many introductory problems the term cooperative attachment is used to describe enhanced binding by tethered sites. It is important to distinguish this effect from allosteric cooperativity. In a genuinely allosteric protein, occupancy at one site changes the intrinsic binding properties of another site through a structural or energetic coupling. Multivalent attachment can appear cooperative even if the sites remain intrinsically identical and independent when measured in isolation. The enhanced second binding event then results from spatial proximity rather than an allosteric change. Both mechanisms can in principle coexist in complex biological molecules, but a simple demonstration of avidity is not proof of positive allostery.

A useful experimental comparison varies epitope density while holding the chemistry of the epitope constant. A monovalent fragment should not acquire a second attachment route merely because more epitopes are present, although its encounter rate and rebinding behavior at a surface may change. An intact bivalent antibody may show a more pronounced change when density enters a range that permits simultaneous attachment. To assign the effect specifically to bivalency, the researcher can also test sparse antigen, soluble monovalent ligand and intact antibody under matched concentration and flow conditions. Controls are needed because densely immobilized antigens can change orientation, steric accessibility or the transport of ligands to and from the surface.

Avidity is biologically useful because many pathogens present repeated structural features. Capsular polysaccharides, arrays of surface proteins and repeated viral envelope components can provide multiple sites for antibody recognition. Stable attachment may facilitate neutralization, agglutination or recruitment of Fc receptors and complement. However, not every multivalent attachment results in a large aggregate. An antibody that binds both arms to the same particle may be strongly retained without linking that particle to another. The distinction between within-particle attachment and between-particle bridging becomes essential when interpreting visible agglutination and soluble antigen precipitation.

14. Precipitation, agglutination and the geometry of immune complexes

The first event in a mixture of specific antibody and antigen is molecular recognition. A visible precipitate or clump can arise subsequently when the interacting molecules form sufficiently large assemblies. These visible changes are often called secondary antigen–antibody reactions because they depend on more than the initial association of an individual site with an epitope. The distinction is useful in experimental interpretation: a negative visible reaction is not equivalent to the absence of molecular binding. Complexes may remain small and soluble even when individual recognition sites are extensively occupied.

Precipitation is the formation of insoluble or poorly soluble immune complexes from initially soluble antigen and antibody. It can occur when antibody molecules connect multiple antigen molecules, which in turn bind additional antibody molecules. Repeated cycles of bridging generate larger networks. An antigen must provide enough accessible determinants for network extension, and the antibody must possess sufficient functional valence to connect targets. A soluble monovalent hapten cannot by itself support this kind of extended cross-linked lattice with a conventional antibody, because once attached it provides no second determinant to which another antibody can bind.

A single antigen molecule may display multiple different epitopes. In a polyclonal serum, antibodies to distinct epitopes can contribute to bridging even if each epitope is present only once on that molecule. With a monoclonal antibody, a protein bearing only one accessible copy of its particular determinant may fail to form an extensive lattice, even though the protein contains many other epitopes recognized by other antibodies. This makes the relevant antigen valence dependent on the antibody population being considered. A count of all possible recognizable surface features is not the same as the number of sites that can simultaneously be occupied by the available antibodies.

The classical precipitin curve can be examined by keeping antibody quantity fixed while increasing soluble antigen. When antigen is very scarce relative to available antibody sites, individual antigen molecules tend to be occupied by antibody molecules without extensive network growth. The excess antibody can limit the availability of free determinants through which additional cross-links could form. This range is known as the antibody-excess zone or prozone. The amount of precipitate may be low even though antibody concentration is high and antigen recognition occurs. Diluting a serum sample can move the ratio toward conditions that allow a visible reaction, explaining an otherwise counterintuitive positive result after dilution.

At intermediate antigen-to-antibody ratios, the opportunities for extended bridging may be greatest. The corresponding region is called the zone of equivalence. It does not necessarily occur at equal molar concentrations of antigen and antibody molecules, and equal masses are even less informative. A pentameric antibody, a bivalent IgG and a protein with several available epitopes present different numbers and geometries of binding sites. The point of maximum precipitation is therefore an experimentally determined consequence of the system. Affinity, valence, epitope accessibility, solubility and the composition of the antibody population all influence where the maximum occurs.

At high antigen concentration relative to antibody, the available antibody sites can be occupied by separate antigen molecules without connecting them into a large network. Small soluble complexes may predominate. This is the antigen-excess region, also termed the postzone in the classical context. Adding more antigen to a mixture beyond the optimum may therefore decrease visible precipitation. The nonmonotonic behavior of precipitation must be distinguished from a simple equilibrium occupancy curve, which increases toward saturation as free monovalent antigen rises. The two graphs describe different measured quantities: one measures network formation or insoluble complex, and the other measures occupation of individual sites.

Classical quantitative studies of antigen–antibody precipitation, associated with Michael Heidelberger and Forrest Kendall, helped turn immune reactions into measurable chemical relationships. The conceptual importance of these studies was not merely that a precipitate appeared, but that the amount of precipitated material varied systematically with the relative quantities of reacting components. The resulting evidence supported the analysis of immunological reactions through stoichiometry and molecular association. Modern structural knowledge explains why the observed ratios depend on valence and the ability to generate a network. Historical precipitin data should not be represented by an arbitrary teaching curve as if the latter were a digitized original experiment.

Agglutination refers to the visible clumping of particles or cells carrying antigenic determinants. Unlike precipitation, the targets in this reaction are already particulate before antibody is added. An antibody that bridges determinants on different particles can connect them into clusters. The large size and multivalent nature of IgM often make it effective at agglutinating suitable targets, but the same valence does not guarantee bridging if its sites bind primarily within one particle. Cell-surface charge, the spacing of antigens and physical separation between particles also influence the reaction. A failure to agglutinate does not establish that an antibody has failed to coat the particles.

The ABO blood group system provides a familiar conceptual example. The A and B determinants are carbohydrate features on the surface of red cells, and antibodies against these determinants can cause agglutination under appropriate conditions. The identity of the determinant, not the cell's entire molecular composition, is what is recognized. Group O red cells lack the A and B modifications but retain the precursor H antigen in the usual ABO model. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that group O cells possess no surface antigens. The biological diversity of blood-group systems also means that a simple ABO reaction is not a complete evaluation of compatibility. Here the system illustrates the connection between surface determinants, antibody binding and visible particle bridging.

The distinction between direct and indirect consequences of recognition is particularly clear in red-cell experiments. Some antibodies coat cells without causing easily visible direct agglutination under the chosen conditions. A secondary reagent that recognizes the bound immunoglobulin can provide additional bridging between coated cells. The resulting clumping indicates the presence of cell-associated antibody in the appropriate controlled assay, rather than a newly generated specificity of the primary antibody. This principle shows why the physical readout of an assay can require a separate molecular interaction from the first recognition event. Details of diagnostic techniques should be studied in the methods unit rather than inferred from a simplified lattice diagram.

A set of dilution experiments can help distinguish alternative explanations for an absent visible reaction. If a sample gives no clumping when concentrated but becomes positive at intermediate dilution and negative again at extreme dilution, antibody excess is a plausible interpretation. A series that changes the amount of antigen while holding the antibody fixed can identify an optimal ratio for precipitation. Appropriate antigen-only, antibody-only and irrelevant-antibody controls help exclude spontaneous aggregation. A separate binding assay can reveal whether small complexes form in a region with no visible network. Without these controls, a negative result could reflect low affinity, inadequate epitope accessibility, an unsuitable ratio or a genuinely absent antibody, and the visible observation alone cannot decide among them.

15. Experimental inference, perturbations and biological applications

A central skill in immunology is to identify which step an experiment actually measures. Imagine that a mutation in an antibody leaves its association with soluble antigen unchanged, but the antibody no longer stimulates uptake of antigen-coated particles by macrophages. The result suggests a defect after recognition. A plausible explanation is that the mutation affects an Fc receptor interaction, because phagocytic uptake depends on communication between the coated particle and the macrophage. Nevertheless changes in antibody density on the particles or differences in aggregation could also influence uptake. Demonstrating equal coating under the conditions of the functional experiment is an important control before assigning the defect exclusively to Fc.

A second mutation might reduce binding to the native antigen but leave recognition of a linear peptide unchanged. One possibility is that the antibody recognizes the peptide sequence in both cases but a nearby structural feature in the native antigen has altered access. Another is that the antibody adopts a different binding orientation when interacting with the intact protein. The comparison does not by itself identify the exact amino acid contacts. Testing antigen variants with verified folding, assessing competition and comparing structural information can narrow the possible explanations. At examination level, the strongest answer is often the one supported by all observations without claiming more detail than the evidence establishes.

Equilibrium dialysis offers a classical way to reason about direct affinity measurements with a small diffusible antigen. A membrane permits free small ligand to pass but retains antibody on one side. When equilibrium is reached, the free ligand concentration is the same on both sides if there are no additional gradients or interactions. The antibody-containing compartment can have a greater total ligand concentration because some molecules are bound to antibody and retained indirectly. Subtracting the free concentration from the total in that compartment gives the bound concentration under the ideal assumptions. By comparing bound ligand with known total binding-site concentration over a range of free ligand concentrations, one can estimate Kd and assess the number of sites. The method depends on the membrane excluding antibody and on the ligand not interacting substantially with the membrane itself.

A linear representation of the one-site equation can also help understand how binding data can reveal capacity. From Kd = [B][A]/[BA] and [B] = Bt − [BA], one obtains [BA]/[A] = Bt/Kd − [BA]/Kd. A graph of bound divided by free ligand concentration against bound concentration has slope −1/Kd and horizontal intercept Bt in the ideal independent-site model. This is a form of Scatchard analysis. Curvature can reflect heterogeneous site affinities, cooperative behavior or experimental complications, and should not automatically be interpreted as one specific mechanism. Transforming measured quantities can distort the distribution of experimental errors; direct nonlinear fitting is often preferable for estimating parameters. The graphical model remains useful for NET-style reasoning because it connects the slope and intercept to definite physical quantities.

An antibody's cross-reactivity can be investigated with competition using two related monovalent ligands. If ligand A and ligand B occupy the same antibody site in mutually exclusive fashion, the fraction of sites occupied by A in an ideal equilibrium system is ([A]/Kd,A)/(1 + [A]/Kd,A + [B]/Kd,B). This follows by expressing each complex concentration relative to the concentration of free sites and applying total site conservation. It predicts that a sufficiently large concentration of a weakly recognized alternative can compete with a low concentration of a more strongly recognized target. Specificity should therefore be interpreted in relation to both affinity and the concentrations present in the test. A molecule with weak but real affinity may have negligible effect in one physiological setting and a detectable effect in an assay deliberately using a high competitor concentration.

One reason to study cross-reactivity is that related microbial proteins may share a structural determinant, permitting an antibody to recognize more than one strain. Such recognition may be beneficial if the shared site is accessible and functionally important. It can also complicate detection when the aim is to distinguish closely related antigens. In another biological context, a determinant on a microbial protein may resemble a host structure, raising the possibility of molecular mimicry. Cross-binding alone is not sufficient to establish autoimmune disease; the reactive molecules must reach an appropriate target, and the resulting biological response must overcome regulatory constraints and produce relevant damage. Detailed mechanisms of tolerance failure and autoimmunity are considered in a later chapter.

Applications in biological research exploit the modular specificity of antibodies. A researcher can use a primary antibody to identify a particular protein in a complex mixture or tissue and another reagent to report where the primary antibody is bound. Interpretation requires knowing whether the antigen has remained folded, whether the epitope is accessible and whether related proteins are present. A signal can change because the amount of target has changed, but also because modification, cleavage or conformation has changed accessibility. The choice of antibody and the physical treatment of the sample therefore determine what a positive or negative result can mean. Appropriate controls and an independent measure of the target are often needed when making a strong biological claim.

Several previously studied units converge in this chapter. The beta-sheet architecture and disulfides of immunoglobulin domains illustrate protein folding and stability. The association model uses equilibrium and kinetic concepts previously applied to biomolecular interactions and enzyme behavior, while requiring care not to equate an equilibrium Kd with a Michaelis constant. Epithelial transport of IgA exemplifies vesicular trafficking and polarized membrane transport. Fc receptor clustering connects extracellular recognition to cell signaling. Opsonization and complement recruitment link adaptive specificity to innate effector mechanisms. Understanding these connections enables a student to explain an unfamiliar experiment by reconstructing the relevant causal chain rather than recalling a list of antibody classes.

The next logical question is how the immune system obtains a large population of molecules with distinct variable-region sequences while retaining a smaller number of constant-region formats. The current chapter has established the molecular structure on which that diversity acts: heavy and light chains combine to form recognition sites, and their constant-region architecture determines how the resulting antibody functions. The next chapter will distinguish gene rearrangements that generate a naive receptor repertoire, subsequent changes that can refine binding and changes that alter the class of antibody produced. Keeping these mechanisms separate from the physical chemistry of recognition is the foundation for interpreting both molecular and cellular immunology.

Worked quantitative examples

Example 1. Site concentration

A 0.30 mg/mL IgG solution has approximate molar mass 150,000 g/mol. Convert: 0.30 mg/mL = 0.30 g/L. Molecule concentration = 0.30/150000 = 2 × 10⁻⁶ M. For two independent accessible sites per IgG, site concentration is 4 µM. This is a theoretical capacity, not the number already occupied.

Example 2. Free-ligand occupancy

At free antigen 30 nM and Kd 10 nM, θ = 30/(10 + 30) = 0.75. At 90 nM, θ = 0.90. Doubling or tripling ligand does not yield the same proportional increase in occupancy near saturation.

Example 3. Ligand depletion

At Bt = 20 nM, At = 15 nM, Kd = 5 nM, the sum is 40 nM and discriminant = 1600 − 1200 = 400 nM². x = (40 − 20)/2 = 10 nM. Half of the sites are occupied; free ligand is 5 nM. The larger root is physically impossible.

Example 4. Rate constants

kon = 2 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹; koff = 4 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Kd = 2 × 10⁻⁸ M = 20 nM. Dissociation half-life = 0.693/0.004 = 173.3 s without rebinding. A surface experiment with rebinding may show a slower apparent decay.

Example 5. Independent sites

At θ = 0.4, the chance that both sites of ideal IgG are occupied is 0.4² = 0.16; exactly one is 2 × 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.48; at least one is 0.64. The probabilities assume two identical independent sites and monovalent ligand.

Example 6. Free-energy comparison

For Kd changing from 100 nM to 1 nM at 298 K, ΔΔG° = RT ln(1/100) = 8.314 × 298 × (−4.605) J/mol ≈ −11.41 kJ/mol. The second interaction is more favorable at the same standard state.

Example 7. Competitive binding

Free A = 20 nM with Kd,A = 10 nM; free B = 100 nM with Kd,B = 50 nM. Both concentration-to-Kd ratios equal 2. Fraction bound to A = 2/(1 + 2 + 2) = 0.4; B also 0.4 and empty 0.2. A has stronger affinity but not higher occupancy here.

Example 8. Scatchard interpretation

In an ideal plot of bound/free against bound concentration in nM, a line has slope −0.2 nM⁻¹ and horizontal intercept 12 nM. Kd = 5 nM, Bt = 12 nM and vertical intercept = 12/5 = 2.4. These parameters describe independent binding sites, not multivalent lattice formation.

Part B — conceptual practice

Original practice questions. Experimental scenarios and numerical values are illustrative unless explicitly identified as historical evidence.

Question 1

Which distinction between antigenicity and immunogenicity is correct?

  1. Every antigen independently induces antibody production.
  2. Immunogenicity is the equilibrium binding constant of an antibody.
  3. Recognition and induction are experimentally distinct properties.
  4. Only proteins can be antigenic.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Recognition and induction are experimentally distinct properties.

An existing antibody can bind a hapten that alone fails to induce an adaptive response. Immunogenicity requires an appropriate host and activation context; affinity measures a particular molecular interaction, not the capacity to induce a response.

Question 2

Which feature most directly makes a small hapten useful in a classical hapten–carrier system?

  1. It supplies a recognizable B-cell determinant while the carrier supplies helper peptides.
  2. It supplies both conventional B- and T-cell determinants without a carrier.
  3. It can be recognized only after proteolysis of the carrier.
  4. It makes linked antigen uptake unnecessary.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. It supplies a recognizable B-cell determinant while the carrier supplies helper peptides.

The hapten supplies a B-cell determinant, while the protein carrier supplies peptides for MHC II. Neither processing of the hapten itself nor unlinked uptake replaces linked recognition.

Question 3

What defines a discontinuous B-cell epitope?

  1. All amino acids are adjacent in sequence but different in charge.
  2. A peptide must be bound to MHC I.
  3. Residues separated in primary sequence converge in the recognized three-dimensional surface.
  4. The determinant is located only in the Fc region.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Residues separated in primary sequence converge in the recognized three-dimensional surface.

Discontinuous determinants depend on the folded spatial relationship of residues. MHC-associated peptide recognition describes conventional T cells, not this type of B-cell epitope.

Question 4

Which statement best describes the ligand of a conventional αβ TCR?

  1. A soluble native protein independent of presentation.
  2. A peptide together with an appropriate MHC surface.
  3. The MHC molecule alone without a bound peptide.
  4. An isolated peptide regardless of MHC allele.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. A peptide together with an appropriate MHC surface.

The conventional αβ TCR generally contacts both peptide and MHC. Native antigen recognition is characteristic of BCRs; neither isolated peptide nor an empty MHC surface reproduces the usual composite ligand.

Question 5

A typical IgG contains how many polypeptide chains?

  1. Four: three heavy and one light.
  2. Four: two heavy and two light.
  3. Two: one heavy and one light.
  4. Twelve: one for each domain.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Four: two heavy and two light.

Domains are folded regions within chains. The twelve Ig domains of a conventional IgG do not correspond to twelve separately synthesized chains.

Question 6

What distinguishes the five major human antibody classes?

  1. Whether the light chain is κ or λ.
  2. The number of CDRs in VL alone.
  3. The chemical identity of the antigen.
  4. The heavy-chain constant-region class.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The heavy-chain constant-region class.

Kappa and lambda are light-chain types that can pair with all major heavy-chain classes. The antigen-binding sequence determines recognition, not the heavy-chain isotype.

Question 7

How many principal CDR loops contribute to one conventional VH–VL binding site?

  1. Three.
  2. Six.
  3. Two.
  4. Twelve.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Six.

Each of the two variable domains contributes three principal CDRs. Twelve describes the total across the two sites, not the number at one site.

Question 8

Which statement about an immunoglobulin fold is correct?

  1. It is principally a four-helix bundle.
  2. It contains two packed β sheets in a characteristic domain architecture.
  3. Each domain is an independent disulfide-linked chain.
  4. Its variable loops remain unfolded until ligand binds.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. It contains two packed β sheets in a characteristic domain architecture.

The immunoglobulin domain is a β-sandwich with characteristic connecting loops. Domains are parts of polypeptide chains, and the fold is normally established before antigen binding.

Question 9

What is the characteristic papain product of conventional IgG?

  1. One F(ab′)₂ with intact Fc.
  2. Two heavy chains and two light chains only.
  3. Five IgG subunits.
  4. Two separate Fab fragments and an Fc fragment.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Two separate Fab fragments and an Fc fragment.

Papain cleaves above the hinge disulfides in the standard model. Reduction separates chains; pepsin characteristically retains linked F(ab′)₂ while degrading much of Fc.

Question 10

Which fragment retains two antigen-binding sites but lacks intact Fc?

  1. Fab.
  2. Fc.
  3. F(ab′)₂.
  4. An isolated CH3 domain.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. F(ab′)₂.

F(ab′)₂ retains both arms and hinge linkage. A Fab has only one site; isolated Fc and CH3 lack the antigen-specific variable-domain pair.

Question 11

Which form of IgM is typically pentameric?

  1. Membrane IgM on a naive B cell.
  2. All isolated μ heavy chains.
  3. Every IgM molecule regardless of location.
  4. Secreted IgM containing J chain.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Secreted IgM containing J chain.

Membrane IgM is a single immunoglobulin unit. Pentameric secreted IgM commonly contains J chain; secreted hexamers are an additional form.

Question 12

What is the principal structural origin of the secretory component of secretory IgA?

  1. The rearranged VH region.
  2. The extracellular portion of epithelial pIgR.
  3. The μ heavy chain.
  4. The J chain of a plasma cell.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The extracellular portion of epithelial pIgR.

The receptor is cleaved during epithelial transport. J chain comes from the antibody-producing cell and assists polymer assembly and receptor interaction.

Question 13

Which immunoglobulin is most directly associated with FcεRI-mediated mast-cell sensitization?

  1. IgD.
  2. IgE.
  3. IgA.
  4. IgG2.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. IgE.

FcεRI binds the Fc of IgE with high affinity. Subsequent cross-linking by appropriate antigen, rather than sensitization alone, commonly initiates rapid mediator release.

Question 14

Which mechanism commonly permits naive mature B cells to coexpress IgM and IgD with the same specificity?

  1. Independent new V(D)J rearrangements for each constant region.
  2. Class-switch recombination deleting Cμ in every naive mature B cell.
  3. Exchange of κ and λ chains after antigen-independent maturation.
  4. Alternative RNA processing of the same rearranged variable-region information.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Alternative RNA processing of the same rearranged variable-region information.

Alternative processing of a shared primary transcript permits IgM and IgD with identical V(D)J regions. Deletional class switching and new variable-region rearrangement are distinct mechanisms.

Question 15

Which receptor is particularly important for IgG salvage and human placental transfer?

  1. FcγRI (CD64).
  2. Polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR).
  3. FcRn.
  4. FcεRI.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. FcRn.

FcRn binds IgG at acidic pH and mediates recycling and placental transfer. FcγRI primarily supports effector functions, pIgR transports polymeric IgA and IgM, and FcεRI recognizes IgE.

Question 16

An inherited immunoglobulin constant-region allelic variant within a species is an example of what?

  1. Allotype.
  2. Isotype.
  3. Idiotype.
  4. Paratope.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Allotype.

Allotypes are inherited allelic variants within a species. Isotypes distinguish classes/subclasses, idiotypes concern variable-region antigenic features, and paratopes are binding surfaces.

Question 17

Which is an appropriate definition of affinity?

  1. The overall stability of multivalent attachment.
  2. Strength of a single defined binding-site–determinant interaction.
  3. The maximum number of sites available on one antibody.
  4. The concentration of antibody required to give visible precipitation.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Strength of a single defined binding-site–determinant interaction.

Affinity is the strength of a defined single-site interaction, expressed by Kd or Ka. Multivalent stability is avidity; capacity and precipitation thresholds depend on different variables.

Question 18

Which combination usually increases single-site affinity?

  1. Increased Kd and increased Ka.
  2. Increased Kd and decreased Ka.
  3. Increased valence with Kd unchanged, by definition.
  4. Decreased Kd and increased Ka.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Decreased Kd and increased Ka.

Ka is reciprocal to Kd in the concentration convention. Increased valence can improve avidity without changing the intrinsic affinity.

Question 19

Which property is essential for extended precipitation of initially soluble antigen?

  1. Maximum site occupancy regardless of antigen valence.
  2. A complement-activating Fc region on every antibody.
  3. Sufficient functional valence on both interacting partners to extend a lattice.
  4. Antigen excess sufficient to saturate all antibody sites.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Sufficient functional valence on both interacting partners to extend a lattice.

Extended networks need functional multivalency and suitable stoichiometry. High occupancy in antigen excess may instead cap bridges; complement is not required for the basic precipitation reaction.

Question 20

Which process describes visible clustering of antigen-bearing particles already present in suspension?

  1. Agglutination.
  2. Affinity maturation.
  3. Class-switch recombination.
  4. Equilibrium dialysis.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Agglutination.

Agglutination is a physical consequence of interparticle links. Affinity maturation and class switching alter antibody populations; dialysis is a method to examine binding.

Question 21

A serum binds H–P and H–Q but not P or Q alone. Free H blocks both reactions. What is the strongest interpretation?

  1. Antibodies are directed against carrier P only.
  2. Antibodies necessarily recognize a conformation shared by P and Q but independent of H.
  3. The serum binds a junction epitope that cannot interact with free H.
  4. The serum contains an antibody population recognizing H or a closely related H-dependent determinant.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The serum contains an antibody population recognizing H or a closely related H-dependent determinant.

Recognition on two distinct carriers and inhibition by free H favor an H-dependent determinant. Carrier-only specificity is contradicted by the controls; a junction strictly dependent on carrier cannot explain free-H competition.

A hapten-specific B cell captures H–P but receives help only from T cells specific for a peptide of P. Why?

  1. Capture of the conjugate makes carrier peptide available for presentation by the B cell.

  3. The B cell must replace its hapten-specific variable region with a carrier-specific one.
  4. The carrier protein must bind the BCR independently of the hapten.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Capture of the conjugate makes carrier peptide available for presentation by the B cell.

Linked recognition permits different B- and T-cell determinants on the same captured conjugate. The BCR retains its original specificity, and the helper TCR recognizes peptide–MHC, not intact hapten on BCR.

The findings support native-conformation dependence but cannot prove sequence discontinuity, because a contiguous sequence may require a folded geometry. Controls for loading, aggregation and denaturation remain necessary.

Comparable peptide–MHC abundance argues against a simple loading or expression deficit. Altered receptor contacts or peptide geometry are plausible; a general signaling defect would require additional evidence.

Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Recognition is retained while a downstream constant-region-dependent function is impaired.

Normal Fab binding and equal target coating support a downstream Fc-mediated defect. The experiment does not eliminate alternative complement-mediated uptake mechanisms.

Question 29

A mutant epithelium lacks pIgR but neighboring plasma cells make normal J-chain-containing IgA. Which observation is predicted most directly?

  1. A loss of all plasma-cell IgA production.
  2. Inability of IgA heavy chains to assemble into dimers despite normal J chain.
  3. Reduced receptor-mediated delivery of secretory IgA into the lumen.
  4. Increased receptor-derived secretory component in the lumen.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Reduced receptor-mediated delivery of secretory IgA into the lumen.

The epithelial pIgR defect specifically compromises transcytosis and delivery of receptor-derived secretory component. Normal J-chain-containing IgA production argues against a primary synthesis or assembly defect.

Question 30

Mast cells are sensitized with a single IgE specificity. A monovalent antigen binds, but an appropriately spaced bivalent antigen triggers strong mediator release. What is the best explanation?

  1. Bivalent antigen clusters FcεRI complexes through receptor-bound IgE.
  2. Monovalent antigen fails to bind the IgE paratope.
  3. Bivalency necessarily changes the intrinsic affinity of each Fab.
  4. FcεRI occupancy alone is sufficient to explain antigen-triggered secretion.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Bivalent antigen clusters FcεRI complexes through receptor-bound IgE.

Monovalent antigen can bind without bridging receptor-bound IgE. Appropriate bivalency clusters FcεRI complexes and initiates signaling; it need not change intrinsic single-site affinity.

Question 31

An IgG FcRn-binding mutant and wild-type IgG have identical Fab Kd and equal initial serum amounts. The mutant disappears faster. Which process is most plausibly affected?

  1. Loss of antigen specificity through variable-region replacement.
  2. Reduced binding of the Fab to its antigen.
  3. FcRn-dependent protection from degradation and recycling.
  4. An increase in the intrinsic Fab dissociation rate is established by the serum clearance measurement.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. FcRn-dependent protection from degradation and recycling.

FcRn-dependent recycling and protection from lysosomal degradation contribute to IgG persistence. Similar Fab Kd does not imply identical kinetics, but serum clearance alone does not establish an altered Fab off-rate.

Question 32

A 0.15 g/L preparation of conventional 150 kDa IgG has what theoretical concentration of binding sites?

  1. 1 µM.
  2. 150 µM.
  3. 0.15 nM.
  4. 2 µM.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 2 µM.

0.15/150000 = 10⁻⁶ M IgG molecules. Two sites per molecule give 2 × 10⁻⁶ M, assuming both are functional and accessible.

Question 33

Two antibodies have Kd values 2 nM and 8 nM. At free antigen 8 nM, what are their predicted occupancies, respectively?

  1. 0.50 and 0.80.
  2. 0.80 and 0.50.
  3. 0.20 and 0.50.
  4. 0.80 and 0.20.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 0.80 and 0.50.

Apply θ = [A]/(Kd + [A]) independently: 8/10 = 0.8 and 8/16 = 0.5. Smaller Kd favors greater occupancy.

Question 34

At free monovalent ligand equal to Kd, what fraction of ideal independent-site IgG molecules carries exactly one ligand?

  1. 0.25.
  2. 0.75.
  3. 0.50.
  4. 1.00.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 0.50.

Each site is half occupied. Exactly one is 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.5. A quarter is doubly occupied and three quarters carry at least one ligand.

Question 35

An experiment has 8 nM total sites, 8 nM total ligand and Kd = 2 nM. What equilibrium complex concentration is closest?

  1. 6.40 nM.
  2. 4.88 nM.
  3. 8.00 nM.
  4. 1.12 nM.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 4.88 nM.

Mass balance gives x = (18 − √(324 − 256))/2 ≈ 4.88 nM. Treating total ligand as free would incorrectly yield 6.4 nM bound.

Question 36

Antibody X: kon = 10⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 0.02 s⁻¹. Antibody Y: kon = 10⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 0.002 s⁻¹. Which statement follows?

  1. X has tenfold lower Kd and faster dissociation.
  2. Their Kd values are equal; Y dissociates more slowly.
  3. Y has tenfold lower Kd and faster dissociation.
  4. Their kinetics and affinities are both identical.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Their Kd values are equal; Y dissociates more slowly.

Both ratios equal 2 × 10⁻⁸ M. Y has a tenfold smaller koff and longer dissociation half-life; equal equilibrium constants do not imply equal kinetics.

Question 37

After removal of free ligand, an ideal complex has koff = 0.05 s⁻¹. What fraction remains after 20 s?

  1. Approximately 0.95.
  2. Approximately 0.50.
  3. Approximately 0.368.
  4. Approximately 0.05.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Approximately 0.368.

The surviving fraction is exp(−0.05 × 20) = exp(−1). The first-order model requires negligible rebinding; half-life would be about 13.86 s.

Question 38

At 298 K a mutation changes Kd from 1 nM to 10 nM. What is the approximate change in standard association free energy?

  1. −5.71 kJ/mol.
  2. +57.1 kJ/mol.
  3. +5.71 kJ/mol.
  4. 0 kJ/mol.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. +5.71 kJ/mol.

ΔΔG° = RT ln(10) ≈ +5.71 kJ/mol. The positive change means association becomes less favorable at the same standard state.

Question 39

Free A = 10 nM, Kd,A = 5 nM; free B = 60 nM, Kd,B = 20 nM. What is the ideal fraction of sites occupied by A in mutually exclusive competition?

  1. 2/3.
  2. 1/2.
  3. 1/6.
  4. 1/3.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 1/3.

The dimensionless ratios are 2 and 3. The denominator is 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, so A occupancy is 2/6. The term 1 accounts for unoccupied sites.

Question 40

The occupancy graph shows two curves with half-maximal occupancy at 1 and 4 nM. At equal free ligand, which is true?

  1. The 1 nM curve has higher occupancy except where the curves converge at the limiting boundaries.
  2. The 4 nM curve has higher affinity because its Kd is larger.
  3. The curves prove different numbers of sites per IgG.
  4. The 1 nM curve must correspond to IgM.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The 1 nM curve has higher occupancy except where the curves converge at the limiting boundaries.

In a normalized one-site model, smaller Kd yields higher occupancy. Isotype and total binding capacity are not identified from normalized curves alone.

Question 41

A Scatchard plot in nM units has slope −0.25 nM⁻¹ and x-intercept 16 nM. Which values fit the ideal independent-site model?

  1. Kd = 16 nM; site capacity = 4 nM.
  2. Kd = 0.25 nM; site capacity = 16 nM.
  3. Kd = 4 nM; site capacity = 64 nM.
  4. Kd = 4 nM; site capacity = 16 nM.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Kd = 4 nM; site capacity = 16 nM.

Slope is −1/Kd and x-intercept is total site capacity. The y-intercept would be 4, not a molar site concentration.

Question 42

A monoclonal IgG binds one accessible epitope per soluble antigen molecule, but no precipitate forms. Which modification most directly restores lattice potential?

  1. Present repeated accessible copies of the recognized epitope on each soluble antigen assembly.
  2. Reduce antibody to monovalent Fab.
  3. Replace the antigen with free monovalent hapten.
  4. Eliminate the antibody's variable domains.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Present repeated accessible copies of the recognized epitope on each soluble antigen assembly.

Extended networks require both antigen and antibody bridging potential. Repeated compatible epitopes allow further branching; Fab and monovalent hapten cannot extend it.

Question 43

An antiserum yields weak precipitation at very low and very high antigen doses but strong precipitation at intermediate doses. Which measured quantity explains this pattern?

  1. The fractional occupancy of each independent antibody site.
  2. An intrinsic Kd that is minimal only at equivalence.
  3. Extended insoluble network formation rather than simple fractional occupancy.
  4. A fixed one-to-one complex concentration independent of valence.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Extended insoluble network formation rather than simple fractional occupancy.

Precipitation reflects the formation of extended networks. Both antibody excess and antigen excess can limit bridging; simple occupancy rises with free antigen and does not predict the bell-shaped precipitation curve.

Question 44

A concentrated sample is negative in an agglutination test but becomes positive after moderate dilution at unchanged particle dose. Which hypothesis is most appropriate?

  1. The concentrated sample may be in antibody excess; a dilution series and controls should test this.
  2. Antigen excess is demonstrated because dilution reduces antibody concentration.
  3. The undiluted result proves the antibody is absent.
  4. A dilution-independent threshold in intrinsic affinity is established.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The concentrated sample may be in antibody excess; a dilution series and controls should test this.

The prozone is plausible because reducing antibody can restore bridging at unchanged particle dose. Dilution does not by itself establish changes in affinity, and controls are required to exclude matrix effects.

Question 50

  1. BCR binding → uptake of linked material → carrier peptide presentation → helper T-cell cooperation.
  2. Carrier peptide presentation → helper TCR engagement → hapten binding to the naive BCR as the obligatory initial uptake step.

Question 51

A scientist measures equal binding of two anti-toxin IgG preparations but only one blocks toxin receptor engagement. Which statement is justified?

  2. Binding may target different sites; a functional neutralization assay is needed.

Correct answer: B. Binding may target different sites; a functional neutralization assay is needed.

Antibodies can bind distinct epitopes, only some of which interfere with toxin receptor engagement. A functional assay is needed; equal binding does not establish equal protection or different isotypes.

Question 52

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Every antigen is an immunogen.

Correction: Recognition can occur without effective induction; free haptens are the classical counterexample. Reasoning and examination cue: The word antigen historically suggests generation. A test can distinguish a binding assay from an immunization response.

2. Foreignness guarantees an immune response.

Correction: Processing, available clones, context, dose and tolerance influence the outcome. Reasoning and examination cue: Foreignness is a trend, not a sufficient condition. Compare two hosts exposed to the same material.

3. A free hapten cannot bind antibody.

Correction: It can bind; it generally needs a suitable carrier to elicit an effective response. Reasoning and examination cue: Confusion between induction and recognition predicts the wrong competition result.

4. The helper T cell must bind the same epitope as the BCR.

Correction: It can recognize a carrier peptide presented by the hapten-specific B cell. Reasoning and examination cue: Linked recognition joins material, not identical determinants. Test conjugate versus unlinked mixture.

5. Linear means independent of conformation.

Correction: A continuous sequence may require a particular accessible shape. Reasoning and examination cue: Sequence labels describe residue order, not every structural constraint. Compare peptide and native antigen.

6. T cells always bind free native antigen.

Correction: Conventional αβ TCRs generally bind peptide–MHC complexes; unconventional subsets are exceptions. Reasoning and examination cue: BCR and TCR are both adaptive receptors but see different ligands.

7. Every Ig domain is a separate chain.

Correction: A conventional IgG has four chains, each containing multiple domains. Reasoning and examination cue: The twelve-domain drawing may be misread as twelve polypeptides; reduction distinguishes chains.

8. Each CDR is an independent binding site.

Correction: Three VH and three VL loops contribute to one conventional paratope. Reasoning and examination cue: Count sites, not loops, in a structure-labeling question.

9. κ versus λ determines Ig class.

Correction: Heavy-chain constant regions define Ig classes. Reasoning and examination cue: Light-chain names and isotype terminology may be conflated. Compare γκ and γλ.

10. Fab contains an intact Fc.

Correction: Fab retains recognition but lacks the major Fc region. Reasoning and examination cue: Fragments are defined by cleavage and functional tests. Distinguish papain from pepsin.

11. IgM always has ten sites.

Correction: Membrane IgM is bivalent; the common secreted pentamer has ten potential sites. Reasoning and examination cue: The secreted polymer model is often incorrectly applied to membrane receptors.

12. Secretory component and J chain are the same.

Correction: J chain is produced by antibody-producing cells; secretory component comes from pIgR. Reasoning and examination cue: Both associate with secretory IgA, but a receptor knockout affects them differently.

13. IgE binding to mast cells always causes immediate degranulation.

Correction: Conventional strong activation commonly requires appropriate cross-linking of FcεRI complexes. Reasoning and examination cue: Sensitization is receptor occupancy; compare monovalent and multivalent antigen.

14. A larger Kd means higher affinity.

Correction: A lower Kd favors site occupancy at lower free ligand. Reasoning and examination cue: The word constant can obscure the inverse relationship; calculate half-saturation.

15. IgM always has greater avidity than IgG.

Correction: Avidity depends on intrinsic affinities and target geometry. Reasoning and examination cue: Valence offers potential attachments, not guaranteed simultaneous contacts.

16. Equal Kd implies equal on and off rates.

Correction: Many kinetic pairs have the same ratio koff/kon. Reasoning and examination cue: Equilibrium and kinetics are distinct; compare washout curves.

17. No precipitate means no binding.

Correction: Small soluble complexes may form in antigen or antibody excess. Reasoning and examination cue: Visible network formation is a secondary physical readout; test dilution and direct binding.

18. Equivalence always means equal antigen and antibody molarity.

Correction: Effective valence, geometry and composition determine the optimal ratio. Reasoning and examination cue: Equal numbers of molecules are not necessarily equal numbers of accessible sites.

Revision and mastery

Adaptive recognition links molecular specificity to biological response. Antigens are recognized determinants, whereas immunogens can induce responses in suitable hosts. Native epitopes and peptide–MHC ligands convey distinct kinds of information. A conventional antibody uses paired variable domains to recognize antigen and constant domains to determine important effector and distribution properties. Its chemical affinity is a single-site property; multivalent avidity, lattice formation and biological function additionally depend on geometry, access and context.

Essential equations

  • Kd = [B][A]/[BA] = koff/kon; Ka = 1/Kd.
  • θ = [A]/(Kd + [A]), using free antigen.
  • For depleted ligand: x² − (Bt + At + Kd)x + BtAt = 0; use the smaller physical root.
  • t½ = ln(2)/koff under first-order dissociation without rebinding.
  • ΔG° = RT ln(Kd/c°); c° = 1 M.
  • For independent bivalent IgG: probability of any occupancy = 1 − (1 − θ)².
