1. Recognition is not the same as an immune response

Imagine a protein on the surface of a bacterium that has entered a vertebrate host. The protein is an object that can be recognized in several different ways. An innate receptor might detect a chemical pattern shared among many bacteria. A B lymphocyte might bind a small surface feature of this particular protein, whereas a conventional T lymphocyte might recognize a fragment derived from the protein only after another cell has displayed that fragment in association with a major histocompatibility complex molecule. All three recognition events concern the same biological object, but the information available to each receptor is different. The adaptive system exploits these differences so that a cell can select a target, generate a response and direct appropriate effector mechanisms against that target.

An antigen is a molecular structure that is specifically recognized by an adaptive immune receptor. For an antibody or B-cell receptor, recognition involves direct interaction with a chemical surface on a molecule or molecular assembly. For most conventional alpha-beta T-cell receptors, the recognized surface is a complex formed by an antigen-derived peptide and a major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule. The definition therefore does not require the antigen to be a whole pathogen, a foreign protein or even a molecule capable of inducing an immune response by itself. One bacterium carries numerous molecular antigens, and a single protein antigen can provide multiple distinct sites of recognition. Conversely, a small chemical group can provide a single recognizable structure even when it has little ability to stimulate antibody production independently.

Antigenicity describes the capacity for specific recognition or binding by the relevant receptor or antibody. Immunogenicity describes the capacity to induce an adaptive immune response in a specified host under specified conditions. These are logically related but experimentally different properties. To demonstrate antigenicity one might expose an existing antibody to a candidate molecule and measure the amount of complex formed. To demonstrate immunogenicity one must establish that exposure has stimulated an appropriate adaptive response, for example by comparing antigen-specific antibody levels before and after exposure while including appropriate controls. The second observation depends on many cellular events that are absent from the first assay. A positive binding reaction therefore does not establish that the bound molecule can independently activate naive lymphocytes.

The distinction also applies to the host's own molecules. An antibody raised in one species against a conserved cellular protein can recognize a similar protein in a second species. The ability to bind the protein is antigenicity. Whether the protein stimulates a new response in an intact animal depends on the history and state of its lymphocyte population. The immune system ordinarily limits potentially harmful responses to self components through tolerance, not because self proteins lack chemically recognizable surfaces. A self molecule may be an autoantigen if it is recognized by an autoreactive lymphocyte or autoantibody. The existence of such recognition and the occurrence of pathological autoimmunity are separate questions, because the biological response depends on access, activation, regulation and the downstream effector consequences.

Consider a viral envelope protein that attaches to a cell surface receptor during infection. An antibody binding directly over the attachment surface can prevent that interaction. A second antibody might bind to a distant exposed region and have no detectable effect on entry. A third might recognize the protein only after the envelope has been disrupted. All three can be antigen-specific. They differ in their functional effects because the location and accessibility of the recognized structure determine whether binding alters a biologically important process. This illustrates why neither the total mass of antibody nor its ability to recognize purified antigen alone is enough to predict protection against a pathogen.

At the molecular level, the word specificity means that an antibody preferentially associates with some molecular structures over others. It does not imply that there is exactly one possible molecule in the universe to which it can bind. Adaptive receptors exist as a large population of different molecular surfaces, and related ligands may share features that fit one such surface. Cross-reactivity is a consequence of this finite chemical recognition repertoire, not an exception to the existence of specificity. The distinction will matter when interpreting apparently positive binding to two related proteins and when considering how a microbial determinant might resemble a host determinant.

It is useful to separate recognition, activation and execution as three levels of analysis. Recognition is a molecular interaction. Activation is a regulated change in the behavior of a cell, including signaling, changes in gene expression and, where appropriate, proliferation or differentiation. Execution is the resulting biological action, such as blocking a toxin, facilitating uptake of bacteria by phagocytes or initiating a complement reaction. A soluble antibody can perform recognition without undergoing cellular activation itself. It can nevertheless recruit cells that execute a response through receptors for its constant region. This separation explains the modular architecture that we will examine after learning the nature of the antigen.

2. Why some antigens induce responses more readily than others

Immunogenicity is not a single fixed number printed on a molecule. It is an outcome that reflects both the antigen and the responding organism. Two animals exposed to the same purified protein may generate different responses if their MHC alleles favor presentation of different peptides or their prior exposures have left different populations of responsive lymphocytes. The same individual may respond differently to a protein encountered through a damaged barrier than to the protein encountered repeatedly in a non-inflammatory setting. Thus a description of an immunogenic material is always implicitly tied to a dose, route, host and set of biological conditions.

Foreignness is a useful predictor because adaptive lymphocytes capable of attacking normal self components are normally removed, rendered less responsive or restrained. Nevertheless foreignness is not sufficient. A foreign molecule may be physically inaccessible, unstable or incapable of providing the signals needed to activate the relevant cells. Nor is foreignness necessary in every case; immune responses against self determinants occur when protective tolerance mechanisms fail. The relevant variable is not simply chemical difference from a reference host but the availability and activation potential of the corresponding lymphocyte population. A highly conserved foreign protein may contain only a few surfaces or peptides sufficiently different from self to provide effective targets in one host.

Molecular size often correlates with the probability that a material can display multiple B-cell epitopes and provide peptides for T-cell help. Large proteins can contain numerous folded surfaces and processed peptide sequences. Many small soluble chemicals contain no peptide sequence at all and do not supply conventional helper T-cell epitopes. However, size is a trend rather than an absolute boundary. A large homogeneous synthetic polymer may offer limited chemical variety or be poorly processed, whereas a short peptide appropriately presented in an inflammatory context can induce a response. A numerical molecular weight threshold cannot distinguish all immunogens from all non-immunogens. The relevant questions concern accessible recognition sites, processing and accompanying activation signals.

Chemical complexity affects the diversity of potential interactions. A folded protein provides polar, charged and nonpolar groups in a spatial arrangement shaped by its amino acid sequence. A complex carbohydrate can display repeated sugar linkages and substituents that are absent from host glycans. These structures are recognized differently even when their masses are similar. Repetitive bacterial capsular polysaccharides are important because their regularly spaced features can engage multiple B-cell receptors. Some can stimulate antibody responses without the classical peptide-specific helper T-cell interaction, although the quality and persistence of the response differ from those commonly elicited by protein antigens. We will return to the cellular mechanisms in the chapter devoted to B-cell activation.

Processing is another determinant. Conventional helper T-cell responses generally require that a protein yield fragments that bind the host's MHC class II molecules. A chemically stable protein may contain numerous candidate peptide sequences but release very few appropriate fragments during uptake and degradation. Alternatively, its fragments may be abundant but unsuitable for the particular MHC alleles of the host. In that case B-cell binding to the native protein may still occur while efficient T-cell cooperation is limited. The distinction makes it possible to predict different results from assays that measure native antigen binding and assays that measure cellular responses to processed antigen.

The route by which an antigen is encountered changes the biological surroundings of recognition. A material that remains in the gut lumen may never reach the same populations of antigen-presenting cells as a material entering a tissue through a damaged surface. A healthy mucosal environment can support non-inflammatory immune regulation in response to many harmless substances. Inflammation can instead favor antigen-presenting cell activation, costimulation and subsequent lymphocyte responses. This does not mean that every orally encountered antigen induces tolerance or that every injected antigen induces immunity. Local tissue conditions, the nature of the material, its physical distribution and the state of the responding cells all contribute to the result.

An adjuvant is a component used to enhance or shape an immune response to an accompanying antigen. Some adjuvants promote innate recognition or inflammatory signals; others change the localization or physical presentation of the antigen. These effects can increase the likelihood that the antigen is encountered by suitably activated antigen-presenting cells. It is incorrect to imagine that an adjuvant simply increases the intrinsic affinity of all antibodies for an antigen. Affinity is a property of an individual binding interaction. An adjuvant can alter which cells become activated and how responses develop, but it does not chemically transform an existing antibody into a higher-affinity molecule merely by being present during an assay.

Dose is not a guarantee of a monotonic adaptive response. At a very low dose there may be insufficient antigen available to the appropriate cells. At other doses, responsive clones can be stimulated effectively. In some experimental settings prolonged or very large antigen exposure without appropriate signals can promote reduced responsiveness rather than stronger immunity. Clearance, persistence and tissue injury may also vary with dose. Therefore a graph of the amount of antigen administered against antibody production cannot be interpreted as a simple binding curve: it contains several biological processes, each of which may have its own concentration and time dependence. The shape must be established experimentally rather than assumed.

A useful experimental distinction concerns antigen concentration versus antigen presentation. Suppose two preparations of the same protein are compared at equal total mass. In the first, the protein remains soluble and is rapidly cleared; in the second, the protein is associated with a particulate carrier that changes uptake and retention. A difference in the resulting antibody response does not establish that the protein molecules have different epitopes. To separate these explanations, the researcher can test the two preparations against an existing antibody, verify their structural integrity, measure their distribution and compare immune activation under matched conditions. Only if the molecular recognition assay also changes is there direct evidence that the displayed binding determinants differ.

Immunodominance is the unequal contribution of different antigenic determinants to an immune response. If a protein contains ten distinguishable surfaces, a polyclonal antiserum may bind strongly to only a few. Such dominance depends on accessibility, the frequencies and binding properties of available clones, competition and regulatory conditions. For T cells, processing and peptide binding to MHC molecules also affect which determinants become prominent. An immunodominant epitope is not necessarily the most protective one. A pathogen may expose abundant variable structures that attract many antibodies while retaining a less accessible but functionally constrained site. This distinction is important when considering whether a measured response reflects effective interference with the biological activity of an antigen.

3. Haptens, carriers and linked recognition

Small chemical determinants supplied a particularly clear way to distinguish antigenicity from immunogenicity. A hapten is a small molecule that can be specifically recognized by an antibody but ordinarily fails to elicit an effective antibody response on its own. In classic experimental systems the hapten is attached to a larger immunogenic carrier, often a protein. The carrier contributes properties that the free chemical group lacks, including the ability to be processed into peptides recognized by conventional helper T lymphocytes. The conjugate presents the hapten as a B-cell target while simultaneously making carrier-derived peptide information available for cellular cooperation.

Karl Landsteiner's investigations with chemically modified proteins and small chemical determinants helped establish that antibody recognition depends on fine features of molecular structure. Related compounds could differ in their capacity to inhibit a binding reaction, even though they shared a substantial part of their chemical framework. The experimental principle was to generate antibodies against a determinant in a larger immunogenic context and then examine their ability to interact with related structures. Small changes in the position or chemical identity of a substituent could change the strength of recognition. The conceptual advance was that immune specificity could be investigated as molecular chemistry instead of treating the response to a foreign substance as an indivisible property.

Consider a B lymphocyte carrying a receptor that recognizes hapten H. If H is covalently coupled to carrier protein P, binding of the receptor to H can promote uptake of the entire conjugate. Within the cell, protein P can be degraded into peptides, and some of those peptides can be displayed by class II MHC molecules. A previously activated helper T cell specific for a peptide from P can then interact with the B cell and deliver signals that support its response. The B cell retains its specificity for H even though the cooperating T cell recognizes information derived from P. Their targets do not have to be the same epitope; the two recognition events are connected by uptake of the same antigenic material.

This arrangement is called linked recognition. The linkage matters because it gives a B cell that has recognized a surface structure access to the peptide determinants of the associated carrier. If the free hapten and an unrelated protein are merely mixed, a hapten-specific B cell that internalizes hapten does not automatically acquire enough of the protein to display the appropriate helper determinant. The mixture is therefore not equivalent to a covalent conjugate for a classical hapten-carrier experiment. There are other routes of antigen uptake and physical associations other than covalent bonds, so the strongest general statement concerns linkage within the material internalized and processed by the B cell rather than an absolute requirement for one particular chemical bond.

A carrier-specific helper response can be important even when the desired antibodies recognize only the attached carbohydrate or chemical group. This explains the broad rationale of conjugate vaccines against some encapsulated bacteria. A bacterial polysaccharide linked to a carrier protein can permit carbohydrate-specific B cells to obtain help from T cells that recognize protein-derived peptides. The desired antibody can still bind the polysaccharide on the bacterium. The carrier has not replaced the original determinant, but has altered the cellular circumstances under which the determinant is recognized and responded to. The detailed comparison of immunization types belongs later in this subunit.

A simple experiment separates anti-hapten and anti-carrier recognition. After immunization with H coupled to P, the resulting serum can be tested against H linked to a different carrier Q, against unmodified P and against unmodified Q. Binding to H–Q that is absent with Q alone supports the presence of anti-hapten antibodies or antibodies against a shared linkage-dependent determinant. Binding to P alone supports an anti-carrier component. If a soluble free H molecule inhibits binding to H–Q, this further supports recognition of H itself. It remains important to compare the density and accessibility of H on the different conjugates, because equal total protein concentration does not guarantee equal numbers of available hapten sites.

Free hapten is often useful as a competitive inhibitor in a recognition assay because it can occupy the binding site of an anti-hapten antibody without forming a large network. A monovalent hapten occupies only one binding site and cannot by itself connect separate antibody molecules into an extended lattice. Hence a decrease in precipitation after adding the hapten need not indicate chemical destruction of antibody or denaturation of the conjugated antigen. It can result from ordinary reversible competition. This gives an experimental way to study binding strength without assuming that precipitation is itself the first molecular event.

There are also boundaries to the classical hapten model. The way a small molecule is attached to a protein can produce a newly recognizable surface composed partly of the attachment site and neighboring protein residues. An antibody to that composite structure may bind the conjugated form strongly and the free molecule weakly. Small molecules can modify host proteins and create novel determinants in biological settings, but not every small molecule acts through the same pathway. In particular, some unconventional T-cell recognition mechanisms involve nonpeptide ligands or interactions not adequately described by the standard peptide-carrier model. Such exceptions do not alter the fundamental distinction between recognition and immunogenicity, and they should not be used to replace the classical linked recognition mechanism in a question that explicitly describes it.

4. B-cell and T-cell epitopes represent different molecular information

The part of an antigen that contributes directly to receptor recognition is called an epitope or antigenic determinant. Its complementary surface on an antibody is called the paratope. For a folded protein, an epitope often occupies only a small fraction of the molecular surface. Many additional regions may be exposed, and each can be recognized by a different antibody population. It is consequently more precise to describe an antibody as specific for a determinant on a protein than as recognizing every part of that protein. When two antibodies compete for binding, their epitopes may overlap, or the two antibodies may interfere sterically even if the exact contacted residues differ.

A linear or continuous B-cell epitope contains a contiguous sequence of amino acid residues in the primary structure. An antibody against such a region may recognize a synthetic peptide that reproduces that sequence. Yet linear does not mean that three-dimensional conformation is irrelevant. A peptide must adopt a shape and orientation compatible with the antibody binding site, and the matching sequence must be accessible in the intact protein if the antibody is to recognize the native state. A sequence buried inside a tightly folded protein may become available only after unfolding, cleavage or another structural change. Thus the ability to bind a peptide does not automatically predict access to the same sequence in a living cell or intact virus.

A discontinuous or conformational B-cell epitope is formed by residues that may be widely separated in the primary sequence but close together after folding. These residues collectively produce the molecular surface contacted by an antibody. For example, residues 15, 62 and 114 of a hypothetical protein might be brought together by its tertiary structure. A short peptide containing residues 10–20 would reproduce only part of that surface. Denaturation that separates the residues may abolish recognition even though every peptide bond remains intact. Some conformational epitopes also include contributions from separate subunits of an assembled protein, making the quaternary structure important to binding.

These categories are informative but not perfectly exclusive. A contiguous stretch can form a conformation-dependent epitope if the antibody requires its native secondary structure. A discontinuous epitope can occasionally contain one dominant contiguous region that accounts for a significant proportion of binding. A result from a peptide-binding assay must therefore be interpreted with evidence from intact antigen. The experimental question is not merely whether the recognized residues occur consecutively, but whether the antibody recognizes the same accessible spatial arrangement in the tested material.

Changes in glycosylation offer a further example. An antibody can recognize a carbohydrate determinant on a glycoprotein directly, recognize a surface that contains both carbohydrate and polypeptide features, or bind a nearby protein region whose accessibility is altered by the glycan. Removing the glycan can therefore reduce, increase or leave binding unchanged, depending on the interaction. If a glycosidase-treated preparation loses recognition, the researcher must consider whether the glycan was itself part of the epitope or whether the treatment indirectly changed the protein structure. A separate probe for overall folding or an independent antibody against a distant region can help distinguish these possibilities.

The most familiar conventional T-cell epitope is a peptide that is recognized in the context of an MHC molecule. A T-cell receptor usually contacts both exposed residues of the peptide and part of the MHC surface. The peptide alone is not generally sufficient to reproduce the recognized ligand. Some peptide residues act principally as anchors that stabilize association with the MHC groove, while others contribute to the surface contacted by the receptor. A substitution at an anchor can reduce T-cell activation by preventing presentation even if the substituted residue was never directly contacted by the receptor. A substitution at a receptor-facing residue may leave peptide binding to MHC largely intact yet impair recognition.

A B-cell epitope is not required to match a T-cell peptide in the same antigen. A B cell can bind a folded outer surface of a protein, internalize it and present a peptide from a buried region that was never accessible to the B-cell receptor. Conversely, a surface determinant made predominantly of carbohydrate may be recognized by B cells but cannot be assumed to yield a conventional peptide epitope for T cells. The cell that processes the associated protein produces a different kind of molecular signal. This is precisely the distinction exploited by linked recognition.

MHC polymorphism helps explain why T-cell epitope recognition can vary across individuals who encounter the same pathogen. A given peptide may be effectively presented by one MHC allele but not another. In a teaching experiment, two antigen-presenting cell populations can be supplied with the same peptide while expressing different MHC molecules. If a T-cell clone responds only to one population, the result is compatible with MHC-restricted recognition, provided that peptide loading and cell viability are controlled. It does not establish that the antigen cannot be recognized by any T cell in the other individual; different MHC alleles may present other peptides and recruit other clones.

Class I MHC-associated peptides often come from proteins synthesized within cells, whereas class II MHC-associated peptides commonly originate from material taken up from outside cells. These are useful organizing principles for the next chapters, but neither statement is an absolute rule. Dendritic cells can present some extracellular antigens through class I by cross-presentation. Some intracellular constituents can also enter class II processing pathways. Unconventional T-cell subsets recognize nonclassical presenting molecules or other ligands, including lipid-associated recognition by CD1-restricted T cells. The phrase conventional peptide–MHC recognition is therefore a deliberate qualification, not a claim about every T lymphocyte.

To investigate the behavior of a particular antibody experimentally, compare a native folded protein, the same protein after verified unfolding, and a set of peptides spanning its sequence. Strong native binding together with weak binding to both the unfolded protein and the individual peptides supports a conformational determinant, although altered adsorption or nonspecific loss of antigen must be excluded. Binding to one peptide and the unfolded protein suggests a continuous determinant, but additional testing is needed to determine whether the same sequence is available in the native structure. In contrast, to examine conventional T-cell recognition, one should compare presentation of the relevant peptide with suitable MHC and presentation controls, not simply test binding of a T-cell receptor to the native folded protein.

5. How biochemical experiments revealed antibody architecture

The recognition of antigens by circulating proteins was understood before the chemical structure of those proteins was established. Early measurements of antiserum activity showed that a soluble blood component could bind a target specifically. But the binding behavior did not reveal how many chains formed the active molecule, whether its recognition sites were identical or how one protein could both recognize an antigen and recruit other defense mechanisms. Researchers therefore needed to separate biological activity into measurable molecular components. The experiments of Rodney Porter and Gerald Edelman provided complementary answers to these problems and earned them the 1972 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Porter's 1959 study of rabbit gamma globulin used papain to fragment antibody molecules. An enzyme that cuts a polypeptide at susceptible positions is a useful probe of molecular organization because the resulting fragments can be separated and tested independently. The important observation was that two similar fragments retained antigen-binding capacity, whereas another fragment behaved differently and could crystallize. The two binding fragments became known as Fab fragments, and the third as the Fc fragment. The result established a physical separation between the regions responsible for recognizing antigen and the region that supplies many other antibody functions. The separated binding fragments were approximately fifty kilodaltons each, consistent with the approximate mass of a typical intact IgG molecule being about one hundred and fifty kilodaltons.

In an independent approach, Edelman investigated the polypeptide constituents of immunoglobulins by disrupting the disulfide bonds connecting chains. Reduction breaks covalent links between cysteine residues, which can allow subunits to separate under suitable denaturing conditions. The appearance of larger and smaller polypeptides supported the existence of heavy and light chains. This strategy addressed a question different from the proteolytic fragmentation experiment. Papain showed that different molecular regions retained distinct functional properties, whereas chemical reduction showed that the intact protein consisted of multiple polypeptide chains connected by disulfide bonds. Combining these observations permitted a model in which two identical heavy and two identical light chains assemble into a symmetric molecule.

It is important to understand what a fragment experiment proves. If an isolated Fab binds antigen, the structural information necessary for that interaction is contained in the fragment under the experimental conditions. It does not follow that the Fc region is useless or that the fragment can perform every function of intact antibody. The original antibody has two arms, and removing one may reduce the stability of interactions with multivalent surfaces. It also eliminates Fc-dependent activities from the fragment. The experimental advantage of cleavage is precisely that it permits the separate investigation of binding, cross-linking and effector functions instead of treating a positive antigen-binding reaction as evidence for all of them.

Pepsin digestion provides a useful contrasting probe. Under suitable conditions it cuts IgG below the hinge disulfide bonds, so that the two antigen-binding arms remain connected in an F(ab′)₂ fragment, while much of the Fc region is degraded. The F(ab′)₂ fragment retains bivalency and can cross-link antigen, whereas an individual Fab is monovalent. If a multivalent antigen precipitates with intact IgG and F(ab′)₂ but not with Fab, the simplest explanation is the loss of antibody cross-linking capacity in Fab rather than a loss of specificity. Actual proteolysis products depend on enzyme conditions and antibody subclass; the standard textbook comparison assumes complete generation of the characteristic fragments.

Structural analysis by X-ray crystallography extended the chain and fragment model. A three-dimensional structure can reveal which amino acid loops approach the antigen, how domains pack together and which surfaces are exposed for interaction with other proteins. In a complex with antigen, the contacts provide structural explanations for binding specificity. A static crystal structure does not by itself report the rates of association and dissociation or the full distribution of flexible antibody conformations in solution. These require other types of evidence. The power of the structural model comes from agreement among independent observations, including chain composition, fragmentation patterns, binding experiments and structures of isolated domains or complexes.

A teaching version of the fragmentation experiment can be organized around three preparations made from the same antibody: intact IgG, Fab and F(ab′)₂. Each preparation is tested for binding to a soluble monovalent determinant, bridging two copies of a determinant and interacting with an Fc-binding receptor. If the fragments are pure and properly folded, each retains a binding site, only the intact molecule and F(ab′)₂ possess two sites, and only the intact preparation has the normal Fc region. The three measurements are not redundant: they distinguish molecular recognition, multivalency and communication with effector systems. A negative Fc-receptor result with Fab is thus expected, not evidence that the antibody has lost antigen specificity.

6. The four-chain immunoglobulin molecule

A conventional IgG molecule is built from four polypeptide chains. Two heavy chains are identical to one another in the usual single-specificity antibody, and two light chains are similarly identical. Each heavy chain has an approximate mass of fifty kilodaltons and each light chain approximately twenty-five kilodaltons, although exact masses differ with amino acid sequence, subclass and attached carbohydrates. The total mass is therefore about one hundred and fifty kilodaltons. A molecular mass estimate describes the mass of a single assembled molecular species in daltons; molar mass expresses the corresponding amount in grams per mole. Confusing these units with a concentration in grams per liter can lead to large errors when comparing the number of antibodies in different samples.

The term heavy chain refers to the larger of the two types of polypeptide and does not mean the chain occupies only the lower portion of a Y-shaped diagram. Each heavy chain runs continuously from the tip of an arm into the stem. Each light chain runs beside the amino-terminal region of a heavy chain and ends before the stem. Thus each arm contains contributions from one heavy and one light chain. A covalent disulfide bond ordinarily joins the light chain to its associated heavy chain, and further disulfide bonds connect the heavy chains, particularly in the hinge region of IgG. Numerous noncovalent interactions also stabilize the assembly. Breaking a particular covalent bond does not necessarily immediately dissociate all domains under native conditions because the noncovalent interactions can persist.

The amino terminus of each chain is the beginning of its polypeptide sequence, and the carboxy terminus is the end. The antibody's antigen-binding sites lie near the amino termini of the heavy and light chains. The carboxy-terminal portions of the two heavy chains contribute to the Fc region. This orientation is important when interpreting a schematic. If an antibody has been drawn upside down to show attachment of its Fc to a cell receptor, the location of the antigen-binding surfaces has not changed in relation to chain sequence. The apparent top and bottom of a diagram are graphical conventions, not intrinsic properties of the molecule.

Each light chain has a variable domain (VL) and a constant domain (CL). A typical IgG heavy chain has one variable domain (VH) and three constant domains, CH1, CH2 and CH3, with a hinge between CH1 and CH2. A domain is a structural unit within a polypeptide that has a characteristic folded organization. Domain and chain are not interchangeable terms: the heavy chain is one continuous polypeptide containing several domains. In a standard IgG the four chains collectively contain twelve immunoglobulin domains, eight from the two heavy chains and four from the two light chains. Four of those twelve domains are variable domains. The number of antigen-binding sites is nevertheless two, because each site is formed by the cooperation of one VH and one VL domain.

The variable regions differ substantially in sequence among antibodies with different binding properties. Constant regions are comparatively conserved within a particular immunoglobulin class or subclass and provide interaction surfaces and structural properties characteristic of that class. Constant does not mean absolutely invariant in all individuals or across all species. Genetic polymorphism and differences between isotypes are important examples of variation. Nor does the term variable region imply that every amino acid in that region changes between antibodies. Most of the folded domain requires a reasonably conserved framework, with greater diversity concentrated in particular loops that form the antigen-recognition surface.

The two light-chain types in humans are kappa and lambda. A conventional antibody normally contains either two kappa chains or two lambda chains, not one of each. Both can be associated with heavy chains of any of the five major human immunoglobulin classes. It is the heavy-chain constant region, rather than the light-chain type, that defines whether an antibody is called IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE or IgD. A question that describes a light-chain replacement must therefore be considered in terms of potential changes in assembly or recognition, not an automatic change in heavy-chain isotype. There are specialized antibodies with unusual architectures in some vertebrates, so the two-identical-light-chain statement describes the conventional human molecule.

The standard immunoglobulin fold is based on two beta sheets packed against one another. Conserved hydrophobic residues help stabilize the internal core, and an intradomain disulfide bond provides additional structural support in typical domains. The edges of the beta strands are connected by loops. In variable domains some of these loops have a direct role in recognition. The architecture demonstrates a general principle of protein evolution: a conserved scaffold can tolerate variation at selected surface positions without losing the overall folding pattern. Changes in buried framework residues may destabilize the domain, whereas some changes in exposed loops can alter binding specificity while preserving the fold.

Disulfide bonds operate at two structural levels. Intrachain disulfides form between two cysteine residues within a single polypeptide and stabilize the folded domains. Interchain disulfides join separate heavy or light chains. A reducing reagent that reaches the bonds may disrupt both categories, depending on conditions. Under reducing and denaturing electrophoresis an ordinary IgG yields heavy-chain and light-chain bands because the intact covalent assembly is broken. Under nonreducing denaturing conditions, many antibodies retain their interchain disulfides and can migrate largely as intact covalent molecules. A gel band is not a direct count of all domains; migration reports the size and behavior of the polypeptides or covalently associated assemblies under the selected conditions.

Glycosylation also contributes to the properties of the assembled molecule. Human IgG normally bears conserved N-linked carbohydrate at an asparagine in each CH2 domain. These glycans influence Fc architecture and the ability to interact with certain Fc receptors. Altering their composition can affect effector activity without changing the amino acid sequence of the antigen-binding site. Glycosylation elsewhere, including in variable regions of some antibodies, can also influence accessibility or binding. Consequently a mutation or processing change that affects glycosylation should not automatically be interpreted as a modification of the antigen epitope: the change may occur on the antibody itself and affect recognition, effector action or both.

The familiar Y-shaped representation is an average conceptual diagram, not a rigid three-dimensional object. The hinge and other flexible domain junctions permit movements of the arms relative to the stem and to one another. This flexibility allows the two sites to accommodate antigenic determinants at different orientations and separations. At the same time there are limits imposed by chain length, steric obstruction and the energetic costs of adopting particular configurations. A repeated epitope can be present at a density that is high in chemical terms but still poorly arranged for simultaneous binding by both arms. The difference between antigen density and usable geometry is essential to understanding avidity.

7. From variable-domain sequence to a specific paratope

A binding site is generated where the VH and VL domains come together. Each variable domain contains three especially variable loop regions known as complementarity-determining regions, commonly abbreviated CDR1, CDR2 and CDR3. Together the six principal loops contribute to the antigen-recognition surface of one conventional antibody arm. The surrounding framework supports their positions and shapes. An intact conventional IgG therefore contains two copies of this composite recognition surface, one at each arm, rather than six or twelve independent sites. The distinction between a loop contributing to a site and an entire binding site is particularly important in structural-labeling questions.

Different numbering schemes define the precise boundaries of CDRs in somewhat different ways. For NET and SET reasoning the essential point is that hypervariable surface loops are enriched in residues important for binding. It is too strong to claim that only residues formally assigned to CDRs can influence antigen recognition. Framework residues can affect loop positioning, domain stability or, in some cases, contact antigen directly. Likewise, not every residue within a CDR necessarily contributes equally to binding energy. Some side chains make important contact, others help position neighboring side chains, and some may be largely solvent exposed. Functional importance must be established by perturbation or structural evidence rather than inferred solely from sequence variability.

A small molecule may bind within a relatively deep pocket between the variable domains. A peptide can associate with a groove or a more open surface, and an antibody against a protein often recognizes a broad irregular patch on that protein. These are useful geometric patterns, not an absolute classification of paratope shape. The epitope can include charges, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, nonpolar surfaces and protruding groups. Complementarity involves a three-dimensional arrangement of these features. Two objects can be geometrically similar while having incompatible electrostatic or hydration properties, just as two chemically similar groups may fail to bind if their spatial orientation differs.

The fact that both heavy and light variable domains contribute explains why replacing one light chain can alter antigen recognition even when the heavy-chain sequence is unchanged. The new VL can change the shape of the interface, remove a contacting residue or alter the orientation of the VH loops. A resulting antibody may recognize the same antigen with reduced affinity, acquire a different cross-reactivity or fail to fold properly. None of these possibilities can be decided from heavy-chain class alone. The generation of natural antibody diversity through selection and rearrangement of these sequences will be considered in the next chapter. Here we are concerned with the structural consequence of combining two variable domains.

CDR3 of the heavy chain often contributes strongly to the center of the binding surface and can vary extensively in sequence and length. This property helps explain its importance in the diversity of recognition. However, ranking CDR3 as always the sole determinant of specificity would be misleading. Some antibodies depend heavily on contacts made by the other heavy-chain CDRs or by the light-chain loops. Antigens differ in size, shape and chemistry, and individual antibodies use distinct combinations of contacts. The structure of a complex and the effects of mutations are more informative than a universal rule about one loop.

Consider an antibody that binds a protein through a positively charged side chain positioned close to a negatively charged group in the epitope. Substitution of the positive residue by an uncharged one may decrease favorable electrostatic interaction. Yet an observed reduction in binding can also arise if the substituted residue maintains a loop conformation. A useful control is to check whether the mutant protein remains folded and assembled like the original antibody, using a property independent of the target interaction. If the structural control is normal and changing the complementary antigen residue has a related effect, the inference of a local contact becomes stronger.

An antibody and its antigen need not behave as perfectly rigid pieces. Prior to binding, each molecular partner samples a set of conformations determined by its internal motions and interactions with the solvent. Binding can favor a conformation already present or induce small adjustments after initial contact. The observed complex reflects a balance between favorable intermolecular interactions and the cost of adopting that arrangement. This is why a static lock-and-key drawing is useful for introducing specificity but inadequate as a complete physical explanation. Flexibility makes recognition possible for molecules that are not perfectly complementary in a single unbound snapshot, while energetic and steric constraints still limit the range of accepted structures.

An instructive prediction follows when two antibodies recognize overlapping but distinct epitopes. If antibody A binds a region shared with antibody B, simultaneous occupation may be impossible because the contacting surfaces overlap. Even if the residues contacted by the antibodies are not identical, the molecular bodies may collide. Competition between antibodies thus establishes interference under the tested conditions; it does not alone map every contact residue. Conversely, two antibodies that bind the same protein simultaneously may recognize sufficiently separated regions, or the antigen may be multimeric and offer separate copies of the same region. The topology of the antigen must be known before the competition pattern can be used to assign epitope relationships.

8. Fab, Fc and the division of immune labor

The antigen-binding region of one arm of IgG includes the entire associated light chain and the amino-terminal VH and CH1 portions of the heavy chain. This is the Fab region. The stem of the IgG diagram is formed by the remaining carboxy-terminal heavy-chain domains, primarily CH2 and CH3, and constitutes the major Fc region. The hinge connects the recognition and effector portions, allowing the arms to orient toward the antigen while the stem remains available to other molecules. This architecture separates the identification of a target from the means of acting against it, enabling different antibodies to employ related effector mechanisms despite recognizing entirely different antigens.

Fc receptors on immune cells bind characteristic surfaces of antibody constant regions. An antibody attached to a bacterial surface through Fab exposes Fc regions that can be recognized by receptors on phagocytic cells. Multiple simultaneous interactions can cluster activating Fc receptors and promote uptake. The antibody has therefore converted a molecular pattern on a bacterium into a signal that can be read by a host cell without requiring that cell to have a specific receptor for every microbial antigen. This is a form of opsonization. Opsonization facilitates recognition and uptake; it does not imply that the antibody itself physically engulfs the bacterium or catalyzes all subsequent killing reactions.

Not all Fc receptors have the same effects. Some participate in activation, others can contribute to inhibitory feedback, and specialized receptors help transport or preserve immunoglobulins. For example, the inhibitory Fc gamma receptor FcγRIIB can dampen B-cell responses when appropriately co-engaged with the B-cell antigen receptor. Fc-mediated interactions therefore provide both recruitment of defense functions and regulation. A mutation that disrupts one Fc receptor interaction may diminish a specific effector process while leaving other processes intact. The consequences depend on the receptor, immunoglobulin subclass, cell type and surrounding signals.

Complement provides a second major example. A complement component such as C1q can recognize appropriately clustered Fc regions on certain antigen-bound antibodies. IgM and several IgG subclasses are particularly important in classical complement activation. The arrangement of antigen-bound antibodies matters because efficient triggering requires compatible geometry for the initiation complex. Merely adding soluble unbound IgG does not normally activate the pathway strongly. The resulting complement cascade can produce deposition of opsonins, inflammatory signals and membrane attack in suitable targets, as described in the preceding chapter. Fab supplies target recognition; Fc supplies a connection to the complement pathway.

Neutralization differs because in many circumstances antigen binding itself can directly block a biological interaction. A toxin may need to recognize a cell-surface receptor before entering a susceptible cell. An antibody attached to the toxin at the relevant surface can interfere with that binding. A Fab fragment may retain this direct neutralization capacity even in the absence of Fc. Other neutralization situations benefit from bivalency, cross-linking or Fc-mediated clearance, so the presence of a functional Fab does not establish that the fragment will be as effective as the intact antibody in every context. The critical experiment compares equal accessible binding-site concentrations and observes the particular biological process that is being inhibited.

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, or ADCC, illustrates a further separation of recognition from execution. An NK cell can express FcγRIIIa, also known as CD16a, which binds Fc regions of certain IgG antibodies on a target cell. Engagement and clustering of the receptor can stimulate cytotoxic activity against the coated cell. The NK cell does not need a clonally rearranged antigen receptor specific for the exact molecular epitope recognized by the antibody. Antibody recognition supplies targeting, while the NK cell contributes the machinery for cytotoxicity. This should be distinguished from the conventional cytotoxic T-cell receptor recognition of a peptide–MHC complex.

Membrane-bound immunoglobulin and secreted immunoglobulin share the same fundamental recognition architecture but differ in their cellular context. Membrane immunoglobulin contains a membrane-anchoring region and associates with the signaling proteins Igα and Igβ, also designated CD79a and CD79b. Together these form the B-cell receptor complex. Secreted antibody lacks the membrane anchor characteristic of the receptor form and can act at sites remote from the cell that produced it. The membrane form recognizes antigen and participates in initiating a cellular response, whereas the soluble form can directly bind extracellular targets and recruit other immune mechanisms. The same specificity can therefore be deployed as a sensor on a lymphocyte or as an effector molecule released by its differentiated descendants.

It is useful to distinguish isotype, allotype and idiotype before examining individual antibody classes. Isotype refers to class and subclass differences shared as part of the immunoglobulin repertoire of a species, especially the differences in heavy-chain constant regions. Allotype refers to inherited allelic differences between individuals within a species in immunoglobulin sequences, often in constant regions. Idiotype refers to the collection of distinctive antigenic features associated with the variable region of a particular immunoglobulin, termed idiotopes individually. An idiotope may lie in or near the antigen-binding site, but idiotype is not simply another word for the antigen epitope. One antibody molecule can be an antigen for another antibody directed against its immunoglobulin features.

An anti-isotype reagent directed against a human IgG constant-region feature can recognize many different human IgG molecules regardless of the original antigens they bind. An anti-allotype reagent can distinguish an inherited immunoglobulin variant under suitable conditions. An anti-idiotypic reagent may distinguish antibodies with particular variable-region features even when their heavy-chain class is the same. These differences are conceptually useful for interpreting the results of secondary detection. For example, detection of bound primary antibody by an anti-IgG reagent reveals the class of the primary antibody being detected, not the identity of the original antigen by itself. Appropriate negative controls are required to demonstrate that the primary antibody, rather than the secondary detection reagent, establishes antigen specificity.

9. IgM and IgG: multivalent recognition and systemic defense

The immunoglobulin classes are named according to the heavy-chain constant regions. Human IgM contains mu heavy chains, IgG gamma chains, IgA alpha chains, IgE epsilon chains and IgD delta chains. In a conventional antibody the corresponding variable domains provide antigen recognition in each class. Variation among constant regions determines domain number, polymerization tendencies, receptor interactions and tissue distribution. This allows the immune system to express related recognition information in molecular formats that behave differently. The genetic mechanism by which activated B cells can change heavy-chain constant-region usage is addressed in the next node; the present chapter emphasizes what those molecular formats accomplish.

Membrane IgM is a monomeric immunoglobulin unit associated with signaling partners on the B cell. It is bivalent in the conventional form, possessing two antigen-binding sites. Secreted IgM is commonly a pentamer containing five immunoglobulin units and a joining chain, or J chain, which helps organize polymer assembly. Each unit contributes two potential binding sites, giving a typical pentamer ten sites. Secreted hexameric IgM lacking J chain can also occur. The phrase ten sites describes the theoretical number of recognition sites in the conventional pentamer; it does not guarantee that ten separate epitopes on a particular antigen are simultaneously occupied.

IgM heavy chains have four constant domains rather than the three found in conventional IgG. IgM lacks the classic IgG hinge but retains other structural flexibility. Its large polymeric form is particularly useful for binding repetitive determinants such as those displayed on some bacterial surfaces. If one site detaches, other occupied sites may retain the molecule nearby, favoring rebinding. This can result in a stable overall association despite moderate or relatively low affinity of the individual interactions. The property is especially useful early in a humoral response before selection has produced populations of highly refined binding sites, though high-affinity IgM and later IgM responses also exist.

Secreted IgM is efficient at initiating classical complement under appropriate antigen-bound conditions. Binding to a surface can arrange the polymer so that C1q can engage exposed Fc features productively. In contrast, ordinary soluble pentameric IgM circulating without appropriate target engagement is not simply an uncontrolled initiator of complement. The combination of polymeric architecture and conditional recognition protects against indiscriminate activation while providing effective recruitment when repeated antigen is encountered. A single properly engaged IgM pentamer may provide an activation geometry that generally requires several nearby antigen-bound IgG molecules, but this comparison should not be interpreted as a guarantee independent of surface organization and subclass.

Because of its large size, IgM is concentrated primarily within the vascular compartment relative to smaller antibody classes, although it is also present at other sites and can be transported into secretions in appropriate polymeric forms. The size advantage for multivalent recognition thus entails a tradeoff: the same molecular assembly that provides many sites can influence movement through tissue spaces. A high serum concentration is not equivalent to high exposure at every anatomical site. Biological effectiveness depends on the distribution of the antibody, the location of the target and the effector molecules available there.

IgG is typically a monomer with two binding sites and is the major immunoglobulin class in normal human serum by mass. Its comparatively small size facilitates distribution into extracellular fluids. Humans possess four IgG subclasses, IgG1 through IgG4, differing in hinge properties and constant-region features. They do not all activate complement or bind Fc receptors with equal effectiveness. IgG3 and IgG1 are generally effective activators of classical complement, whereas IgG4 is generally ineffective through this route. IgG2 can activate complement under some conditions, especially when antigen arrangement is favorable. Subclass differences illustrate why a broad statement about IgG function may require qualification when an experimental question identifies a particular subclass.

IgG can neutralize pathogens and toxins, promote phagocytosis through Fc receptors and, in suitable contexts, recruit NK cells and complement. These functions are not encoded in five distinct recognition sites within a molecule. The Fab arms determine the target and the Fc region supplies contacts with different effector partners. A change in subclass can change recruitment while preserving the main antigen-recognition sequence. Nevertheless an experiment comparing subclasses should verify binding directly, because differences in hinge flexibility, geometry or even constant-region influences can affect apparent interaction with a multivalent target.

The neonatal Fc receptor, FcRn, contributes to the persistence of IgG. IgG taken into acidic intracellular compartments can bind FcRn and be protected from degradation, then be returned to extracellular conditions where the interaction weakens and IgG is released. This pH-dependent binding cycle helps account for the prolonged persistence of many IgG molecules relative to several other antibody classes. FcRn also contributes to maternal IgG transfer across the human placenta. These activities involve interaction with Fc rather than recognition of the foreign antigen. A change that selectively disrupts FcRn binding can therefore alter the distribution or persistence of an antibody even if its Fab affinity remains normal.

Maternal transfer of IgG illustrates passive immunity. The recipient obtains preformed recognition molecules without necessarily having generated the corresponding adaptive lymphocyte response. Protection may persist while the transferred antibodies remain sufficiently abundant, but their presence is not evidence that the recipient has established antigen-specific memory. In a developmental or physiological comparison, maternal antibody transfer also differs among mammalian species. Human placental transfer is a useful representative example, not a universal rule that every species acquires maternal antibodies through the same tissue or route.

A conventional IgG4 antibody can undergo a phenomenon called Fab-arm exchange, generating molecules with two different antigen-binding arms under physiological conditions. This is a significant exception to the simple statement that all IgG molecules always carry two identical paratopes. For standard examination problems, an unspecified conventional single-specificity IgG is assumed to have two equivalent recognition sites unless the question states otherwise. The exception demonstrates why biological models are best treated as well-defined reference cases. A feature of one subclass should not be generalized to all antibodies, just as the properties of a pentameric secreted IgM should not be assigned to membrane IgM.
