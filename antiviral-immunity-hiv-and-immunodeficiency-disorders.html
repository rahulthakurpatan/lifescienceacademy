1. The biological problem posed by viruses

A virus has a distinctive relationship with its host. Unlike a bacterium that may multiply independently in an extracellular fluid, a virus depends on a living cell to reproduce. Its genetic information is enclosed in a protein coat and, for many viruses, an envelope derived from a cellular membrane. Outside a susceptible cell, the infectious particle is a virion. Once the viral genome is expressed within a cell, the immune system faces a changed problem. The viral components are no longer all accessible from extracellular fluid, and the resources used for producing new particles belong to the host itself. A defense that destroys every cell containing an unfamiliar molecule would be dangerous, whereas a defense that never interferes with infected cells would permit expansion of infection. Antiviral immunity therefore combines mechanisms that reduce extracellular infectivity, restrict intracellular replication, and selectively eliminate cells when preservation is no longer the best outcome.

The distinction between extracellular and intracellular infection explains why an antibody response alone cannot be taken as a complete description of antiviral immunity. Antibodies bind accessible structures on a particle or on a cell surface. Their large size and usual extracellular location prevent them from freely entering the cytosol of a living cell. A virus that has already reached an intracellular compartment may therefore continue to express viral proteins despite the presence of abundant circulating antibodies. Yet an antibody can still be highly protective by preventing the next round of infection. The relevance of an immune mechanism depends on the physical location of its target and on when it acts in the infection cycle. These considerations also explain why prevention of infection and reduction of disease after infection are related but not identical measurements.

Viral infection usually begins in a limited population of susceptible cells. Susceptibility means that a cell permits the relevant stages of infection, not simply that it belongs to a particular tissue. Epithelial barriers, mucus, fluid movement and local protective molecules may limit the number of infectious particles reaching those cells. If some particles nevertheless establish infection, cell-intrinsic defenses become important. A cell-intrinsic defense is a process operating inside a potentially infected cell that reduces its ability to support infection. It does not require the arrival of a lymphocyte. For example, changes in RNA metabolism or protein synthesis can reduce viral production, while alarm signals released by an infected cell can change the condition of neighboring cells. These local events may precede the development of a measurable circulating adaptive response.

A useful way of understanding the whole response is to separate recognition, communication and execution. Recognition identifies a pattern associated with infection or a specific antigen. Communication conveys information between cells, often through a soluble cytokine. Execution changes the biological outcome: a virion loses infectivity, an infected cell makes fewer particles, or a cell that was producing virus is removed. A cytokine that warns adjacent cells is not interchangeable with an antibody that blocks viral binding, and neither is identical to a cytotoxic lymphocyte. All contribute to protection, but their experimental readouts are different. For instance, the amount of viral RNA in a tissue may decrease without complete elimination of all infectious virus, and a reduction in infectious virus does not imply that every viral antigen has disappeared.

The time required to mobilize each response also matters. Preexisting physical barriers and constitutive intracellular defenses are immediately available. Induced innate responses can arise during the early hours and days after recognition. Antigen-specific lymphocytes already exist before exposure, but an initial response usually requires selection, activation, proliferation and differentiation of relatively rare clones. The resulting adaptive effector population becomes especially important once an infection extends beyond the capacity of local defenses. This sequence should not be memorized as a rigid timetable. The responses overlap, and their timing depends on the virus, tissue, inoculum and prior immunity. In a previously exposed host, memory cells and residual antibodies can change the starting conditions considerably.

An infected organ can be damaged in two broad ways. Viral activity may interfere directly with ordinary cellular function or cause cell death. Alternatively, defensive mechanisms may injure the tissue while limiting infection. Killing a virus-producing cell can be beneficial at the level of the organism even when it reduces the function of the affected tissue. In an organ with readily replaced epithelial cells, a given amount of cell loss may be tolerable; in tissue with limited regeneration, the consequences may be greater. An effective response must therefore be judged both by control of infection and by preservation of physiological function. The immune system is not simply a system for maximizing inflammatory intensity.

A familiar comparison with extracellular bacteria clarifies the special antiviral emphasis. A soluble complement protein, antibody or phagocyte can often contact the extracellular bacterial surface directly. Viruses also have extracellular phases in which antibody and other soluble defenses matter. Once viral protein production occurs in a host cell, however, antigen display and intracellular defense acquire a much larger role. The conventional CD8 T-cell response depends on the presentation of intracellularly derived peptides, while natural killer cells can respond to alterations in the condition of a potential target. Thus a cellular response is not a substitute name for all innate immunity, and a humoral response is not a substitute name for all adaptive immunity. Both categories contain distinct mechanisms whose usefulness varies during infection.

The host does not need to recognize the identity of every virus before responding. Some receptors detect broad molecular patterns that occur during viral infection, whereas clonally distributed antigen receptors recognize particular molecular structures. The resulting dual architecture solves two problems at once: the host can react early without waiting to generate a specialized effector population, and it can later target infected material with considerably finer specificity. The next sections examine the first part of that architecture, beginning with the evidence that an infected cell can transmit protective information to another cell.

2. Discovery and meaning of interferon

An early observation in virology was that exposure to one virus could interfere with subsequent infection. Such interference was not necessarily evidence for an antibody, because the second virus could be antigenically different from the first and because the phenomenon could occur in isolated tissues. Investigators therefore had to distinguish interference caused directly by competition between viruses from interference mediated by a host product. This distinction is experimentally important. If protection requires both viruses to be present together inside the same cell, competition for cellular resources might explain it. If a transferable substance from one population of cells changes the susceptibility of a separate population, an intercellular signal becomes a plausible explanation.

In 1957 Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann reported experiments using chick embryo tissue and influenza virus preparations. Their studies supported the existence of a transferable factor produced in response to viral stimulation that made other tissue less susceptible to infection. The factor became known as interferon. The historical importance of this finding was not that it identified all modern interferon pathways; those were established later. It was that the experiment separated the initial stimulus from a host-mediated protective state. This conceptual distinction remains central to immunology. A host cell can respond to evidence of infection by changing the biological properties of neighboring cells that have not yet encountered a virus.

The logic of a transfer experiment can be understood without specialized laboratory technique. Suppose one group of cells receives a nonproductive viral stimulus and releases a soluble substance. Fluid from that group is separated from the cells and applied to a second group before the latter encounters a test virus. If the second group subsequently has less infectious viral output than a group exposed to control fluid, something transferable may account for the difference. A convincing interpretation requires controls for residual infectious material, cell viability, changes in the physical condition of the fluid and nonspecific toxicity. Even if transfer reduces viral yield, that result alone does not identify the responsible protein. Later purification, characterization, receptor studies and genetic evidence establish the identity and causal pathway more specifically.

Modern immunology distinguishes several interferon families. Type I interferons include interferon alpha and interferon beta, which are particularly prominent in the early antiviral response. Type II interferon is interferon gamma, produced especially by activated NK and T cells. It is important in communication with macrophages and other immune functions and can also contribute to antiviral defense. Type III interferons include interferon lambda molecules, which have important roles at epithelial barriers. All are cytokines, but belonging to the same broad naming family does not make their receptors, producing cells or physiological distributions identical. An examination statement that equates every interferon with interferon gamma therefore confuses categories.

Cytokines are secreted or otherwise released signaling proteins that influence cells expressing appropriate receptors. An interferon need not bind a viral envelope or enter a virion to change its infectivity. Its principal action is to bind a receptor on a host cell and initiate changes in cellular activity. The host cell subsequently produces or activates molecules that can interfere with stages of infection. This explains why an antiviral state may affect several unrelated viruses. It also explains why the presence of interferon in a fluid does not establish that every cell in the tissue has responded. A cell lacking the appropriate receptor or unable to transduce its signal may remain poorly protected even when exposed to the same extracellular concentration.

Interferon production and interferon responsiveness are distinct biological properties. A cell may detect an intracellular abnormality and secrete interferon, yet its own downstream response may be defective. Conversely, a cell that is poor at producing interferon may respond normally to interferon released by its neighbors. This separation between sender and receiver makes interpretation of genetic and pharmacological observations more precise. Measuring secretion alone cannot distinguish the two kinds of failure. A separate measure of an interferon-dependent response, such as the induction of a representative host gene, provides information about the receiver. The distinction also connects this chapter directly to the general receptor and signaling principles in subunit 4.A.

The word antiviral should not be interpreted as proof of absolute resistance. Cellular responses can lower the probability of infection or the yield of virus without reducing either quantity to zero. The effectiveness of a particular interferon response differs between cellular contexts and viruses, and an established infection may already have produced considerable injury by the time the response is strong. Interferon signaling can also produce systemic symptoms and other costs. A response valuable during early local infection may become harmful when unusually prolonged or widespread. The central biological question is how much protection is achieved in a particular tissue and at what physiological cost.

This historical development illustrates an important progression in experimental reasoning. Initial observations revealed a phenomenon, transfer experiments supported a host-derived signal, biochemical work identified molecular families, and receptor and gene studies revealed signaling pathways and effector functions. None of those levels of evidence should be mistaken for the entire chain. For examination purposes, the central outcome of the discovery is that virus recognition can induce soluble host signals that prepare uninfected cells, independently of antigen-specific antibody formation.

3. Recognizing viral infection and communicating danger

Cells contain their own DNA and RNA, so the mere presence of a nucleic acid cannot be a sufficient universal definition of infection. Recognition depends on features of the molecule and the cellular compartment in which it appears. Double-stranded RNA can arise during the replication of many RNA viruses, and some viral RNAs possess terminal features or structures that differ from common mature cellular RNA. DNA appearing in an abnormal cytosolic location can also be interpreted as a danger-associated signal. These distinctions are relative rather than absolute. Host-derived molecules can sometimes occupy inappropriate compartments or acquire unusual structures, which helps explain why protective recognition systems can contribute to inflammatory disease if regulation fails.

Pattern-recognition receptors connect these molecular signs to an induced response. Some are located in endosomal compartments, where they encounter material taken up from outside the cell. Toll-like receptor 3 is a representative detector of double-stranded RNA, while TLR7 recognizes certain single-stranded RNA-associated signals and TLR9 detects characteristic DNA motifs. Other sensors operate in the cytoplasm, including RIG-I and MDA5 in viral RNA recognition and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways. The important examination-level distinction is not an exhaustive list of viral ligand structures but the relationship between receptor location and the source of the recognized material. Endosomal sensing can occur in a cell that has taken up infected material without itself supporting productive infection.

A viral nucleic acid encountered by a receptor does not automatically produce secretion. Receptor engagement must be converted into intracellular signaling, including activation of transcription factors that control interferon and inflammatory genes. Interferon regulatory factors are transcriptional regulators with an important role in this process; other pathways can contribute additional inflammatory signals. In a cell where recognition and downstream communication are both functional, gene expression changes and secreted cytokines can reach nearby cells. If the recognition step fails, the signal may not be generated even though the interferon receptor remains intact. If a later transcriptional response fails, the sensor may recognize its ligand without producing a strong cytokine output.

Different cell types can make different contributions to the alarm. Epithelial cells are strategically located at interfaces where many infections begin. They may produce interferon and respond locally, altering the availability of susceptible cells in the vicinity of infection. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells have a specialized capacity to produce substantial type I interferon responses under appropriate stimulation. Other dendritic cells are particularly important for capturing antigen and initiating antigen-specific responses. Macrophages and additional cells can also participate. The magnitude and source of the cytokine response depend on the tissue and infection; it is incorrect to assign all type I interferon production to one leukocyte class.

Autocrine signaling occurs when a secreted mediator acts on the cell that produced it. Paracrine signaling occurs when the mediator influences nearby cells. Both can contribute to antiviral protection. An infected cell may receive its own interferon signal, while adjacent uninfected cells enter a state in which some stages of infection become less efficient. This local communication is valuable because a virus often spreads from an initially infected cell to its neighbors. Altering the neighborhood can reduce opportunities for further spread before antigen-specific lymphocytes have expanded. However, the warning signal is neither perfectly confined to the infected cell nor guaranteed to reach all cells in an organ at equal concentrations.

The relation between recognition and inflammation can be clarified by considering two hypothetical observations. In the first, an infected cell population releases interferon but little of a selected inflammatory cytokine. In the second, a different population releases considerable inflammatory cytokine but little interferon. These findings need not be contradictory, because transcriptional pathways overlap without being identical. Distinct cell types, receptor inputs and regulatory states may favor different outputs. A student should avoid inferring that an inflammatory response always demonstrates a fully functional antiviral interferon system. Separate measurements are required to establish which response has occurred.

Not all detection is necessarily direct recognition of a viral molecule. Infection may damage cellular structures and generate signals associated with tissue injury. Immune cells can respond to these altered host signals even when they do not identify the virus itself. This helps recruit additional defense, but it also means that an inflammatory signal can persist after a substantial fraction of infectious particles has been cleared. In a graph showing inflammation rising later than viral load, the delayed peak could reflect continued activation by antigen, tissue damage, or changes in immune cell abundance. Timing alone does not establish which explanation is correct.

The organization of the innate response has evolutionary significance. A defense based on a broadly shared molecular pattern can respond to a range of infectious agents without requiring a different gene rearrangement for each virus. Such a system has existed in different forms across diverse organisms. Vertebrate interferons and their receptors are part of a particular multicellular immune architecture, whereas plants and many invertebrates rely on other intracellular and intercellular defense systems, including RNA-based responses in appropriate contexts. These systems should not be equated molecule for molecule. The comparative principle is that living organisms must detect and restrict intracellular genetic parasites, but the molecular solutions vary among lineages.

The availability of several detection pathways provides a degree of functional redundancy. Loss of one sensor need not eliminate all interferon production, since other sensors may recognize a different feature of the same infection or act in other cell types. Conversely, normal signaling in one cell line cannot demonstrate that all tissues are protected. A loss-of-function phenotype is therefore interpreted in relation to the recognized stimulus and the biological setting. This is a recurring theme in immunodeficiency: the consequence of losing an immune component depends on which alternative defenses remain available.

4. From interferon receptor to antiviral state

Type I interferons act through a cell-surface receptor complex known as IFNAR. The receptor has two principal subunits, IFNAR1 and IFNAR2, and is associated with intracellular tyrosine kinases. The extracellular portions participate in ligand recognition, whereas the cytoplasmic receptor-associated machinery communicates the binding event to intracellular signaling proteins. A type I interferon is a protein ligand, so it does not need to cross the membrane to activate this pathway. The signal instead changes the biochemical state of molecules on the cytoplasmic side of the receptor. This is an example of how a hydrophilic extracellular signal can control nuclear gene expression.

JAK1 and TYK2 are the principal receptor-associated Janus kinases in the conventional type I interferon pathway. Following receptor activation, the signaling machinery promotes phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2. These STAT proteins associate with IRF9 to form a transcriptional complex commonly called ISGF3. The complex influences transcription of responsive genes through appropriate regulatory DNA sequences, including interferon-stimulated response elements. The sequence connects the binding of an extracellular ligand to changes in the abundance of particular cellular proteins. It is therefore useful to distinguish a signal transducer such as a kinase from a transcriptional regulator such as the assembled STAT-containing complex.

The overall response is not merely a switch in a single enzyme. A cell may induce many interferon-stimulated genes whose products affect different biological processes. Some products influence the availability or stability of viral RNA, while others affect protein synthesis or other stages of infection. Because these proteins are made by the host, the cell's translational capacity and general metabolic condition influence how effectively the response develops. An increase in the transcript of an interferon-stimulated gene does not necessarily establish that the corresponding protein has accumulated or that virus production has already fallen. A complete experimental interpretation connects multiple levels of the process rather than treating messenger RNA abundance as the final phenotype.

The classical protein kinase R pathway illustrates the relationship between a host defense signal and viral RNA recognition inside a cell. PKR is a host protein kinase that can become activated in the presence of suitable RNA signals. Activated PKR phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2. The initiation factor is normally required to deliver initiator transfer RNA during translation initiation. Its phosphorylation reduces effective initiation by altering the availability of the active initiation machinery. Consequently, protein synthesis can decrease, including the production of proteins needed for viral multiplication. The process is not limited to viral messenger RNAs, so prolonged inhibition can also burden the cell.

Another classical interferon-associated defense involves oligoadenylate synthetase and RNase L. Appropriate RNA signals activate oligoadenylate synthetase, which produces unusual oligoadenylates with 2′–5′ linkages. These products activate the host ribonuclease RNase L. Cleavage of RNA changes the availability of molecules needed for viral gene expression. As with translational inhibition, the defense can have costs because cellular RNAs may also be affected. The important conceptual distinction is between an enzyme that synthesizes a signaling product and the ribonuclease that directly cleaves RNA. The interferon receptor does not itself digest viral RNA, and the synthesis of an oligoadenylate is not the same biochemical reaction as RNA cleavage.

Mx proteins are interferon-inducible GTPases associated with restriction of particular viruses. Their effects vary with the specific protein, virus and cellular context. They demonstrate that not every antiviral effector uses RNA cleavage or inhibition of translation. Energy-dependent conformational changes and molecular interactions can also reduce the efficiency of viral processes. For examination preparation, the important classification is that PKR is a kinase, RNase L is an RNA-cleaving enzyme activated through the OAS pathway, and Mx proteins are GTPases. A question may supply an unfamiliar experimental phenotype and ask which kind of host defense could plausibly explain it; identifying the underlying biochemical function is more useful than memorizing a list of protein names.

The antiviral state is a temporary physiological condition, not a permanent transformation of a cell into a different cell type. Induction of defense genes requires time, and their abundance can fall when signaling decreases. Receptor regulation, inhibitory signaling proteins and turnover of messenger RNAs and proteins constrain the duration of the response. This restraint is biologically valuable because maintaining high expression of defense molecules continuously would consume resources and could interfere with ordinary cell functions. The appropriate response is one that limits infectious spread while allowing recovery of normal tissue activity when the threat subsides.

A functional defect can occur at several distinct levels. A receptor deficiency can impair responses to extracellular type I interferon even if the cell has functional nucleic-acid sensors. A defect in signaling proteins can interfere with the induction of many downstream genes despite normal receptor expression. Loss of an individual effector may affect a more restricted part of the antiviral response because other induced proteins remain functional. The pattern of gene expression, rather than simply the presence or absence of one cytokine, helps localize a defect. For example, the ability of a cell to produce interferon in response to stimulation cannot establish whether it can also generate a normal downstream transcriptional response.

Type II interferon should be contrasted with this sequence. Interferon gamma acts through a different receptor system and commonly signals through STAT1-containing transcriptional complexes. It is associated especially with cellular immune communication, macrophage activation and changes in antigen presentation. These functions complement antiviral defenses but are not identical to the canonical type I ISGF3 response. Type III interferons also use a distinct receptor, whose expression is more restricted in important epithelial settings, although aspects of downstream signaling resemble type I responses. These differences explain why two cytokines can induce overlapping genes without being physiologically interchangeable.

A useful experimental distinction is between the amount of ligand available and the sensitivity of the receiving cell. The same extracellular cytokine concentration may produce different gene-expression responses in two tissues because receptor abundance or signaling competence differs. Conversely, the same response magnitude can sometimes result from different ligand concentrations if cells differ in responsiveness. This is why a concentration measured in serum is not a direct quantitative measurement of every cell's antiviral state. The relevant biological information includes the distribution of the signal, the identity of the responding cell and the downstream changes that actually alter infection.

5. Natural killer cells and the early cellular response

An antiviral state may preserve a cell by reducing its support for infection. There are nevertheless circumstances in which a cell remains a source of viral material and cannot be adequately protected by an intracellular response. The immune system then benefits from identifying and eliminating that cell. Natural killer cells, usually abbreviated NK cells, are innate lymphocytes that participate in this form of surveillance. Their cellular lineage and use of cytotoxic granules resemble aspects of T-cell biology, but their recognition strategy differs from that of conventional antigen-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes. They can respond to changes in the state of target cells without first expanding a clone selected by a rearranged antigen receptor.

NK cells integrate activating and inhibitory inputs. Many healthy nucleated cells display MHC class I molecules on their surfaces. Certain inhibitory NK-cell receptors recognize appropriate self MHC class I ligands and transmit signals that oppose activation. Other receptors respond to signs of cellular stress or altered cell-surface composition. The outcome depends on the integrated balance of signals rather than on a single universal target marker. This arrangement reduces the likelihood of damaging healthy tissue while permitting responses against altered cells. Recognition of an appropriate inhibitory ligand is therefore a form of information about the target, rather than simply a molecular obstacle to immunity.

Missing-self recognition describes a situation in which normal inhibitory information from self molecules is reduced. A cell with diminished appropriate MHC class I expression may become more susceptible to NK-cell attack if activating inputs are sufficient. The principle has special significance in antiviral defense because class I antigen presentation is also used by CD8 T cells. A target that displays less appropriate peptide–MHC can become less readily recognized by a conventional cytotoxic T cell while losing inhibitory signals to NK cells. These recognition systems are complementary rather than redundant. However, the extent of NK activation depends on more than a single measurement of total class I expression, and not every infected cell loses class I molecules.

A stress-induced activating ligand provides a second kind of information. The presence of a stress signal on a target can contribute to NK-cell activation even when the target still expresses some class I molecules. The relevant comparison is the strength and quality of both activating and inhibitory inputs in a specific receptor context. This helps explain why normal tissues are generally spared despite the presence of lymphocytes with destructive potential. It also explains why two altered cells with similar class I abundance may have different susceptibilities to the same NK-cell population. An interpretation based only on a single ligand can overlook other relevant changes in the target.

Cytokines produced during innate responses can increase NK-cell activity. Type I interferons, interleukin 12, interleukin 15 and interleukin 18 have important contributions in suitable contexts. These signals influence the survival, activation or effector functions of NK cells, and their actions are not all equivalent. NK cells can also produce interferon gamma, contributing to communication with macrophages and other immune cells. Thus their contribution is not restricted to direct killing. A study measuring a cytokine produced by NK cells and a study measuring elimination of target cells assess different aspects of the same population.

When a suitable target is recognized, NK cells establish a contact region called an immunological synapse. Cytotoxic granules can be directed toward the contact, promoting the focused delivery of effector molecules. Perforin contributes to the delivery of granzymes, which promote regulated cell-death processes within the target. This mechanism helps limit indiscriminate exposure of surrounding cells to destructive molecules. Nevertheless, eliminating a target also removes a host cell and can contribute to tissue injury when extensive. A reduction in infected-cell abundance should therefore be interpreted together with indicators of tissue integrity.

NK cells can participate in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. In this process antibodies bind accessible antigens on the surface of a target cell, and Fc receptors on an effector cell recognize the constant region of the bound antibody. Human NK cells commonly use CD16, also known as Fc gamma receptor IIIa, in this context. The antibody contributes antigen specificity while the NK cell supplies the killing machinery. NK cells do not need to synthesize the antibody themselves. This is a particularly clear example of cooperation between adaptive humoral recognition and an innate cellular effector.

The absence of detectable antibody-dependent cytotoxicity does not prove that all NK functions are absent. A defect in Fc receptor-mediated recognition could leave responses to stress-associated ligands or missing-self conditions relatively intact. Similarly, a defect in granule-mediated killing may leave cytokine production detectable. Distinguishing activation, contact formation, cytokine release and cytotoxic execution is essential when interpreting functional experiments. The same logical distinction applies to conventional cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which also require several steps between antigen recognition and target death.

A comparison of NK cells and CD8 T cells should therefore focus first on recognition. Both can remove infected cells and both may use perforin and granzymes. Their receptor architectures, activation requirements and relation to previous exposure differ. NK cells are classically classified as innate lymphocytes, whereas conventional CD8 T cells belong to the adaptive system and recognize peptide–MHC through clonally distributed T-cell receptors. Some NK populations can exhibit memory-like enhanced responses after prior stimulation, so the statement that no NK cell can display any memory-like behavior is too absolute. Such nuance does not alter the fundamental distinction between conventional NK recognition and antigen-specific T-cell receptor recognition.

From the standpoint of immunodeficiency, a selective loss of NK-cell activity can compromise control of certain viral infections without necessarily eliminating serum antibodies or all T-cell functions. The effect depends on how strongly the infection relies on early innate cellular defense and which adaptive mechanisms remain available. A combined defect affecting multiple lymphocyte populations may produce a broader pattern of vulnerability. The next stage of antiviral immunity adds a different recognition strategy, based on intracellular antigen processing and the expansion of specific cytotoxic T-cell clones.

6. Antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell responses

A cell infected by a virus can produce viral proteins in its cytoplasm. Because the plasma membrane separates these proteins from most extracellular immune receptors, direct recognition requires an additional mechanism. The class I major histocompatibility complex provides that mechanism by displaying small samples of intracellular protein-derived peptides at the cell surface. This display is not a comprehensive inventory of all intracellular material. It is a continuously generated representation whose composition depends on protein abundance, protein degradation, peptide transport, binding preferences of the relevant MHC molecules and other cellular conditions. In an infected cell, some displayed peptides may originate from viral proteins. An antigen-specific T cell can recognize a suitable peptide–MHC combination without entering the infected cell.

In the conventional class I processing pathway, proteins are degraded into peptides, a fraction of which can be transported into the endoplasmic reticulum through TAP. Appropriate peptides participate in the assembly of stable class I complexes, which subsequently reach the cell surface. This general mechanism was developed in node 4.C.06; the emphasis here is on how it makes intracellular infection accessible to immune recognition. The expression of a viral protein and the presentation of a corresponding peptide are related but distinct events. A cell may contain a protein without displaying a particular peptide in sufficient abundance or in the appropriate class I molecule. Thus, antigen quantity alone cannot establish recognition by a particular T-cell clone.

The T-cell receptor recognizes a combined molecular surface formed by the peptide and the MHC molecule. The CD8 coreceptor associates with class I MHC and contributes to signaling and recognition. The specificity of this interaction explains MHC restriction: a T cell selected for a particular peptide in a particular MHC context may not respond when that peptide is displayed by a different MHC molecule. Classic experiments by Zinkernagel and Doherty in the 1970s, using virus-infected targets and mouse cells of different genetic backgrounds, demonstrated the importance of sharing appropriate histocompatibility determinants for effective virus-specific cytotoxicity. The experiments helped establish the conceptual basis for MHC-restricted cellular immunity. Recognition is neither a response to viral antigen alone nor a response to all self MHC irrespective of the peptide.

The activation of a naive CD8 T cell is a separate process from recognition by an already differentiated cytotoxic T lymphocyte. In secondary lymphoid organs, activated dendritic cells can present antigen and provide costimulatory signals and an appropriate cytokine environment. These signals support clonal proliferation and differentiation of rare antigen-specific precursors. A dendritic cell need not itself be productively infected to contribute to a class I response, because antigen obtained from other cells can sometimes be cross-presented. This is especially valuable when a virus infects tissue cells that are poorly equipped to activate naive T cells. The antigen-presenting cell connects an event in peripheral tissue to the formation of a systemic adaptive effector population.

An effector cytotoxic T lymphocyte, or CTL, can then migrate into infected tissue and recognize a target presenting its cognate peptide–MHC complex. Recognition supports formation of an immunological synapse and a focused effector response. The CTL may release perforin and granzymes, initiating regulated death of the infected cell. Another representative mechanism involves interactions between Fas ligand and Fas on suitable targets. These pathways remove cells in which virus production may otherwise continue. Cytotoxicity is not the only useful CD8 effector function: cytokines produced by activated cells can also modify the infection environment or suppress viral replication in appropriate circumstances. The relative contribution of these mechanisms varies with the infection and tissue.

The distinction between target recognition and killing can be tested through conceptual experiments. If CTLs attach to and recognize antigen-bearing cells but the targets remain viable, the defect could lie downstream of receptor engagement. If a target is not recognized, an intact killing apparatus in the CTL will not compensate for the absence of appropriate recognition. Changes in surface peptide presentation, antigen-specific receptor function, effector differentiation or cell contact may produce superficially similar reductions in killing. Additional readouts, such as recognition-associated signaling, antigen presentation and effector release, help distinguish these possibilities. The principle is applicable to many CSIR-NET questions concerning loss-of-function phenotypes.

A person or experimental animal with a weak CD8 response does not necessarily lack all antiviral defense. Type I interferons, NK cells and neutralizing antibodies can still contribute. However, persistent production of virus inside cells may be less effectively controlled when antigen-specific cytotoxic effectors are impaired. The extent of the deficit depends on the pathogen, whether antibodies can prevent dissemination, the accessibility of infected tissue and the strength of alternative defenses. A comparison between two viruses cannot assume that loss of a single immune arm has an identical effect in both.

The strength of a primary CD8 response depends not only on the initial number of precursor cells but also on their proliferation, survival, migration and differentiation. CD4 helper cells support aspects of activation, effector maintenance and memory formation. Some acute responses may develop considerable effector activity even when a particular helper input is reduced, whereas prolonged responses and high-quality memory may be more dependent on sustained help. Therefore, a failure of CD4 support can affect CD8 protection without requiring that every CD8 cell be directly infected or destroyed. This distinction becomes central when studying HIV-associated immune dysfunction.

A sufficiently strong CTL response can lower the number of infected cells while temporarily increasing tissue damage. In an infected epithelium, removal of affected cells can expose underlying tissue until repair occurs. Inflammation and recruitment may contribute further to the temporary impairment of organ function. Consequently, the peak of clinical disease need not coincide exactly with the peak in viral abundance. The timing and severity of symptoms integrate direct viral injury, immune-mediated injury and tissue repair. A graph showing declining infectious virus but continuing disease is not necessarily evidence that antiviral effectors have failed.

As the amount of infection decreases, most expanded effector cells are no longer needed in the same numbers. Contraction of the expanded population is followed by maintenance of a smaller memory population in many successful responses. Memory CD8 cells may circulate through the body or remain in particular tissues, where they can respond rapidly to subsequent encounter with relevant antigen. Memory is an altered starting condition for a future response, not proof that the pathogen can never reenter a cell. Its contribution should be distinguished from the immediate prevention of entry by residual antibodies. Together these mechanisms can reduce both the extent and duration of subsequent infection.

7. Antibodies, mucosal protection and the humoral response

Antibodies are extracellular recognition molecules whose antigen-binding surfaces can identify accessible structures on virions. A neutralizing antibody reduces the ability of a particle to establish infection. It may interfere with the particle's interaction with a susceptible cell or with another essential extracellular or entry-associated event. The exact antigenic structure recognized depends on the virus and antibody. Neutralization is a functional property rather than a synonym for binding. Two antibodies may bind the same viral protein with similar measurable affinity while differing in their ability to prevent infection because they recognize different surfaces or approach the antigen in different geometrical orientations.

The importance of epitope accessibility can be understood from protein structure. A viral protein may contain many regions that can be recognized after the protein has been isolated, fragmented or denatured. Some of those regions may be buried in the intact infectious particle. A strong binding signal against an isolated antigen is therefore not necessarily evidence for neutralization of intact virions. In basic biotechnology, this distinction separates a reagent selected for antigen detection from one evaluated for functional protection. Conversely, an antibody may recognize a conformational epitope formed by residues brought together by protein folding. A mutation or change in conformation can affect recognition without eliminating the entire protein. These relationships extend the antigen–antibody concepts learned in 4.C.04 into the setting of viral protection.

Antibodies vary in their distribution across the body. Secretory IgA is particularly relevant at mucosal surfaces, where it can encounter infectious particles before or during their interaction with epithelial barriers. IgG is important in blood and many tissue fluids. Early in a primary response, IgM can contribute through multivalent binding and complement activation, while class-switched antibodies emerge as the response develops. This description gives typical roles rather than exclusive assignments. Immunoglobulin class affects location, interaction with effector molecules and molecular organization, but neutralizing specificity also depends on the variable region of the individual antibody.

A neutralizing antibody may reduce the spread of infection without entering an infected cell. Imagine a population of infected cells releasing extracellular particles. If a fraction of newly released particles is prevented from infecting additional cells, the number of newly infected cells may decrease over subsequent rounds. The initially infected cells can remain temporarily active, but their contribution to expansion of the infection becomes smaller. This mechanism is particularly useful when extracellular dissemination is important. It may be less complete when infection persists within long-lived cells or spreads through close cellular interactions that reduce accessibility to some antibodies. Thus, lack of complete clearance does not establish absence of a useful antibody response.

Antibody binding can also recruit additional effector mechanisms. Fc regions can interact with appropriate Fc receptors on phagocytic cells or NK cells, and certain antibody–antigen arrangements can initiate the classical complement pathway. These activities may promote clearance of antibody-coated particles or cells. A distinction is needed between an antibody whose antigen-binding fragment directly prevents infectivity and an antibody whose protective effect depends strongly on recruitment of an Fc-mediated function. Both may contribute in an intact organism, but their relative contributions can differ in simplified experimental systems that lack particular effector cells or complement proteins.

B-cell activation during antiviral immunity depends on recognition of native antigen and, for many protein antigens, assistance from antigen-specific helper T cells. B cells can internalize recognized material and present processed peptides through MHC class II, allowing appropriate interactions with helper cells. This links the specificity of the B-cell receptor to the recognition system of the T-cell receptor. Signals including CD40 interactions and cytokines support important aspects of proliferation, differentiation, class switching and affinity maturation. The resulting antibody response can improve not only in quantity but also in quality. A higher concentration of antigen-binding immunoglobulin is therefore only one possible indicator of a developing response.

Plasma cells and memory B cells contribute differently to future protection. Long-lived plasma cells can maintain circulating antibodies after the initial infection has resolved. Memory B cells can respond to renewed antigen exposure by proliferating and generating new antibody-secreting cells. Preexisting neutralizing antibody acts without waiting for a new clonal expansion, whereas memory B-cell activation has its own time course. The relative contribution of these two components varies among infections and between individuals. A person may retain a measurable memory population despite a decrease in circulating antibody, and the two measurements should not be interpreted as identical.

Passive transfer experiments provide a clear logical test of antibody contribution. If serum containing antigen-specific antibodies protects a recipient relative to appropriate control serum, a transferable soluble component has contributed to protection. Additional experiments may distinguish antibody-mediated protection from effects of other soluble constituents by comparing purified immunoglobulin or by assessing antigen-specific binding and functional activity. Such protection does not demonstrate transfer of the donor's entire immunological memory. Memory lymphocytes and ongoing antibody secretion are distinct from the transient presence of transferred antibody. These concepts prepare the ground for the subsequent chapter on active and passive immunization.

A binding assay and a neutralization assay answer different questions. In a binding assay, a detectable interaction with antigen provides evidence of recognition under the measurement conditions. In a neutralization assay, reduction in infectivity provides functional evidence. A serum sample may contain a mixture of neutralizing and non-neutralizing antibodies, so changes in the total binding signal need not track neutralization proportionally. Furthermore, a neutralization result obtained with one viral antigenic variant cannot automatically be transferred to all related variants. At the examination level, the key lesson is to match the biological claim to the measured endpoint.

The concentration of antibody required for a particular protective effect depends on several interacting factors. Affinity, accessible binding sites, viral particle abundance and the environment of the interaction all contribute. Multivalent binding can produce avidity effects that are not captured by a single-site affinity constant. Hence an equilibrium binding fraction calculated for a simple receptor–ligand system may help explain saturation but cannot alone predict the fraction of viruses rendered noninfectious in a complex organism. A mathematical model remains valuable when its assumptions are clearly stated and when the conclusion is restricted to what the model actually represents.

8. Integrating the antiviral response across time and tissues

The immune response to a virus cannot be understood as a set of isolated pathways acting independently. Consider a respiratory epithelial surface at the beginning of a primary infection. Mucus and barrier-associated molecules reduce the exposure of susceptible cells. Some infected cells produce innate warning signals, and adjacent cells may become less supportive of infection. Chemokines and other mediators alter local cell recruitment. Antigen-bearing dendritic cells can connect these events to activation of lymphocytes in draining lymphoid tissue. As specific effector cells and antibodies accumulate, they contribute to controlling remaining intracellular sources and extracellular infectious particles. The relative sizes of these contributions change during the response rather than remaining constant.

The temporal relation between viral multiplication and immune activation can be represented by a simplified effective growth model. Suppose V represents an amount of infectious virus in a defined compartment. During a short interval, let each unit of V contribute to additional infectious virus at an effective rate g and be removed or rendered noninfectious at an effective rate c. If both rates are proportional to V and remain approximately constant during that interval, the net change follows dV/dt = (g − c)V. The solution V(t) = V(0) exp[(g − c)t] follows by integrating dV/V over time. Both g and c have dimensions of inverse time, so the exponent is dimensionless. The quantity g in this model is not the intrinsic replication rate of an individual virion; it summarizes production in the chosen compartment.

If effective production exceeds removal, the model predicts exponential growth. If removal exceeds production, it predicts exponential decline. The biological advantage of multiple immune defenses is that they can affect different parts of this balance. Cellular antiviral restriction can reduce effective production, antibodies can lower the amount of infectious virus capable of establishing new infection, and cellular cytotoxicity can remove some sources of virus. Although such effects may not be mathematically separable in a real infection, the simplified representation clarifies why a moderate change in each of several mechanisms can collectively change the direction of infection dynamics.

For a hypothetical example, suppose the measured quantity increases from 100 to 400 units over two days during an interval in which the effective net rate is constant. The ratio is four, equivalent to two doublings. The net rate is ln(4)/2 per day, approximately 0.693 per day. If a later interval shows the same quantity decreasing from 400 to 100 units over two days, the corresponding net rate is −0.693 per day. These calculations characterize the net trend. They do not identify whether reduced production, increased removal or a change in available target cells caused the shift. Distinguishing those alternatives requires additional biological evidence.

In reality, a single exponential curve rarely describes an entire infection. Susceptible cells may become limited; infected cells may die; innate and adaptive responses may grow, decay or change localization. Viral material measured in a sample may include both infectious and noninfectious forms. In a tissue that is expanding or being repaired, changes in sample composition can also affect measurements. The model is best used for short intervals or as a conceptual approximation. Extrapolating it beyond the interval without examining the assumptions can lead to biologically impossible predictions, such as unlimited growth in a finite cell population.

A second useful quantitative distinction concerns the fraction of targets affected by a defense mechanism. Suppose an assay initially contains 500 target cells and 100 remain infected after an intervention, compared with 250 infected in a control containing the same number of viable cells. The treated infection fraction is 100/500 or 0.20; the control fraction is 250/500 or 0.50. The relative reduction in infection fraction is (0.50 − 0.20)/0.50 = 0.60, or 60 percent. The absolute difference is 30 percentage points. Confusing relative reduction with an absolute percentage-point difference is a common quantitative error. Both describe the same data from different perspectives.

Even this carefully computed reduction does not demonstrate a particular molecular mechanism. Fewer infected cells might reflect decreased initial entry, inhibition of an intracellular stage, reduced spread, or selective loss of infected cells. The assumption that both samples have the same number of viable cells reduces one possible source of confusion but does not eliminate all differences in cell condition. An experiment linking a receptor, cytokine or cellular effector to protection requires measurements that distinguish the proposed mechanism from alternatives. In this way, quantitative calculation and experimental design complement one another.

The balance between resistance and tolerance is another useful conceptual distinction. Resistance reduces pathogen abundance or infectious activity. Tolerance in this physiological sense reduces the damage associated with a given burden of infection. It should not be confused with immunological tolerance to self antigen, which was discussed in earlier nodes. Two hosts with similar measured viral abundance may experience different degrees of tissue dysfunction if their tissue repair, inflammatory regulation or vulnerability differs. Conversely, a host with a stronger inflammatory response may show a smaller burden but considerable injury. The best interpretation of protection includes both pathogen-centered and host-centered measurements.

A coordinated response also requires termination. When antigen and danger signals decline, continuing maximal activation of cytotoxic cells or interferon production is not necessarily beneficial. The immune system has regulatory mechanisms that limit prolonged activation, and many activated populations contract once stimulation decreases. At the tissue level, surviving cells and regenerative processes contribute to recovery. Persistent infection can interfere with this return to homeostasis because antigenic stimulation continues. The consequences may include chronic inflammation and reduced quality of some antigen-specific responses. The magnitude of an activated cell population can therefore coexist with poor functional control.

Memory changes the dynamics of subsequent encounters. Preexisting antibodies may reduce the initial effective infectious exposure, and antigen-experienced lymphocytes may develop effector responses more quickly than naive populations. The benefit depends on whether the relevant antigens are recognized and whether protective immune components are present where they are needed. A secondary response is not necessarily a repeat of the primary curve with a fixed multiplier. The tissue location, duration since earlier exposure and antigenic similarity influence the response. These principles explain why the immune history of a host matters when comparing infection outcomes.

9. Viral persistence, immune recognition and incomplete clearance

The presence of a vigorous immune response does not guarantee elimination of every infected cell or all viral genetic material. Some infections are cleared rapidly, while others persist for long periods. Persistence is a biological outcome rather than a single molecular mechanism. It may reflect long-lived infected cells, continuing cycles of infection, restricted antigen expression during intervals of latency, incomplete access of immune effectors to particular compartments or changes in the quality of the immune response. These mechanisms can coexist. For examination purposes, distinguish a virus that remains present despite immune activity from a host that is completely incapable of mounting an immune response.

An immune system normally recognizes available molecular information. An antibody needs accessible antigen; a conventional CTL needs an appropriate peptide–MHC complex. If the relevant antigen is not available in a particular state of infection, the corresponding immune response may be unable to act efficiently at that moment. This does not imply that the immune cell has lost its specificity or that all protective antibodies are absent. It means the relationship between an effector and its target depends on the state of the infection. Latent infection is one setting in which this distinction is important, because a cell may contain a viral genome while producing relatively little of the antigen normally associated with active infection.

The general concept of immune evasion refers to pathogen-associated processes or conditions that reduce the effectiveness of host immune recognition or control. At the NET/SET level, it is sufficient to recognize the categories of altered antigen recognition, incomplete innate activation and persistence in states that are not readily detected. These categories should be used to explain observed immune outcomes, not as a detailed account of pathogen engineering or replication. A persistent infection cannot be attributed to any one category without evidence. For example, the detection of antibodies alongside a continuing viral signal demonstrates that antibody production occurred, but it does not establish why complete clearance was unsuccessful.

Variation in antigenic structures can change the recognition of a viral particle by some antibodies. Since a polyclonal response usually contains antibodies with different specificities, altered recognition by one clone does not imply the loss of all serum binding or all protection. Similarly, class I presentation and T-cell receptor recognition depend on particular molecular combinations; differences in antigen or host MHC can change the response in a clone-specific manner. The population-level outcome involves multiple clones and several immune mechanisms, so single-molecule observations need to be interpreted in the broader biological setting.

Chronic stimulation can change the physiological state of T cells. Antigen-experienced cells exposed to prolonged antigen and inflammatory signals may exhibit reduced proliferative or effector capacity, a phenomenon often described as functional exhaustion in appropriate contexts. Exhaustion is not simply the absence of T cells. A population can be numerically detectable while displaying a diminished ability to produce particular cytokines or eliminate targets. Conversely, a lower absolute cell count does not by itself establish exhaustion, because the remaining cells may retain strong per-cell function. Counts and functional measurements are complementary indicators rather than interchangeable definitions.

Persistent stimulation also alters the environment in which B cells, helper T cells, antigen-presenting cells and innate effectors interact. Dysregulated activation may interfere with tissue organization or maintenance of immune-cell populations. As a result, the consequences of persistence can extend beyond the immune response to the original virus. HIV is a particularly instructive example because it affects an important organizing population of the adaptive immune system. Instead of merely escaping one antibody clone, the infection can progressively disturb the coordination of several otherwise distinct defense mechanisms.

It is important not to equate immune persistence with an inevitable clinical progression in all circumstances. Outcomes differ among viruses, hosts and environments, and immune control can remain substantial even when a pathogen is not completely eliminated. A latent state may be controlled for long periods without ongoing extensive tissue injury. Persistent antigen and active infectious-virus production are also not identical observations. A molecular test detecting a viral sequence may answer a different question from an assay of infectious particles or an evaluation of disease. Proper interpretation depends on the biological material measured and the limitations of the method.

10. HIV as an acquired disruption of immune coordination

The historical recognition of AIDS began with unusual opportunistic illnesses reported in 1981, including Pneumocystis pneumonia. The pattern suggested that an acquired defect in cellular defense was permitting infections that are often controlled in immunocompetent hosts. Subsequent identification of HIV connected that syndrome to a specific infectious cause. The history matters because the syndrome was recognized through the consequences of impaired immunity before all details of the responsible virus were understood. In contemporary terminology, Pneumocystis jirovecii is a fungal organism, and advanced immune impairment can also increase susceptibility to infections such as cryptococcosis. These examples illustrate reduced host defense rather than a unique diagnostic rule.

Human immunodeficiency virus, abbreviated HIV, is important to immunology because an infection can progressively impair the system needed to control many other infections. The term HIV refers to the virus, whereas acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, or AIDS, refers to an advanced state of immune deficiency associated with HIV infection. These words describe different biological entities. Infection with HIV does not by itself establish that a person has reached AIDS. Effective medical management can substantially alter the course of infection, so the natural history described for uncontrolled infection should not be interpreted as the inevitable experience of every person living with HIV.

CD4 is a cell-surface molecule expressed by helper T lymphocytes and certain other cells. Its normal immune role is related to recognition of peptide–MHC class II complexes by appropriate T cells. HIV has a particular association with CD4-expressing susceptible cells, especially CD4 T cells; other susceptible cells may include cells of the monocyte–macrophage lineage. The mere presence of CD4 is not the only determinant of cellular susceptibility. This chapter emphasizes the immunological consequence of disturbing CD4 helper-cell populations rather than the detailed molecular processes of viral entry and replication, which belong to the host–pathogen subunit.

The importance of CD4 T cells follows from their coordinating functions. A helper T cell does not perform every effector reaction itself. It can support B-cell differentiation and high-quality antibody responses, promote or sustain aspects of cytotoxic T-cell immunity, and activate macrophages through appropriate cell interactions and cytokines. Regulatory subsets also contribute to controlling immune responses. Losing helper-cell populations can therefore produce deficiencies in several downstream activities even when the cells performing those activities remain present. This is a systems-level example of how removal of an intermediate component can affect multiple branches of a network.

During early uncontrolled HIV infection, viral abundance can rise rapidly and CD4-cell populations may decline. Innate responses and HIV-specific adaptive responses subsequently contribute to reducing the initial peak in viral abundance. In many individuals, a period of relative virological and immunological stability follows, although infection persists. The absence of prominent symptoms during part of this period does not imply that the virus has been eliminated or that all immune functions are normal. Over time without effective control, progressive CD4 loss and immune dysfunction may develop, with considerable variability in the rate and pattern of change.

The early decline and partial recovery of circulating CD4 cells cannot be interpreted solely as a simple measure of the number of cells directly killed by virus. Changes in cell trafficking, distribution between blood and tissues, death of infected and uninfected cells, activation-associated injury and changes in replenishment can all contribute to observed counts. A blood sample is a window into a circulating compartment, not a complete inventory of all lymphoid tissue. HIV-associated immune damage in lymphoid and mucosal tissues also influences coordination of immune responses. This distinction prevents a misleading interpretation in which the measured blood count is assumed to report a single molecular process directly.

HIV-specific cytotoxic T-cell responses help limit infected-cell populations during infection. Antibodies are also produced against HIV-associated antigens. The existence of both responses demonstrates that adaptive immunity has not simply been absent from the beginning. However, ongoing infection may persist despite these defenses, and the loss of effective helper-cell support and chronic immune activation can contribute to deteriorating control. Antibody positivity indicates recognition and exposure in an appropriate testing context, not proof that all infectious virus has been eliminated. The antiviral response to HIV must therefore be distinguished from immune protection against unrelated infections that may become inadequate as the disease progresses.

The persistence of infection involves long-lived cellular sources of viral genetic material and changes in immune effectiveness over time. At the level needed here, a reservoir is a population or compartment in which virus-associated genetic material capable of contributing to continuing infection remains present despite substantial suppression of circulating virus. The existence of a reservoir illustrates why lowering a measurement of circulating virus is not automatically equivalent to eradicating every relevant infected cell. Detailed molecular explanations of reservoir establishment are beyond the current scope. The important immunological point is that apparent control of one measured compartment can coexist with persistent infection elsewhere.

HIV also demonstrates that immune deficiency and immune activation are not opposites in every biological setting. Chronically stimulated immune populations can remain activated while losing effective coordination or functional capacity. Inflammation, activation-related cell turnover and disrupted tissue environments can accompany declining CD4-cell numbers. Therefore, an elevated inflammatory marker does not establish adequate protection against opportunistic infection. The immune system may be active in one dimension and deficient in another. This insight is relevant not only to HIV but also to other conditions in which immune regulation and defense are simultaneously disturbed.

The relationships between viral burden and CD4-cell abundance are often represented by two curves. In a simplified untreated-course diagram, viral burden initially rises and then falls from its early peak as immune control develops, while the CD4 count may initially fall and partially recover. During prolonged uncontrolled infection the overall CD4 trend may be downward, and viral burden may eventually rise. Such a diagram is schematic, not a diagnostic prediction for a particular individual. The curves vary with host factors, time, treatment and the measurement method, and they do not show every event occurring in lymphoid tissue. Their value is to demonstrate changing relationships between the pathogen and the defense system.

A quantitative interpretation of CD4 measurement requires care with denominators. Suppose a blood sample contains 1,800 lymphocytes per microlitre and CD4 T cells constitute 25 percent of these lymphocytes. Under this definition, the estimated absolute CD4 count is 1,800 × 0.25 = 450 cells per microlitre. If the percentage remains at 25 percent but the lymphocyte count falls to 1,000 per microlitre, the absolute count falls to 250 per microlitre. A constant percentage is therefore compatible with a changing absolute abundance. In an examination question, the stated denominator must be used; a percentage among T cells cannot be multiplied by the total lymphocyte count without knowing the T-cell fraction.

The broad biological consequences of CD4 loss can be predicted by tracing helper-dependent functions. A macrophage may still be capable of engulfing a pathogen but receive inadequate activating signals. A B cell may recognize native antigen but receive poor support for a high-quality T-dependent response. A CD8 cell may remain present but show impaired maintenance or memory under particular conditions. These defects help explain susceptibility to opportunistic infections, which take advantage of reduced host defenses. Different opportunists exploit different weaknesses, and the precise pattern cannot be inferred from CD4 abundance alone without considering the rest of the host response.

An opportunistic infection is an infection that occurs more readily or becomes more severe when usual host defenses are compromised. It need not be caused by an organism that is harmless in all immunocompetent hosts. Some organisms can cause disease in a healthy host and produce more extensive disease when immunity is impaired. The concept is therefore relational: the biological opportunity available to the organism changes as host defense changes. HIV-associated susceptibility illustrates why studying the host immune system is essential to understanding disease outcomes even when the infectious agent itself has not changed.

Finally, HIV should not be conflated with every acquired immune deficiency. Malnutrition, medical immunosuppression and other conditions can also impair immune responses, but they may alter different combinations of cells and functions. Similarly, an inherited defect of lymphocyte development can produce severe combined immunodeficiency without viral infection being its cause. Comparing these situations through the affected immune component is more scientifically useful than grouping them solely by the severity of infection. The remaining sections develop this classification systematically.

11. Understanding primary and secondary immunodeficiency

Immunodeficiency means that one or more components of immune defense are unable to provide their usual level of protection. The term does not imply that every immune response is absent. A defect may primarily affect the number of a particular immune-cell population, the ability of those cells to recognize stimuli, the capacity to communicate with other cells, or the execution of an effector function. The consequences therefore depend on the particular component that fails. A person with a defect in antibody production may retain substantial cellular immunity, whereas a defect in early lymphocyte development may affect several adaptive and innate lymphocyte populations simultaneously.

Primary immunodeficiencies are commonly described today within the broader category of inborn errors of immunity. Many arise from pathogenic variants affecting proteins necessary for immune development, recognition, signaling or execution. Although the underlying abnormality can be present from conception, its clinical consequences need not become evident immediately after birth. The onset of disease depends on the affected mechanism, environmental exposure, residual activity and other sources of protection. A congenital disorder and an inherited disorder are closely related concepts in this chapter, but the terms are not logically identical: a congenital condition is present at birth, whereas an inherited condition is transmitted genetically. A new pathogenic variant can also cause an inborn immune disorder without an obvious family history.

Secondary or acquired immunodeficiency develops as a consequence of another condition or exposure that impairs an initially available immune system. HIV-associated CD4 loss is a major example. Severe malnutrition, some medical treatments and certain systemic diseases may produce additional forms of immune impairment. A secondary disorder is not necessarily mild, temporary or readily reversible. Conversely, an inherited disorder does not necessarily eliminate every immune function. The etiological classification answers how the defect arose; the functional classification answers which components of defense are affected. Keeping these two classifications separate is essential in interpreting biological cases.

Immunodeficiencies have contributed to scientific understanding because they reveal the functions of individual components in a whole organism. When a specific molecular defect is associated with an otherwise characteristic immune phenotype, the affected pathway can be linked to the resulting change in susceptibility. The evidence becomes more convincing when the genetic abnormality, cellular phenotype and functional measurement agree. A single observed infection is usually insufficient to infer a unique molecular cause. Many organisms can cause disease under several forms of immune impairment, and exposure history also influences which infections are observed. Textbook associations are useful patterns, not universal rules for individual diagnosis.

Deficiencies of B cells or antibodies illustrate impairment of adaptive humoral defense. Deficiencies of T cells illustrate disruption of adaptive cellular responses and, in the case of helper-cell defects, secondary effects on other populations. Combined deficiencies affect both major adaptive arms, though their exact lymphocyte counts vary with the molecular cause. Phagocyte defects impair processes such as recruitment, engulfment or intracellular microbial killing. Complement defects affect one or more soluble effector pathways. Innate sensing and cytokine pathway defects can also compromise relatively selective responses. These categories provide a way to reason from normal biological function to a plausible phenotype rather than memorizing disorder names as disconnected facts.

The term immunodeficiency must also be distinguished from autoimmunity and hypersensitivity. Autoimmunity involves recognition or response directed against the organism's own constituents in a way that can contribute to disease. Hypersensitivity refers to damaging immune responses associated with particular immunological mechanisms. An immunodeficiency is defined by inadequate defense or deficient immune function, but these categories can overlap in real biological systems. Defective lymphocyte regulation, abnormal clearance of immune material or persistent infection can contribute to inflammation or autoimmune manifestations in some inborn errors of immunity. The coexistence of poor protection and immune-mediated damage is therefore not paradoxical.

The following sections use representative disorders to establish causal chains. The sequence starts with a normal component and asks what happens when it is absent, defective or insufficient. For each disorder, the relevant questions are whether the affected cells develop, whether they are present in a measurable compartment, whether they perform their expected task, and which remaining mechanisms can compensate. This approach also prevents a frequent error in which any reduction in immune function is automatically described as a reduction in the total number of white blood cells.

12. B-cell and antibody deficiencies

Normal humoral immunity depends on a developmental sequence in which B-lineage cells rearrange antigen-receptor genes, express intermediate receptor complexes, receive appropriate developmental signals and eventually mature. A mature B cell can respond to antigen and differentiate into populations that support antibody production and memory. A defect early in this developmental sequence can therefore reduce both the number of circulating mature B cells and the capacity to make immunoglobulins. A defect later in differentiation may leave mature B-cell numbers relatively preserved while impairing antibody production or the formation of particular immunoglobulin classes.

X-linked agammaglobulinemia, often abbreviated XLA, is a classical example of a defect in B-cell development. The condition is associated with pathogenic variants in the gene encoding Bruton's tyrosine kinase, BTK. This intracellular kinase contributes to signals needed during B-cell maturation and receptor-dependent responses. When BTK function is severely impaired, progression through important developmental checkpoints becomes inefficient, leading to a marked reduction in mature B cells and broad impairment of immunoglobulin production. This causal relationship links the general signaling concepts of subunit 4.A with the B-cell developmental principles introduced in node 4.C.07.

The developmental phenotype is particularly informative. If serum immunoglobulins are very low and mature B lymphocytes are also markedly reduced, the observations suggest that the problem may occur before a mature antibody-secreting population can be established. The defect is not simply that a normally developed plasma cell is unable to release one selected antibody molecule. Conversely, if mature B cells are present in substantial numbers but specific antibody responses are weak, a later functional or differentiation defect becomes a relevant possibility. Neither pattern alone identifies a particular gene, but each helps distinguish stages of the underlying biological process.

The inheritance pattern of classical XLA reflects the location of BTK on the X chromosome. In the usual textbook model, a male with a pathogenic variant in his single X-linked BTK copy has no second X-linked copy to provide normal function. A heterozygous female carrier generally retains sufficient function in the developing B-cell population, although biological exceptions and individual variation exist. In a simplified pedigree where the mother is heterozygous and the father has an unaffected X chromosome, each son has a one-half probability of inheriting the variant. Each daughter has a one-half probability of inheriting the maternal variant and being a heterozygous carrier. These are conditional probabilities for each child, not a prediction that exactly half of the children in a small family must be affected or carriers.

The importance of antibodies in protection becomes evident from the susceptibility associated with broad humoral deficiency. Antibodies can neutralize extracellular particles and toxins, promote opsonization and help recruit complement. When antibody production is impaired, clearance of certain extracellular bacteria, including encapsulated bacteria, may be less effective. A capsule can interfere with direct recognition or ingestion by phagocytes, making opsonization especially valuable. Antibody defects can also compromise defense against particular viruses, including some enteroviruses. It is therefore incorrect to conclude that antibodies protect only against bacteria or that all antiviral defense is automatically normal when T-cell function is preserved.

Early-life antibody protection provides an important natural example of compensation. Maternal IgG transferred before birth can contribute to a newborn's circulating antibody pool for a limited period. This antibody was produced by the mother and is not evidence that the infant has established its own normal antigen-specific response. As maternally derived antibody declines, the ability of the child's own B-cell system to sustain protection becomes increasingly important. A severe B-cell developmental defect may consequently become more evident after this temporary source of antibody decreases. The exact timing differs among individuals and cannot be inferred from a single universal age threshold.

Other humoral deficiencies demonstrate that low immunoglobulin concentration is not always equivalent to absence of mature B cells. In common variable immunodeficiency, for example, patients may have circulating B cells yet show impaired antibody production and differentiation. In selective IgA deficiency, the disturbance is predominantly related to IgA rather than a uniform loss of every immunoglobulin class. Some individuals with selective IgA deficiency have few noticeable manifestations, partly because other mechanisms can contribute to mucosal protection. These comparisons illustrate why the type of antibody defect and the availability of compensatory mechanisms are biologically relevant.

A second contrast concerns class switching. In a disorder where signals necessary for normal T-dependent class switching are disrupted, some immunoglobulin production may remain while class-switched responses are impaired. Defects involving CD40 ligand, for example, can interfere with important interactions between helper T cells and B cells and can also affect cellular immunity through other CD40-dependent functions. A phenotype commonly described in relation to antibody class therefore need not be exclusively a B-cell-intrinsic defect. The affected molecule participates in a network of cell interactions, and its absence can have wider consequences than the serum immunoglobulin pattern initially suggests.

When interpreting a hypothetical antibody-deficiency dataset, it is helpful to compare total immunoglobulins, mature B-cell numbers and the ability to produce a response to a defined antigen. These measurements are not substitutes for one another. A total immunoglobulin measurement summarizes molecules present in the sampled fluid, while a cell count provides a population measurement and an antigen-specific functional response provides evidence of performance. A B-cell population can be present without producing an effective response, and measurable antibody can originate from an external source rather than the host's own cells. The combination of observations makes a functional inference more reliable.

13. T-cell and severe combined immunodeficiencies

T cells depend on the coordinated development of lymphoid precursors, thymic maturation, antigen-receptor formation, selection and survival. Defects in these processes can reduce the number or competence of conventional T lymphocytes. Since helper and cytotoxic populations contribute to several branches of host defense, impaired T-cell immunity may affect control of viruses, intracellular microbes and opportunistic fungi, as well as T-dependent antibody responses. The pattern is generally broader than the direct loss of a single antibody class. However, not every T-cell defect has the same severity or cellular distribution.

A thymic developmental defect illustrates the importance of the tissue environment. The thymus provides signals and cellular interactions necessary for normal T-cell maturation. If this environment is severely impaired, production of mature T cells can decrease even if the original hematopoietic precursors are present. DiGeorge syndrome, associated in many cases with a 22q11.2 deletion, can include thymic hypoplasia and variable impairment of T-cell immunity. The immune phenotype varies considerably, and the condition involves additional developmental consequences not required for this chapter. The relevant lesson is that an immune deficiency can arise from a defect in an organ supporting development rather than directly from an abnormal antigen receptor in every affected cell.

Severe combined immunodeficiency, abbreviated SCID, describes a group of disorders characterized by profound impairment of T-cell immunity together with major disruption of adaptive humoral function. The term combined refers to the functional involvement of both T-cell and B-cell defense. It does not require that the numerical count of each lymphocyte population be zero. In some forms, B cells are detectable but fail to participate effectively in adaptive protection because necessary helper interactions are missing or cytokine signaling is defective. Natural killer cells may also be reduced in some forms. Different genetic causes of SCID can therefore produce different patterns of measurable lymphocyte subsets.

A classical X-linked form of SCID involves IL2RG, which encodes the common gamma chain shared by several cytokine receptor systems. The shared nature of this protein explains why a single molecular defect can affect responses to more than one cytokine. In particular, disrupted signaling through pathways important for T-cell and NK-cell development contributes to the typical severe phenotype. B cells can remain numerically present in the usual textbook pattern, designated T−B+NK−, but effective antibody responses are substantially impaired. The plus symbol in this notation indicates relative presence of the measured population, not proof of normal immune function.

The relationship between receptor components and developmental signals can be understood through the general principles of signaling. A cytokine may be available in the extracellular environment, yet a precursor cell unable to assemble or use an essential receptor complex cannot receive the relevant developmental instruction normally. Another cell type may rely on a different set of cytokines or use a pathway not affected in the same way. Consequently, a shared receptor defect does not necessarily reduce all leukocytes equally. The characteristic lymphocyte distribution reflects the dependencies of particular developmental pathways, rather than a nonspecific destruction of every bone marrow-derived cell.

A different mechanism occurs in SCID associated with deficiency of adenosine deaminase, ADA. ADA participates in purine metabolism by catalyzing deamination of adenosine and deoxyadenosine. Severe loss of its function can lead to accumulation of harmful purine-related metabolites, including elevated deoxyadenosine nucleotide pools. These metabolic disturbances are particularly damaging to developing lymphocytes and can interfere with their survival and proliferation. Classical severe ADA deficiency may consequently produce marked reductions in T, B and NK populations. This example links the nucleotide metabolism of Unit 1 to the development and function of the immune system.

Defects involving the recombination-activating genes RAG1 or RAG2 provide another route to severe combined immune impairment. Conventional B and T cells require rearrangement of antigen-receptor gene segments during development. When this process fails severely, the cells cannot progress normally through receptor-dependent developmental checkpoints. NK cells do not require the same antigen-receptor gene rearrangement, so they can remain present in a typical severe RAG-deficient phenotype, designated T−B−NK+. Partial-function variants may produce more complex or less severe phenotypes. A numerical classification should therefore always be understood as a representative pattern rather than a rule that covers every variant.

Comparing IL2RG, ADA and RAG-associated defects reveals three distinct causal pathways leading to a shared syndrome. The first disrupts important cytokine-receptor signals, the second alters a metabolic environment necessary for lymphocyte survival, and the third impairs generation of conventional antigen receptors. Each can substantially impair adaptive immunity, but the expected patterns of lymphocyte development differ. The comparison is useful because a student can predict a phenotype from the normal molecular function rather than rely solely on memorizing the names of disorders. It also illustrates that a syndrome is a collection of clinical or biological features, not necessarily a single gene defect.

The breadth of susceptibility in severe combined immunodeficiency reflects the loss of complementary defenses. Antibody responses against extracellular organisms are compromised, while antigen-specific cellular responses against intracellular infection are also severely affected. Deficiency of other cell populations may further reduce early protection. The existence of innate phagocytes and complement does not restore all missing adaptive functions. Conversely, the presence of some residual adaptive cells does not necessarily indicate that their numbers and functions are sufficient for protection. Functional consequences depend on the degree of residual competence and the biological challenges encountered.

An important comparison is between inherited SCID and acquired HIV-associated immunodeficiency. Both can disturb multiple adaptive functions, but the origins and characteristic cellular changes differ. In classical SCID, a defect in immune development or related function may be present from early life. In HIV infection, susceptibility is associated with progressive disruption of an already developed immune system, especially through CD4-cell loss and altered immune coordination. The comparison should not be interpreted as identical cellular phenotypes. It demonstrates that profound functional consequences can arise either from failure to establish a network normally or from later disruption of a network that was previously established.

14. Phagocyte defects: recruitment, engulfment and killing

Phagocytes provide a useful contrast with adaptive lymphocyte deficiencies because their functions include a series of mechanically and biochemically distinct operations. A neutrophil may need to reach an infected site, recognize material directly or through opsonins, engulf it, and carry out antimicrobial processes within the resulting compartment. Failure at any of these stages can reduce protection. A normal number of circulating neutrophils does not demonstrate that these cells can migrate into tissue, engulf a microorganism or kill the material they ingest. A functional interpretation must consider the individual stages of the response.

Chronic granulomatous disease, CGD, is a classical example of defective oxidative antimicrobial function in phagocytes. The disorder results from pathogenic variants affecting components or function of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase system. This enzyme complex transfers electrons from NADPH to molecular oxygen, supporting production of superoxide and related reactive species during the respiratory burst. The term respiratory burst refers to a rapid increase in oxygen-consuming oxidant production associated with phagocyte activation, rather than simply an increase in mitochondrial ATP production. The biochemical change contributes to antimicrobial processes within phagocytic compartments.

The respiratory burst illustrates a use of reducing equivalents that differs from their ordinary roles in biosynthesis or mitochondrial energy metabolism. Electrons from NADPH are directed to oxygen through the oxidase machinery. Superoxide can participate in the formation of additional reactive oxygen species, and phagocytes also possess antimicrobial mechanisms that do not depend solely on this pathway. In CGD, loss of normal oxidase activity impairs important components of microbial killing. The cells may still ingest material, but ingestion does not guarantee effective elimination. This distinction explains why assays of phagocytosis and assays of oxidative activity assess different aspects of cellular competence.

The name granulomatous disease reflects the tendency toward formation of organized inflammatory cell aggregates in appropriate settings. When material is not effectively eliminated, continuing stimulation and recruitment can contribute to persistent inflammation. A granuloma is not simply a collection of organisms, nor does its presence identify a unique disease. Granulomatous responses arise in several biological contexts. In CGD, a deficiency in antimicrobial killing can coexist with substantial inflammation, reinforcing the distinction between the intensity of an immune response and its effectiveness.

The susceptibility pattern often includes particular bacterial and fungal infections, with textbook emphasis on organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and Aspergillus. The common teaching association with catalase-positive organisms is useful as a starting point but is not an absolute classifier of every infection in CGD. Microbial susceptibility depends on multiple microbial and host properties, and the absence of an organism's catalase does not guarantee that it can always be controlled. The strongest mechanistic inference from CGD is the importance of oxidase-dependent phagocyte defense, not a universal rule derived from a single microbial enzyme.

Leukocyte adhesion deficiency offers a useful contrast at an earlier stage of the phagocyte response. In a representative form involving defective beta-2 integrin function, normal recruitment of leukocytes from blood into tissues is impaired. A blood sample may contain abundant neutrophils, but their accumulation at the infected tissue can be inadequate. This is a fundamentally different problem from one in which neutrophils reach the tissue and engulf microbes but have defective oxidant production. The comparison extends the adhesion concepts from subunit 4.B into functional immunity.

A hypothetical functional comparison can illustrate this distinction. Suppose two samples have comparable neutrophil numbers. Cells in sample A show poor adhesion-dependent migration but normal oxidative activity when appropriately activated. Cells in sample B show relatively normal recruitment-related behavior but poor oxidative activity. These observations indicate different failed operations. A count alone would not distinguish them, and a single global label such as weak immunity would conceal their mechanistic differences. A well-designed question or experiment supplies enough independent measurements to localize the likely failure without claiming a definitive clinical diagnosis from a simplified dataset.

Oxidative-burst assessment is one way to study the relevant biological function. Fluorescent probes whose signals change in relation to oxidant production can provide an indirect measurement of phagocyte oxidative activity. Comparison with an appropriate reference population and attention to cell viability and the measurement conditions are essential for interpretation. The readout does not directly measure every antimicrobial mechanism, and a reduced signal does not, by itself, establish which oxidase component is abnormal. It is a functional indicator that can complement cellular counts and molecular evidence.

Further reading: GeneReviews: CGD; Merck Manual: overview.

15. Complement deficiencies and acquired immune impairment

Complement is a set of interacting soluble and membrane-associated components that contributes to innate and antibody-associated defense. Because its activation is organized into pathways that share downstream components, the effect of a deficiency depends on the location of the failed component. An early pathway defect may impair a particular activation route while leaving another route available. A defect in a shared central component can affect more than one route. A defect near the terminal membrane-attack stage can preserve some upstream functions while reducing the formation of a functional terminal complex. The pathway architecture developed in 4.C.03 makes these relationships intelligible.

C3 has a central role because activation of the major complement pathways converges on its cleavage and downstream consequences. C3b and related products participate in opsonization, which promotes recognition of coated material by appropriate phagocytic receptors. Severe deficiency of C3 can therefore impair an important mechanism for clearance of extracellular microbes. Upstream pathway initiation alone does not restore that missing central effector function. The consequences can be broad because multiple activation routes normally contribute to generation of C3-derived products.

Deficiencies in terminal complement components such as C5 through C9 can impair formation or function of the membrane attack complex. Classical examination associations include increased susceptibility to invasive infections caused by Neisseria species. The relationship is mechanistically important because terminal complement activity is particularly relevant to defense against these organisms. However, a deficiency in C8, for example, does not imply complete absence of antibodies, upstream complement activation or phagocyte function. The specific loss illustrates the limits of compensation by remaining immune mechanisms.

Early classical pathway defects can also influence clearance of immune complexes and other material. Consequently, certain complement deficiencies can be associated with immune-mediated disorders as well as infection susceptibility. This is another reason the terms immunodeficiency and autoimmunity should not be treated as mutually exclusive categories. A component may normally promote microbial defense in one context and support appropriate handling of self-associated material in another. Loss of the same component can affect both functions.

Functional tests of complement pathways provide a useful example of interpreting an integrated biological measurement. CH50 assesses classical-pathway hemolytic activity, including the shared terminal pathway, whereas AH50 assesses alternative-pathway hemolytic activity. If classical activity is markedly impaired while alternative activity is preserved, an early classical-pathway defect becomes a possible explanation. If both activities are impaired, a defect in a shared component becomes one possibility, but other explanations include consumption of components and sample-related factors. These assays are therefore useful for pathway localization, not proof of a unique molecular abnormality without additional evidence.

Secondary immune impairment can arise through several mechanisms. Malnutrition may influence the integrity of epithelial barriers, the availability of substrates for synthesis and cell proliferation, and the function of innate and adaptive immune populations. Immune activation requires metabolic resources, including amino acids and energy, and tissue maintenance also depends on adequate nutrition. The relationship is bidirectional in many settings because infection and inflammation can themselves affect appetite, metabolism and nutrient balance. It would therefore be misleading to attribute every observed immune abnormality in a malnourished population to one isolated nutrient or one universally affected cell type.

Medical immunosuppression illustrates an intentional reduction in selected immune functions for a therapeutic purpose. The effects depend on the biological target of the intervention. Some interventions reduce proliferation of immune populations, some alter inflammatory signaling, and others act on more specific cell populations or mediators. The biological tradeoff is that a process suppressed to reduce damaging immunity may also be important for host defense against some infections. Not all immunosuppressive interventions produce the same susceptibility pattern, and no particular clinical treatment regimen is needed to understand this principle.

Other secondary conditions may alter production, distribution, survival or loss of immune components. For example, disruption of bone marrow function can change the supply of several leukocyte populations, whereas a condition involving abnormal loss of proteins can reduce concentrations of soluble immune molecules. These mechanisms differ from inherited failure of a particular receptor or enzyme. The final functional measurements may nevertheless resemble those seen in an inborn disorder. The history of the condition, molecular findings and temporal development help distinguish the origin of the impairment.

The most useful summary of immunodeficiency is therefore a matrix of causes and functions. The cause can be inherited or acquired, and the affected function can involve recognition, development, cellular communication, production of soluble molecules or effector execution. A single disorder may occupy more than one functional category. By mapping the defect onto the normal response, the student can make defensible predictions about which observations would be informative and which forms of protection could be relatively preserved. This approach leads naturally to experimental reasoning about immune competence.

16. Evidence, measurement and integrated examination reasoning

An investigation of immune function begins with a precisely framed question. A cell count asks whether a measured population is present in a specified compartment. An immunoglobulin concentration asks how much of a class of soluble antibody is present under the measurement conditions. A functional assay asks whether a defined biological activity occurs. A molecular analysis asks whether a particular protein, gene or associated pathway is altered. Each kind of evidence can contribute to an explanation, but none is a universal replacement for the others. The strongest mechanistic interpretation arises when independent measurements support the same causal chain.

Flow cytometry provides a conceptual example of population assessment. Fluorescently labeled reagents can identify cells expressing combinations of surface markers, allowing estimation of the proportions of different populations within a defined set of events. CD3 is commonly used in identification of T cells, CD19 in identification of B cells, and appropriate combinations including CD16 or CD56 in identification of NK populations. Interpretation depends on the combination of markers and the selected population, because an individual marker need not be exclusive to a single cell type. The method provides information about phenotype and abundance, which must be distinguished from a direct measurement of the ability to carry out an immune response.

A functional cytotoxicity measurement can compare target-cell survival in the presence and absence of relevant effector cells. If target survival is reduced in the effector-containing condition, the difference is consistent with an effector-associated loss of targets. Interpretation requires consideration of spontaneous target death, differences in target abundance and the conditions under which the populations were compared. Measuring a decrease in target number does not establish which recognition receptor or death pathway was responsible. To distinguish those possibilities, the experimental question must be narrowed and supported by appropriate independent observations.

For example, imagine an educational dataset in which 80 percent of targets survive in a control condition and 50 percent survive in a condition containing an immune effector. If the initial target numbers are comparable, an absolute difference of 30 percentage points in survival is observed. The reduction relative to control survival is (80 − 50)/80 = 0.375, or 37.5 percent. Neither value is the same as assuming that half of all original targets were specifically killed by the effector, because some target loss occurred in the control. The interpretation depends on the denominator chosen and on the biological meaning of the reference condition.

Consider next a hypothetical subject with a low concentration of multiple immunoglobulin classes, a very small mature B-cell population and a relatively preserved T-cell count. This combination is compatible with a broad B-cell developmental or survival defect. Contrast it with a subject who has severely reduced T-cell abundance, detectable B cells and poor antibody responses. The second pattern is consistent with a disturbance of adaptive immune coordination or combined immunodeficiency. A genetic cause cannot be identified uniquely from these simplified observations, but the patterns help distinguish two different functional architectures. This is the appropriate level of inference for an examination vignette unless additional molecular information is provided.

A third hypothetical case has a relatively normal lymphocyte distribution and normal serum immunoglobulins, but stimulated phagocytes generate a much weaker oxidative response than a matched control. The evidence points toward a defect in an innate effector process rather than a generalized failure of adaptive lymphocyte production. If phagocyte uptake of material is also normal under the conditions of the assay, the distinction between ingestion and intracellular antimicrobial activity becomes clearer. Again, the data support a functional localization; identifying a specific pathogenic variant would require additional evidence.

The same disciplined reasoning applies when comparing immune responses to viral infection. A sample with abundant interferon and weak induction of representative downstream genes suggests a difference between the presence of the signal and the response of its target cells. A sample with strong antibody binding but little neutralization suggests a distinction between antigen recognition and prevention of infectivity. A sample with antigen-specific T cells that recognize a target yet do not efficiently eliminate it suggests that an effector step may be compromised. These patterns show why a successful immune response cannot be judged from any single broad category of measurement.

Cross-concept integration is the final objective. Antiviral defense draws on pattern recognition, cytokine signaling, antigen processing, adaptive clonal selection, cell death, antibody binding and immune memory. Immunodeficiencies expose what happens when particular links in this network fail. Together, these subjects demonstrate that immunity is a coordinated biological system rather than a collection of independently memorized cell types. The following chapter on vaccination applies this understanding to the controlled development of protective memory. Its central question will be how prior exposure to antigen can alter the starting conditions of a future response without requiring the organism to experience the full disease.

Worked numerical examples

All numerical datasets are hypothetical teaching examples, not reported experimental or patient measurements.

1. Net rate of viral abundance

In a defined hypothetical compartment, 100 infectious units become 400 over two days. For constant net proportional growth, r = ln(400/100)/2 = 0.6931 day⁻¹. Doubling time is ln(2)/r = 1 day. This net rate does not identify the immune mechanism.

2. Net decline and half-life

If 400 units become 100 over two days, the effective net rate is ln(100/400)/2 = −0.6931 day⁻¹. The modeled halving time is 1 day, provided the rate remains constant.

3. Relative reduction versus percentage points

A control has 250/500 infected cells and a comparison has 100/500. Fractions are 0.50 and 0.20; absolute difference is 30 percentage points, relative reduction is 60%. Equivalent viable denominators are assumed.

4. CD4 count from a defined gate

Given 2,000 lymphocytes/µL and 30% CD4 T cells among lymphocytes, absolute CD4 = 600 cells/µL. If CD4 is 30% of T cells instead and T cells are 60% of lymphocytes, CD4 = 360 cells/µL. The denominator changes the answer.

5. Conditional X-linked inheritance

With a heterozygous mother and unaffected father in a simple Mendelian model, the probability that a son inherits the pathogenic BTK variant is 1/2. Assuming equal probabilities of the two sexes, the probability per pregnancy of an affected son is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Actual observed family proportions can differ.

6. Control-normalized target survival

A target-only control has 80% survival and an effector condition has 50%. The decline relative to control is (80−50)/80 = 37.5%; absolute difference is 30 percentage points. This does not isolate the molecular killing pathway.

7. Simple binding occupancy model

For a single independent binding site at equilibrium, let R denote free receptor, L free ligand, and RL complex, all in concentration units. Kd = [R][L]/[RL], so [RL]/[R] = [L]/Kd. The occupied fraction is [RL]/([R]+[RL]) = [L]/(Kd+[L]). At L = 3Kd, θ = 3/4 = 0.75. Kd and L share concentration units, so θ is dimensionless. The model assumes one independent site and free ligand at equilibrium. It does not establish that 75% of virions are neutralized.

8. Antibody contribution versus residual susceptibility

In a hypothetical neutralization comparison, 120 infectious units are detected in the reference and 30 in the antibody condition. Relative infectivity is 30/120 = 0.25 and reduction is 75%. This result concerns the specified infectivity endpoint, not the proportion of antibody molecules bound.

Part B: conceptual practice

Original examination-style questions, not previous-year questions. All numerical scenarios are hypothetical.

Q1. Which immune mediator primarily establishes an induced antiviral state in neighboring cells?

  1. C3b deposited on a bacterium
  2. Type I interferon
  3. Secreted perforin acting as a receptor
  4. Membrane IgD on a naive B cell
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Type I interferon

Type I interferon binds host-cell receptors and changes gene expression. C3b promotes opsonization; perforin participates in killing rather than receptor signaling; membrane IgD is an antigen receptor.

Q2. What was the central inference of Isaacs and Lindenmann's 1957 interferon work?

  1. Only an antibody against influenza could explain interference
  2. Virions reproduce independently of host tissue
  3. All antiviral responses require cytotoxic lymphocytes
  4. A transferable host-derived activity could reduce susceptibility to viral infection
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. A transferable host-derived activity could reduce susceptibility to viral infection

Transferable protection supports a soluble host response. It does not require a virus-specific antibody or demonstrate independent viral reproduction or obligatory CTL involvement.

Q3. Which receptor is a representative endosomal sensor of double-stranded RNA?

  1. TLR3
  2. CD8
  3. BTK
  4. FcγRIIIa
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. TLR3

TLR3 detects double-stranded RNA in an endosomal setting. CD8 is a T-cell coreceptor, BTK is a kinase, and FcγRIIIa recognizes appropriate antibody Fc regions.

Q4. Which complex is central to the canonical type I interferon-induced transcriptional response?

  1. RAG1–RAG2–TdT
  2. C5b–C6–C7
  3. STAT1–STAT2–IRF9
  4. CD3–CD19–CD56
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. STAT1–STAT2–IRF9

STAT1, STAT2 and IRF9 form ISGF3. RAG proteins and TdT contribute to receptor diversification, complement proteins participate in terminal activation, and the listed cell markers do not form such a transcriptional complex.

Q5. Which enzyme phosphorylates eIF2α in a classical antiviral restriction pathway?

  1. RNase L
  2. Oligoadenylate synthetase
  3. PKR
  4. Adenosine deaminase
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. PKR

PKR is a protein kinase; phosphorylation of eIF2α can limit initiation of translation. RNase L cleaves RNA, OAS synthesizes activating oligoadenylates, and ADA participates in purine metabolism.

Q6. Which is the direct RNA-cleaving effector of the OAS-associated pathway?

  1. RNase L
  2. Mx GTPase
  3. IFNAR2
  4. STAT2
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. RNase L

OAS generates 2′–5′ oligoadenylates that activate RNase L. Mx proteins are GTPases; IFNAR2 is a receptor component, and STAT2 participates in signal-dependent transcription.

Q7. What is a typical basis of NK missing-self recognition?

  1. Recognition of every viral peptide by a rearranged TCR
  2. Binding of peptide to MHC II by CD4
  3. Absence of all antibodies in the circulation
  4. Reduced appropriate inhibitory signals from self MHC I
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Reduced appropriate inhibitory signals from self MHC I

Loss of appropriate inhibitory self-MHC information can favor activation when activating signals are sufficient. The other options describe different adaptive mechanisms or an unrelated systemic condition.

Q8. Which human NK-cell receptor commonly mediates IgG-dependent cellular cytotoxicity?

  1. CD19
  2. CD16
  3. CD4
  4. IFNAR1
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. CD16

CD16 (FcγRIIIa) recognizes the Fc portion of suitable target-bound IgG. CD19 marks the B lineage, CD4 is a coreceptor, and IFNAR1 is a type I interferon receptor subunit.

Q9. What does an antiviral conventional CD8 T cell typically recognize?

  1. A cognate peptide–MHC I complex
  2. Any intact extracellular virion irrespective of specificity
  3. Unbound Fc regions alone
  4. An intact native antigen on MHC II without processing
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. A cognate peptide–MHC I complex

The TCR recognizes a composite peptide–MHC I surface, supported by CD8. Native extracellular antigen is more directly accessible to antibodies/BCRs; Fc recognition and conventional CD4-associated class II recognition are distinct.

Q10. Which process can allow an uninfected dendritic cell to prime a class I-restricted response using externally obtained antigen?

  1. Class-switch recombination
  2. Alternative complement activation
  3. Cross-presentation
  4. Missing-self recognition
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Cross-presentation

Cross-presentation places externally acquired antigen-derived peptides on MHC I in suitable APCs. The other processes concern antibody genes, complement or NK recognition.

Q11. Which pair directly participates in granule-dependent cytotoxicity?

  1. C3 and factor B
  2. Perforin and granzymes
  3. IgA and J chain
  4. RAG1 and RAG2
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Perforin and granzymes

Perforin facilitates delivery of granzymes that initiate target-cell death. Complement, IgA organization and antigen-receptor rearrangement are different processes.

Q12. What distinguishes a neutralizing antibody from an antibody that merely binds viral antigen?

  1. It necessarily belongs to IgM
  2. It directly enters every infected cell
  3. It necessarily recognizes a linear epitope
  4. It decreases infectivity under appropriate functional conditions
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. It decreases infectivity under appropriate functional conditions

Neutralization is a functional endpoint, not an isotype or epitope classification. Binding alone, cytosolic entry and linear epitope recognition are not obligatory conditions.

Q13. Which immunoglobulin is particularly important in mucosal secretions?

  1. Membrane IgD only
  2. IgE only
  3. IgG restricted to all epithelial lumina
  4. Secretory IgA
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Secretory IgA

Secretory IgA is a prominent mucosal antibody. Other isotypes have overlapping roles but are not generally equivalent to the predominant secretory IgA system.

Q14. Which statement best describes immune memory after infection?

  1. Memory requires constant high antigen exposure in every case
  2. Long-lived antibodies and memory lymphocytes may make distinct contributions
  3. Only antibody binding is evidence of T-cell memory
  4. All secondary responses provide sterilizing protection
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Long-lived antibodies and memory lymphocytes may make distinct contributions

Residual antibody can act immediately; memory cells can respond to renewed antigen. Continuous antigen, antibody binding as a T-cell readout, and guaranteed sterilizing protection are not defining conditions.

Q15. Which cell population is especially affected by HIV-associated adaptive immune disruption?

  1. Mature erythrocytes
  2. Only neutrophils
  3. CD4 helper T cells
  4. Only plasma cells
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. CD4 helper T cells

CD4 T-cell loss and dysfunction affect several immune branches. Other cell populations may be affected indirectly, but none of the alternatives defines the characteristic adaptive disturbance.

Q16. What is the distinction between HIV and AIDS?

  1. HIV is the virus; AIDS is an advanced HIV-associated immune-deficiency syndrome
  2. HIV and AIDS are identical names for a viral particle
  3. AIDS is a hereditary B-cell developmental defect
  4. HIV means any secondary immunodeficiency
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. HIV is the virus; AIDS is an advanced HIV-associated immune-deficiency syndrome

The virus and the advanced syndrome are distinct. HIV infection does not inevitably imply immediate AIDS; other primary and secondary deficiencies are different entities.

Q17. Which gene is classically associated with X-linked agammaglobulinemia?

  1. RAG2 in every case
  2. C8
  3. BTK
  4. CYBB
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. BTK

BTK is required for important B-cell developmental and receptor signals. RAG2 is linked to receptor-rearrangement disorders, C8 to complement, and CYBB to oxidase-related CGD.

Q18. What does the typical severe IL2RG-associated T−B+NK− phenotype imply?

  1. B cells necessarily have normal functional responses
  2. All leukocytes are absent
  3. NK cells require conventional V(D)J rearrangement
  4. B cells may be numerically present despite impaired adaptive humoral function
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. B cells may be numerically present despite impaired adaptive humoral function

Combined impairment concerns function, not the absence of every cell. B cells can remain present without effective help; NK cells do not require conventional BCR/TCR rearrangement.

Q19. Which process is primarily impaired in chronic granulomatous disease?

  1. Maternal IgG transfer
  2. Phagocyte NADPH oxidase-dependent antimicrobial activity
  3. B-cell receptor gene rearrangement
  4. Synthesis of every complement protein
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Phagocyte NADPH oxidase-dependent antimicrobial activity

CGD reflects impaired oxidase-dependent microbial defense. It does not principally prevent maternal antibody transfer, receptor rearrangement or global complement synthesis.

Q20. Which association is characteristic of terminal complement deficiency?

  1. Increased susceptibility to invasive Neisseria infections
  2. Obligate absence of mature B cells
  3. Typical T−B−NK+ SCID
  4. Failure of all interferon synthesis
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Increased susceptibility to invasive Neisseria infections

Terminal complement contributes importantly to defense against Neisseria. B-cell loss, a RAG-associated profile and interferon synthesis are unrelated functional categories.

Q21. Two cell samples secrete equal IFN-β after an innate stimulus. Sample X induces an ISG after externally added IFN-β; Y does not. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. Y cannot produce interferon
  2. Y has impaired interferon responsiveness under the test conditions
  3. X lacks all pattern-recognition receptors
  4. Both samples necessarily have identical antiviral function
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Y has impaired interferon responsiveness under the test conditions

Equal secretion separates sender output from receiver response. Externally supplied ligand bypasses the production step; the data support impaired downstream responsiveness in Y, without establishing the exact affected molecule.

Q22. A hypothetical host cell has normal IFNAR signaling but defective PKR activation. Which outcome is most defensible?

  1. The PKR-dependent translation-restriction branch may be impaired while other ISG functions remain
  2. All OAS activity must be absent
  3. Interferon secretion is necessarily impossible
  4. The antigen-specific B-cell response necessarily fails
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The PKR-dependent translation-restriction branch may be impaired while other ISG functions remain

PKR is one effector branch downstream of inducible defense. Other branches need not fail merely because PKR does. Neither antibody specificity nor interferon secretion is logically determined by this observation.

Q23. Condition A shows little IFN production after a stimulus but a normal gene-expression response to supplied IFN. Which functional localization follows?

  1. A complete absence of all IFN receptors is demonstrated
  2. The defect must affect the terminal complement complex
  3. A problem upstream of or at endogenous IFN production is plausible
  4. The ISG response must be absent in every tissue
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. A problem upstream of or at endogenous IFN production is plausible

The normal response to supplied IFN demonstrates receiver competence in this test. The defect may involve stimulus detection or endogenous production; the data do not identify a unique gene or all tissues.

Q24. Two targets differ in appropriate inhibitory self-MHC ligand abundance. Target X has low ligand and strong activating signals; Y has high ligand and weak activating signals. Which result fits the NK integration model?

  1. Y is more susceptible because stronger inhibitory self-MHC recognition favors killing
  2. Both must be equally susceptible because only total MHC I abundance matters
  3. X necessarily produces the same NK response if activating inputs are removed
  4. X is more likely to induce NK cytotoxicity
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. X is more likely to induce NK cytotoxicity

The activating/inhibitory balance favors NK activity against X under the stated assumptions. Class I can deliver inhibitory information; NK receptors are essential and other immune responses cannot be inferred globally.

Q25. An antigen-specific CTL recognizes peptide P on MHC allele M. Which target provides its cognate recognition surface?

  1. A different peptide displayed by M
  2. P displayed only by an unrelated allele
  3. Free P without MHC
  4. P displayed by M
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. P displayed by M

Conventional recognition depends on the composite peptide–MHC surface. A matching peptide or MHC alone is not generally sufficient for this defined clone.

Q26. An effector CTL shows antigen-associated signaling on contact, but target-cell death is reduced. What should be investigated before concluding that antigen presentation failed?

  1. Whether all targets contain no peptide
  2. Whether all CTLs have failed to undergo antigen-specific priming
  3. Downstream effector execution and target susceptibility
  4. Whether target-cell recognition is completely independent of class I MHC
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Downstream effector execution and target susceptibility

Recognition-associated signaling makes a downstream defect plausible. It does not prove all presentation is normal, but target death also depends on execution and target response; the other choices do not follow.

Q27. An antibody binds isolated viral protein strongly but has little effect on infectivity of intact particles. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Antigen binding and functional neutralization differ
  2. The antibody necessarily cannot bind any antigen
  3. The protein must be absent from all virions
  4. Its Fc-dependent functions must be completely absent
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Antigen binding and functional neutralization differ

The isolated-antigen interaction may not block an essential accessible function of intact particles. The data establish binding but not neutralization; they do not establish the status of Fc-dependent functions or the entire particle composition.

Q28. A reference sample yields 120 infectious units; a comparison with antibody yields 30. What is the reduction relative to the reference?

  1. 25%
  2. 75%
  3. 90%
  4. 400%
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 75%

(120−30)/120 = 0.75. The remaining fraction is 25%, not the reduction; 90 is an absolute count and 400% does not represent the specified ratio.

Q29. In a simplified constant-rate model, 100 units increase to 800 over three days. What is the doubling time?

  1. 1 day
  2. 3 days
  3. 8 days
  4. 0.125 day
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 1 day

800/100 = 8 = 2³, so three doublings occur over three days. The effective net rate is ln(8)/3 = ln(2) day⁻¹. This is not a mechanism-specific estimate.

Q30. A control has 40% infected cells; a comparison has 10%. What are the absolute difference and relative reduction?

  1. 75 percentage points and 30%
  2. 30 percentage points and 75%
  3. 10 percentage points and 40%
  4. 30 percentage points and 25%
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 30 percentage points and 75%

Absolute difference is 40−10 = 30 points. Relative reduction is 30/40 = 0.75; 25% is the residual fraction.

Q31. A sample contains 1,500 lymphocytes/µL; 60% are CD3+ T cells, and 40% of those are CD4 T cells. What is the absolute CD4 count?

  1. 600 cells/µL
  2. 900 cells/µL
  3. 1,000 cells/µL
  4. 360 cells/µL
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 360 cells/µL

1,500 × 0.60 × 0.40 = 360. The 40% denominator is the T-cell population, not all lymphocytes; 900 is the total T-cell count.

Q32. Two hypothetical blood samples each have 20% CD4 among lymphocytes; their total lymphocyte counts are 2,000 and 1,000/µL. Which is correct?

  1. Both absolute counts are 200/µL
  2. The second has twice as many CD4 cells
  3. Absolute CD4 counts are 400 and 200/µL
  4. Identical percentages establish identical immunity
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Absolute CD4 counts are 400 and 200/µL

The denominator differs: 0.20 × 2,000 = 400 and 0.20 × 1,000 = 200. Percentages do not establish absolute abundance or functional competence.

Q33. An individual with chronic HIV has measurable antigen-binding antibodies and declining helper T-cell function. Which interpretation is consistent with both observations?

  1. The antibodies establish sterilizing protection regardless of viral burden
  2. Declining CD4 function proves no adaptive response ever occurred
  3. Antibody production can coexist with incomplete viral clearance and impaired immune coordination
  4. CD4-cell loss can be attributed solely to antibodies from these observations
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Antibody production can coexist with incomplete viral clearance and impaired immune coordination

Antigen recognition and effective eradication are distinct. Chronic infection can coexist with antibody production and compromised helper-dependent defense; none of the other causal claims follows.

Q34. A hypothetical subject has very few mature B cells, very low multiple immunoglobulin classes and relatively preserved T-cell counts. Which functional defect is most compatible?

  1. Isolated C9 deficiency
  2. Isolated oxidative-burst defect
  3. Only altered NK inhibition
  4. Severe B-cell development or survival defect
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Severe B-cell development or survival defect

The concordance between low mature B-cell abundance and broad humoral impairment localizes the likely problem to the B lineage. Complement and phagocyte defects need not explain both findings.

Q35. A heterozygous BTK-variant carrier mother and an unaffected father have a son under a simple X-linked model. What is the conditional probability that he inherits the variant?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/2
  3. 1
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 1/2

A son receives his X chromosome from the mother, with a one-half chance of the variant. The one-quarter figure instead applies per pregnancy to an affected son when the two sexes are equally probable.

Q36. A severe inherited defect impairs cytokine receptor signaling needed for T and NK development. B cells remain detectable but antibody responses are poor. Which representative profile matches?

  1. IL2RG-associated T−B+NK− SCID
  2. RAG-associated T−B−NK+ SCID
  3. Isolated terminal complement deficiency
  4. Classical XLA with preserved T cells
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. IL2RG-associated T−B+NK− SCID

Common gamma-chain loss can impair T/NK development and adaptive coordination despite B-cell presence. The other profiles differ in affected pathways or populations.

Q37. A severe defect prevents conventional antigen-receptor gene rearrangement. Which lymphocyte phenotype is typical if other developmental pathways are intact?

  1. T+B−NK−
  2. T−B−NK+
  3. T+B+NK−
  4. T−B+NK−
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. T−B−NK+

T and B cells require receptor rearrangement for development, whereas NK cells do not require the same rearrangement process. The other patterns do not reflect this dependency.

Q38. A severe purine-metabolism defect produces broad lymphocyte toxicity. Which combination is most compatible with classical ADA-SCID?

  1. Normal lymphocyte development with only C8 deficiency
  2. Exclusive absence of IgA
  3. Marked reduction of T, B and NK populations
  4. Only high neutrophil oxidative activity
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Marked reduction of T, B and NK populations

ADA deficiency can produce a severe metabolic disturbance affecting lymphocyte survival across lineages. The other profiles are unrelated or too selective.

Q39. Hypothetical cells show normal microbial uptake but a markedly reduced stimulated oxidative-burst signal. What is the best functional inference?

  1. Phagocyte oxidative antimicrobial function is impaired
  2. The cells must lack all ability to ingest material
  3. The result establishes CYBB rather than any other oxidase-related gene
  4. The primary defect must involve immunoglobulin secretion
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Phagocyte oxidative antimicrobial function is impaired

Preserved uptake with reduced oxidative activity localizes the observed defect beyond ingestion. A functional result cannot by itself identify the gene or establish global antibody loss.

Q40. A subject has many circulating neutrophils but poor adhesion-dependent tissue recruitment. Oxidative activity is normal in the test. Which stage is most plausibly affected?

  1. Antibody class switching
  2. Thymic positive selection
  3. Cytosolic RNA cleavage by RNase L
  4. Leukocyte recruitment
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Leukocyte recruitment

Cell abundance and tissue access differ; adhesion-dependent recruitment can fail despite normal count and oxidase activity. The alternatives refer to other immune operations and do not explain a selective defect in tissue access.

Q41. In a simplified complement comparison, CH50 is very low but AH50 is preserved. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

  1. All terminal components must be absent
  2. All B cells must be absent
  3. The result uniquely identifies C1q without additional evidence
  4. An early classical pathway defect is plausible
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. An early classical pathway defect is plausible

Classical and alternative assays share terminal components. Preserved alternative activity supports a possible classical-specific defect, but a single assay pattern does not identify the precise cause.

Q42. Both CH50 and AH50 are markedly reduced with otherwise normal sampling controls. Which possibility deserves consideration?

  1. A shared central or terminal complement defect
  2. Isolated CD4 depletion is proved
  3. Only class-switch recombination failed
  4. An early classical defect is the only possible cause
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. A shared central or terminal complement defect

Both assays depend on common components. A shared failure is plausible, though consumption and other explanations remain possible. The alternative interpretations are not established by the assays.

Q43. In an effector experiment control target survival is 80% and test survival is 40%. What is the control-relative reduction in survival?

  1. 40%
  2. 80%
  3. 50%
  4. 20%
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 50%

(80−40)/80 = 50%. The absolute difference is 40 percentage points. It is incorrect to equate every target loss with the specific effector without accounting for the control.

Q44. Two hosts have similar measured viral burdens; one has less tissue dysfunction. Which distinction is illustrated?

  1. The healthier host necessarily has no virus
  2. Different host tolerance to damage may exist even at similar resistance outcomes
  3. The more injured host must have fewer antibodies
  4. Equal viral burden guarantees equal tissue injury
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Different host tolerance to damage may exist even at similar resistance outcomes

Pathogen-centered measurements and host damage are distinct. Differences in regulation, repair or susceptibility could alter tissue function; the data do not identify a specific cause.

Integrated and advanced practice

Original examination-style questions, not previous-year questions. All numerical scenarios are hypothetical.

Q45. Assertion: Type I interferon may protect a nearby uninfected cell. Reason: The cell can respond to secreted interferon through a host receptor.

  1. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion
  2. Assertion true; reason false
  3. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion

Receptor-mediated response in neighboring cells is the causal basis of paracrine protection. Interferon need not directly contact a virion.

Q46. Assertion: B-cell presence in a typical T−B+ SCID phenotype guarantees normal T-dependent antibody responses. Reason: Helper T cells contribute to B-cell differentiation and antibody quality.

  1. Both true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Assertion false; reason true
  3. Both true but unrelated
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Assertion false; reason true

The assertion confuses counts with function. The reason is correct and explains why preserved B-cell numbers do not guarantee effective humoral defense.

Q47. Which combination of statements is correct? I. NK and CTL can use perforin. II. Conventional CTL recognition uses peptide–MHC I. III. All NK cells require rearranged TCRs. IV. Antibody binding proves neutralization.

  1. I and III only
  2. II and IV only
  3. I, II, III and IV
  4. I and II only
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. I and II only

I and II are correct. III confuses NK recognition with conventional T-cell recognition; IV confuses a binding endpoint with a functional endpoint.

Q48. Match the representative defects to functions: 1 BTK; 2 RAG; 3 phagocyte NADPH oxidase; 4 terminal complement. a receptor rearrangement; b B-cell development; c oxidative activity; d membrane attack.

  1. 1-b, 2-a, 3-c, 4-d
  2. 1-a, 2-b, 3-d, 4-c
  3. 1-d, 2-c, 3-a, 4-b
  4. 1-c, 2-d, 3-b, 4-a
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 1-b, 2-a, 3-c, 4-d

BTK supports B-cell development, RAG mediates receptor rearrangement, NADPH oxidase supports respiratory burst, and terminal complement forms the membrane-attack complex.

Q49. Refer to Figure 9 (hypothetical log-linear viral growth and decline). Between days 2 and 4, which conclusion follows from the plotted values?

  1. The measured infectious quantity halves each day under the constant-rate model
  2. The virus is completely absent on day 4
  3. The curve uniquely proves antibody-mediated control
  4. The day-4 count is four times the day-2 count

The plotted sequence is 400, 200, 100. This represents halving each day. A positive count remains and the curve cannot establish the particular mechanism.

Q50. Refer to Figure 11 (CD4 helper-cell coordination). If helper-cell function falls, which combination is plausible?

  1. Only IgA loss with all other functions guaranteed normal
  2. Direct loss of all B-cell antigen receptors with no effect on macrophages
  3. Selective failure of NK missing-self recognition with all helper-dependent functions preserved
  4. Impaired T-dependent B-cell help, CTL support and macrophage activation
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Impaired T-dependent B-cell help, CTL support and macrophage activation

CD4 cells support multiple immune branches. Downstream functions may decline without disappearance of the downstream populations. Complement synthesis and erythrocytes are not the specific dependencies shown.

Q51. A patient has recurrent infection and autoimmune manifestations. Which general statement is scientifically valid?

  1. Autoimmunity excludes every form of immunodeficiency
  2. Immune deficiency and immune dysregulation can coexist
  3. Any infection excludes immune-mediated tissue injury
  4. Immunodeficiency always implies absence of all leukocytes
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Immune deficiency and immune dysregulation can coexist

Components involved in defense may also help maintain regulation and clearance of self-associated material. Loss can lead to both susceptibility and inappropriate immune activation.

Q52. A vaccine-associated antibody response is considered in the next node. Which prerequisite distinction is necessary for interpreting protective memory?

  1. Every memory cell continuously secretes antibody
  2. Passive antibody transfer necessarily creates durable recipient memory
  3. Residual antibody can act immediately; memory lymphocytes can expand after re-exposure
  4. Antibody concentration alone directly counts memory T cells
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Residual antibody can act immediately; memory lymphocytes can expand after re-exposure

Preexisting antibody and recall-capable lymphocytes are different contributors. Plasma cells secrete antibody; passive transfer supplies molecules rather than necessarily creating recipient memory.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Interferon is a virus-specific antibody.

    It is a host cytokine that induces signaling and cellular defense. Antibody specificity and cytokine responsiveness are different recognition systems; exams often exchange these categories.

  2. A cell making IFN must respond normally to IFN.

    Sender and receiver pathways are separable. External-ligand response tests distinguish them.

  3. Type I and type II IFNs are interchangeable.

    IFN-α/β and IFN-γ use different receptors and canonical signaling arrangements; shared biological effects do not make them equivalent.

  4. IFNAR cleaves viral RNA directly.

    IFNAR triggers signaling; induced effectors can alter RNA metabolism. Separate receptor and effector functions.

  5. PKR and RNase L catalyze the same reaction.

    PKR phosphorylates a protein; RNase L cleaves RNA. Questions often swap their substrates.

  6. Any MHC I loss guarantees NK killing.

    Reduced inhibition favors activation only in an appropriate receptor and activating-signal context.

  7. NK cells are conventional antigen-specific CTLs.

    They differ in recognition architecture despite overlapping cytotoxic effectors.

  8. A peptide alone guarantees CTL recognition.

    The appropriate peptide–MHC complex and TCR specificity matter.

  9. A target presenting antigen must die.

    Recognition is distinct from downstream killing and target susceptibility.

  10. All antiviral antibodies neutralize.

    Binding need not interfere with infectious activity; functional testing is separate.

  11. Memory and circulating antibody are interchangeable.

    Residual molecules and recall-capable cells have different kinetics and roles.

  12. HIV infection equals AIDS immediately.

    HIV is the pathogen; AIDS is an advanced syndrome. Infection course varies.

  13. A positive HIV antibody response means the virus is eliminated.

    Recognition can coexist with persistent infection.

  14. CD4 percentage equals absolute count.

    Absolute count depends on the explicitly defined denominator.

  15. Primary deficiency always presents at birth.

    An inborn defect may become evident later; maternal antibodies and exposure influence timing.

  16. SCID always means no B cells.

    Combined deficiency concerns both adaptive functions; some forms retain B-cell counts.

  17. CGD prevents engulfment in every case.

    Oxidase-dependent antimicrobial activity can be impaired despite uptake.

  18. Neisseria susceptibility proves a unique complement gene.

    Terminal deficiency is a characteristic association, not a unique diagnosis.

  19. Immunodeficiency excludes autoimmunity.

    Defective defense and dysregulation may occur together.

  20. All low CH50 results identify one classical component.

    Integrated assays localize possible pathway failure but require further evidence.

Revision and mastery

Antiviral immunity acts on three connected targets: extracellular infectivity, cellular susceptibility and infected-cell populations. Pattern recognition initiates cytokine signaling; interferon induces an antiviral state; NK cells integrate activating and inhibitory signals; CTLs recognize cognate peptide–MHC; antibody neutralizes or recruits other effectors. CD4-cell coordination explains why HIV-associated helper-cell loss has broad consequences. Deficiencies are best classified both by origin and by failed function.

Essential quantitative relationships

  • Constant effective net rate: r = ln(V₂/V₁)/Δt; V(t) = V₀ exp(rt). The rate describes the selected interval only.
  • Absolute subset count = population count × correctly defined subset fraction.
  • Relative reduction = (reference − comparison)/reference; distinguish from absolute percentage-point difference.
  • Single-site equilibrium occupancy: θ = L/(Kd + L); not equivalent to neutralization.
