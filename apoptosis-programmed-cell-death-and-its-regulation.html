1. Why cells must sometimes die

A multicellular organism cannot maintain its tissues simply by instructing cells to grow and divide. Cells also have to be removed at appropriate times. During embryonic development, some cells are produced in excess and later eliminated; in adults, many tissues continuously replace old or damaged cells with new ones. A cell that has accumulated dangerous DNA damage may be more harmful to its neighbors if it survives and continues dividing than if it is eliminated. The mechanism of elimination is therefore as important as the mechanism of multiplication. Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death in which the cell activates a coordinated molecular dismantling program. The usual outcome is removal of the dying cell while limiting the escape of its contents into surrounding tissue.

Consider a small epithelial compartment containing a hundred cells. If ten divisions each produce one net additional cell and ten other cells are removed, the population remains at a hundred. This stable population is not a static population; individual members are being replaced. If divisions continue but removal decreases, the compartment expands. If removal exceeds replacement, it becomes depleted. The important biological variable is thus the balance between addition and loss, not simply the speed of the cell cycle. A defect in apoptosis can increase cell number even if the cell-cycle machinery is unchanged.

An important distinction concerns the word programmed. Programmed cell death refers most precisely to regulated death that occurs as part of a physiological program, such as development. Apoptosis is a particular molecular and morphological mode of regulated cell death that can also be activated by pathological stress. The two terms are often used almost interchangeably in introductory texts, but not every regulated death is apoptosis. In particular, some cell deaths with membrane rupture are also regulated. The name apoptosis cannot be assigned just because a cell is destined to die.

Another distinction is between a cell that has stopped proliferating and a cell that has died. A quiescent cell may leave the division cycle temporarily while retaining metabolic activity and the capacity to re-enter. A senescent cell typically remains metabolically active despite a durable arrest. A damaged cell can pause at a checkpoint, repair its DNA and resume proliferation. Apoptosis instead destroys essential cellular structures and normally leads to irreversible loss of viability. Not all non-dividing cells are dying; not all apoptosis occurs in cycling cells. Neurons can undergo apoptosis even though most mature neurons are not actively dividing.

Three earlier concepts are particularly helpful. The plasma membrane is a selective permeability barrier with an asymmetric distribution of lipids; mitochondria have an outer membrane and an inner membrane enclosing an intermembrane space; and proteases are enzymes that hydrolyze peptide bonds. Chromatin is DNA associated with histones and other proteins. These structures will not merely disappear during apoptosis. Each is changed by specific mechanisms. The next step is to recognize the changes before asking how the cell generates them.

2. The recognizable sequence of cellular changes

In the early 1970s, John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie brought together morphological observations from different animal tissues and described a characteristic pattern of cell deletion. Their 1972 paper distinguished this pattern from the swelling and disintegration associated with many forms of severe injury [1]. Its importance was not that dying cells had never been seen before, but that similar structural changes in different contexts suggested an organized process rather than a random collapse.

The first recognizable alteration in many apoptotic cells is a reduction in cell volume. Ion movement across the plasma membrane, including loss of potassium and chloride in many systems, changes the osmotic distribution of water. Water leaves, and the cytoplasm becomes more compact. This apoptotic volume decrease is not an infallible first event and its timing varies. The key contrast is with the early osmotic swelling that often follows failure of ion pumps during classical necrotic injury. A shrinking cell need not be apoptotic unless other evidence supports that interpretation.

In the nucleus, chromatin becomes densely packed, often forming dark masses at its periphery. This condensation is called pyknosis. Nuclear material may then break into discrete fragments, a process called karyorrhexis. These nuclear changes involve loss of structural support and enzymatic processing of chromosomal DNA. They should not be confused with metaphase chromosome condensation: mitotic chromosomes are individually organized for segregation, whereas apoptotic chromatin is being dismantled and is not directed into an organized spindle.

The outline of an apoptotic cell also changes. Rounded protrusions of its plasma membrane, called blebs, arise when forces in the cortical actin–myosin network deform the surface. Blebbing differs from a membrane tear. A bleb initially remains enclosed by membrane; its shape reflects pressure and altered attachment between membrane and cortex. Some dying cells eventually divide into membrane-bound fragments containing portions of cytoplasm, organelles and nuclear material. These are apoptotic bodies. Formation of such bodies can aid engulfment, but is not an obligatory feature in every cell type.

A central feature is the relative preservation of plasma-membrane barrier function during much of the dismantling. Cytoplasmic constituents are packaged rather than immediately spilled into tissue. This does not mean that every cellular membrane remains intact. In the intrinsic pathway, the outer mitochondrial membrane deliberately becomes permeable long before the plasma membrane necessarily loses its barrier function. The two membranes must be identified separately in examination diagrams.

Finally, neighboring cells or professional phagocytes remove the apoptotic cell and its fragments. The phagocyte uses lysosomal enzymes to digest material inside an enclosed compartment. If engulfment is delayed or absent, the corpse may eventually rupture; this later event is called secondary necrosis. Cultured cells, where efficient phagocytes may be absent, commonly progress to this condition. Therefore, membrane integrity is an important stage-dependent observation rather than an absolute definition.

A sequence drawn from fixed tissue sections is an inference: each image represents a different cell at one moment. Time-lapse microscopy of the same cell can establish the order of visible changes. Molecular assays are needed to decide which pathway generated them. For example, strong chromatin condensation accompanied by executioner caspase activity supports apoptosis more strongly than condensation alone. Morphology provides the question that the molecular evidence must answer.

3. Discovery of an encoded death pathway

How could scientists distinguish active self-destruction from passive deterioration? A useful model organism is the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. Its somatic cell lineage is highly reproducible: investigators can follow individual cells from divisions of the fertilized egg and identify cells that normally disappear at defined stages. In the canonical hermaphrodite developmental lineage, 1,090 somatic cells are generated and 131 undergo programmed death, leaving 959 somatic cells in the adult. These numbers refer to the standard somatic lineage, not to all cells including the germ line.

If deaths were simply the unavoidable result of mechanical damage during development, it would be difficult to explain why specific mutations selectively prevent them. Ellis and Horvitz showed in 1986 that loss of function in ced-3 or ced-4 prevented most normal programmed cell deaths [2]. Cells that were expected to vanish remained visible. Some surviving cells could develop further. A mutation that removes a gene product and causes cells to survive is evidence that the product normally participates in killing, rather than maintaining survival. The experiment thus reversed the intuitive assumption that every cellular enzyme must protect life.

Other mutations implicated ced-9, a protective gene. In the basic model, CED-9 restrains CED-4; CED-4 promotes activation of the protease CED-3. Cells selected to die express EGL-1, a protein capable of binding CED-9 and relieving its restraint on CED-4. Released CED-4 assembles an activation complex for CED-3, which cleaves cellular substrates. This creates a logical series of inhibition, release of inhibition, and enzyme activation. Loss of CED-9 can cause inappropriate death; however, if the downstream execution gene ced-3 is also disabled, much of the excessive death is suppressed. This is an example of genetic epistasis: the phenotype of a downstream mutation can mask the effect of an upstream mutation.

Observe the controls implicit in this reasoning. The normal lineage supplies an expected death count and location. A mutant must be compared at equivalent developmental stages, since delayed disappearance is not the same as permanent survival. Engulfment-defective mutants may retain corpses that are already dead, whereas execution-defective mutants retain cells that never underwent the normal death process. Counting persistent objects without determining whether they are living cells or corpses would confuse two entirely different steps.

Subsequent work connected nematode proteins to mammalian proteins: CED-3 to caspases, CED-4 to Apaf-1 and CED-9 to protective Bcl-2 family members. EGL-1 resembles the BH3-only group of mammalian death-promoting proteins [3]. This conservation supports a shared principle of regulated protease activation. It does not imply identical mechanisms in every organism. Mammalian Apaf-1 normally responds to cytosolic cytochrome c released from mitochondria; CED-4 activation in the worm does not require that mammalian cytochrome c release step. The resemblance is therefore strongest at the level of functional organization.

A useful prediction follows. If a gene is required to activate death, its loss should protect cells from a signal acting upstream of it. If another gene normally inhibits death, its loss should sensitize cells or cause inappropriate death only if the downstream machinery is functional. This distinction between an activator and a brake will recur in both mammalian pathways.

4. Caspases convert a signal into coordinated proteolysis

The cell needs a machinery that can act rapidly once death is selected. If every target molecule had to be destroyed by a separately transcribed gene, dismantling would be slow and difficult to coordinate. A family of proteases known as caspases provides a common execution system. The name derives from cysteine-dependent protease activity and the preference for cleavage after aspartate residues in substrates. The catalytic cysteine belongs to the enzyme, whereas the aspartate at the cleavage site belongs to the substrate; confusing these two amino acids reverses the mechanism.

Most apoptotic caspases are synthesized as inactive or weakly active precursors called procaspases, also known as zymogens. A zymogen is a protein whose catalytic function is restrained until an activation event occurs. Procaspases contain regions that later contribute to large and small catalytic subunits, together with an amino-terminal prodomain of variable length. The catalytic domains associate into active dimers. This pre-existing pool allows the cell to respond to a signal without first synthesizing every enzyme from the beginning.

A mechanistic distinction separates initiator caspases from executioner caspases. Initiators such as caspase-8 and caspase-9 have long prodomains that mediate recruitment to activation platforms. The platforms place procaspase molecules close together, favoring dimerization and activation. Proteolytic processing can stabilize or regulate activation but cleavage alone is not a universal explanation of initiator activation. Caspase-9 is recruited to the apoptosome, whereas caspase-8 is commonly recruited to a membrane-proximal complex assembled by death receptors. In both cases, a signal is translated into concentrated molecular interactions.

Executioner caspases, particularly caspase-3 and caspase-7, are generally present as inactive dimers with short prodomains. Cleavage at appropriate sites by active upstream caspases rearranges regions of the catalytic machinery and makes the enzyme competent to cleave substrates. Caspase-6 participates in some downstream events, including nuclear structural changes. Executioners do not require recruitment to the same activation platforms as initiators. They can act on numerous proteins distributed through the cell.

One active initiator can activate multiple molecules of executioner, and each active executioner can cleave multiple target proteins. This catalytic amplification explains how a limited number of triggering events can produce large structural changes. It does not mean each molecule acts only once or that a fixed number of downstream molecules is always activated. The extent depends on substrate abundance, inhibitors, localization and duration of activity. Proteolytic cleavage is often irreversible for the cleaved molecule, unlike reversible binding of many signaling proteins. The destruction of multiple essential substrates makes full recovery increasingly unlikely.

Not every caspase belongs to the apoptotic execution chain. Certain caspases have important inflammatory functions; even an apoptotic caspase can participate in restricted nonlethal processes in appropriate contexts. A fluorogenic substrate cleaved by a caspase family member indicates protease activity, not automatically the death of every cell in the sample. To infer that the death program was executed, combine a relevant activity measurement with a cell-level outcome.

The cell also controls this potentially dangerous catalytic machinery. Inhibitor of apoptosis proteins, particularly XIAP in mammals, can directly suppress selected active caspases. The activation state of a caspase therefore depends not only on the presence of its precursor but on the activation platform and the inhibitory environment. A western blot showing large amounts of intact procaspase-3 is not equivalent to an assay showing active caspase-3. The former demonstrates availability; the latter addresses function.

The two main mammalian initiation routes solve different biological problems. The intrinsic pathway responds mainly to the internal state of the cell, while the extrinsic pathway responds to particular extracellular ligands. Both converge on executioner caspases, making it possible to activate a broadly similar dismantling program through different upstream decisions.

5. Intrinsic apoptosis: a decision at the outer mitochondrial membrane

A mitochondrion has an outer membrane facing the cytosol and a highly folded inner membrane surrounding the matrix. The intermembrane space lies between them. Cytochrome c is a small heme-containing protein associated with the outer face of the inner membrane in the intermembrane compartment. During oxidative phosphorylation it carries electrons between respiratory complexes III and IV. Its location is therefore integral to energy metabolism. When cytochrome c becomes available in the cytosol, however, it can act as a signal for apoptosis.

Why should the release of a mitochondrial protein convey information about a cell's condition? The outer membrane represents a physical separation between this potential signal and the cytosolic activation machinery. As long as the barrier is preserved, most of the relevant cytochrome c remains unavailable to Apaf-1. The mitochondrial decision involves changing that barrier. Mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) is the process by which the outer membrane becomes permeable to proteins normally retained within the intermembrane compartment. It is not identical to a collapse of the proton gradient across the inner membrane, although mitochondrial function often deteriorates after MOMP.

The Bcl-2 family regulates MOMP. Its members share structural features known as Bcl-2 homology (BH) regions, but they have opposite effects on survival. Anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2, Bcl-xL and Mcl-1 limit membrane permeabilization by binding and restraining death-promoting family members. Effector proteins Bax and Bak directly participate in creating the permeable state. A third functional group, the BH3-only proteins, includes proteins such as Bim, Bid, Puma, Noxa and Bad. These connect particular forms of stress to the balance of protection and activation.

In a healthy cell, many Bax molecules are cytosolic or dynamically associated with mitochondria, whereas Bak is usually associated with the outer mitochondrial membrane. Their mere presence is not sufficient to permeabilize the membrane. They must undergo activation-associated conformational changes, expose surfaces that allow association, and assemble into oligomers in the membrane. Oligomers are assemblies of multiple copies of a molecule. Bax and Bak assemblies remodel the lipid bilayer so that intermembrane-space proteins can escape. One need not assume that every pore has the same rigid protein architecture; the essential examination principle is effector-dependent membrane permeabilization.

BH3-only proteins increase the likelihood of Bax/Bak activation in two related ways. Some, especially activated Bid and Bim, can directly promote effector activation in appropriate systems. Others act strongly by binding anti-apoptotic proteins and preventing them from restraining the pro-apoptotic proteins. These interactions can coexist, and the precise classification depends partly on cellular context. The simplified expression 'Bcl-2 inhibits apoptosis' is correct for the protective protein Bcl-2, but not for the entire family. Bax, Bak and BH3-only members are death-promoting.

The logic is a competition between protective capacity and stress-associated pressure. Imagine a cell in which the anti-apoptotic proteins already restrain several death-promoting molecules. A small additional stress may occupy the remaining protective capacity without causing release. If additional stress exceeds that capacity, effector activation becomes possible. This threshold idea explains why two cells with the same amount of Bax can respond differently to the same stimulus. A cell's sensitivity depends on which proteins are expressed, their binding partners and how strongly they are activated; a single ratio of Bax to Bcl-2 is only a rough heuristic, not a universal quantitative law.

Loss of trophic support, certain types of DNA damage, prolonged cellular stress and loss of appropriate matrix attachment can all favor intrinsic apoptosis. They do not activate identical BH3-only proteins in every cell. A growth factor can promote survival through pathways such as PI3K–Akt; Akt-dependent phosphorylation of Bad can promote its sequestration by 14-3-3 proteins, reducing its ability to neutralize protective Bcl-2 relatives. Withdrawal of that support may allow Bad and other death-promoting signals to become effective. The example illustrates how an extracellular survival signal can regulate the intrinsic route without itself being a death-receptor ligand.

In DNA damage responses, stabilization of the transcription factor p53 can increase expression of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Puma and Noxa. But p53 can also activate p21, a cell-cycle inhibitor, favoring arrest and repair rather than immediate death. Damage intensity, duration, cell type and other regulatory signals influence the outcome. It is incorrect to regard every activation of p53 as a commitment to apoptosis or every apoptosis event as p53-dependent. Once Bax/Bak-mediated MOMP occurs widely across mitochondria, however, the cell often moves beyond reversible checkpoint control toward a much more destructive state.

A straightforward perturbation helps locate the gate. Overexpress Bcl-2 and apply a stimulus that ordinarily causes mitochondrial apoptosis. If the protective capacity is effective, cytochrome c remains mainly mitochondrial and executioner activation decreases. Add cytochrome c directly to a competent cytosolic extract from those cells and the upstream block may be bypassed. Conversely, deleting both Bax and Bak can strongly impair canonical MOMP despite the production of upstream BH3-only signals. These experiments distinguish the trigger from the mitochondrial gate and the gate from the downstream response.

6. From cytochrome c release to the apoptosome

Once the outer mitochondrial membrane becomes permeable, cytochrome c reaches the cytosol. It does not itself cleave cellular proteins or DNA. It binds a cytosolic adaptor called apoptotic protease activating factor 1, abbreviated Apaf-1. An adaptor is a protein that brings molecular partners together and helps organize their interactions. In the absence of the appropriate stimulus, Apaf-1 generally occupies an inactive conformation.

Cytochrome c binding favors opening of the Apaf-1 conformation, together with nucleotide-dependent rearrangement involving ATP or dATP. Activated Apaf-1 molecules associate into a wheel-like oligomer, typically a seven-membered mammalian apoptosome. The structure acts as an activation platform. It recruits procaspase-9 through interactions between compatible caspase recruitment domains, or CARDs. These are protein interaction domains, not the catalytic sites of the enzymes. By recruiting procaspase-9 into the complex, the platform favors its activation and provides the context for efficient processing of executioner procaspases.

Active caspase-9 activates procaspase-3 and procaspase-7; the executioners then cleave numerous substrates. The apoptosome is therefore not a bag containing all caspases, nor is it a mitochondrial organelle. It is a cytosolic protein assembly that forms in response to a mitochondrial signal. In a diagram, the mitochondrial outer membrane should be above or beside the site of cytochrome c release; Apaf-1 and procaspase-9 should be located outside the mitochondrion.

A second consequence of MOMP helps explain why activation is sometimes efficient despite caspase inhibitors. Smac/DIABLO, another mitochondrial intermembrane-space protein, can be released alongside cytochrome c. It binds certain inhibitor of apoptosis proteins, including XIAP, relieving their restraint on apoptotic caspases. Cytochrome c promotes formation of the activation platform; Smac reduces a downstream inhibition. These are distinct contributions. Removing XIAP may increase the response to an existing activation signal but is not equivalent to automatically providing the missing signal from Apaf-1.

Classical cell-free experiments provided evidence for the sequence. Liu and colleagues in 1996 investigated the activation of apoptotic changes in extracts and identified cytochrome c and dATP as key requirements in their system [4]. Li and colleagues subsequently connected Apaf-1 and caspase-9 to the nucleotide-dependent activation complex [5]. A useful way to understand the experimental logic is to compare extracts lacking a required component with extracts supplemented by that component. If processing of caspases is lost when cytochrome c is removed and restored when purified cytochrome c is supplied, this supports a functional role rather than mere correlation. A control with the relevant nucleotide absent, and a separate control without active downstream machinery, help establish what is required.

Cell-free reconstitution has a limitation: physically disrupting a cell can mix components that would normally be separated by membranes. The ability of cytochrome c to activate a cytosolic extract does not alone establish that mitochondrial release occurs in living cells. Fractionation of intact-cell populations before and after a death stimulus, or imaging of cytochrome c redistribution, supplies complementary evidence. Fractionation requires controls for mitochondrial contamination of the cytosolic sample. A protein normally restricted to the mitochondrial matrix should not appear in a clean cytosolic fraction simply because the preparation damaged organelles.

The role of ATP also deserves care. The apoptosome uses nucleotide-dependent conformational changes and orderly cell death can require ongoing metabolic functions. However, apoptotic cells often lose mitochondrial ATP-generating capacity after MOMP, and glycolysis or residual ATP can support some processes. There is no universal ATP concentration that distinguishes apoptosis from every other mode of death. A very severe energy crisis may favor necrotic injury, while moderate injury can allow an apoptotic program to unfold. The biological outcome depends on both damage and remaining cellular capacity.

A useful reasoning test is to compare a Bax/Bak-deficient cell with an Apaf-1-deficient cell. The first may fail to release cytochrome c in response to intrinsic stress; the second can release it but cannot efficiently use the canonical apoptosome route. Both can show reduced caspase-3 activation in an appropriate experiment, but only measurement of upstream events separates their defects. This is why an executioner readout alone cannot locate the point of pathway failure.

7. Extrinsic apoptosis: information from the cell surface

The intrinsic pathway is suited to detecting the internal state of a cell, but tissues also need the capacity to instruct particular cells to die. A cell of the immune system, for example, may deliver a death-inducing signal to a target. In the canonical extrinsic pathway, a specific ligand binds a death receptor on the target cell's plasma membrane. A death receptor is a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor family with a cytosolic interaction region called a death domain. Fas, also called CD95 or APO-1, is the classic example.

Fas ligand (FasL) binds the extracellular region of Fas and promotes receptor clustering and productive rearrangements. The intracellular death domains of receptors then recruit the adaptor FADD, Fas-associated death domain protein. FADD also contains a death effector domain (DED). Its death domain interacts with Fas; its death effector domain recruits procaspase-8, which also has DEDs. The two types of domain have different binding roles. By using a modular adaptor, the receptor can transmit information across the membrane without itself being a protease.

The receptor-associated complex containing Fas, FADD and procaspase-8 is called the death-inducing signaling complex, or DISC. Procaspase-8 molecules are concentrated at the complex. Proximity favors dimerization and subsequent processing, producing catalytic activity that can be propagated to downstream targets. Caspase-10 can participate in human death-receptor signaling in some settings. For general pathway reasoning, caspase-8 is the principal initiator to track. If FADD is missing, the receptor may still bind ligand but efficient recruitment of the initiator is impaired.

Active caspase-8 can cleave executioner procaspases directly. The same receptor signal also has another important target: Bid, a BH3-only member of the Bcl-2 family. Cleavage of Bid produces truncated Bid (tBid), which promotes Bax/Bak-dependent MOMP in appropriate cells. The receptor signal thereby gains access to the intrinsic pathway, leading to release of cytochrome c, apoptosome formation and enhanced executioner activation. The two pathways should not be drawn as isolated mechanisms in which the outputs never interact.

The relative need for mitochondrial amplification varies. Cells often called type I in the Fas model can produce sufficient direct executioner activity from DISC-associated caspase-8 to undergo apoptosis without a strong requirement for mitochondrial amplification. In type II cells, direct signaling is insufficient, partly because downstream inhibitors can restrain the amount of executioner activity. Bid-dependent mitochondrial amplification is then important. These are functional categories of response, not universal permanent labels for all cells or all death receptors. Overexpression of Bcl-2 may strongly protect a type II cell from Fas-induced death but provide much less protection in a type I cell.

Extrinsic signaling itself is regulated. c-FLIP proteins resemble procaspase-8 in recruitment domains and can compete or interact with it at signaling platforms; different isoforms and abundance influence the resulting activity. A short form may inhibit productive procaspase-8 activation, whereas the long form can have more context-dependent effects. The important principle is that receptor occupancy does not guarantee executioner activation.

TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) is another death-domain-containing receptor, but it illustrates why one should not equate every receptor of this family with obligatory apoptosis. TNF binding commonly initiates a membrane-associated signaling complex that can promote inflammatory and pro-survival gene expression, including via NF-kappaB. Under conditions in which survival signaling is inadequate, a cytosolic complex involving FADD and caspase-8 can form and initiate apoptosis. The Fas DISC should not be copied unchanged onto TNFR1. TRAIL receptors DR4 and DR5 provide additional examples of death receptor initiation, without requiring separate pathway catalogs here.

A separate immune mechanism uses cytotoxic lymphocytes. Perforin assists delivery of granzymes to the target-cell cytosol; granzyme B can cleave death-related substrates, including executioner procaspases and Bid. It can therefore activate the target's dismantling machinery without relying solely on Fas receptor engagement. Perforin is not a caspase and granzyme B is a serine protease rather than a cysteine-dependent caspase. In an experiment blocking Fas, cytotoxic lymphocytes may still kill via the granule-mediated route. The existence of more than one route is biologically useful because defective target-cell receptor signaling does not necessarily make an infected or abnormal cell impossible to eliminate.

8. How executioner caspases dismantle the cell

Activation of executioner caspases is not identical to the sudden dissolution of the entire cell. Caspases cleave peptide bonds in selected substrates; those cleavages alter the functions of particular molecular systems. Some cleavages disable proteins, others liberate an active fragment. The characteristic morphology results from coordinated changes in the nucleus, cytoskeleton and membrane lipid organization.

Consider DNA fragmentation. DNA is not a substrate for caspase proteolysis, because a caspase hydrolyzes peptide rather than phosphodiester bonds. In many mammalian cells, the relevant nuclease is caspase-activated DNase (CAD, also known as DFF40). In a living cell, CAD is associated with its inhibitor ICAD (DFF45), which also serves as a folding chaperone for CAD. When caspase-3 cleaves ICAD, CAD is freed to act. It enters or acts within the nucleus and cleaves chromosomal DNA, frequently at accessible linker regions between nucleosomes.

A nucleosome contains DNA wrapped around a histone core; linker DNA separates adjacent cores. Protection of DNA by nucleosome-associated proteins makes periodic internucleosomal fragmentation possible. On agarose gel electrophoresis, fragments can appear as a ladder of bands separated by roughly 180–200 base pairs or multiples of that interval, depending on chromatin repeat length. The ladder is a distribution of fragment lengths, not a count of dying cells. Random DNA damage may give a broad smear, but neither a ladder nor its absence independently establishes how a cell died. Some apoptotic cells lack a prominent ladder because nuclease activation, chromatin structure or assay timing differs.

Caspases also cleave components of the nuclear lamina, the protein network lining the inner nuclear membrane. Loss of structural support facilitates changes in nuclear shape and fragmentation. Caspase-6 can be important for lamin cleavage in some systems. Caspase-3 and related enzymes process other nuclear proteins, including poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), involved in responding to DNA damage. Cleavage of PARP1 helps terminate its normal repair-related functions and limits futile expenditure of NAD+ during dismantling. Detecting the characteristic cleaved PARP1 fragment by immunoblot provides a useful biochemical marker, although it is not an independent measure of long-term survival.

The actin cortex immediately beneath the plasma membrane normally supports cell shape and helps control surface tension. Caspase-dependent cleavage of proteins such as ROCK1 can produce active kinase fragments that promote myosin light-chain phosphorylation and actomyosin contraction. Increased contractile pressure, together with altered attachment of the cortex to the membrane, can drive bleb formation. Inhibition of this contractile process may reduce blebbing without necessarily preventing the activation of caspases or DNA fragmentation. An apoptotic cell with few blebs is not necessarily a surviving cell.

A crucial membrane change involves phosphatidylserine (PS), an anionic phospholipid normally enriched in the cytosolic leaflet of the plasma membrane. ATP-dependent aminophospholipid flippases help maintain this asymmetry by returning PS from the outer leaflet toward the cytosolic side. During apoptosis, caspase-dependent changes can inactivate the relevant flippases and activate lipid scramblases such as Xkr8, which allow redistribution of phospholipids across the bilayer. PS therefore appears at the outer surface without the entire bilayer becoming freely permeable to macromolecules. The exposed lipid serves as a recognition signal for removal.

These changes are connected but not interchangeable. A mutation in CAD may abolish prominent DNA laddering while leaving caspase activation, PS exposure and eventual death intact. A defect in membrane scrambling may weaken PS-dependent clearance while nuclear fragmentation proceeds. A defect in upstream caspase activation may prevent several changes simultaneously. Comparing multiple readouts reveals whether a perturbation affects a shared upstream cause or just one downstream branch.

Even if executioner activity is suppressed after mitochondrial damage, the cell may remain severely compromised. MOMP can cause loss of respiratory function and additional injury that eventually kills cells through caspase-independent mechanisms. Therefore a broad caspase inhibitor can prevent several classic apoptotic features and still fail to ensure long-term functional survival. A useful definition of successful protection requires evidence that cells remain viable and, when appropriate, can resume proliferation or function after removal of the original stimulus.

9. Recognition, engulfment and disposal

Dismantling a cell is only half the task. A tissue must also recognize and remove the corpse. Efferocytosis is the engulfment of dying or dead cells, especially apoptotic cells, by phagocytes or other cells capable of phagocytosis. The process prevents corpses from accumulating, restricts exposure of internal cellular material and can promote the resolution of local inflammation.

Dying cells can release molecules that attract phagocytes, often called find-me signals. These include extracellular nucleotides in certain contexts. Attraction is not the same as recognition: a recruited phagocyte still has to identify which cell should be engulfed. External phosphatidylserine provides an important eat-me signal. Some receptors recognize PS directly, while other receptors bind bridging proteins that connect PS on the dying cell to a phagocyte surface receptor. For example, MFG-E8 can link PS-containing membranes to particular integrins, whereas Gas6 or protein S can connect PS to TAM family receptor signaling. The examination principle is that exposed lipid is recognized on one cell and transduced into an engulfment response in another.

A healthy cell usually keeps most PS on its inner leaflet and may express surface molecules that restrain inappropriate engulfment. Consequently, the distinction between a living and dying target depends on the combined recognition environment. PS exposure can also occur transiently in certain non-apoptotic situations, including activation of some cell types. It is not a universal proof of irreversible death and is not by itself sufficient to describe the molecular route by which a cell died.

Once a phagocyte recognizes an appropriate target, signals reorganize its actin cytoskeleton. Membrane protrusions extend around the target or apoptotic fragment and fuse to create a membrane-bound phagosome. Fusion and maturation involving lysosomal compartments expose the enclosed material to acidic hydrolases. The apoptotic cell thus undergoes internal dismantling, while much of the final digestion takes place in the phagocyte. Lysosomes participate here as a downstream disposal system; this is not the same as the dying cell digesting its own cytoplasm by autophagy.

The usual limited inflammatory response to apoptotic clearance reflects several cooperating mechanisms: the plasma membrane restricts leakage during much of execution, phagocytosis removes material before rupture, and phagocyte responses can promote anti-inflammatory signaling. The description 'apoptosis is non-inflammatory' is useful only as a typical contrast. If corpses remain unengulfed and rupture, intracellular components can enter extracellular space. Certain forms of regulated death with apoptotic features also occur in immunogenic contexts. The absence of inflammation is therefore neither a necessary nor a sufficient molecular test of apoptosis.

The distinction between execution and clearance can be tested genetically or experimentally. Imagine three cultures with identical rates of induction of apoptosis. In one, phagocytes are functional; in another, PS recognition is disrupted; in the third, caspase activation is inhibited before execution. The first may contain few visible corpses because removal is fast. The second can contain many corpses with cleaved caspase-3 because death proceeds but engulfment is delayed. The third may show little apoptotic morphology because execution is suppressed. Corpse abundance alone could misleadingly suggest that the second culture had a higher death induction rate than the first. Measuring initiation, execution and clearance separately avoids this error.

10. Apoptosis in development, homeostasis and disease

The cell population balance introduced at the start can now be interpreted molecularly. Growth factor signaling may both stimulate proliferation and protect cells from death. Withdrawal of such signals therefore changes the survival rate as well as the division rate. In some developing neural populations, more cells are generated than can be supported by the available trophic factors from target tissues. Cells that establish adequate survival support are more likely to persist; others activate a death program. This helps match the size of the neuronal population to the needs of the tissue. It does not imply that all neuronal death or all developmental pruning proceeds through apoptosis.

During vertebrate limb development, removal of interdigital tissue contributes to the separation of digits in many species. Cells in these regions receive developmental cues that favor death. The resulting tissue remodeling is a spatially organized process: the same core death machinery can be activated in one small region while neighboring cells remain viable. Different patterns of survival and remodeling can help preserve webbing where it is adaptive. The outcome depends on signals determining which cells activate the pathway, rather than different kinds of caspase being necessary for every digit.

The immune system uses regulated cell deletion both while constructing a functional repertoire and after an immune response. During lymphocyte development, cells with dangerously self-reactive receptors may be eliminated by selection mechanisms that include apoptosis. Following an immune response, many expanded effector populations are reduced when antigenic or trophic signals decline. Apoptosis thus contributes to prevention of inappropriate immune activation and restoration of cell numbers. Fas pathway defects can lead to lymphocyte accumulation and autoimmunity, illustrating why regulation of cell removal is not merely a developmental concern.

Cells with unrepaired genomic damage can threaten the integrity of a tissue. A checkpoint response may initially delay proliferation and permit repair. If the damage and accompanying signals favor intrinsic apoptosis, the cell can be removed rather than transmitting its damage through future divisions. The relationship between arrest and death is conditional: a cell may arrest and later recover, remain senescent or undergo apoptosis. The presence of a DNA lesion does not establish which outcome occurred.

Cancer illustrates the consequence of defective removal. BCL2 overexpression in many follicular lymphomas, associated with the classic t(14;18) rearrangement, increases survival of cells that would otherwise be more susceptible to apoptosis. The rearrangement can place BCL2 under the influence of immunoglobulin regulatory elements. Its important conceptual consequence is survival rather than a direct replacement for cyclin–CDK activity. Increased survival alone can enlarge a population and permit additional changes to accumulate, although malignant progression generally involves more than one alteration. Conversely, excessive apoptosis in vulnerable cell populations can contribute to tissue loss; a general increase in apoptosis is not intrinsically beneficial. The appropriate rate depends on the tissue and physiological state.

Loss of extracellular matrix attachment can also induce intrinsic apoptosis in certain anchorage-dependent cells, a phenomenon called anoikis. Adhesion normally communicates information about the cell's location and environment. If appropriate attachment signals disappear, protective signaling can decline and the Bcl-2 family balance can shift toward death. Cells that resist anoikis can survive in environments that would normally eliminate them. Detailed signaling and tumor invasion are separate topics, but the example emphasizes that apoptosis can respond to loss of a normal condition rather than to the arrival of a toxin.

The molecular machinery is not identical in every organism. The conserved nematode pathway provides one example. Plant programmed cell death is another important boundary: plants use regulated death in development and defense, such as differentiation of xylem tracheary elements, but they do not possess the canonical animal caspase–Apaf-1 system. Plant metacaspases differ in substrate specificity from animal caspases. It is therefore inappropriate to assume that an animal-style Fas–caspase-8 pathway must exist whenever a plant cell dies in a regulated way. The term apoptosis in this chapter primarily describes the well-characterized animal mechanism.

11. Experimental evidence: choose the readout that answers the question

The experimental study of apoptosis often begins with a deceptively simple question: is a treatment killing cells by an apoptotic mechanism? A rigorous answer requires several independent observations. The total cell number may fall because proliferation slows, cells detach during handling, apoptosis increases, or another death process occurs. Even if death is established, the pathway responsible remains uncertain. Each assay measures a different property, so its result must be interpreted at the correct stage.

Annexin V is a protein that binds phosphatidylserine in a calcium-dependent manner. A fluorescently labeled annexin V reagent can bind exposed PS on the outer surface of intact cells [6]. In the standard dual-staining interpretation, propidium iodide (PI), a nucleic acid-binding dye, enters cells only when plasma-membrane permeability is sufficiently increased. Thus annexin V and PI together report two different events: exposure of a membrane lipid and loss of barrier function.

In a flow cytometer, each recorded cell event is assigned fluorescence intensities for the two markers. If annexin V is on the horizontal axis and PI on the vertical axis, the lower left region contains double-negative events, usually compatible with intact viable cells. The lower right contains annexin-positive/PI-negative events, commonly associated with early apoptosis. The upper right contains double-positive events, compatible with late apoptosis or secondary necrosis but also with primary membrane-disruptive injury. The upper left contains annexin-negative/PI-positive events, compatible with damaged membranes in cells without detectable annexin binding at that moment. These are operational categories, not unique mechanistic identities.

An untreated or vehicle-treated population provides the baseline distribution. Single-stained samples establish the contribution of each dye to the fluorescence channels; unstained cells provide information about autofluorescence. A positive apoptosis control shows that the assay can detect the expected changes. Exclusion of debris and doublets must be consistent between conditions, and detached cells should not be discarded without consideration because dying cells often detach. Calcium is required for annexin V binding, so an incompatible buffer can cause a misleading negative signal. At examination level, these controls matter more than instrument-specific settings.

TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling) detects accessible DNA ends, typically by adding labeled nucleotides to free 3-prime hydroxyl termini with terminal transferase. It is useful for localizing cells with fragmented DNA in tissue. It does not establish that the breaks were generated by CAD; breaks can appear during other injury processes and sample handling. A TUNEL-positive nucleus with apoptotic morphology and activated executioner caspases is stronger evidence than an isolated TUNEL-positive measurement. Conversely, early apoptotic cells may not yet have enough accessible DNA ends to stain prominently.

A western blot uses antibodies to detect particular proteins or their cleavage products after separation by size. Antibodies recognizing cleaved caspase-3 or cleaved PARP1 can provide evidence of executioner activation and substrate processing. Loading controls are needed for meaningful comparisons. An intact precursor band may decline because it is cleaved or because total protein is lost; measuring only the disappearance of a band can be ambiguous. Measuring both precursor and appropriate cleavage product, with a functional activity readout when possible, is more informative.

Fluorogenic or colorimetric peptide substrates can report caspase-related enzymatic activity through the release of a detectable group after proteolysis. A substrate containing a preferred sequence may be cleaved by more than one caspase; a signal should not be assigned to a unique enzyme solely from a short peptide sequence. Genetic loss of the candidate caspase or an appropriately controlled inhibition experiment can strengthen the assignment. Caspase activity measured in a mixed population is also not the same as the fraction of individual cells dying: a small subset of strongly active cells may dominate a bulk signal.

To determine whether the intrinsic pathway was used, examine cytochrome c redistribution by imaging or subcellular fractionation and assess caspase-9 or apoptosome dependence. To determine whether death-receptor signaling was necessary, compare responses with loss or blockade of Fas, FADD or caspase-8, while checking that the cells retain the capacity for mitochondrial apoptosis. Mitochondrial membrane potential dyes report changes related to the inner membrane electrochemical gradient. A decrease can accompany mitochondrial injury, but does not directly prove outer-membrane permeabilization. Conversely, a transiently preserved potential does not exclude cytochrome c release.

Time is a vital experimental variable. An initial increase in annexin-positive/PI-negative events followed by increasing double-positive events, together with caspase activation and characteristic morphology, is consistent with a population progressing through apoptosis toward secondary membrane breakdown. A sudden PI-positive increase without earlier detectable caspase activity is more compatible with early membrane injury, although sparse sampling can miss intermediate stages. Compare several times, use independent readouts and avoid naming a pathway from one final endpoint. The best assay combination is selected according to whether the question concerns the trigger, initiation, execution, membrane integrity, clearance or durable survival.

12. Quantitative reasoning without confusing prevalence and rate

Apoptosis is not defined by a universal mathematical equation, but cell counting and experimental percentages are important at CSIR-NET level. Begin with the simplest accounting relation for a closed population. Let N0 denote the number of viable cells at the start of an interval, B the net additional viable cells produced by completed divisions, and D the viable cells lost to death during that interval. If there is no migration or other loss, the final viable count N1 equals N0 plus B minus D. All three terms are numbers of cells. Each completed division of a viable mother into two viable daughters contributes one net additional cell, not two, because the mother is already counted.

For example, a compartment initially contains 2,000 viable cells. During a day 300 successful divisions occur, and 260 cells die by apoptosis, with no other losses. The final viable count is 2,000 + 300 − 260 = 2,040 cells. Net change relative to the initial count is 40/2,000 × 100 = 2%. If an intervention halves apoptotic deaths to 130 but does not change division, the count becomes 2,170, which is 8.5% above the initial population. This example shows that a survival-promoting mutation can change net growth without changing the rate of division.

Flow cytometry introduces a separate denominator. Suppose 10,000 correctly gated cell events are recorded. There are 7,000 double-negative events, 1,800 annexin-positive/PI-negative events, 900 double-positive events and 300 annexin-negative/PI-positive events. The early-apoptosis-compatible fraction is 1,800/10,000 = 18%. The total annexin-positive fraction is (1,800 + 900)/10,000 = 27%. The PI-positive fraction is (900 + 300)/10,000 = 12%. The double-positive cells are included in both marker-positive fractions; adding 27% and 12% would double-count them. The fraction positive for at least one marker is (1,800 + 900 + 300)/10,000 = 30%. Do not label all 30% as proven apoptosis. The groups describe staining patterns and need mechanistic corroboration.

A percentage at one time is also not a death rate. In a simplified stable compartment, let J be the number of cells entering a detectable apoptotic stage per hour and let tau be their average time, in hours, in that stage before they exit it or are cleared. If J and tau are approximately constant and the observation is made after the system reaches a steady state, the average number A visible in that stage is J × tau. The relation follows because each hour J cells enter and each contributes tau cell-hours of detectable occupancy; the average number present equals the entry rate multiplied by average residence time. If total tissue cell number N is approximately constant, the snapshot fraction f equals A/N = J × tau/N. Units cancel: cells per hour times hours divided by cells gives a dimensionless fraction.

For a tissue with N = 5,000 cells, suppose 50 cells are visibly apoptotic at a stable snapshot and the detectable stage lasts an average of 2 hours. J is 50/2 = 25 cells per hour, or 0.5% of the population per hour. If clearance slows so that residence time rises to 4 hours while entry remains 25 per hour, expected visible corpses rise to 100, giving 2% snapshot prevalence rather than 1%. Thus the observed fraction doubles with no increase in the rate of induction. This calculation assumes a constant population, stable entry and defined detectability; it should not be applied to a sharply changing culture or to a mixture of unknown stages.

Consider another hypothetical test. Untreated cells show 4% early-apoptotic-compatible events; a DNA-damaging stimulus produces 34%, and the same treatment in the presence of an inhibitor produces 10%. The induced excess in the untreated inhibitor comparison is 34 − 4 = 30 percentage points. If the inhibitor-alone baseline is also 4%, the residual excess is 10 − 4 = 6 points. The apparent suppression of the treatment-induced excess is (30 − 6)/30 × 100 = 80%. The result is not proof that 80% of all deaths were prevented: the assay reports a snapshot, baseline equivalence is an assumption, and alternative forms of death might remain. If inhibitor-alone baseline differs, its actual value must be used.

These examples illustrate a transferable principle: identify precisely what is being counted, the denominator, the interval, and the biological meaning. A cell death percentage, a marker-positive fraction and a rate of entry into death are different quantities. Distinguishing them prevents errors in experimental inference and numerical questions.

13. Integrating apoptosis with other cellular outcomes

Classical apoptosis, necrotic injury and autophagy are easiest to distinguish by their primary biological processes rather than by one morphological feature. Apoptosis is a regulated dismantling mode commonly associated with caspase activation and orderly removal. In classical necrotic injury, catastrophic disruption of cellular homeostasis often causes swelling, loss of membrane integrity and release of intracellular components. Some necrotic modes are themselves regulated, so the historical contrast between programmed apoptosis and entirely accidental necrosis is too absolute. Detailed necrotic injury mechanisms belong to the next chapter.

Autophagy is a process of delivering intracellular material to lysosomes for degradation and recycling. It may help cells adapt to nutrient deprivation or remove damaged organelles, and it can be active in viable cells. An autophagosome is a double-membrane carrier that encloses intracellular material; it is not an apoptotic body, which is a fragment of a dying cell bounded by its plasma membrane. Seeing autophagic structures in a dying cell does not establish that autophagy caused the death. Evidence of causation would require showing that inhibition of the relevant autophagy process prevents or substantially changes the death, with appropriate controls. The detailed pathway is developed separately in Node 2.D.10.

The same stress can produce different outcomes at different intensities or in different cell types. A moderate disturbance can induce repair or autophagy, a persistent disturbance can initiate apoptosis, and a severe disruption may produce early membrane failure. The order is not a universal three-stage progression. A cell exposed to severe injury may die without traversing a recognizable apoptotic stage, while a cell undergoing apoptosis can later lose membrane integrity after clearance fails.

The distinction between mitochondrial and plasma-membrane events is particularly important. In intrinsic apoptosis, outer mitochondrial membrane permeabilization is an upstream event. PI uptake measures permeability of the plasma membrane. A cell may therefore show cytosolic cytochrome c yet remain PI-negative. There is no contradiction: different membranes are being tested. Similarly, cytochrome c's respiratory function in the intermembrane space is distinct from its adaptor-activating function in the cytosol.

Think through four perturbations before leaving the chapter. Preventing Bax/Bak activation blocks a major upstream intrinsic event. Removing Apaf-1 interrupts the next step even if mitochondrial release occurs. Suppressing executioner caspases can prevent many structural changes after initiation. Preventing phagocytic recognition delays disposal after the cell has undergone the death program. All may change the number of apoptotic cells seen in tissue, but at different points and sometimes in opposite directions. A pathway can be reconstructed by locating the last event that still occurs and the first event that fails.

The unifying lesson is that apoptosis has separable stages: a survival-versus-death decision, pathway-specific initiator activation, shared execution, and clearance. A cell can show evidence of one stage without every later stage having occurred. For examination purposes, trace the causal chain and use multiple readouts, rather than memorizing a single marker or equating an endpoint with a mechanism. The following practice problems apply this reasoning to genetic, biochemical and cell-count observations.

12. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style questions. Select the best answer, then compare the reasoning.

1. Which observation best distinguishes early classical apoptosis from early catastrophic plasma-membrane injury?

  1. Shrinkage with maintained membrane barrier
  2. Immediate unrestricted leakage
  3. DNA synthesis
  4. Chromosome alignment
Answer and reasoning

A. Shrinkage with retained barrier function fits early apoptosis. B favors membrane-disruptive injury; C is replication and D is mitosis, neither a death criterion.

2. What is the direct catalytic substrate of a caspase?

  1. DNA phosphodiester bonds
  2. Peptide bonds in selected proteins
  3. Phospholipid headgroups
  4. ATP at the respiratory chain
Answer and reasoning

B. Caspases hydrolyze peptide bonds, typically after Asp. DNA is cleaved by nucleases such as CAD, not caspases. C concerns lipid chemistry; D is unrelated to protease specificity.

3. Which pair is correctly assigned?

  1. Caspase-9 — DISC
  2. Caspase-8 — Apaf-1 platform
  3. Caspase-9 — apoptosome
  4. CAD — death receptor
Answer and reasoning

C. Apaf-1 recruits caspase-9; Fas-associated DISC recruits caspase-8. CAD is an execution-phase nuclease, not a membrane receptor.

4. Which protein is directly involved in mitochondrial permeabilization?

  1. FADD
  2. ICAD
  3. PARP1
  4. Bax
Answer and reasoning

D. Bax and Bak form membrane-permeabilizing assemblies. FADD recruits death receptor initiators; ICAD restrains CAD; PARP1 participates in DNA damage response.

5. Assertion: Bcl-2 overexpression can protect from intrinsic apoptosis. Reason: Bcl-2 can restrain pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. The protective effect follows from limiting activation of Bax/Bak and MOMP. The reason is therefore causal, not merely associated.

6. Which change directly permits CAD activity in the classical pathway?

  1. Cytochrome c cleavage of DNA
  2. Proteolysis of ICAD
  3. Fas binding to nuclear DNA
  4. Increased cyclin B
Answer and reasoning

B. Caspase cleavage of ICAD liberates CAD. Cytochrome c is a signaling protein; Fas is a membrane receptor; cyclin B is a cell-cycle regulator.

7. In early apoptosis PS exposure is best explained by:

  1. Mandatory plasma-membrane rupture
  2. DNA condensation alone
  3. Altered lipid scrambling and flippase function
  4. Lysosomal acidification of the extracellular medium
Answer and reasoning

C. Caspase-dependent scrambling and loss of inward lipid transport redistribute PS while the membrane can remain PI-impermeable. A confuses lipid asymmetry with permeability; B and D do not directly account for PS redistribution.

8. Which molecule connects Fas signaling to mitochondrial amplification?

  1. Histone H1
  2. Cyclin D
  3. PI
  4. Truncated Bid
Answer and reasoning

D. Caspase-8 cleavage of Bid produces tBid, which promotes Bax/Bak activation. Histone and cyclin proteins serve other processes; PI is an assay dye.

9. A viable senescent cell is distinguished from a fully dismantled apoptotic cell because it:

  1. Can maintain metabolism despite arrest
  2. Must replicate every hour
  3. Has necessarily lost its plasma membrane
  4. Has necessarily activated CAD
Answer and reasoning

A. Senescence entails durable proliferation arrest while viability and metabolism can persist. B contradicts arrest; C and D are not defining features.

10. Which statement correctly compares C. elegans and mammals?

  1. Mammalian caspases are plant metacaspases
  2. CED-4 and Apaf-1 share activation-platform logic but differ in cytochrome c dependence
  3. CED-9 directly cleaves DNA
  4. CED-3 is a survival-only receptor
Answer and reasoning

B. The conserved relationship concerns activation and protection, not identical mitochondrial signals. CED-9 is protective and CED-3 is a caspase; A conflates distinct protein families.

13. Part C — Mechanism, data and quantitative reasoning

Original examination-style questions. Select the best answer, then compare the reasoning.

1. A DNA-damaging treatment causes cytochrome c release in wild type and mutant X. Cleaved caspase-9 and caspase-3 appear only in wild type. Which defect is most consistent?

  1. Loss of Bax/Bak
  2. Loss of Apaf-1
  3. Loss of engulfment receptor
  4. Increased PS scrambling
Answer and reasoning

B. Release shows the outer-membrane step occurred. Apaf-1 loss prevents canonical caspase-9 activation after release. A predicts impaired release, C is downstream clearance, D is downstream lipid redistribution.

2. A Fas-stimulated cell shows caspase-8 activity but no cytochrome c release after Bcl-2 overexpression. Caspase-3 activation and death are strongly reduced. Interpret.

  1. Fas did not bind ligand
  2. Bcl-2 directly destroys Fas
  3. Cell requires mitochondrial amplification
  4. Death is necessarily autophagy
Answer and reasoning

C. Initiation occurred because caspase-8 is active. The missing release and reduced execution indicate mitochondrial amplification is required in this response (type II-like). A conflicts with activity; B is not the canonical action of Bcl-2; D has no supporting evidence.

3. In a different Fas-treated cell, Bax/Bak deletion prevents cytochrome c release but caspase-3 activation and death remain robust. What follows?

  1. Direct execution by receptor-initiated caspase-8 can suffice
  2. MOMP must have occurred
  3. CAD is upstream of Fas
  4. Caspase-8 is a mitochondrial protein
Answer and reasoning

A. The receptor pathway can activate executioners without strong mitochondrial amplification (type I-like). B contradicts the observed block; C reverses order; D misplaces caspase-8.

4. A mutant lacks CAD. Caspase-3 cleavage and PS exposure occur, but internucleosomal DNA laddering is absent. Which prediction is justified?

  1. All apoptosis is prevented
  2. Fas cannot bind ligand
  3. Bcl-2 has necessarily increased
  4. DNA fragmentation branch is impaired; other apoptotic events can continue
Answer and reasoning

D. CAD acts downstream of executioners. Removing it separates DNA fragmentation from other branches. A overstates a single effector defect; B and C concern upstream events not implied.

5. In a worm, loss of ced-9 causes excess death, while simultaneous loss of ced-3 strongly suppresses it. What is the best interpretation?

  1. CED-3 acts as a brake on CED-9
  2. CED-3 acts downstream of CED-9-dependent restraint
  3. CED-9 is a nuclease
  4. The genes cannot share a pathway
Answer and reasoning

B. Removing the protective brake requires downstream execution machinery to cause death. Removing CED-3 masks the excessive death phenotype. A reverses function; C is incorrect; D ignores the genetic interaction.

6. A gated sample has 5,000 events: 3,500 double negative, 900 annexin+/PI−, 400 double positive and 200 annexin−/PI+. What are the annexin-positive and PI-positive fractions?

  1. 18%, 4%
  2. 26%, 8%
  3. 26%, 12%
  4. 30%, 12%
Answer and reasoning

C. Annexin positive = (900+400)/5000 = 26%; PI positive = (400+200)/5000 = 12%. A omits double positives; B omits PI-only events; D counts all marker-positive events for annexin.

7. A steady tissue contains 6,000 cells, 120 of which are in a detectable apoptotic stage lasting an average of 3 h. Estimate entry rate under steady-state assumptions.

  1. 40 cells/h
  2. 360 cells/h
  3. 20 cells/h
  4. 2 cells/h
Answer and reasoning

A. A=Jτ, so J=120/3=40 cells/h. B multiplies rather than divides; C and D confuse different denominators. The snapshot prevalence is 2%, not a 2% per-hour rate.

8. A tissue starts with 1,500 cells. There are 180 completed divisions, 90 apoptotic losses and 30 other losses. No migration. Final viable number?

  1. 1,740
  2. 1,620
  3. 1,560
  4. 1,380
Answer and reasoning

C. N=1500+180−90−30=1560. A double-counts daughters; B ignores the death total incorrectly; D omits additions.

9. TUNEL signal increases after treatment, but no caspase activation or apoptotic morphology is measured. What conclusion is most defensible?

  1. Intrinsic apoptosis proved
  2. Extrinsic apoptosis proved
  3. All cells are dead
  4. DNA ends increased; death mechanism remains unresolved
Answer and reasoning

D. TUNEL detects accessible DNA ends, not a unique enzyme or route. A and B assign pathways without evidence; C confuses a molecular readout with all-cell viability.

10. In a fractionation experiment, cytosolic cytochrome c increases after treatment, but a mitochondrial matrix marker also appears in the same cytosolic preparation. What is the key concern?

  1. Possible contamination or organelle disruption during preparation
  2. Cytosolic fraction proves MOMP specifically
  3. Matrix marker proves death-receptor clustering
  4. Caspase-8 activity is established
Answer and reasoning

A. The matrix marker flags contamination or organelle damage, weakening a specific release inference. B ignores control failure; C and D are unrelated to fraction purity.

11. A drug increases annexin-positive/PI-negative events from a 5% baseline to 45%. In inhibitor-treated samples the baseline is 5% and drug plus inhibitor gives 15%. Suppression of drug-induced excess?

  1. 30%
  2. 75%
  3. 66.7%
  4. 100%
Answer and reasoning

B. Excess without inhibitor =40 points; with inhibitor=10 points. Suppression=(40−10)/40=75%. A is a percentage-point difference, C uses an inappropriate denominator, D ignores residual excess.

12. A pan-caspase inhibitor removes blebbing and cleavage of PARP1 after widespread MOMP, but cells cannot form colonies after recovery. What does this show?

  1. Inhibitor necessarily increased proliferation
  2. MOMP is downstream of CAD
  3. Suppression of apoptotic morphology need not rescue long-term survival
  4. All cells became quiescent
Answer and reasoning

C. Mitochondrial dysfunction can remain lethal despite blocking executioner activity. A contradicts the outcome; B reverses sequence; D is not established by colony loss alone.

13. Two samples have equal active-caspase-positive induction rates. Sample B has twice as many persistent apoptotic corpses as A because its phagocytes fail to recognize exposed PS. Most likely difference?

  1. Faster S phase
  2. Longer corpse residence time
  3. Increased Apaf-1 expression must be present
  4. Reduced execution in B proved
Answer and reasoning

B. A longer clearance time increases snapshot corpse abundance without higher induction. A and C lack evidence; D conflicts with equal measured induction.

14. A cell-free extract from Bcl-2-overexpressing cells responds to added cytochrome c plus nucleotide with caspase activation. The intact cells resisted DNA damage-induced MOMP. Interpretation?

  1. Cytosolic activation machinery is competent; upstream membrane gate was protective
  2. Bcl-2 is a caspase
  3. Cytochrome c is a death receptor
  4. The extract proves Fas dependence
Answer and reasoning

A. Direct addition bypasses the membrane gate. B and C misidentify molecules; D is untested. Extract competence supports, but does not alone establish, all intact-cell pathway details.
