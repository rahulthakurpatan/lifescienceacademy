1. The developmental problem: organ identity in a flower

A young flower begins as a small mound of dividing cells. At this stage the floral apex has neither mature pollen-bearing anthers nor conspicuous petals, yet it will generate structures with very different shapes, cellular compositions and reproductive functions. The outer structures protect the bud, the next group often contributes to attraction of pollinators, the stamens produce pollen, and the central carpels contain the ovules. Development must therefore coordinate both the location of each primordium and the kind of organ that it becomes. The challenge is not simply to make a large number of cells. It is to assign different developmental programmes to neighbouring cell populations that contain essentially the same inherited genome.

A primordium is the early group of cells from which a particular organ develops. In plants, most cells are immobilized relative to their neighbours by cell walls, so the conversion of a primordium into a petal or a stamen involves coordinated changes in growth, division orientation, differentiation and tissue organization rather than migration of the cells to a distant anatomical location. The primary decision about identity can be studied independently of the later process of making a correctly shaped organ. A primordium in a position normally occupied by a stamen can instead make a petal-like structure. The position may be approximately normal even though the fate is abnormal. This type of change is called a homeotic transformation. The distinction between position and fate is the essential starting point for reading floral mutant experiments.

Consider a flower in which a normally green sepal becomes larger and white. This observation alone does not prove that the organ has become a petal. A mutation in chlorophyll biosynthesis might make a sepal pale without changing its fundamental identity. A mutation that affects the distribution of cell divisions might enlarge it. A stronger claim requires the comparison of multiple features, such as epidermal cell morphology, vascular and tissue organization, the pattern of expression of organ-specific genes and the developmental history of the primordium. Similarly, a stamen that fails to produce viable pollen can still be recognizably a stamen. Male sterility is not a synonym for replacement of stamens by petals or carpels.

The most informative mutants change the identity of several organs in a coherent spatial pattern. When the second and third groups of floral primordia are repeatedly affected while the first remains comparatively normal, the common effect points toward a regulator that acts in a shared region. An explanation based on a separate defective structural protein for each organ would require two independent changes. A single mutation affecting two adjacent organ types instead suggests that they share part of their regulatory machinery. This is the conceptual insight behind the classical genetic analysis of Arabidopsis and Antirrhinum. The repeated combinations of organ changes allow a student to reconstruct the normal developmental system from abnormal outcomes.

A developmental regulator differs from an ordinary structural component in the scope of its effects. A protein needed to form a cell wall in one tissue may influence the final mechanical properties of that tissue. A regulatory protein that controls the expression of many genes can redirect a cell population into a substantially different programme. In practice the distinction is not absolute, because growth and fate influence one another, but the patterns observed in classical floral homeotic mutants strongly support an upstream role for specific transcriptional regulators. The molecular identities of several of these regulators were subsequently established, connecting the genetic argument to biochemical evidence.

This chapter treats the flower as an experimental system. The question to ask of each example is not simply which gene name belongs to which organ. Instead, determine what the normal region of activity is, what the mutation changes, what developmental programmes remain possible, what the resulting organ resembles and which parts of the explanation require additional evidence. The general model of floral identity introduced in the preceding curriculum node is used as a predictive framework. Its application to two independently studied flowering plants will show both why it works and why a textbook model should not be treated as a universal description of every allele and every species.

Evidence and further reading: Bowman et al. (1989), classical homeotic mutant analysis.

2. Two model plants and their normal flowers

Arabidopsis thaliana is a small flowering plant of the mustard family, Brassicaceae. Its suitability for genetic study reflects a combination of practical properties: it is small enough to grow many individuals in limited space, it has a relatively short generation interval under favourable conditions, and self-fertilization permits the maintenance of genetically defined lines. Controlled crosses are also possible. Recessive mutations can therefore be recovered in selfed progeny, maintained in heterozygotes when the homozygous mutant is sterile, and combined with other mutations to investigate genetic interactions. The usefulness of Arabidopsis is not that every feature of its flower is universal, but that observations can be linked to heritable changes with unusual clarity.

A typical Arabidopsis flower has four sepals, four petals, six stamens and a central gynoecium formed from two fused carpels. Four of the stamens are longer than the other two. The gynoecium is the female reproductive organ system, and the carpels form the structures that enclose ovules. These numbers provide a useful baseline, but the four whorls refer to four positional and developmental domains, not to four individual organs. The fused central structure must not be scored as though it represented only one carpel. Similarly, the six stamens form the third whorl even though they differ in length. An organ count and a whorl count are measurements of different properties.

Antirrhinum majus, the garden snapdragon, belongs to Plantaginaceae. Its mature flower is visually striking because the corolla is bilaterally symmetric rather than radially symmetric. A bilateral flower has a plane of reflection symmetry, whereas a radially symmetric flower can be divided into similar sectors through more than one plane. Snapdragon petals are fused into a corolla with distinct upper and lower regions. The typical flower has five sepals, five petals in the fused corolla, four fertile stamens and a reduced fifth stamen position, together with a central gynoecium of two fused carpels. These differences make Antirrhinum valuable for separating the conserved identity of an organ from the species-specific rules that govern its shape, number and symmetry.

A petal in Arabidopsis and a petal in Antirrhinum are related developmental structures even though the mature organs differ greatly in shape and architecture. Petal identity can be specified by related regulatory mechanisms while other regulators determine whether the primordia grow separately or produce a fused corolla, whether upper and lower regions grow differently, and which epidermal cell types differentiate in particular regions. In the same way, stamens may differ in length or fertility without losing their identity as stamens. The genetic comparison is meaningful precisely because the mature flowers are not identical.

Both plants allow a geneticist to ask whether a change in a gene has a reproducible effect on particular positions in the developing flower. In an experiment based on mature flowers, the researcher first needs a wild-type reference with a clearly established arrangement. In a study of immature buds, the developmental stage must also be considered. A small third-whorl primordium sampled too early may have none of the mature characteristics of a stamen; comparison with a mature wild-type flower would create a misleading impression of developmental arrest. Comparisons should therefore be made between equivalent stages, as far as the mutation permits. For mutants that alter the developmental schedule itself, a time series and independently recognizable landmarks are more informative than a single chronological age.

The wild-type arrangement can be represented as a sequence from outside to centre: sepal, petal, stamen, carpel. This sequence is common to the two model plants at the level of identity classes. It does not mean that all organs arise simultaneously, that every primordium has an equivalent rate of growth, or that the geometry of the whorls can be ignored. A flower with displaced or missing primordia cannot always be divided into four simple visible rings. The experimenter then uses early microscopy and the position of neighbouring organs to establish the original developmental region before assigning a transformed identity.

3. From visible homeosis to the genetic model

The historical importance of floral homeotic mutations lies in the way they connected an observable anatomical change to a general theory of developmental patterning. Before molecular cloning of the responsible regulators, systematic mutant analysis in the two plants had already suggested that organ identities depended on combinations of activities. The 1989 work of Bowman, Smyth and Meyerowitz described recessive Arabidopsis mutations affecting floral organ development. Their 1991 genetic analysis compared single and combined mutant phenotypes. Independent work in Antirrhinum provided parallel evidence. The influential synthesis by Coen and Meyerowitz in 1991 brought these genetic patterns together into what became known as the ABC model.

A useful starting observation is that loss of the B activity has two consequences rather than one: the petal position can develop a sepal-like organ, and the stamen position can develop a carpel-like organ. If B were simply a petal construction gene, the third-whorl effect would be unexplained. If B were simply a stamen construction gene, the second-whorl effect would be unexplained. A more economical explanation is that B is required in both regions but cooperates with different activities in each. In the second whorl it acts alongside the outer-domain programme; in the third whorl it acts alongside an inner-domain programme. Removal of B then reveals the different programmes that remain in the two positions.

This reasoning is based on the comparison of phenotypes rather than an assumption that transcription factors are already known. At the stage of genetic model building, an activity can represent one gene, several genes, or interacting proteins. Calling a gene a B-class gene is a statement about its role in the flower, not a statement that it belongs to a particular biochemical family by definition. Molecular identification is a separate result. The flower thereby offers an elegant example of how a genetic model can precede a biochemical explanation.

The classical framework gives A activity to the outer two whorls, B to the middle two and C to the inner two. A without B specifies a sepal, A with B a petal, B with C a stamen, and C without B a carpel. Classical A and C activities are treated as antagonistic, so removal of one may allow the other to act in an expanded domain. For a strong C-class loss mutant, the second-whorl petal programme can consequently appear in the third whorl, where B activity is retained and A expands. The fourth region no longer executes normal carpel development. This four-region prediction alone is insufficient to explain the continued initiation of organs in some C mutants; floral meristem termination must also be considered.

The use of double mutants tests whether these explanations are consistent when two regulatory functions are removed. For example, removing both B and C in the simplified model, while allowing A to expand, predicts sepal-like identities instead of petals and stamens. If the centre also lacks normal meristem termination, additional organs may form. The point of the double mutant is that it distinguishes the loss of B from the retention of C. A single B mutant develops a carpel-like organ in the third whorl because C remains. A B C double mutant lacks that remaining activity. Memorizing the single-mutant phenotype without considering what remains would lead to a wrong double-mutant prediction.

Real flowers need not behave as ideal Boolean diagrams. Individual genes may have partially overlapping functions, and a mutation may reduce rather than eliminate a protein's activity. Organ primordia can be absent or reduced in number, particularly in strong or complex mutant backgrounds. Some regions may form intermediate structures, with features of more than one identity. These observations do not invalidate the genetic logic. Instead they reveal developmental roles and dependencies that a minimal model intentionally leaves out. The 1991 Arabidopsis study explicitly described organ-number and organ-loss phenotypes in addition to transformations. Therefore an examination question that specifies a complete class loss and an idealized ABC model can have a simpler answer than a question that asks for the phenotype of a particular allele in an actual plant.

The general experimental strategy is to develop a model that explains the largest number of observations with a small set of causal assumptions, derive predictions, and then test those predictions with new genotypes or expression experiments. A model is stronger when it predicts what has not yet been observed. Simply assigning an activity retrospectively to every abnormal organ would be much less informative. The comparison with Antirrhinum helps because genes discovered in another species can reveal whether the same patterning logic is present beyond the original genetic system.

Evidence and further reading: Bowman et al. (1991), combined floral homeotic mutants.

4. Arabidopsis outer-whorl mutants: APETALA1 and APETALA2

The word APETALA in a gene name reflects a historically conspicuous mutant phenotype, but the name is not a precise description of its full developmental function. APETALA1, abbreviated AP1, has an important role in establishing floral meristem identity as well as in the normal development of the outer floral organs. A strong ap1 mutant can produce bract-like structures in place of ordinary sepals, lose or reduce petals, and initiate secondary flowers in the axils of abnormal outer organs. The appearance of secondary flowers is a valuable clue. An ordinary floral meristem is committed to making a flower; a structure that repeatedly initiates additional floral meristems instead retains or regains aspects of shoot-like developmental organization.

If all that a student knew about AP1 were that it belonged to a classical A-class list, the expected phenotype might be the ideal A-loss sequence of carpels and stamens in the two outer whorls. The actual ap1 phenotype is not adequately explained by that sequence. Removing one member of a functional class does not necessarily remove all class activity, and AP1 also participates in specifying the developmental context within which the whorl model operates. Thus AP1 illustrates two independent cautions: a single gene need not equal an entire functional class, and a gene that contributes to organ identity may also affect meristem identity. In experimental data these roles can be partially separated by comparing alleles, developmental stages and combined genotypes.

APETALA2, or AP2, is the other standard Arabidopsis A-function example. Unlike AP1, AP2 belongs to the AP2/ERF transcription factor family, not the MADS-box family. This molecular distinction is useful in an examination because most canonical Arabidopsis floral organ identity factors commonly listed together are MADS-domain proteins. AP2 is the major exception to that generalization. Its function also extends beyond a simple description of outer organ development. The proper restriction of reproductive C activity is influenced by AP2, and changes in AP2 function can allow inappropriate AGAMOUS expression in outer floral positions.

In an idealized strong loss of A activity with C expanded outward, the first whorl becomes carpel-like, the second stamen-like, the third remains stamen-like and the fourth remains carpel-like. This pattern follows directly from the remaining combinations: C, B plus C, B plus C, C. Strong ap2 alleles can display related outer-whorl homeosis, but exact numbers, organ absence and intermediate phenotypes vary. In a real ap2 mutant a position with no organ cannot literally be classified as a stamen just because a model would predict stamen identity if a primordium were present. The underlying distinction is between the failure to initiate or maintain an organ and a transformation of an organ that develops.

The spatial restriction of AG has been examined by locating its RNA in developing flowers. Early AG transcript is normally detected in the primordia of the third and fourth whorls, not throughout the young flower. Ectopic AG expression in an ap2 background supports the idea that AP2 contributes to establishing the outer boundary of C activity. The evidence should be interpreted at the developmental stage examined. The distribution of AG expression can change as tissues differentiate, so an RNA pattern from a later stage does not automatically describe the original identity-specification domain. The absence of an early transcript from a region is also not proof of permanent absence throughout that organ's life.

AP2 activity is regulated by microRNA172, a small RNA that can suppress the expression of AP2-like targets at a post-transcriptional level. Consequently, the distribution of AP2 RNA should not be treated as identical to the distribution of functional AP2 protein. This is one reason that the two-band A and C model is best understood as a diagram of functional activities rather than a literal picture of every transcript. A reporter that measures transcription may show a broader expression pattern than the active protein if post-transcriptional regulation differs spatially. Distinguishing RNA, protein abundance and functional activity is a useful connection to the earlier curriculum on gene expression.

Consider two abnormal flowers with reduced petals. In the first, the outer organs become bract-like and secondary floral structures appear; in the second, outer reproductive-like organs appear and the expression boundary of AG moves outward. These observations suggest different primary developmental defects even though both flowers have fewer normal petals. The first points toward the combined floral meristem and outer-organ functions of AP1, whereas the second is consistent with disruption of outer-domain regulation by AP2. Phenotype descriptions are therefore much more informative than the statement that a plant is apetalous. A student should name the affected region, the identity of the replacement organ and any change in meristem behaviour before selecting a candidate regulatory gene.

Evidence and further reading: Bowman et al. (1991), early and late AG expression.

5. Arabidopsis B-class mutants: APETALA3 and PISTILLATA

APETALA3 (AP3) and PISTILLATA (PI) are two well-established Arabidopsis B-class genes. Their encoded proteins contain a MADS DNA-binding domain. The normal combined activity of the two proteins is essential for the correct development of petals in the second whorl and stamens in the third whorl. In strong loss-of-function mutants of either gene, petals are replaced by sepal-like structures and stamens by carpel-like structures. The central carpel region is not converted into petals merely because the flower has lost its normal B activity. B function is not normally the principal identity activity of that central domain.

Why do mutations of two separate genes generate similar homeotic transformations? One plausible explanation is that the products of the two genes are needed together for a common regulatory function. If the active unit is a protein complex containing AP3 and PI, eliminating either subunit prevents the formation of that unit even when the other subunit remains present. The functional activity of the pair can then be represented as a logical conjunction: both partners must contribute. Such a pattern is distinct from complete functional redundancy, in which either of two genes acting alone can maintain a process and the phenotype appears only when both are lost.

The genetic evidence is compatible with the partnership hypothesis, but similar phenotypes alone cannot establish a physical interaction. Two enzymes that act in successive steps of a biochemical pathway may produce similar loss phenotypes without forming a stable complex. To show that two transcription factors physically cooperate, researchers need independent biochemical or molecular evidence, such as detection of protein interaction and sequence-specific binding by the complex. Studies of related B-class proteins in Antirrhinum supplied particularly clear evidence for a heterodimer with DNA-binding activity. Later work on Arabidopsis floral MADS proteins identified their associations in larger organ-identity complexes.

The consequence of losing B in the second whorl is different from that in the third because the remaining positional context is different. In the second region A-class activity remains, and with E-class support the programme favours sepal identity rather than petal identity. In the third region C activity remains, and with E-class support the programme favours carpel identity rather than stamen identity. The same molecular defect therefore leads to two anatomically distinct outcomes. This is a general property of combinatorial regulation: the function of a regulator depends on the other factors present in a cell population.

The timing of B activity matters as well. In classical temperature-sensitive allele experiments, a plant with a condition-dependent defective protein can be shifted between temperatures at different stages. If an early shift alters the final identity while a late shift has little influence on the gross identity, the result identifies a time window during which normal activity is essential for the measured outcome. However, an unaffected gross organ type after a late shift does not exclude subtle late roles in differentiation. The method detects the time sensitivity of a chosen phenotype and a particular mutant allele. It does not establish that every downstream target is switched simultaneously or that a normal protein has identical behaviour under every environmental condition.

Loss of B function also has reproductive consequences. Replacement of stamens by carpel-like structures eliminates or disrupts the tissues that normally support pollen formation. The absence of functional pollen is therefore expected as a downstream consequence, rather than evidence that AP3 or PI must act solely in the male gametophyte. A defect restricted to pollen differentiation could leave the anther and filament architecture largely intact, whereas an identity defect changes the organ in which pollen would otherwise develop. These alternative explanations can be distinguished by examining early floral anatomy, mature organ morphology and the later presence of gametophytic cells. This links organogenesis to reproduction without confusing the developmental identity of the sporophytic organ with the subsequent formation of the haploid gametophyte.

Evidence and further reading: Bowman et al. (1989), conditional allele and homeotic phenotypes.

6. AGAMOUS: reproductive fate and termination of floral growth

AGAMOUS (AG) is the canonical Arabidopsis C-function gene. In a normal flower its early activity is concentrated in the inner regions, where the combination with B contributes to stamen identity and the activity without B contributes to carpel identity. AG encodes a MADS-domain transcription factor, and its expression is regulated in space and time. A strong ag mutant lacks normal stamens and carpels. Stamens are replaced by petals, and the central region continues to generate floral organs in a reiterated pattern. This last phenotype demonstrates that AG does more than determine the type of tissue differentiated in a fixed number of pre-existing primordia.

A normal inflorescence meristem can continue to produce new flowers on its flanks. In contrast, a floral meristem is normally determinate: its central stem-cell system eventually ceases to maintain a self-renewing population. The central reproductive structure can then complete its development rather than being replaced by further rounds of floral primordia. The continued growth of the ag floral centre is a failure of this termination process. Meristem identity and meristem determinacy are related but not identical. A floral meristem can retain enough floral character to keep producing floral organs while failing to stop at the normal time.

The relationship between AG and WUSCHEL (WUS) helps explain the abnormal centre. WUS is a regulator of stem-cell maintenance in shoot and floral meristems. Early in floral development, stem-cell activity is necessary because sufficient cells must be generated to establish the floral organs. WUS contributes to activation of AG in the developing flower, together with other floral regulators. Later AG contributes to repression of WUS, thereby limiting continued stem-cell activity. A regulatory arrangement in which an early maintenance system helps initiate its own eventual shutdown allows the growing meristem to terminate after the appropriate developmental programme has been established.

One experimentally supported route involves KNUCKLES (KNU), a transcriptional repressor activated downstream of AG. The activation of KNU is temporally controlled; its later activity helps reduce WUS expression. Other AG-dependent repression routes also contribute, so the pathway AG to KNU to WUS is a useful core mechanism rather than an exhaustive network. A timed reduction of WUS is different from the complete absence of WUS at the beginning of flower formation. If stem-cell maintenance ended too soon, too few cells would remain to support normal organ initiation. If it persisted too long, the centre could continue producing tissue and organs. The required outcome is therefore temporal control rather than a permanent instruction to suppress all stem-cell activity.

Sun and colleagues reported experimental evidence linking KNU to floral determinacy in 2009. Prolonged WUS expression in knu flowers and the response to manipulated KNU expression helped place KNU downstream in the termination pathway. Particularly informative is the conceptual possibility of separating a determinacy defect from an organ-identity defect. Restoring termination in a C-class mutant does not by itself provide the missing combinations of factors needed to restore stamens and carpels. A rescued stem-cell phenotype can therefore coexist with persistent abnormalities in reproductive-organ identity. Conversely, restoration of one reproductive organ marker does not demonstrate that the entire centre has become determinate.

The mature ag flower is often drawn as a sequence of sepals, petals, more petals and a further internal flower. This is a schematic representation of reiteration, not a fixed numerical formula for the number of organs formed. Additional rounds vary with developmental conditions, allele and the amount of residual meristem activity. An examination answer should distinguish the third-whorl homeosis, which follows from the remaining B and expanded A activities, from the repeated central growth, which follows from loss of the C-dependent termination programme. Both derive from mutation of AG, but they arise through distinguishable biological consequences.

It is also important to recognize that a flower with extra petals does not necessarily carry an AG mutation. A meristem-size mutation may generate additional primordia without changing their identities; a mutation in growth regulation may make petal-like structures larger or more conspicuous; and mutations in other floral regulators may alter identity or termination. A compelling genetic diagnosis requires evidence from the inner organ identities, the centre's behaviour and, when available, expression or complementation results. Counting petals is a useful observation, not a sufficient molecular test.

Evidence and further reading: Sun et al. (2009), AG–KNU–WUS timing.

7. SEPALLATA and the need for combinatorial partners

A model based on A, B and C activities predicts how three spatial functions distinguish four organ identities. However, it does not follow that these three activities are biochemically sufficient to make the corresponding organs in any cell of a plant. A vegetative leaf can express an introduced floral regulator without necessarily becoming a completely differentiated floral organ. The competent developmental context matters. An important genetic discovery explained part of this dependence on context: the activities of the classical identity factors require additional partners encoded by SEPALLATA genes.

Pelaz and colleagues reported in 2000 that the combined loss of SEPALLATA1, SEPALLATA2 and SEPALLATA3 produced an unusual floral phenotype in Arabidopsis. Petals, stamens and carpels were replaced by sepal-like organs. The phenotype was not simply a duplicate of the single B-class or single C-class loss phenotype. Removing B alone would preserve carpel identity in the inner region, and removing C alone would preserve petal identity in the second whorl while producing abnormal inner organs. In the combined SEP mutant, the normal identities dependent on these classes were broadly disrupted. The observation suggested that the additional genes participate in the realization of both B and C functions rather than merely assigning a fifth independent region of the flower.

The importance of using a combined mutant becomes clear when related genes can substitute for one another. Suppose three genes produce proteins with partially overlapping activities. A mutation in one may leave enough activity for normal gross organ identity because the remaining genes still contribute. The absence of a dramatic phenotype in that single mutant is not proof that the gene has no developmental role. By combining mutations, the experimenter reduces the available pool of related activity and can reveal a function that was hidden by redundancy. A comparison between single, double and triple mutants is therefore a test of a model about the relationship among gene products.

SEPALLATA4 supplies additional overlapping function. The combined loss of the four SEP genes is associated with floral organs acquiring leaf-like characteristics rather than the simpler sepal-like identities of the triple mutant. This distinction is valuable because it prevents a misleading description of E as a factor needed for petals and reproductive organs only. The modern ABCE model represents E activity as a partner for floral organ identity more generally, including sepal identity. A triple mutant retains SEP4 activity; it is not equivalent to the idealized removal of the entire E class. A comparison of the two genotypes helps distinguish residual function from a qualitatively different rule.

With E included, the simplest functional combinations are A with E in the sepal region, A with B and E in the petal region, B with C and E in the stamen region, and C with E in the carpel region. This is not intended as a count of individual protein molecules. A single class symbol may represent multiple proteins, and a molecular complex may contain two molecules belonging to a class or draw upon overlapping family members. The class-level representation helps predict organ identities, while a physical model of protein interactions explains how those identities are implemented.

In a B-class mutant with functional E factors, the remaining complexes in the two affected whorls can still support alternative floral identities. In a severe E-class mutant, several of these alternative floral complexes may themselves be impaired. This explains why removing E is not equivalent to removing B and C while leaving all other functions intact. The distinction is especially useful in multiple-statement examinations where the same symbols are used both to describe genetic domains and to represent proposed molecular components.

The D function is conventionally associated with ovule identity and involves AGAMOUS-related MADS-domain factors, including SEEDSTICK in Arabidopsis. It is a useful extension of the floral identity framework, but an ovule is not a fifth concentric whorl equivalent to the four main floral organ regions. Ovules arise within the carpel context. The specification of their identity must also be separated from subsequent development of the female gametophyte inside the ovule. The former is an organ and tissue patterning problem; the latter is treated in the gametogenesis chapter. A D-related phenotype therefore cannot be interpreted solely by assigning an extra symbol to a hypothetical fifth outer or inner ring.

The revised model illustrates a characteristic feature of developmental genetics. A remarkably useful initial theory can survive the discovery that its implementation is more complex than originally imagined. The A, B and C combinations remain a powerful method for interpreting the characteristic homeotic patterns. The discovery of E factors clarifies why those combinations require additional floral context and why an experiment that eliminates a group of partners can disrupt several identities at once. Rather than memorize the old and new models as unrelated lists, understand E as an improvement motivated by an experimental result that the simpler representation did not fully explain.

Evidence and further reading: Ditta et al. (2004), SEP4 and combined E-class mutants.

8. Molecular mechanism: shared DNA recognition and selective complexes

The proteins encoded by AP3, PI, AG, AP1, SEP and many related floral genes are members of a transcription factor family characterized by a conserved MADS domain. A transcription factor alters gene expression through sequence-specific interactions with DNA and through interactions with other regulatory proteins. The conserved domain helps explain why proteins involved in different floral identities have related biochemical properties. Yet sequence similarity alone cannot specify which organ will develop, because the biological effect depends on where and when a protein is expressed, which partners are present and which genes its complexes regulate.

The MADS domain participates in DNA recognition. Many plant developmental MADS proteins also contain regions that influence partner selection, complex formation and transcriptional regulation. At an examination level, the main structural principle is that a protein can carry both a DNA-recognition surface and an interaction surface. Mutations affecting these two functions need not have the same molecular consequences. A mutation may preserve interaction with a partner but reduce DNA binding, or it may preserve the DNA-binding region while destabilizing the interaction necessary for a functional complex. Either could reduce developmental activity without preventing accumulation of the corresponding transcript.

A frequently discussed recognition element for floral MADS-domain proteins is the CArG box. It is a short DNA sequence with a characteristic central region rich in adenine and thymine, often represented as CC(A/T)6GG. Real binding preferences can vary, and a matching sequence in a genome does not guarantee that a particular complex binds that location in a living cell. Accessibility of DNA, the composition of the protein complex and the regulatory environment all influence occupancy. In addition, binding is not automatically equivalent to activation; a complex may stimulate or repress expression depending on the target and associated factors.

The interaction between AP3 and PI supplies one level of combinatorial specificity. A DNA-binding complex containing both factors can function in the two central whorls, but the different partners in those regions help determine which set of developmental target genes is regulated. In the petal region, association with AP1 and SEP-like factors supports a petal programme. In the stamen region, association with AG and SEP-like factors supports a stamen programme. A change in one partner can therefore alter the output of a complex even if its other components remain available. This molecular logic is consistent with the genetic conclusion that B is necessary for two different identities.

Honma and Goto reported in 2001 that combinations of floral MADS-domain factors could induce floral-organ characteristics in leaves and supplied interaction evidence involving SEP3. In the developmental interpretation, combinations of regulators have a greater ability to redirect tissue identity than a single identity factor acting without its expected partners. Such experiments address sufficiency, whereas a loss-of-function phenotype primarily addresses necessity. The two kinds of evidence complement one another. Removing a required component shows that the normal programme depends on it; introducing an appropriate combination tests whether the programme can be initiated in an abnormal context.

The floral quartet concept describes a higher-order arrangement of interacting MADS-domain proteins that bind two DNA recognition sites and may bring intervening DNA into a loop. This provides a conceptual bridge between the class-level combinations and the regulation of target genes. The key point is not that every target is controlled by one rigid four-protein assembly with identical geometry. Rather, proteins can cooperate in larger complexes, and their joint interaction with DNA can create a regulatory response that differs from the activity of an isolated factor. Structural and biochemical details vary among complexes and targets.

The interaction has a physical basis. Formation of a protein complex is favoured when the free energy of the associated state is sufficiently low relative to the separated components. The availability of the partners also matters: even a favourable interaction cannot create a large number of complexes if one partner is absent. Sequence-specific DNA binding similarly depends on protein availability and affinity for the relevant site. Noncovalent interactions, such as electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, help stabilize protein–DNA recognition. The process is not a separate enzyme reaction consuming a fixed ATP molecule for each floral organ specified. Energy is used by cellular processes that synthesize and maintain regulatory molecules and establish growth, but a transcriptional identity decision is not appropriately described by an invented energy budget for petal versus stamen formation.

This molecular perspective explains how two spatially similar expression patterns can generate different developmental outcomes. A transcript may be present in both whorls while its encoded protein forms different complexes in each. A constitutively expressed B factor may fail to confer a B-dependent identity outside the region containing a compatible partner. A mutation that disrupts the partner-binding surface may cause a homeotic phenotype despite near-normal transcription of the corresponding gene. In each case the step that fails lies between expression and functional transcriptional regulation, a distinction often hidden by the casual statement that a gene is either switched on or switched off.

Downstream targets connect the identity system with visible organogenesis. Once a regulatory combination alters gene expression, changes in cell proliferation, polarity, cell-wall expansion and cell-type differentiation can gradually produce the anatomical characteristics of a particular organ. There need not be a single downstream target whose activation is sufficient to construct an entire stamen. Instead a regulatory factor coordinates multiple developmental processes, some direct and some mediated by additional regulators. An altered expression pattern in mature tissue can consequently be a downstream effect of the homeotic conversion rather than the initial cause. To distinguish direct from indirect regulation, the researcher must examine the temporal order and obtain independent evidence for the regulatory relationship.

Evidence and further reading: Honma and Goto (2001), combinatorial MADS complexes.

9. Antirrhinum: DEFICIENS and GLOBOSA as a conserved B function

In snapdragon, the classical genes DEFICIENS (DEF) and GLOBOSA (GLO) provide an independent genetic system for studying the B function. Strong mutations in either disrupt normal petal and stamen development. The outer perianth identity becomes evident in the second region, and female reproductive characteristics occur in organs of the third region. The exact mature shape can be affected by the characteristic fused corolla and bilateral architecture of Antirrhinum, so the standard identity transformation should not be confused with the four-petalled morphology of Arabidopsis. The informative comparison is the positional change from petal toward sepal and from stamen toward carpel.

In 1990 the identification of DEF as a member of the MADS-box family supplied a molecular clue to how a floral homeotic gene could act. Subsequent studies of DEF and GLO in 1992 investigated their expression and DNA-binding properties. Evidence from protein binding experiments showed that the two proteins could form a heterodimer that recognized specific DNA motifs. A heterodimer is a complex of two different molecular subunits; it differs from a homodimer, which consists of two copies of the same protein. Because the two B-class proteins cooperate in the functional complex, a mutation of either gene can produce a broadly similar pattern of identity disruption.

The biochemical result is important because it tests an inference suggested by the classical genetics. Similar mutant phenotypes had already indicated that DEF and GLO participate in a common developmental function. Physical interaction and sequence-specific DNA binding provide a more direct explanation of this shared requirement. However, the presence of the heterodimer in an assay does not demonstrate that it regulates every gene affected in the mutant flower. A specific target must be supported by evidence linking the complex to the relevant DNA regulatory region and showing a corresponding change in expression or function.

DEF and GLO also participate in maintenance of their expression patterns through cross-regulatory interactions. In the second and third whorls, the two proteins can bind regulatory regions associated with their own genes, providing a molecular basis for a feedback mechanism. When an initiating signal establishes expression in the appropriate region, the production of both partners can help maintain the functional state. If either partner is lost, the absence of the heterodimer can compromise this reinforcement. The resulting decline in expression may amplify the initial functional defect. This is different from a model in which one gene is always wholly upstream of the other in a one-directional pathway.

The distinction between initiation and maintenance is essential for interpreting expression experiments. A mutant could establish an initial expression domain but fail to sustain it as the organ grows. A late observation of absent expression would then be misleading if interpreted as proof that the domain was never initiated. Time-resolved measurements of RNA or protein allow these alternatives to be distinguished. Conversely, the mere presence of early RNA does not demonstrate that a functional complex forms. For a mutation that preserves transcription but alters protein interaction, the early expression signal could look normal while the downstream organ programme fails.

Classical conditional DEF alleles also allow the relationship between developmental stage and function to be investigated. A time-dependent change in activity can affect later petal growth and differentiation as well as early identity. Because snapdragon petals form a complex corolla, a change in cell expansion or epidermal differentiation can have an especially conspicuous effect on mature appearance. It is important to distinguish these later manifestations from the early decision about whether the primordium follows a petal programme at all. A transformed organ can retain some positional features of the original whorl while expressing the tissue characteristics of an alternative identity.

The correspondence between DEF and AP3, and between GLO and PI, is supported by both related sequences and functional evidence. A student should be able to predict that removal of the homologous B partner in either model system will preferentially alter the middle two organ regions. Nevertheless, a direct comparison must account for possible changes in other whorls and organ numbers. Some Antirrhinum B-mutant studies report effects on initiation of central carpel development as well. These observations show that a failure in the middle regions can influence later organ initiation in another region, even though the minimal identity model places the principal B function in whorls two and three. The direct functional domain of a regulator and the complete anatomical consequences of its mutation are not necessarily identical.

The conserved feature is therefore an interaction-based B-class mechanism, not a promise that every detail of the mutant flowers will match. This is precisely why comparative development is valuable: similar results in two distinct morphological backgrounds strengthen the general explanation, while different results reveal additional features of the developmental context. The appropriate examination response first states the conserved transformation, then qualifies the prediction if the question provides a real allele, a stage-specific experiment or a species-specific effect.

Evidence and further reading: Tröbner et al. (1992), DEF–GLO interaction and DNA binding.

10. PLENA and FARINELLI: separating reproductive identity and fertility

PLENA (PLE) is the principal classical C-function gene in Antirrhinum. Loss of its function leads to a broad disruption of reproductive-organ identity, with inner organs acquiring perianth-like characteristics and the floral centre losing normal determinacy. In the simplest comparison, this corresponds functionally to the loss of AGAMOUS activity in Arabidopsis. In both cases a regulator required for inner organ development is also involved in the termination of the floral meristem. A flower with repeated inner organs thus supports the same broad inference about the relationship between reproductive identity and meristem growth.

The snapdragon system also contains a related C-lineage gene called FARINELLI (FAR). Davies and colleagues described the comparison of ple and far mutants in 1999. Despite similarities in their encoded MADS-domain factors, the single mutant phenotypes were very different. Whereas ple mutants showed broad reproductive-to-perianth homeosis and a failure of normal termination, far mutants could retain recognizable floral organ identity but display partial male sterility. In the latter situation the existence of an anatomically identifiable stamen does not guarantee normal reproductive performance. A developmental regulator can contribute to the differentiation or function of reproductive tissue without being the sole determinant of gross organ identity.

One possible explanation for the distinct single mutant phenotypes is unequal redundancy. PLE makes a major contribution to C-class identity and termination, while FAR contributes overlapping or additional functions whose loss is more limited in the presence of intact PLE. If both genes contributed identically and could fully compensate for one another, neither single mutant should have a major identity phenotype under ordinary conditions. The dramatic phenotype of ple therefore rules out a simple model of perfect interchangeability. It does not rule out partial overlap in some processes, and combined mutant or expression analyses can reveal interactions not apparent in either single mutant.

The study of PLE and FAR also revealed that the regulation of B-domain boundaries differs from the simple Arabidopsis comparison. When expression of B-class genes is examined in mutants lacking C-lineage functions, the outcome depends on interactions among the regulatory genes, not simply the identity of the final organ. It is safer to distinguish the broad class-level prediction from the observed distribution of a specific transcript. A gene may influence the boundary of another activity through a direct regulatory interaction or through an indirect consequence of changing tissue identity. The classical rule that A and C oppose one another does not supply a complete map of every molecular interaction in both species.

Fertility offers a useful independent measurement. A flower can retain the correct number and approximate shape of stamens but produce a reduced proportion of viable pollen grains. Another flower may lack stamens because those primordia become petal-like organs. Both plants can exhibit reduced seed set, but the causal sequence differs. In the first, the defect may concern tissue differentiation or gamete production within an identifiable reproductive organ. In the second, the required organ context is missing. Counting seeds alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms, because successful seed formation also requires appropriate female development, pollination, fertilization and later seed maturation.

The distinction also matters for genetic maintenance of mutant lines. A complete loss of reproductive organ identity can prevent ordinary self-fertilization, so homozygous plants may not be able to produce the progeny needed for further genetic analysis. Heterozygous carriers with normal fertility may allow the mutation to be maintained. A partially male-sterile mutant may still function as a female parent if its female reproductive organs are competent, but this possibility must be tested rather than assumed from its name. Reciprocal cross data help identify whether a fertility reduction is associated predominantly with the male or female contribution.

Taken together, the PLE and FAR examples show why a gene name should not be equated mechanically with an ABC function. A functional class is a description of a biological contribution; related proteins can differ in the amount and timing of that contribution. PLE and FAR provide a particularly useful case in which a conserved regulatory family supports related developmental processes but the responsibilities are distributed unequally. The next question is whether a similar function in two species always belongs to the genes most closely related by descent.

Evidence and further reading: Davies et al. (1999), PLENA and FARINELLI.

11. Evolution of gene families: function is not the same as ancestry

When two species diverge from a common ancestor, each descendant lineage inherits many of the genes of that ancestor. A pair of genes whose separation reflects a speciation event are termed orthologues. A pair whose separation reflects a gene duplication event are termed paralogues. These terms describe evolutionary relationships, not the degree of resemblance between two mutant phenotypes. Orthologues can evolve different roles, and paralogues can retain overlapping or similar roles. The distinction becomes particularly important when a regulatory gene family has undergone duplication before two species diverged.

The main C function in Arabidopsis is carried by AG, whereas in Antirrhinum it is carried predominantly by PLE. A comparison based only on mutant phenotype would tempt one to call AG and PLE orthologues. Evolutionary analyses indicate a more interesting situation. An ancestral duplication of a C-lineage gene produced two lineages before the divergence of the relevant core eudicot species. Arabidopsis AG and Antirrhinum FAR belong to one of these lineages, often termed the euAG lineage. Antirrhinum PLE and the Arabidopsis SHATTERPROOF genes belong to the other lineage. The predominant C function is thus carried by different descendants of the ancestral duplication in the two plants.

This relationship has a simple consequence for how a comparative table should be read. The entry AG versus PLE is an appropriate functional comparison, because strong loss of each produces broad reproductive homeosis and altered floral determinacy. It is not an appropriate assertion of strict orthology. The entry AG versus FAR describes a closer correspondence of evolutionary lineage, but the far single mutant does not reproduce the severe ag phenotype. These two comparisons answer different questions and can both be correct. An examination item that uses the words orthologue and functional counterpart interchangeably is therefore biologically imprecise.

Causier and colleagues in 2005 used comparative genomic evidence, including the arrangement of genes in corresponding genomic regions, to support the evolutionary relationship. Conserved arrangement, known as synteny, adds evidence beyond an isolated sequence comparison. When surrounding genes are conserved in corresponding regions, their arrangement can help distinguish genes inherited from the same ancestral locus from related genes that originated through duplication. Sequence-based evolutionary trees and genomic context together help reconstruct the history of a family. The phenotype then reveals how developmental functions have been distributed among the descendants.

Following duplication, both copies may initially possess similar capacities. Later mutations in regulatory sequences can change where a gene is expressed, when its expression begins or how much protein accumulates. Changes in the encoded protein can alter DNA recognition, interaction with partners or transcriptional regulation. Some ancestral functions may be retained by both copies, some predominantly by one, and some may be modified or lost. The term subfunctionalization describes the partitioning of functions that were originally combined in one ancestral gene. It is a useful conceptual model here, provided that it is not used to claim an exact ancestral expression pattern that has not been established.

The family history illustrates how a developmental output can remain relatively conserved while its molecular implementation changes. Both model plants still require coordinated specification of male and female reproductive organs and eventual termination of floral growth. Natural selection can maintain these essential outcomes even when the relative contributions of duplicated regulators diverge. This does not imply that evolution intentionally selects a particular gene name or always preserves an identical regulatory network. It means that heritable changes compatible with functional reproduction can accumulate in different lineages.

A cross-species rescue experiment can test whether an introduced factor can perform a function in another species. For instance, if a related protein from one plant restores an organ identity in a mutant of the other plant when expressed in the appropriate domain, the outcome supports some conservation of biochemical function. However, restoration under an artificial promoter does not show that the gene normally has the same expression domain in its own species. Nor does functional rescue establish orthology; a paralogous protein can supply similar activity when placed in the appropriate context. An unsuccessful rescue is equally ambiguous if the foreign protein is not expressed at the right concentration, fails to interact with the host partners or is present at the wrong developmental stage.

The comparison of AP1 and Antirrhinum SQUAMOSA provides another caution. These related genes contribute to floral meristem identity in their respective plants, but the outer-organ functions attributed to AP1 in Arabidopsis cannot simply be transferred without qualification to SQUAMOSA in Antirrhinum. Classical A function is consequently less straightforward to generalize across the two systems than the B-class correspondence. A well-supported comparative explanation identifies the particular function that is conserved, the specific function that differs and the type of evidence used to establish each claim. Generalizing from two eudicot models to all angiosperms requires additional comparative evidence.

12. How genetic screens identify developmental regulators

In a forward genetic screen, the researcher begins with an observable phenotype and works toward identification of the gene responsible. Floral development is particularly well suited to this approach because each flower is a repeated developmental structure and a characteristic homeotic transformation can often be recognized by inspection. A heritable mutation is detected when genetically related plants repeatedly produce an abnormal pattern under conditions in which wild-type plants produce the expected organs. The investigator must separate mutations affecting reproductive timing or plant health from mutations specifically affecting the identity of a floral organ. A plant that never makes flowers cannot be classified as having a petal-to-sepal transformation.

A recessive mutation may be present in an apparently normal heterozygous plant. Let a functional allele be denoted B and a defective allele b at one required B-class locus. If one functional copy provides sufficient activity for normal morphology, B/b individuals appear normal at the level being scored. Self-fertilization of such plants can generate B/B, B/b and b/b offspring in the familiar Mendelian proportions. The homozygous recessive individuals can reveal the phenotype even when they are themselves infertile. This explains why a developmental mutation need not be transmitted through a reproductively defective homozygote for its inheritance to be studied.

Under the assumptions of equal segregation, random union of gametes, equal viability and complete penetrance, each gamete of a heterozygote has probability one half of carrying b. The probability that both gametes carry b is one half multiplied by one half, or one quarter. This is the expected fraction of b/b progeny from selfing; the three remaining quarters are expected to carry at least one B allele. The expected numbers can be calculated by multiplying the total progeny count by the respective probabilities. An observed proportion is not expected to equal the theoretical ratio exactly in a finite sample. The important question is whether the deviation is compatible with sampling variation or suggests a failure of one or more assumptions.

A deviation from the predicted ratio can arise without changing the basic developmental mechanism. If b/b seedlings have reduced survival, fewer homozygotes will reach the stage at which floral phenotypes can be scored. If the mutant allele alters transmission through one type of gamete, the frequency among progeny will differ from the simple expectation. If its visible effect is incompletely penetrant, some genetically mutant individuals will appear normal by the chosen criterion. Penetrance is the fraction of individuals of a genotype that display a defined phenotype; expressivity describes the degree or form of the phenotype among affected individuals. A gene can therefore show variable expressivity even when nearly every individual with the genotype has some recognizable abnormality.

In a complementation test, two recessive mutations producing similar phenotypes are brought together in one diploid individual. Suppose one mutant lacks AP3 function and another lacks PI function. If the strains have otherwise functional copies of the other locus, their progeny can inherit a functional AP3 allele from the PI-mutant line and a functional PI allele from the AP3-mutant line. The resulting individual can form both proteins and restore B activity. Complementation would support the interpretation that the original mutations affect different genes, even though the two original phenotypes are similar. The cross must be possible; sterile homozygotes may require a suitable maintenance strategy using carriers, and genotype verification is essential.

If two recessive mutations affect the same gene, their combined progeny may lack a functional allele at that locus and continue to display the abnormal phenotype. This is the ordinary explanation of failure to complement. However, noncomplementation is not an absolute proof of allelism in every biological system. Dosage-sensitive interactions between different loci, dominant-negative effects or unusual intragenic complementation can complicate interpretation. The introductory model assumes recessive loss-of-function alleles and sufficient activity from one wild-type copy. A strong conclusion is supported by molecular evidence locating the mutations and by the restoration of function when an appropriate normal gene is provided.

The distinction between mutation in a gene and mutation in its regulatory DNA is also important. A coding-region mutation may produce a protein that cannot bind DNA, cannot associate with its partner or is unstable. A regulatory mutation may leave the protein's sequence entirely normal but alter its expression domain. Both can cause abnormal organ identity, yet their molecular signatures differ. A coding mutation might be rescued by expressing the normal coding sequence under the gene's normal regulatory elements. A mutation that changes spatial control may instead require restoration of the relevant regulatory sequence. Introducing a normal protein under a broadly active promoter is not equivalent to restoring its usual developmental pattern.

Following the identification of a candidate gene, a geneticist seeks independent evidence that the candidate is responsible rather than merely linked to the mutation. Several independently recovered alleles with different molecular lesions and similar effects strengthen the connection. The cosegregation of a sequence change with the mutant phenotype in a cross is informative, but a nearby variant can cosegregate because of linkage. Rescue with a functional copy, or a separately produced allele that recreates the phenotype, provides a stronger test. These principles explain how the historically observed petal and stamen defects were eventually connected to specific molecular regulators.

The genetic approach also makes it possible to separate the direct effect of an identity regulator from secondary defects. An organ may become abnormal because the primordium received the wrong combination of regulators, or because a neighbouring organ failed to develop and altered the local environment. If a mutation changes two different tissue types, the phenotype alone cannot show whether the gene acts independently in both cell populations or whether the second effect follows indirectly from the first. Spatially restricted rescue or temporally controlled restoration can help distinguish these possibilities. The interpretation must always be tied to the actual readout rather than a general assumption that all visible abnormalities are direct targets of the mutated gene.

13. Predicting combined mutants and interpreting epistasis

The clearest way to solve a class-level problem is to reconstruct the functional combination in each region rather than attempt to remember a separate pattern for every genotype. Begin by writing the four normal identities from outside to centre. Assign A activity to regions one and two, B to two and three, C to three and four, and E support to all four. Remove the activity specified in the question. Next apply any explicitly stated boundary rule, such as the expansion of A when C is absent in the classical model. Only then translate the remaining combinations into predicted identities. This sequence prevents the common error of removing an organ's usual identity without asking which alternative activity remains.

For a complete B loss, the first and fourth regions retain their usual core combinations. The second region retains A and E but loses the partner that distinguishes a petal from a sepal. The third region retains C and E but loses the partner that distinguishes a stamen from a carpel. The result is a sepal, sepal, carpel, carpel identity pattern under the idealized assumptions. The third and fourth regions are not necessarily represented by two normal fused gynoecia in a real flower; the prediction concerns identity rather than the precise morphology, organ number or fusion behaviour.

For a complete C loss, the outer domains are not the primary site of C activity, so their normal core identity is retained. In the inner domain, A expands under the classical antagonism rule. The third region still contains B and now contains expanded A, so its predicted identity is petal rather than stamen. The central region has no normal C activity to support carpel identity or normal termination. If represented only by its remaining A and E combination, it can be described as sepal-like, but the real ag phenotype includes repeated inner floral structures. A correct answer therefore depends on whether the question asks for the simplified local identity rule or for the actual strong mutant's central architecture.

Loss of both B and C illustrates the benefit of using the same reasoning method. B-dependent identity is lost in the two middle regions. The absence of C permits expansion of A into the inner regions in the simplified scheme, and E remains available. Hence the remaining identity combination is compatible with sepal-like organs throughout the flower. The loss of the termination function of C may allow further organs to arise. This pattern is superficially similar to the sep1 sep2 sep3 phenotype, but the mechanisms differ. In one case both B and C activities have been removed; in the other, important partners required for these activities have been removed. Similar gross outcomes can result from failures at different steps of a regulatory system.

The classical complete A loss predicts an expansion of C into the outer regions. The first region then expresses C with E rather than A with E and becomes carpel-like. The second region expresses B with C and E and becomes stamen-like. The two inner regions retain their normal class combinations. If both A and B are lost while C expands, all four regions are predicted to have C and E and therefore carpel-like identity. These statements describe the logic of the original functional model; a real ap1 or ap2 allele should not be substituted for complete A loss without a qualification.

When a question specifies removal of both A and C, the normal antagonistic boundary cannot be resolved merely by saying each expands into the other's region: neither function is available to expand. The remaining B activity in regions two and three is not one of the normal complete identity combinations, and the outer and central regions lack their normal defining factors. The simple model cannot determine a fully normal organ identity for those incomplete combinations. In an examination it is reasonable to expect abnormal or leaf-like structures in a severe loss of floral identity, but the precise morphology requires empirical evidence or further rules supplied by the question. This is an example of underdetermination rather than a defect in reasoning.

Epistasis describes a situation in which the phenotypic effect of an allele at one locus depends on the genotype at another locus. For example, if a B-class protein requires an E-class partner to generate a particular floral identity, removal of E may prevent that identity even when the B-class genes remain transcriptionally active. An introduced B factor might then fail to restore its usual phenotype in an E-deficient background. This is consistent with a partner-dependent function. It does not necessarily establish a linear hierarchy in which the E gene is transcribed strictly downstream of B. A protein interaction system can generate epistatic patterns without a simple one-directional order of gene activation.

The difference between epistasis and redundancy is worth preserving. Two B factors may both be essential partners in the same active complex: removing either eliminates the required activity. In contrast, two E-related genes may partially substitute for one another: removing one leaves enough combined activity, and removing several reveals a phenotype. Both situations involve genetic interactions, but the single and combined mutant results have different logic. A student who treats all genes in the same class as interchangeable would incorrectly predict that PI can simply replace AP3 in the canonical B pair. A student who treats every gene as an essential partner would incorrectly predict that every single SEP mutation must eliminate floral identity.

Consider an idealized double heterozygote carrying recessive mutations at two independently assorting loci that are both required for B activity. If each functional allele is haplosufficient, normal B activity is expected only in individuals carrying at least one functional allele at each locus. The probability of a functional genotype at one locus is three quarters. Independent assortment makes the probability of retaining function at both loci nine sixteenths. The remaining seven sixteenths lose at least one necessary factor and are predicted to show some B-class mutant phenotype. This numerical pattern differs from the expectation for two perfectly redundant genes, where only the double homozygous recessive genotype would lose all shared function, with probability one sixteenth under the same assumptions.

These simple calculations are useful because they connect the molecular model with observable progeny frequencies. They do not require the assumption that any actual AP3 and PI cross must produce exactly this proportion. Linkage, altered gamete transmission, viability effects, incomplete penetrance and difficulties maintaining sterile genotypes can change the observed results. Moreover, a gross phenotype may not distinguish a single homozygous mutant from a double mutant when both eliminate the same functional complex. Genotyping or appropriately designed crosses are then required to resolve the different classes.

14. Mapping expression domains and testing molecular activity

A normal floral apex is small, and early primordia are not yet identifiable by all the features of a mature organ. An experiment that locates the expression of a candidate regulator during these early stages can test whether its spatial distribution is consistent with its proposed function. RNA in situ hybridization is a method that uses a complementary probe to detect a selected transcript within a preserved tissue section or specimen. The resulting signal provides information about where RNA is present at the stage sampled. For an inner-domain regulator such as AG, an early signal in the third and fourth regions is consistent with its proposed role in reproductive identity.

A signal restricted to the predicted region is supportive evidence, not proof that the transcript causes the eventual organ identity. The transcript might be induced as a consequence of an earlier developmental decision. To strengthen the causal interpretation, expression should be detected before the relevant differentiation becomes visible, and functional manipulation should produce a corresponding change in outcome. The strongest argument combines the spatial relationship, temporal relationship and genetic requirement. A region-specific expression pattern without a relevant phenotype could indicate a marker of identity rather than a regulator of identity.

Specificity controls are essential. An appropriate negative probe control helps determine whether the observed signal depends on sequence complementarity rather than nonspecific staining. A reference transcript known to be detectable in the tissue helps assess whether an apparent absence of signal reflects a technical failure. Equivalent tissue preservation and imaging conditions allow comparisons across genotypes. Even when a signal is convincingly absent, the method's detection threshold must be considered. A low level of transcript might remain below the sensitivity of the assay. Thus absence of detectable RNA is not necessarily proof of absolute absence of all functional protein.

A promoter reporter offers a complementary method. A regulatory region of the candidate gene is used to drive a detectable reporter, such as a fluorescent protein. The resulting pattern indicates activity of the regulatory sequences included in the construct. The result may differ from the endogenous gene if the construct lacks important distant or intronic regulatory sequences. The reporter protein may also persist after transcription decreases, obscuring the time at which expression originally stopped. A carefully interpreted reporter can reveal spatial changes in a developmental mutant, but it should not be described as a perfect measurement of the endogenous protein's active concentration.

Protein detection adds another layer. An antibody specific to a regulatory protein or a functional tagged protein can help establish its localization. The tagged protein should ideally retain biological function, because a large tag could alter stability, localization or interaction with other proteins. For AP2, the distinction between RNA and protein is especially relevant because post-transcriptional regulation can change protein abundance without producing an equivalent change in RNA distribution. An experiment comparing transcript, protein and phenotype can therefore locate the level at which regulation changes. A reporter for transcription alone would not settle the question.

To test whether two factors form a DNA-binding complex, researchers can use a biochemical assay with a defined DNA fragment containing a recognition sequence. A DNA fragment travels through a gel at a rate affected by its size and association with proteins. When a protein complex binds the fragment, the mobility of the DNA can change. A distinct slower-migrating band in the presence of two factors is consistent with complex formation on DNA. This is the principle of an electrophoretic mobility shift assay. If the same DNA fragment produces no corresponding band with either factor alone, the result supports cooperative or partner-dependent binding under the assay conditions.

Additional controls refine the interpretation. A mutated recognition sequence can test whether the shift depends on the specific DNA motif. Competition by an excess of unlabeled normal DNA can test whether binding is sequence-selective. Protein preparations and concentrations should be comparable, because a missing band could reflect inadequate protein rather than an intrinsic failure of binding. The assay shows the capacity of proteins to bind a DNA sequence under controlled conditions, not necessarily that they occupy the same region in every developing organ. Evidence of interaction in the living plant and changes in target gene expression can establish the biological relevance more directly.

A mutation that leaves RNA expression intact but removes a DNA-binding shift is particularly informative. It suggests that the defect is not simply the failure to transcribe the gene. If the mutant protein remains stable and continues to interact with its usual partner, the binding defect can be attributed more specifically to DNA recognition or the geometry required for recognition. If it fails to interact with the partner, the absence of binding may instead be secondary to failure of complex formation. These explanations can be tested with separate interaction and binding readouts. The phenotype by itself cannot discriminate between them.

Measurements of target gene expression can reveal the consequences of losing a regulator. Imagine that a normal petal expresses a gene involved in a characteristic epidermal differentiation process, but a def mutant does not. This could mean the DEF-containing complex normally promotes expression of that gene. It could also mean the mutant organ has become sepal-like and no longer contains the cell types that usually express the gene. Comparing whole mature mutant organs with mature wild-type petals cannot automatically separate direct regulation from the secondary consequences of altered identity. Early samples, when the cell populations remain comparable, are especially valuable for this reason.

A combination of assays can provide a much stronger chain of evidence. First show that the protein is expressed in the appropriate primordium. Next establish that it is capable of binding a relevant regulatory sequence together with a partner. Then observe whether target expression changes soon after the regulator is perturbed, before gross changes in organ composition occur. Finally determine whether restoration of the regulator or the target restores the relevant developmental feature. Each step tests a different link. A single assay rarely establishes the entire developmental chain from gene expression to mature morphology.

15. Necessity, sufficiency and developmental competence

A loss-of-function mutant asks whether the normal activity is required under the conditions tested. When strong mutations of AP3 or PI produce consistent identity changes in the two middle whorls, the normal activities are necessary for the usual petal and stamen identities in that genetic context. Necessity does not mean that either factor is sufficient to direct these identities independently. A flower could require both factors as well as additional partners, and neither protein alone would necessarily establish the entire programme. This distinction is a central principle in interpreting developmental genetics.

A rescue experiment introduces functional activity into a mutant background and examines whether the defect is corrected. The most informative comparison includes a normal reference, a mutant without the introduced activity, and a mutant with the functional sequence expressed in an appropriate spatial and temporal pattern. An additional control expressing a defective version of the protein can help determine whether the observed improvement depends on the proposed activity rather than on introduction of DNA itself. If normal organ identities are restored specifically in the rescued line, the result supports a causal role for the missing gene product.

Restoration must be evaluated with appropriate criteria. A third-whorl structure might regain a stamen-like filament but fail to produce a normal anther. Such a rescue is partial. A flower may recover the normal identity of the second whorl while the third whorl remains abnormal. This spatial difference could reflect insufficient expression in one region or unequal sensitivity of the two programmes to the amount of active protein. It should not be reported as complete restoration of B function without qualification. The researcher can use identity markers, histology, organ morphology and fertility to describe distinct aspects of the outcome.

An ectopic expression experiment places a gene product in a developmental context where it is not normally active. For example, a B-class combination can be introduced into a region that normally has A and E activity but lacks B. Under the simplified model, the addition of functional B to such a region favours petal identity rather than sepal identity. If the experiment produces petal-like outer organs, it supports the idea that the combination can redirect identity in that region. However, expression of only one B partner may fail when the other is absent. A negative result for an isolated factor is therefore not decisive evidence that the B class is irrelevant to petal specification.

Ectopic C activity provides another conceptual test. If C activity is introduced into an outer region where A would normally be active, the result depends on whether C suppresses A, whether the introduced factor participates in functional complexes and whether the cells remain developmentally competent. Under the classical replacement assumption, C in the first region favours carpel identity, and B with C in the second favours stamen identity. If the experimental question explicitly specifies sustained A and C coactivity, the simple classical antagonism rule has been overridden and cannot be used to predict a unique normal identity. The exact result must then be interpreted from the actual data.

The role of developmental competence explains why the same ectopic construct can have different effects in a leaf and in a floral primordium. A primordium already operating within the floral programme can possess regulatory partners and a chromatin environment suitable for interpreting an added factor. A vegetative leaf may lack these features. The observation that selected combinations of floral MADS proteins can redirect leaf development toward floral organ characteristics demonstrates that the regulatory network can overcome some contextual restrictions. It does not show that a complete normal flower, with all spatially organized whorls and proper reproductive performance, can be produced simply by expressing one gene in every leaf cell.

The timing of expression is another variable. If a developmental programme has already established stable tissue differentiation, introducing a regulator late may produce only limited changes. Earlier introduction into an undifferentiated primordium may have a stronger effect on identity. A failure to induce homeosis late in development does not establish that the gene lacks the capacity to affect fate at an earlier time. Similarly, restoring a functional regulator after a primordium was never initiated may not regenerate that missing primordium. The condition of the tissue at the time of manipulation determines which aspects of the phenotype are still recoverable.

Temperature-sensitive mutants are a classical way to investigate the timing of a requirement without relying solely on spatial expression. The def-101 study in Antirrhinum provides an instructive case. Different activity levels and temperatures could produce unequal effects in adjacent regions. A reduced dosage under one condition allowed second-whorl petal development and central carpel initiation while third-whorl development followed an abnormal carpelloid pattern. The result is important because it demonstrates that a single gene can have different quantitative requirements in distinct developmental contexts. The two B-dependent identities cannot always be treated as equally sensitive to every reduction in activity.

The study also examined the expression of DEF and GLO during development and supported the idea that their heterodimer contributes to maintaining expression after organ primordia have been initiated. The timing argues against equating the feedback mechanism with the original establishment of all primordia. If a conditional defect reduces the activity of the complex after the initial expression domains have formed, a later decrease in expression and abnormal growth can follow. Such experiments help distinguish an early positional input from a later self-maintaining regulatory state. This kind of causal separation is precisely what makes a time series more valuable than a single photograph of a mature mutant.

16. Quantitative reasoning: activity, dose and experimental comparisons

Qualitative transformations provide the most familiar examples of floral genetic reasoning, but quantitative observations allow a more precise test of competing explanations. A mutation may produce a partial transformation, alter the fraction of flowers affected or change the number of organs that complete a particular developmental programme. The relevant variable must be defined before counting. If the investigator records the fraction of flowers with any petal-to-sepal transformation, the resulting percentage measures penetrance of a flower-level phenotype. If the investigator counts the number of transformed second-whorl organs per flower, the resulting average measures the extent of a particular manifestation. These are not interchangeable statistics.

Consider a hypothetical population of twenty flowers from a conditional mutant. If twelve show at least one transformed organ, the estimated flower-level penetrance is twelve divided by twenty, or 0.60, equivalent to sixty percent. If each of those twelve flowers has only one transformed petal-position organ, the total number of transformed organs is twelve. Assuming four originally identifiable second-whorl positions per flower, there are eighty positions in the whole sample, and the transformed-position fraction is twelve divided by eighty, or fifteen percent. Thus the same observation can yield sixty percent and fifteen percent, both mathematically correct but answering different biological questions. An examination that provides a denominator must be read carefully.

The assumption that all four original positions are identifiable matters. If the mutation also causes absence of some primordia, counting only surviving organs could bias the estimated transformation fraction. The researcher may need to classify each expected position into categories such as normal identity, transformed identity and missing organ. Reporting these categories separately preserves information that would be lost if missing organs were simply excluded. The distinction is particularly appropriate for classical ap2 and pi phenotypes, in which organ number can be affected as well as identity.

Sampling design is also important. Several flowers from one plant are not equivalent to the same number of flowers sampled from independent plants for every statistical question. Flowers on the same plant share the genotype, growth environment and developmental history of that individual. If the goal is to compare independent transgenic lines or genetic backgrounds, replication across plants and lines is needed. Counting many organs within one unusual flower may make the anatomical description more detailed but does not by itself establish how reproducible the phenotype is across a population. Biological replication improves the strength of the general conclusion.

A simple equilibrium model can clarify why the amount of available protein matters for formation of a heterodimer. Denote the concentrations of free AP3 and free PI by P and Q, and the concentration of the complex by D. In a minimal reversible association, P plus Q forms D. At equilibrium the dissociation constant is defined by Kd equals the product of the free concentrations divided by the concentration of complex. If all concentrations are expressed in nanomolar, Kd has units of nanomolar. A lower Kd corresponds to a stronger association in this model, assuming comparable conditions. This is a general physical chemistry relationship used as a teaching model, not a measured affinity for AP3 and PI in a developing flower.

If one partner is present in large excess, its free concentration can be treated as approximately constant. Let total AP3 concentration be Ptotal, and let Q be the free PI concentration. Since Ptotal equals free P plus D, substitution into the dissociation expression gives D equals Ptotal multiplied by Q divided by the sum of Kd and Q. Dividing by Ptotal gives the bound fraction Q divided by the sum of Kd and Q. When Q equals Kd, half the available AP3 is associated under these assumptions. When Q is much greater than Kd, the fraction approaches one. When Q is zero, the fraction is zero regardless of the total concentration of AP3.

This relationship can be used to reason about a partially active allele without claiming that a whole floral whorl follows a single equilibrium. Suppose a hypothetical interaction has Kd equal to ten nanomolar and the excess partner is available at thirty nanomolar. The bound fraction is thirty divided by forty, or 0.75. If a mutation increases Kd to thirty nanomolar at the same partner concentration, the fraction falls to thirty divided by sixty, or 0.50. The absolute decline is 0.25 of the available protein, corresponding to twenty-five percentage points. The relative decline compared with the original bound fraction is one third. These quantities describe the simplified complex association, not the probability that an organ transforms.

A developing plant contains additional competitors, protein turnover, synthesis and changes in expression. If the partners have comparable total concentrations, the approximation of one in great excess is invalid. The available free concentrations would have to be found by accounting for depletion of both partners. Furthermore, the number of DNA-bound regulatory complexes depends on DNA affinity and other proteins, not merely the concentration of a heterodimer in solution. The calculation is useful for understanding the direction of an effect and the role of limiting partners. It is not a calibrated model of the number of petals or stamens a mutant will produce.

For an even simpler developmental interpretation, imagine two identity programmes with different thresholds of required active B complex. In a hypothetical system, the petal programme operates when a normalized activity exceeds 0.35, and the stamen programme operates when it exceeds 0.65. If a partial mutation reduces activity from 0.85 to 0.50, the remaining activity is above the first threshold but below the second. The model predicts normal or relatively preserved petal identity and abnormal third-whorl identity. Such a threshold illustration is consistent with the general idea that different whorls can have different dosage requirements, but the numbers are invented for reasoning practice. They are not measurements from the def-101 experiment.

The threshold picture also has limitations. A real organ may respond continuously rather than switching sharply at one value. Different cell types within a primordium may have different requirements. A transient high activity early in development can establish a programme that persists after the activity declines. Feedback between B-class partners can make the relationship between the initiating stimulus and final activity nonlinear. Therefore it is better to use the threshold model as a minimal hypothesis and compare it with observed distributions than to regard the numbers as universal developmental constants.

Quantitative rescue data can be interpreted in a similar way. If a normal reference displays forty identifiable petals among forty expected positions, a mutant displays eight, and a rescue line displays thirty-two, the rescue recovers twenty-four of the thirty-two positions lost relative to the normal reference. The recovery of the lost fraction is twenty-four divided by thirty-two, or seventy-five percent. The proportion of normal reference identity attained by the rescue line is thirty-two divided by forty, or eighty percent. The difference matters when comparing experiments that have different starting severities. A recovery metric should state explicitly whether it is based on absolute normal levels or on improvement relative to the mutant baseline.

Mathematical models are most informative when used to ask whether a proposed relationship could explain the data. A reduction in activity that produces a disproportionately large change in the phenotype could reflect a threshold, feedback or interaction with other limiting components. It could also reflect an inaccurate assay of active protein. A quantitative result does not automatically reveal which explanation is correct. To resolve the issue, experimental observations must be collected that distinguish the competing mechanisms, such as measurements of protein interaction, a range of activity levels, or time-resolved expression in each affected whorl.

17. Developmental integration and the limits of a shared model

The identity regulators of a flower act after developmental transitions have established the context in which floral organs will form. A plant can be late flowering yet make anatomically normal flowers once the transition occurs. Another can initiate flowers at a normal time but form petals in stamen positions. These phenotypes distinguish developmental timing from organ identity. In Arabidopsis, the FT-related flowering transition system, LFY and AP1-related meristem specification, and the ABCE organ-identity functions are interconnected but represent different levels of developmental control. Treating them as a single undifferentiated list of flowering genes prevents meaningful causal interpretation.

The distinction is especially important when AP1 is considered. Its participation in meristem identity means that altered outer organs and secondary flowers need not result from a simple change in the A-class domain. Antirrhinum SQUAMOSA and FLORICAULA are useful comparative examples of floral meristem identity regulators, with relationships to AP1 and LFY respectively. They establish the developmental context rather than replacing DEF and GLO as the principal B-class partners. A change in the number of floral meristems on an inflorescence is therefore not the same class of observation as a change in the identities of the second and third whorls within a flower.

A second distinction concerns the identity of an organ and the detailed shape that it develops. Snapdragon flowers exhibit pronounced dorsoventral differences. The gene CYCLOIDEA was identified through studies of floral asymmetry and participates in regulating these differences. A mutation that alters dorsal versus ventral petal characteristics can change the symmetry of the flower without converting every petal into a sepal. Such a phenotype reflects an alteration of regional growth or differentiation within the floral architecture. It provides a clear contrast to the transformations seen in strong B-class mutants. In a comparative experiment, the two types of mutation should be assessed with different readouts: organ identity markers for homeosis, and regional shape and growth patterns for symmetry.

A further distinction concerns organ identity and reproductive fate at a finer scale. A stamen is a diploid sporophytic organ that includes the tissues needed for pollen formation. Pollen grains arise later through the male gametophyte developmental programme. A carpel supplies the sporophytic structures associated with ovules and subsequent seed development. Changing stamen primordia into carpel-like organs therefore modifies the cellular environment in which gametogenesis would otherwise occur. However, not every defect in pollen or ovule production is a floral homeotic mutation. The FAR example shows that male reproductive performance can be affected without a dramatic transformation of all floral identities.

These distinctions matter in practical plant biology. Flowers with extra petal-like organs can be valued for ornamental appearance, but some transformations replace reproductive structures and reduce fertility. Selecting for a visually conspicuous trait may therefore have consequences for seed production. Conversely, an alteration of fertility without conspicuous organ transformation can be useful in understanding how pollen formation depends on reproductive tissues. The chapter's purpose is to identify the developmental principles behind these effects, not to provide a plant-breeding procedure. The causal sequence from gene activity to organ anatomy to reproductive outcome is the transferable concept.

The basic logic also helps interpret variation across flowering plants. The four-whorl scheme is a useful representation for the two eudicot models, but some angiosperms have indistinct perianth identities, different numbers of floral parts or less discrete expression boundaries. The existence of related MADS-domain factors across many flowering plants supports a broadly conserved contribution to floral development. It does not prove that the classical Arabidopsis A-class arrangement exists unchanged in every species. Comparing expression domains and functional evidence is necessary before assigning the same class-level label in a different floral architecture.

An example is a plant with perianth organs that share intermediate characteristics rather than being sharply divided into sepals and petals. It is possible that broad or overlapping regulatory activities contribute to such a pattern, but this cannot be established from morphology alone. The investigator would need to map the relevant gene functions and test what happens when they are altered. A difference in organ terminology may simply reflect morphological diversity. Conversely, a visually similar petal in two distant lineages may have evolved through regulatory changes that are not identical. The comparison between Arabidopsis and Antirrhinum gives a well-supported starting framework for addressing these broader questions.

When solving an unfamiliar examination vignette, first identify the level at which the defect appears. Does the plant fail to transition to reproductive growth? Does it fail to establish a normal floral meristem? Does a primordium appear in the expected position but acquire the wrong identity? Does an appropriately identified organ fail in differentiation or function? Does the meristem fail to terminate after inner organs begin? These questions define alternative explanations that can then be matched to expression, mutant and rescue evidence. A phenotype description that contains more than one defect may require more than one regulatory role for the affected gene, as in the case of AG and AP1.

The mature flower is ultimately the product of multiple interacting developmental decisions. The classical mutants made it possible to separate these decisions experimentally and discover a conserved combinatorial principle. The evidence from B-class heterodimers links genetic activity to protein interaction and DNA binding. The SEP mutant series demonstrates how partially redundant partners can be essential for several organ identities. The PLE and FAR comparisons reveal that related genes can carry unequal portions of an ancestral developmental function. Together these examples establish a general scientific lesson: similar outcomes can be achieved by related but nonidentical molecular systems, and the strongest developmental explanations are built by testing each causal link rather than by assigning a name to a mature shape.

Evidence and further reading: Luo et al. (1996), Antirrhinum floral asymmetry.

  3. Identical native expression domains in both species
  4. Complete restoration of all fertility without measuring it
Answer and reasoning

B. Conservation of some functional capacity in the tested host context

Cross-species rescue shows functional compatibility under the imposed expression conditions. Orthology, native regulation and fertility need independent evidence.

Question 33

KNU expression is restored in an ag background and the centre stops growing, but stamens remain petaloid. How should this be interpreted?

  1. AG has no role in reproductive identity
  2. KNU has fully replaced every AG function
  3. Determinacy rescue is separable from reproductive identity rescue
  4. The plant has reverted genetically to wild type
Answer and reasoning

C. Determinacy rescue is separable from reproductive identity rescue

The termination pathway can be restored without supplying the missing identity complex. The petaloid stamens show that rescue is incomplete; genetic reversion is not demonstrated.

Question 34

A wild-type corolla is bilateral. A mutant corolla becomes nearly radial, but petal markers remain and stamens retain identity. What is the most specific defect?

  1. Complete B-class homeotic loss
  2. Loss of all E function
  3. Failure to initiate flowering
  4. Regional symmetry or growth patterning
Answer and reasoning

D. Regional symmetry or growth patterning

The organ classes remain identifiable; symmetry is an architectural property. B/E defects would change identity broadly, and the presence of flowers excludes failure of flowering.

Question 35

In a sample of 20 flowers, 12 have one transformed petal position each and all 80 expected positions can be identified. Which percentages are correct?

  1. 60% flower-level penetrance and 15% position-level transformation
  2. 15% flower-level penetrance and 60% position-level transformation
  3. 60% for both
  4. 12% for both
Answer and reasoning

A. 60% flower-level penetrance and 15% position-level transformation

12/20 = 0.60 and 12/80 = 0.15. The denominators distinguish penetrance from extent. Interchanging them or using the raw count as a percentage is invalid.

Question 36

Assume Q is in excess at 30 nM. For Kd changing from 10 to 30 nM, what happens to bound P fraction?

  1. It increases from 0.25 to 0.50
  2. It decreases from 0.75 to 0.50
  3. It remains 0.75
  4. It decreases from 0.50 to zero
Answer and reasoning

B. It decreases from 0.75 to 0.50

Fraction Q/(Kd+Q) gives 30/40 and 30/60. Increased Kd weakens association. These values do not directly measure whorl transformation.

Question 37

Normal, mutant and rescue samples have respectively 40, 8 and 32 normal petal identities out of 40. What fraction of identities lost in the mutant is recovered?

  1. 80%
  2. 75%
  3. 60%
  4. 20%
Answer and reasoning

B. 75%

Lost = 32, recovered = 24, so 24/32 = 75%. 80% is the fraction of the wild-type level, 60% the absolute gain per 40, and 20% the mutant baseline.

Question 38

For 80 progeny in a 3:1 test, expected counts are 60 and 20; observed counts are 56 and 24. What is the chi-square statistic?

  1. Approximately 1.067
  2. 4.000
  3. 0.267 only
  4. 16.000
Answer and reasoning

A. Approximately 1.067

Each deviation is four; χ² = 16/60 + 16/20 = 1.067. 0.267 is only one term. With one degree of freedom, this does not reject at 5%.

Question 39

A far-like line has reduced selfed seed set, normal floral identity and low pollen viability. Which experiment best separates male from female contribution?

  1. Counting petals alone
  2. Measuring only flower initiation date
  3. Assuming a carpel transformation
  4. Reciprocal controlled crosses with a fertile reference
Answer and reasoning

D. Reciprocal controlled crosses with a fertile reference

Reciprocal crosses test whether reproductive failure depends on the mutant's role as pollen or ovule parent. Petal counts and timing do not isolate the fertility defect; gross carpel identity is normal.

Question 40

A third-whorl marker is reduced in mature def mutant organs. Which extra observation best supports direct early regulation rather than merely changed tissue composition?

  1. A photograph of the mature mutant only
  2. An unrelated change in leaf size
  3. Rapid early target response before morphological divergence, plus evidence of binding at its regulatory region
  4. A late transcript difference in mixed organs only
Answer and reasoning

C. Rapid early target response before morphological divergence, plus evidence of binding at its regulatory region

Early response combined with binding evidence strengthens a direct regulatory hypothesis. Mature tissue differences may instead reflect changed cell types. An unrelated phenotype is not evidence of direct regulation.

Integrated original practice — assertion, matching and reasoning

Question 41

Assertion: AP3 and PI strong loss mutants can share a phenotype. Reason: their products participate in a common required B activity. Choose the relationship.

  1. Both true; the reason does not explain it
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both true; the reason explains the assertion
Answer and reasoning

D. Both true; the reason explains the assertion

The common required activity accounts for the shared second- and third-whorl transformations. The other alternatives incorrectly deny the functional relationship.

Question 42

Assertion: AG and PLE are strict orthologues. Reason: they supply principal C function in their respective species. Choose the relationship.

  1. Both true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and reasoning

C. Assertion false, reason true

Functional counterparts are not automatically orthologues. The ancestral duplication separates AG and PLE into distinct lineages, although both supply the principal C function.

Question 43

Which statement combination is correct? I. SEP genes may compensate. II. AP2 is a MADS-domain factor. III. FAR loss can reduce male fertility without gross homeosis.

  1. I and II only
  2. I and III only
  3. II and III only
  4. I, II and III
Answer and reasoning

B. I and III only

I and III are supported by mutant evidence. II is false because AP2 belongs to the AP2/ERF family.

Question 44

Match (1) AP3, (2) PI, (3) AG, (4) AP1 with functional comparisons (a) GLO, (b) DEF, (c) PLE, (d) SQUA for meristem identity.

  1. 1-b, 2-a, 3-c, 4-d
  2. 1-a, 2-b, 3-d, 4-c
  3. 1-c, 2-d, 3-b, 4-a
  4. 1-d, 2-c, 3-a, 4-b
Answer and reasoning

A. 1-b, 2-a, 3-c, 4-d

AP3/DEF and PI/GLO are B counterparts, AG/PLE share principal C function, and AP1/SQUA correspond in meristem identity. The match is functional, not a claim of strict orthology for all four.

Question 45

In the idealized B+C loss model with A expanded and E intact, which region can retain stamen identity?

  1. Whorl two only
  2. Whorl three only
  3. None of the four
  4. Whorl four only
Answer and reasoning

C. None of the four

Stamen identity needs B+C+E; both defining activities are absent. All positions have A+E in the stated ideal model and tend toward sepal identity.

Question 46

A schematic shows two proteins forming a DNA-binding heterodimer. Which intervention most directly tests whether DNA recognition depends on the motif rather than only on partner association?

  1. Compare binding to normal and mutated motif DNA using the same protein preparations
  2. Count mature petals without testing DNA
  3. Remove both genes and record germination
  4. Compare unrelated species' chromosome numbers
Answer and reasoning

A. Compare binding to normal and mutated motif DNA using the same protein preparations

Changing only the recognition motif isolates its contribution to binding. Phenotype or chromosome comparisons do not directly test DNA sequence specificity.

Question 47

A hypothetical activity series is 0.20, 0.50 and 0.80. Petal threshold is 0.35 and stamen threshold 0.65. At which value are both programmes above threshold?

  1. 0.50 only
  2. 0.80 only
  3. 0.20 and 0.50
  4. All three
Answer and reasoning

B. 0.80 only

0.80 exceeds both thresholds; 0.50 exceeds only the petal threshold and 0.20 exceeds neither. The thresholds are stipulated, not universal biological values.

Question 48

Which statement correctly distinguishes rescue and ectopic expression?

  1. Both necessarily prove strict orthology
  2. Rescue requires absence of all endogenous floral factors
  3. Ectopic expression always produces a full flower
  4. Rescue tests restoration of a mutant defect; ectopic expression tests activity outside its usual context
Answer and reasoning

D. Rescue tests restoration of a mutant defect; ectopic expression tests activity outside its usual context

Restoration and altered-context expression address different causal questions. Neither necessarily proves ancestry or complete organismal transformation.

Question 49

In a loss-of-function mutant, all central stamens become petals but a separate manipulation stops the meristem at the usual time. Which readout remains abnormal?

  1. Reproductive organ identity
  2. Meristem termination only
  3. Flowering transition necessarily
  4. Root apical meristem organization
Answer and reasoning

A. Reproductive organ identity

The stipulated manipulation restores termination, but the transformed stamens establish an identity defect. The question provides no evidence for transition or root abnormalities.

Question 50

Which comparison best tests whether normal-looking far stamens have a differentiation defect rather than a complete identity transformation?

  1. Compare root length in unrelated species
  2. Compare identity markers and pollen viability in stage-matched wild-type and far stamens
  3. Count only seeds from selfing without other measurements
  4. Compare mature far stamens to early wild-type buds
Answer and reasoning

B. Compare identity markers and pollen viability in stage-matched wild-type and far stamens

Stage-matched identity and fertility readouts distinguish the properties. Seed set alone confounds male and female functions; unmatched stages and roots do not answer the question.

Question 51

Which inference about a transcriptional reporter for AP2 is valid?

  1. Reporter signal necessarily equals AP2 protein activity
  2. Absence of reporter proves absence of all AP2-family genes
  3. Reporter signal can differ from active AP2 protein because of post-transcriptional regulation
  4. Reporter distribution proves direct repression of every AG regulatory sequence
Answer and reasoning

C. Reporter signal can differ from active AP2 protein because of post-transcriptional regulation

A promoter reporter measures included regulatory activity, not necessarily active protein. miR172 and protein stability can affect protein abundance; direct target claims need additional evidence.

Question 52

A new flowering plant has a fused perianth with intermediate sepal/petal characteristics. What is the scientifically defensible approach?

  1. Assume all Arabidopsis A-class rules apply unchanged
  2. Conclude it has no MADS proteins
  3. Infer it is an Arabidopsis mutant without further evidence
  4. Map gene activity and test function before assigning exact ABC domains
Answer and reasoning

D. Map gene activity and test function before assigning exact ABC domains

Comparative development requires functional and spatial evidence. Morphology alone neither establishes conserved class boundaries nor identifies ancestry or genotype.

Revision and student mastery

Integrated conceptual summary

Identity reflects spatially combined activities, not a single gene for each organ. B-class pairs cooperate in whorls 2 and 3; AG/PLE are principal C-function regulators with termination roles, but AG and PLE descend from different duplicate lineages. SEP factors are overlapping partners, AP2 is an AP2/ERF factor, and FAR illustrates fertility without gross homeosis. Distinguish the classic ideal predictions from actual allelic, developmental and species-specific consequences.

Essential relationships

A+E → sepal; A+B+E → petal; B+C+E → stamen; C+E → carpel. Essential independent partners: 9/16 normal, 7/16 mutant after double heterozygote selfing. Complete redundancy: 1/16 double loss. Under excess-Q equilibrium: bound fraction = Q/(Kd+Q). For rescue, regained/lost measures recovery, not absolute normal fraction.

Examination traps and misconceptions

  1. Every pale sepal is a petal. Pigmentation can change independently of identity; check multiple tissue characteristics. NET may supply a colour-only mutant.
  2. Four whorls means four organs. Whorls are regions; organ count and fusion vary. NET may compare 4/4/6/2 with snapdragon.
  3. AP1 and AP2 are interchangeable. AP1 has a meristem role and is MADS; AP2 is AP2/ERF and contributes to outer C-domain restriction.
  4. AP2 RNA equals AP2 function. Post-transcriptional regulation changes active protein. Distinguish reporter, RNA and protein.
  5. One B partner is sufficient. AP3/PI and DEF/GLO cooperate. Loss of either can remove required B function.
  6. Identical phenotype proves same gene. Distinct loci can encode essential partners. Complementation and molecular evidence distinguish them.
  7. B loss must leave the real fourth whorl completely normal. Ideal identity predictions do not rule out secondary initiation defects, particularly in Antirrhinum.
  8. AG merely specifies carpels. AG contributes to stamen identity and floral determinacy. Reiteration is a separate readout.
  9. WUS must be absent from the start. Early stem-cell maintenance is necessary; timed late repression supports termination.
  10. Every SEP single mutant must be dramatic. Partial redundancy can mask single loss; combined mutations reveal function.
  11. Triple SEP loss equals complete E loss. SEP4 remains in the triple; quadruple loss can make leaf-like organs in all whorls.
  12. PLE and AG are strict orthologues. They are principal functional counterparts from different duplicated lineages; FAR is in the euAG lineage.
  13. FAR loss equals PLE loss. FAR loss can impair male fertility without broad reproductive homeosis; PLE is the principal C factor.
  14. A motif match proves direct in-vivo regulation. DNA accessibility, complex composition and causal expression evidence are needed.
  15. An ectopic expression failure disproves necessity. Missing partners, timing or competence may limit sufficiency despite a genuine requirement.
  16. A successful rescue proves all functions restored. Identity, termination and fertility require separate measurements.
  17. Every increase in petal number is ag. Meristem size and growth can change organ counts without reproductive homeosis.
  18. A hypothetical threshold is an empirical constant. Teaching thresholds illustrate possible dosage responses; actual values require measurements.

  • Distinguish organ identity, symmetry, meristem identity, determinacy and fertility.
  • Predict single and double class losses and qualify predictions for real alleles.
  • Reconstruct the C-lineage duplication and compare species-specific gene roles.
  • Evaluate expression, binding, rescue and genetic interaction evidence independently.
