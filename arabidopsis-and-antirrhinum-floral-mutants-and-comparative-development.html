1. The developmental problem: organ identity in a flower

A young flower begins as a small mound of dividing cells. At this stage the floral apex has neither mature pollen-bearing anthers nor conspicuous petals, yet it will generate structures with very different shapes, cellular compositions and reproductive functions. The outer structures protect the bud, the next group often contributes to attraction of pollinators, the stamens produce pollen, and the central carpels contain the ovules. Development must therefore coordinate both the location of each primordium and the kind of organ that it becomes. The challenge is not simply to make a large number of cells. It is to assign different developmental programmes to neighbouring cell populations that contain essentially the same inherited genome.

A primordium is the early group of cells from which a particular organ develops. In plants, most cells are immobilized relative to their neighbours by cell walls, so the conversion of a primordium into a petal or a stamen involves coordinated changes in growth, division orientation, differentiation and tissue organization rather than migration of the cells to a distant anatomical location. The primary decision about identity can be studied independently of the later process of making a correctly shaped organ. A primordium in a position normally occupied by a stamen can instead make a petal-like structure. The position may be approximately normal even though the fate is abnormal. This type of change is called a homeotic transformation. The distinction between position and fate is the essential starting point for reading floral mutant experiments.

Consider a flower in which a normally green sepal becomes larger and white. This observation alone does not prove that the organ has become a petal. A mutation in chlorophyll biosynthesis might make a sepal pale without changing its fundamental identity. A mutation that affects the distribution of cell divisions might enlarge it. A stronger claim requires the comparison of multiple features, such as epidermal cell morphology, vascular and tissue organization, the pattern of expression of organ-specific genes and the developmental history of the primordium. Similarly, a stamen that fails to produce viable pollen can still be recognizably a stamen. Male sterility is not a synonym for replacement of stamens by petals or carpels.

The most informative mutants change the identity of several organs in a coherent spatial pattern. When the second and third groups of floral primordia are repeatedly affected while the first remains comparatively normal, the common effect points toward a regulator that acts in a shared region. An explanation based on a separate defective structural protein for each organ would require two independent changes. A single mutation affecting two adjacent organ types instead suggests that they share part of their regulatory machinery. This is the conceptual insight behind the classical genetic analysis of Arabidopsis and Antirrhinum. The repeated combinations of organ changes allow a student to reconstruct the normal developmental system from abnormal outcomes.

A developmental regulator differs from an ordinary structural component in the scope of its effects. A protein needed to form a cell wall in one tissue may influence the final mechanical properties of that tissue. A regulatory protein that controls the expression of many genes can redirect a cell population into a substantially different programme. In practice the distinction is not absolute, because growth and fate influence one another, but the patterns observed in classical floral homeotic mutants strongly support an upstream role for specific transcriptional regulators. The molecular identities of several of these regulators were subsequently established, connecting the genetic argument to biochemical evidence.

This chapter treats the flower as an experimental system. The question to ask of each example is not simply which gene name belongs to which organ. Instead, determine what the normal region of activity is, what the mutation changes, what developmental programmes remain possible, what the resulting organ resembles and which parts of the explanation require additional evidence. The general model of floral identity introduced in the preceding curriculum node is used as a predictive framework. Its application to two independently studied flowering plants will show both why it works and why a textbook model should not be treated as a universal description of every allele and every species.

2. Two model plants and their normal flowers

Arabidopsis thaliana is a small flowering plant of the mustard family, Brassicaceae. Its suitability for genetic study reflects a combination of practical properties: it is small enough to grow many individuals in limited space, it has a relatively short generation interval under favourable conditions, and self-fertilization permits the maintenance of genetically defined lines. Controlled crosses are also possible. Recessive mutations can therefore be recovered in selfed progeny, maintained in heterozygotes when the homozygous mutant is sterile, and combined with other mutations to investigate genetic interactions. The usefulness of Arabidopsis is not that every feature of its flower is universal, but that observations can be linked to heritable changes with unusual clarity.

A typical Arabidopsis flower has four sepals, four petals, six stamens and a central gynoecium formed from two fused carpels. Four of the stamens are longer than the other two. The gynoecium is the female reproductive organ system, and the carpels form the structures that enclose ovules. These numbers provide a useful baseline, but the four whorls refer to four positional and developmental domains, not to four individual organs. The fused central structure must not be scored as though it represented only one carpel. Similarly, the six stamens form the third whorl even though they differ in length. An organ count and a whorl count are measurements of different properties.

Antirrhinum majus, the garden snapdragon, belongs to Plantaginaceae. Its mature flower is visually striking because the corolla is bilaterally symmetric rather than radially symmetric. A bilateral flower has a plane of reflection symmetry, whereas a radially symmetric flower can be divided into similar sectors through more than one plane. Snapdragon petals are fused into a corolla with distinct upper and lower regions. The typical flower has five sepals, five petals in the fused corolla, four fertile stamens and a reduced fifth stamen position, together with a central gynoecium of two fused carpels. These differences make Antirrhinum valuable for separating the conserved identity of an organ from the species-specific rules that govern its shape, number and symmetry.

A petal in Arabidopsis and a petal in Antirrhinum are related developmental structures even though the mature organs differ greatly in shape and architecture. Petal identity can be specified by related regulatory mechanisms while other regulators determine whether the primordia grow separately or produce a fused corolla, whether upper and lower regions grow differently, and which epidermal cell types differentiate in particular regions. In the same way, stamens may differ in length or fertility without losing their identity as stamens. The genetic comparison is meaningful precisely because the mature flowers are not identical.

Both plants allow a geneticist to ask whether a change in a gene has a reproducible effect on particular positions in the developing flower. In an experiment based on mature flowers, the researcher first needs a wild-type reference with a clearly established arrangement. In a study of immature buds, the developmental stage must also be considered. A small third-whorl primordium sampled too early may have none of the mature characteristics of a stamen; comparison with a mature wild-type flower would create a misleading impression of developmental arrest. Comparisons should therefore be made between equivalent stages, as far as the mutation permits. For mutants that alter the developmental schedule itself, a time series and independently recognizable landmarks are more informative than a single chronological age.

The wild-type arrangement can be represented as a sequence from outside to centre: sepal, petal, stamen, carpel. This sequence is common to the two model plants at the level of identity classes. It does not mean that all organs arise simultaneously, that every primordium has an equivalent rate of growth, or that the geometry of the whorls can be ignored. A flower with displaced or missing primordia cannot always be divided into four simple visible rings. The experimenter then uses early microscopy and the position of neighbouring organs to establish the original developmental region before assigning a transformed identity.

3. From visible homeosis to the genetic model

The historical importance of floral homeotic mutations lies in the way they connected an observable anatomical change to a general theory of developmental patterning. Before molecular cloning of the responsible regulators, systematic mutant analysis in the two plants had already suggested that organ identities depended on combinations of activities. The 1989 work of Bowman, Smyth and Meyerowitz described recessive Arabidopsis mutations affecting floral organ development. Their 1991 genetic analysis compared single and combined mutant phenotypes. Independent work in Antirrhinum provided parallel evidence. The influential synthesis by Coen and Meyerowitz in 1991 brought these genetic patterns together into what became known as the ABC model.

A useful starting observation is that loss of the B activity has two consequences rather than one: the petal position can develop a sepal-like organ, and the stamen position can develop a carpel-like organ. If B were simply a petal construction gene, the third-whorl effect would be unexplained. If B were simply a stamen construction gene, the second-whorl effect would be unexplained. A more economical explanation is that B is required in both regions but cooperates with different activities in each. In the second whorl it acts alongside the outer-domain programme; in the third whorl it acts alongside an inner-domain programme. Removal of B then reveals the different programmes that remain in the two positions.

This reasoning is based on the comparison of phenotypes rather than an assumption that transcription factors are already known. At the stage of genetic model building, an activity can represent one gene, several genes, or interacting proteins. Calling a gene a B-class gene is a statement about its role in the flower, not a statement that it belongs to a particular biochemical family by definition. Molecular identification is a separate result. The flower thereby offers an elegant example of how a genetic model can precede a biochemical explanation.

The classical framework gives A activity to the outer two whorls, B to the middle two and C to the inner two. A without B specifies a sepal, A with B a petal, B with C a stamen, and C without B a carpel. Classical A and C activities are treated as antagonistic, so removal of one may allow the other to act in an expanded domain. For a strong C-class loss mutant, the second-whorl petal programme can consequently appear in the third whorl, where B activity is retained and A expands. The fourth region no longer executes normal carpel development. This four-region prediction alone is insufficient to explain the continued initiation of organs in some C mutants; floral meristem termination must also be considered.

The use of double mutants tests whether these explanations are consistent when two regulatory functions are removed. For example, removing both B and C in the simplified model, while allowing A to expand, predicts sepal-like identities instead of petals and stamens. If the centre also lacks normal meristem termination, additional organs may form. The point of the double mutant is that it distinguishes the loss of B from the retention of C. A single B mutant develops a carpel-like organ in the third whorl because C remains. A B C double mutant lacks that remaining activity. Memorizing the single-mutant phenotype without considering what remains would lead to a wrong double-mutant prediction.

Real flowers need not behave as ideal Boolean diagrams. Individual genes may have partially overlapping functions, and a mutation may reduce rather than eliminate a protein's activity. Organ primordia can be absent or reduced in number, particularly in strong or complex mutant backgrounds. Some regions may form intermediate structures, with features of more than one identity. These observations do not invalidate the genetic logic. Instead they reveal developmental roles and dependencies that a minimal model intentionally leaves out. The 1991 Arabidopsis study explicitly described organ-number and organ-loss phenotypes in addition to transformations. Therefore an examination question that specifies a complete class loss and an idealized ABC model can have a simpler answer than a question that asks for the phenotype of a particular allele in an actual plant.

The general experimental strategy is to develop a model that explains the largest number of observations with a small set of causal assumptions, derive predictions, and then test those predictions with new genotypes or expression experiments. A model is stronger when it predicts what has not yet been observed. Simply assigning an activity retrospectively to every abnormal organ would be much less informative. The comparison with Antirrhinum helps because genes discovered in another species can reveal whether the same patterning logic is present beyond the original genetic system.

4. Arabidopsis outer-whorl mutants: APETALA1 and APETALA2

The word APETALA in a gene name reflects a historically conspicuous mutant phenotype, but the name is not a precise description of its full developmental function. APETALA1, abbreviated AP1, has an important role in establishing floral meristem identity as well as in the normal development of the outer floral organs. A strong ap1 mutant can produce bract-like structures in place of ordinary sepals, lose or reduce petals, and initiate secondary flowers in the axils of abnormal outer organs. The appearance of secondary flowers is a valuable clue. An ordinary floral meristem is committed to making a flower; a structure that repeatedly initiates additional floral meristems instead retains or regains aspects of shoot-like developmental organization.

If all that a student knew about AP1 were that it belonged to a classical A-class list, the expected phenotype might be the ideal A-loss sequence of carpels and stamens in the two outer whorls. The actual ap1 phenotype is not adequately explained by that sequence. Removing one member of a functional class does not necessarily remove all class activity, and AP1 also participates in specifying the developmental context within which the whorl model operates. Thus AP1 illustrates two independent cautions: a single gene need not equal an entire functional class, and a gene that contributes to organ identity may also affect meristem identity. In experimental data these roles can be partially separated by comparing alleles, developmental stages and combined genotypes.

APETALA2, or AP2, is the other standard Arabidopsis A-function example. Unlike AP1, AP2 belongs to the AP2/ERF transcription factor family, not the MADS-box family. This molecular distinction is useful in an examination because most canonical Arabidopsis floral organ identity factors commonly listed together are MADS-domain proteins. AP2 is the major exception to that generalization. Its function also extends beyond a simple description of outer organ development. The proper restriction of reproductive C activity is influenced by AP2, and changes in AP2 function can allow inappropriate AGAMOUS expression in outer floral positions.

In an idealized strong loss of A activity with C expanded outward, the first whorl becomes carpel-like, the second stamen-like, the third remains stamen-like and the fourth remains carpel-like. This pattern follows directly from the remaining combinations: C, B plus C, B plus C, C. Strong ap2 alleles can display related outer-whorl homeosis, but exact numbers, organ absence and intermediate phenotypes vary. In a real ap2 mutant a position with no organ cannot literally be classified as a stamen just because a model would predict stamen identity if a primordium were present. The underlying distinction is between the failure to initiate or maintain an organ and a transformation of an organ that develops.

The spatial restriction of AG has been examined by locating its RNA in developing flowers. Early AG transcript is normally detected in the primordia of the third and fourth whorls, not throughout the young flower. Ectopic AG expression in an ap2 background supports the idea that AP2 contributes to establishing the outer boundary of C activity. The evidence should be interpreted at the developmental stage examined. The distribution of AG expression can change as tissues differentiate, so an RNA pattern from a later stage does not automatically describe the original identity-specification domain. The absence of an early transcript from a region is also not proof of permanent absence throughout that organ's life.

AP2 activity is regulated by microRNA172, a small RNA that can suppress the expression of AP2-like targets at a post-transcriptional level. Consequently, the distribution of AP2 RNA should not be treated as identical to the distribution of functional AP2 protein. This is one reason that the two-band A and C model is best understood as a diagram of functional activities rather than a literal picture of every transcript. A reporter that measures transcription may show a broader expression pattern than the active protein if post-transcriptional regulation differs spatially. Distinguishing RNA, protein abundance and functional activity is a useful connection to the earlier curriculum on gene expression.

Consider two abnormal flowers with reduced petals. In the first, the outer organs become bract-like and secondary floral structures appear; in the second, outer reproductive-like organs appear and the expression boundary of AG moves outward. These observations suggest different primary developmental defects even though both flowers have fewer normal petals. The first points toward the combined floral meristem and outer-organ functions of AP1, whereas the second is consistent with disruption of outer-domain regulation by AP2. Phenotype descriptions are therefore much more informative than the statement that a plant is apetalous. A student should name the affected region, the identity of the replacement organ and any change in meristem behaviour before selecting a candidate regulatory gene.

5. Arabidopsis B-class mutants: APETALA3 and PISTILLATA

APETALA3 (AP3) and PISTILLATA (PI) are two well-established Arabidopsis B-class genes. Their encoded proteins contain a MADS DNA-binding domain. The normal combined activity of the two proteins is essential for the correct development of petals in the second whorl and stamens in the third whorl. In strong loss-of-function mutants of either gene, petals are replaced by sepal-like structures and stamens by carpel-like structures. The central carpel region is not converted into petals merely because the flower has lost its normal B activity. B function is not normally the principal identity activity of that central domain.

Why do mutations of two separate genes generate similar homeotic transformations? One plausible explanation is that the products of the two genes are needed together for a common regulatory function. If the active unit is a protein complex containing AP3 and PI, eliminating either subunit prevents the formation of that unit even when the other subunit remains present. The functional activity of the pair can then be represented as a logical conjunction: both partners must contribute. Such a pattern is distinct from complete functional redundancy, in which either of two genes acting alone can maintain a process and the phenotype appears only when both are lost.

The genetic evidence is compatible with the partnership hypothesis, but similar phenotypes alone cannot establish a physical interaction. Two enzymes that act in successive steps of a biochemical pathway may produce similar loss phenotypes without forming a stable complex. To show that two transcription factors physically cooperate, researchers need independent biochemical or molecular evidence, such as detection of protein interaction and sequence-specific binding by the complex. Studies of related B-class proteins in Antirrhinum supplied particularly clear evidence for a heterodimer with DNA-binding activity. Later work on Arabidopsis floral MADS proteins identified their associations in larger organ-identity complexes.

The consequence of losing B in the second whorl is different from that in the third because the remaining positional context is different. In the second region A-class activity remains, and with E-class support the programme favours sepal identity rather than petal identity. In the third region C activity remains, and with E-class support the programme favours carpel identity rather than stamen identity. The same molecular defect therefore leads to two anatomically distinct outcomes. This is a general property of combinatorial regulation: the function of a regulator depends on the other factors present in a cell population.

The timing of B activity matters as well. In classical temperature-sensitive allele experiments, a plant with a condition-dependent defective protein can be shifted between temperatures at different stages. If an early shift alters the final identity while a late shift has little influence on the gross identity, the result identifies a time window during which normal activity is essential for the measured outcome. However, an unaffected gross organ type after a late shift does not exclude subtle late roles in differentiation. The method detects the time sensitivity of a chosen phenotype and a particular mutant allele. It does not establish that every downstream target is switched simultaneously or that a normal protein has identical behaviour under every environmental condition.

Loss of B function also has reproductive consequences. Replacement of stamens by carpel-like structures eliminates or disrupts the tissues that normally support pollen formation. The absence of functional pollen is therefore expected as a downstream consequence, rather than evidence that AP3 or PI must act solely in the male gametophyte. A defect restricted to pollen differentiation could leave the anther and filament architecture largely intact, whereas an identity defect changes the organ in which pollen would otherwise develop. These alternative explanations can be distinguished by examining early floral anatomy, mature organ morphology and the later presence of gametophytic cells. This links organogenesis to reproduction without confusing the developmental identity of the sporophytic organ with the subsequent formation of the haploid gametophyte.

6. AGAMOUS: reproductive fate and termination of floral growth

AGAMOUS (AG) is the canonical Arabidopsis C-function gene. In a normal flower its early activity is concentrated in the inner regions, where the combination with B contributes to stamen identity and the activity without B contributes to carpel identity. AG encodes a MADS-domain transcription factor, and its expression is regulated in space and time. A strong ag mutant lacks normal stamens and carpels. Stamens are replaced by petals, and the central region continues to generate floral organs in a reiterated pattern. This last phenotype demonstrates that AG does more than determine the type of tissue differentiated in a fixed number of pre-existing primordia.

A normal inflorescence meristem can continue to produce new flowers on its flanks. In contrast, a floral meristem is normally determinate: its central stem-cell system eventually ceases to maintain a self-renewing population. The central reproductive structure can then complete its development rather than being replaced by further rounds of floral primordia. The continued growth of the ag floral centre is a failure of this termination process. Meristem identity and meristem determinacy are related but not identical. A floral meristem can retain enough floral character to keep producing floral organs while failing to stop at the normal time.

The relationship between AG and WUSCHEL (WUS) helps explain the abnormal centre. WUS is a regulator of stem-cell maintenance in shoot and floral meristems. Early in floral development, stem-cell activity is necessary because sufficient cells must be generated to establish the floral organs. WUS contributes to activation of AG in the developing flower, together with other floral regulators. Later AG contributes to repression of WUS, thereby limiting continued stem-cell activity. A regulatory arrangement in which an early maintenance system helps initiate its own eventual shutdown allows the growing meristem to terminate after the appropriate developmental programme has been established.

One experimentally supported route involves KNUCKLES (KNU), a transcriptional repressor activated downstream of AG. The activation of KNU is temporally controlled; its later activity helps reduce WUS expression. Other AG-dependent repression routes also contribute, so the pathway AG to KNU to WUS is a useful core mechanism rather than an exhaustive network. A timed reduction of WUS is different from the complete absence of WUS at the beginning of flower formation. If stem-cell maintenance ended too soon, too few cells would remain to support normal organ initiation. If it persisted too long, the centre could continue producing tissue and organs. The required outcome is therefore temporal control rather than a permanent instruction to suppress all stem-cell activity.

Sun and colleagues reported experimental evidence linking KNU to floral determinacy in 2009. Prolonged WUS expression in knu flowers and the response to manipulated KNU expression helped place KNU downstream in the termination pathway. Particularly informative is the conceptual possibility of separating a determinacy defect from an organ-identity defect. Restoring termination in a C-class mutant does not by itself provide the missing combinations of factors needed to restore stamens and carpels. A rescued stem-cell phenotype can therefore coexist with persistent abnormalities in reproductive-organ identity. Conversely, restoration of one reproductive organ marker does not demonstrate that the entire centre has become determinate.

The mature ag flower is often drawn as a sequence of sepals, petals, more petals and a further internal flower. This is a schematic representation of reiteration, not a fixed numerical formula for the number of organs formed. Additional rounds vary with developmental conditions, allele and the amount of residual meristem activity. An examination answer should distinguish the third-whorl homeosis, which follows from the remaining B and expanded A activities, from the repeated central growth, which follows from loss of the C-dependent termination programme. Both derive from mutation of AG, but they arise through distinguishable biological consequences.

It is also important to recognize that a flower with extra petals does not necessarily carry an AG mutation. A meristem-size mutation may generate additional primordia without changing their identities; a mutation in growth regulation may make petal-like structures larger or more conspicuous; and mutations in other floral regulators may alter identity or termination. A compelling genetic diagnosis requires evidence from the inner organ identities, the centre's behaviour and, when available, expression or complementation results. Counting petals is a useful observation, not a sufficient molecular test.

7. SEPALLATA and the need for combinatorial partners

A model based on A, B and C activities predicts how three spatial functions distinguish four organ identities. However, it does not follow that these three activities are biochemically sufficient to make the corresponding organs in any cell of a plant. A vegetative leaf can express an introduced floral regulator without necessarily becoming a completely differentiated floral organ. The competent developmental context matters. An important genetic discovery explained part of this dependence on context: the activities of the classical identity factors require additional partners encoded by SEPALLATA genes.

Pelaz and colleagues reported in 2000 that the combined loss of SEPALLATA1, SEPALLATA2 and SEPALLATA3 produced an unusual floral phenotype in Arabidopsis. Petals, stamens and carpels were replaced by sepal-like organs. The phenotype was not simply a duplicate of the single B-class or single C-class loss phenotype. Removing B alone would preserve carpel identity in the inner region, and removing C alone would preserve petal identity in the second whorl while producing abnormal inner organs. In the combined SEP mutant, the normal identities dependent on these classes were broadly disrupted. The observation suggested that the additional genes participate in the realization of both B and C functions rather than merely assigning a fifth independent region of the flower.

The importance of using a combined mutant becomes clear when related genes can substitute for one another. Suppose three genes produce proteins with partially overlapping activities. A mutation in one may leave enough activity for normal gross organ identity because the remaining genes still contribute. The absence of a dramatic phenotype in that single mutant is not proof that the gene has no developmental role. By combining mutations, the experimenter reduces the available pool of related activity and can reveal a function that was hidden by redundancy. A comparison between single, double and triple mutants is therefore a test of a model about the relationship among gene products.

SEPALLATA4 supplies additional overlapping function. The combined loss of the four SEP genes is associated with floral organs acquiring leaf-like characteristics rather than the simpler sepal-like identities of the triple mutant. This distinction is valuable because it prevents a misleading description of E as a factor needed for petals and reproductive organs only. The modern ABCE model represents E activity as a partner for floral organ identity more generally, including sepal identity. A triple mutant retains SEP4 activity; it is not equivalent to the idealized removal of the entire E class. A comparison of the two genotypes helps distinguish residual function from a qualitatively different rule.

With E included, the simplest functional combinations are A with E in the sepal region, A with B and E in the petal region, B with C and E in the stamen region, and C with E in the carpel region. This is not intended as a count of individual protein molecules. A single class symbol may represent multiple proteins, and a molecular complex may contain two molecules belonging to a class or draw upon overlapping family members. The class-level representation helps predict organ identities, while a physical model of protein interactions explains how those identities are implemented.

In a B-class mutant with functional E factors, the remaining complexes in the two affected whorls can still support alternative floral identities. In a severe E-class mutant, several of these alternative floral complexes may themselves be impaired. This explains why removing E is not equivalent to removing B and C while leaving all other functions intact. The distinction is especially useful in multiple-statement examinations where the same symbols are used both to describe genetic domains and to represent proposed molecular components.

The D function is conventionally associated with ovule identity and involves AGAMOUS-related MADS-domain factors, including SEEDSTICK in Arabidopsis. It is a useful extension of the floral identity framework, but an ovule is not a fifth concentric whorl equivalent to the four main floral organ regions. Ovules arise within the carpel context. The specification of their identity must also be separated from subsequent development of the female gametophyte inside the ovule. The former is an organ and tissue patterning problem; the latter is treated in the gametogenesis chapter. A D-related phenotype therefore cannot be interpreted solely by assigning an extra symbol to a hypothetical fifth outer or inner ring.

The revised model illustrates a characteristic feature of developmental genetics. A remarkably useful initial theory can survive the discovery that its implementation is more complex than originally imagined. The A, B and C combinations remain a powerful method for interpreting the characteristic homeotic patterns. The discovery of E factors clarifies why those combinations require additional floral context and why an experiment that eliminates a group of partners can disrupt several identities at once. Rather than memorize the old and new models as unrelated lists, understand E as an improvement motivated by an experimental result that the simpler representation did not fully explain.

8. Molecular mechanism: shared DNA recognition and selective complexes

The proteins encoded by AP3, PI, AG, AP1, SEP and many related floral genes are members of a transcription factor family characterized by a conserved MADS domain. A transcription factor alters gene expression through sequence-specific interactions with DNA and through interactions with other regulatory proteins. The conserved domain helps explain why proteins involved in different floral identities have related biochemical properties. Yet sequence similarity alone cannot specify which organ will develop, because the biological effect depends on where and when a protein is expressed, which partners are present and which genes its complexes regulate.

The MADS domain participates in DNA recognition. Many plant developmental MADS proteins also contain regions that influence partner selection, complex formation and transcriptional regulation. At an examination level, the main structural principle is that a protein can carry both a DNA-recognition surface and an interaction surface. Mutations affecting these two functions need not have the same molecular consequences. A mutation may preserve interaction with a partner but reduce DNA binding, or it may preserve the DNA-binding region while destabilizing the interaction necessary for a functional complex. Either could reduce developmental activity without preventing accumulation of the corresponding transcript.

A frequently discussed recognition element for floral MADS-domain proteins is the CArG box. It is a short DNA sequence with a characteristic central region rich in adenine and thymine, often represented as CC(A/T)6GG. Real binding preferences can vary, and a matching sequence in a genome does not guarantee that a particular complex binds that location in a living cell. Accessibility of DNA, the composition of the protein complex and the regulatory environment all influence occupancy. In addition, binding is not automatically equivalent to activation; a complex may stimulate or repress expression depending on the target and associated factors.

The interaction between AP3 and PI supplies one level of combinatorial specificity. A DNA-binding complex containing both factors can function in the two central whorls, but the different partners in those regions help determine which set of developmental target genes is regulated. In the petal region, association with AP1 and SEP-like factors supports a petal programme. In the stamen region, association with AG and SEP-like factors supports a stamen programme. A change in one partner can therefore alter the output of a complex even if its other components remain available. This molecular logic is consistent with the genetic conclusion that B is necessary for two different identities.

Honma and Goto reported in 2001 that combinations of floral MADS-domain factors could induce floral-organ characteristics in leaves and supplied interaction evidence involving SEP3. In the developmental interpretation, combinations of regulators have a greater ability to redirect tissue identity than a single identity factor acting without its expected partners. Such experiments address sufficiency, whereas a loss-of-function phenotype primarily addresses necessity. The two kinds of evidence complement one another. Removing a required component shows that the normal programme depends on it; introducing an appropriate combination tests whether the programme can be initiated in an abnormal context.

The floral quartet concept describes a higher-order arrangement of interacting MADS-domain proteins that bind two DNA recognition sites and may bring intervening DNA into a loop. This provides a conceptual bridge between the class-level combinations and the regulation of target genes. The key point is not that every target is controlled by one rigid four-protein assembly with identical geometry. Rather, proteins can cooperate in larger complexes, and their joint interaction with DNA can create a regulatory response that differs from the activity of an isolated factor. Structural and biochemical details vary among complexes and targets.

The interaction has a physical basis. Formation of a protein complex is favoured when the free energy of the associated state is sufficiently low relative to the separated components. The availability of the partners also matters: even a favourable interaction cannot create a large number of complexes if one partner is absent. Sequence-specific DNA binding similarly depends on protein availability and affinity for the relevant site. Noncovalent interactions, such as electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, help stabilize protein–DNA recognition. The process is not a separate enzyme reaction consuming a fixed ATP molecule for each floral organ specified. Energy is used by cellular processes that synthesize and maintain regulatory molecules and establish growth, but a transcriptional identity decision is not appropriately described by an invented energy budget for petal versus stamen formation.

This molecular perspective explains how two spatially similar expression patterns can generate different developmental outcomes. A transcript may be present in both whorls while its encoded protein forms different complexes in each. A constitutively expressed B factor may fail to confer a B-dependent identity outside the region containing a compatible partner. A mutation that disrupts the partner-binding surface may cause a homeotic phenotype despite near-normal transcription of the corresponding gene. In each case the step that fails lies between expression and functional transcriptional regulation, a distinction often hidden by the casual statement that a gene is either switched on or switched off.

Downstream targets connect the identity system with visible organogenesis. Once a regulatory combination alters gene expression, changes in cell proliferation, polarity, cell-wall expansion and cell-type differentiation can gradually produce the anatomical characteristics of a particular organ. There need not be a single downstream target whose activation is sufficient to construct an entire stamen. Instead a regulatory factor coordinates multiple developmental processes, some direct and some mediated by additional regulators. An altered expression pattern in mature tissue can consequently be a downstream effect of the homeotic conversion rather than the initial cause. To distinguish direct from indirect regulation, the researcher must examine the temporal order and obtain independent evidence for the regulatory relationship.

9. Antirrhinum: DEFICIENS and GLOBOSA as a conserved B function

In snapdragon, the classical genes DEFICIENS (DEF) and GLOBOSA (GLO) provide an independent genetic system for studying the B function. Strong mutations in either disrupt normal petal and stamen development. The outer perianth identity becomes evident in the second region, and female reproductive characteristics occur in organs of the third region. The exact mature shape can be affected by the characteristic fused corolla and bilateral architecture of Antirrhinum, so the standard identity transformation should not be confused with the four-petalled morphology of Arabidopsis. The informative comparison is the positional change from petal toward sepal and from stamen toward carpel.

In 1990 the identification of DEF as a member of the MADS-box family supplied a molecular clue to how a floral homeotic gene could act. Subsequent studies of DEF and GLO in 1992 investigated their expression and DNA-binding properties. Evidence from protein binding experiments showed that the two proteins could form a heterodimer that recognized specific DNA motifs. A heterodimer is a complex of two different molecular subunits; it differs from a homodimer, which consists of two copies of the same protein. Because the two B-class proteins cooperate in the functional complex, a mutation of either gene can produce a broadly similar pattern of identity disruption.

The biochemical result is important because it tests an inference suggested by the classical genetics. Similar mutant phenotypes had already indicated that DEF and GLO participate in a common developmental function. Physical interaction and sequence-specific DNA binding provide a more direct explanation of this shared requirement. However, the presence of the heterodimer in an assay does not demonstrate that it regulates every gene affected in the mutant flower. A specific target must be supported by evidence linking the complex to the relevant DNA regulatory region and showing a corresponding change in expression or function.

DEF and GLO also participate in maintenance of their expression patterns through cross-regulatory interactions. In the second and third whorls, the two proteins can bind regulatory regions associated with their own genes, providing a molecular basis for a feedback mechanism. When an initiating signal establishes expression in the appropriate region, the production of both partners can help maintain the functional state. If either partner is lost, the absence of the heterodimer can compromise this reinforcement. The resulting decline in expression may amplify the initial functional defect. This is different from a model in which one gene is always wholly upstream of the other in a one-directional pathway.

The distinction between initiation and maintenance is essential for interpreting expression experiments. A mutant could establish an initial expression domain but fail to sustain it as the organ grows. A late observation of absent expression would then be misleading if interpreted as proof that the domain was never initiated. Time-resolved measurements of RNA or protein allow these alternatives to be distinguished. Conversely, the mere presence of early RNA does not demonstrate that a functional complex forms. For a mutation that preserves transcription but alters protein interaction, the early expression signal could look normal while the downstream organ programme fails.

Classical conditional DEF alleles also allow the relationship between developmental stage and function to be investigated. A time-dependent change in activity can affect later petal growth and differentiation as well as early identity. Because snapdragon petals form a complex corolla, a change in cell expansion or epidermal differentiation can have an especially conspicuous effect on mature appearance. It is important to distinguish these later manifestations from the early decision about whether the primordium follows a petal programme at all. A transformed organ can retain some positional features of the original whorl while expressing the tissue characteristics of an alternative identity.

The correspondence between DEF and AP3, and between GLO and PI, is supported by both related sequences and functional evidence. A student should be able to predict that removal of the homologous B partner in either model system will preferentially alter the middle two organ regions. Nevertheless, a direct comparison must account for possible changes in other whorls and organ numbers. Some Antirrhinum B-mutant studies report effects on initiation of central carpel development as well. These observations show that a failure in the middle regions can influence later organ initiation in another region, even though the minimal identity model places the principal B function in whorls two and three. The direct functional domain of a regulator and the complete anatomical consequences of its mutation are not necessarily identical.

The conserved feature is therefore an interaction-based B-class mechanism, not a promise that every detail of the mutant flowers will match. This is precisely why comparative development is valuable: similar results in two distinct morphological backgrounds strengthen the general explanation, while different results reveal additional features of the developmental context. The appropriate examination response first states the conserved transformation, then qualifies the prediction if the question provides a real allele, a stage-specific experiment or a species-specific effect.

10. PLENA and FARINELLI: separating reproductive identity and fertility

PLENA (PLE) is the principal classical C-function gene in Antirrhinum. Loss of its function leads to a broad disruption of reproductive-organ identity, with inner organs acquiring perianth-like characteristics and the floral centre losing normal determinacy. In the simplest comparison, this corresponds functionally to the loss of AGAMOUS activity in Arabidopsis. In both cases a regulator required for inner organ development is also involved in the termination of the floral meristem. A flower with repeated inner organs thus supports the same broad inference about the relationship between reproductive identity and meristem growth.

The snapdragon system also contains a related C-lineage gene called FARINELLI (FAR). Davies and colleagues described the comparison of ple and far mutants in 1999. Despite similarities in their encoded MADS-domain factors, the single mutant phenotypes were very different. Whereas ple mutants showed broad reproductive-to-perianth homeosis and a failure of normal termination, far mutants could retain recognizable floral organ identity but display partial male sterility. In the latter situation the existence of an anatomically identifiable stamen does not guarantee normal reproductive performance. A developmental regulator can contribute to the differentiation or function of reproductive tissue without being the sole determinant of gross organ identity.

One possible explanation for the distinct single mutant phenotypes is unequal redundancy. PLE makes a major contribution to C-class identity and termination, while FAR contributes overlapping or additional functions whose loss is more limited in the presence of intact PLE. If both genes contributed identically and could fully compensate for one another, neither single mutant should have a major identity phenotype under ordinary conditions. The dramatic phenotype of ple therefore rules out a simple model of perfect interchangeability. It does not rule out partial overlap in some processes, and combined mutant or expression analyses can reveal interactions not apparent in either single mutant.

The study of PLE and FAR also revealed that the regulation of B-domain boundaries differs from the simple Arabidopsis comparison. When expression of B-class genes is examined in mutants lacking C-lineage functions, the outcome depends on interactions among the regulatory genes, not simply the identity of the final organ. It is safer to distinguish the broad class-level prediction from the observed distribution of a specific transcript. A gene may influence the boundary of another activity through a direct regulatory interaction or through an indirect consequence of changing tissue identity. The classical rule that A and C oppose one another does not supply a complete map of every molecular interaction in both species.

Fertility offers a useful independent measurement. A flower can retain the correct number and approximate shape of stamens but produce a reduced proportion of viable pollen grains. Another flower may lack stamens because those primordia become petal-like organs. Both plants can exhibit reduced seed set, but the causal sequence differs. In the first, the defect may concern tissue differentiation or gamete production within an identifiable reproductive organ. In the second, the required organ context is missing. Counting seeds alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms, because successful seed formation also requires appropriate female development, pollination, fertilization and later seed maturation.

The distinction also matters for genetic maintenance of mutant lines. A complete loss of reproductive organ identity can prevent ordinary self-fertilization, so homozygous plants may not be able to produce the progeny needed for further genetic analysis. Heterozygous carriers with normal fertility may allow the mutation to be maintained. A partially male-sterile mutant may still function as a female parent if its female reproductive organs are competent, but this possibility must be tested rather than assumed from its name. Reciprocal cross data help identify whether a fertility reduction is associated predominantly with the male or female contribution.

Taken together, the PLE and FAR examples show why a gene name should not be equated mechanically with an ABC function. A functional class is a description of a biological contribution; related proteins can differ in the amount and timing of that contribution. PLE and FAR provide a particularly useful case in which a conserved regulatory family supports related developmental processes but the responsibilities are distributed unequally. The next question is whether a similar function in two species always belongs to the genes most closely related by descent.

11. Evolution of gene families: function is not the same as ancestry

When two species diverge from a common ancestor, each descendant lineage inherits many of the genes of that ancestor. A pair of genes whose separation reflects a speciation event are termed orthologues. A pair whose separation reflects a gene duplication event are termed paralogues. These terms describe evolutionary relationships, not the degree of resemblance between two mutant phenotypes. Orthologues can evolve different roles, and paralogues can retain overlapping or similar roles. The distinction becomes particularly important when a regulatory gene family has undergone duplication before two species diverged.

The main C function in Arabidopsis is carried by AG, whereas in Antirrhinum it is carried predominantly by PLE. A comparison based only on mutant phenotype would tempt one to call AG and PLE orthologues. Evolutionary analyses indicate a more interesting situation. An ancestral duplication of a C-lineage gene produced two lineages before the divergence of the relevant core eudicot species. Arabidopsis AG and Antirrhinum FAR belong to one of these lineages, often termed the euAG lineage. Antirrhinum PLE and the Arabidopsis SHATTERPROOF genes belong to the other lineage. The predominant C function is thus carried by different descendants of the ancestral duplication in the two plants.

This relationship has a simple consequence for how a comparative table should be read. The entry AG versus PLE is an appropriate functional comparison, because strong loss of each produces broad reproductive homeosis and altered floral determinacy. It is not an appropriate assertion of strict orthology. The entry AG versus FAR describes a closer correspondence of evolutionary lineage, but the far single mutant does not reproduce the severe ag phenotype. These two comparisons answer different questions and can both be correct. An examination item that uses the words orthologue and functional counterpart interchangeably is therefore biologically imprecise.

Causier and colleagues in 2005 used comparative genomic evidence, including the arrangement of genes in corresponding genomic regions, to support the evolutionary relationship. Conserved arrangement, known as synteny, adds evidence beyond an isolated sequence comparison. When surrounding genes are conserved in corresponding regions, their arrangement can help distinguish genes inherited from the same ancestral locus from related genes that originated through duplication. Sequence-based evolutionary trees and genomic context together help reconstruct the history of a family. The phenotype then reveals how developmental functions have been distributed among the descendants.

Following duplication, both copies may initially possess similar capacities. Later mutations in regulatory sequences can change where a gene is expressed, when its expression begins or how much protein accumulates. Changes in the encoded protein can alter DNA recognition, interaction with partners or transcriptional regulation. Some ancestral functions may be retained by both copies, some predominantly by one, and some may be modified or lost. The term subfunctionalization describes the partitioning of functions that were originally combined in one ancestral gene. It is a useful conceptual model here, provided that it is not used to claim an exact ancestral expression pattern that has not been established.

The family history illustrates how a developmental output can remain relatively conserved while its molecular implementation changes. Both model plants still require coordinated specification of male and female reproductive organs and eventual termination of floral growth. Natural selection can maintain these essential outcomes even when the relative contributions of duplicated regulators diverge. This does not imply that evolution intentionally selects a particular gene name or always preserves an identical regulatory network. It means that heritable changes compatible with functional reproduction can accumulate in different lineages.

A cross-species rescue experiment can test whether an introduced factor can perform a function in another species. For instance, if a related protein from one plant restores an organ identity in a mutant of the other plant when expressed in the appropriate domain, the outcome supports some conservation of biochemical function. However, restoration under an artificial promoter does not show that the gene normally has the same expression domain in its own species. Nor does functional rescue establish orthology; a paralogous protein can supply similar activity when placed in the appropriate context. An unsuccessful rescue is equally ambiguous if the foreign protein is not expressed at the right concentration, fails to interact with the host partners or is present at the wrong developmental stage.

The comparison of AP1 and Antirrhinum SQUAMOSA provides another caution. These related genes contribute to floral meristem identity in their respective plants, but the outer-organ functions attributed to AP1 in Arabidopsis cannot simply be transferred without qualification to SQUAMOSA in Antirrhinum. Classical A function is consequently less straightforward to generalize across the two systems than the B-class correspondence. A well-supported comparative explanation identifies the particular function that is conserved, the specific function that differs and the type of evidence used to establish each claim. Generalizing from two eudicot models to all angiosperms requires additional comparative evidence.
