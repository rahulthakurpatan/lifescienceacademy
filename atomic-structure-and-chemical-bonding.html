1. The chemical foundations of life

Follow a carbon atom through a living cell. It may enter in glucose, become part of an amino acid, and finally leave in carbon dioxide. Its neighbours change, its electron density changes, and the energy of its bonds changes, yet the atom remains carbon. Why? Its chemical identity is determined by its nucleus, whereas its chemical behaviour arises largely from its electronic structure. This difference between identity and behaviour is the starting point for understanding the molecules that make life possible.

An element is a chemical species defined by its nuclear proton number. A compound contains elements combined in definite chemical proportions, whereas a mixture may vary in composition. A molecule is a discrete neutral bonded entity, such as O₂ or glucose; a polyatomic ion, such as bicarbonate, is a bonded entity with net charge. A crystal of sodium chloride is not made of independent NaCl molecules: NaCl gives the simplest ratio of sodium to chloride ions in the extended lattice. A molecular formula describes the number of each type of atom; a structural formula describes connectivity. Ethanol and dimethyl ether both have C₂H₆O but different connectivity, which gives them different properties. Geometry and charge distribution add another level of information beyond connectivity.

Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur constitute the framework of many biological molecules. Carbon forms diverse chains and rings; oxygen and nitrogen create polar regions and reactive sites; phosphorus contributes to nucleotide and phospholipid chemistry; sulfur occurs in thiols, thioesters and disulfides. Inorganic ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺ and iron ions are equally important. Their differences in size, charge, electron configuration and coordination explain why they cannot simply substitute for one another in every protein. We will develop this logic from atomic structure to molecular architecture, rather than treating the periodic table and biochemistry as separate subjects.

2. From atomic theory to quantum mechanics

Dalton's early atomic theory connected definite chemical proportions to discrete particles. The atom, however, was not indivisible. In cathode-ray experiments, Thomson showed that the rays could be deflected by electric and magnetic fields and behaved as negatively charged particles. Similar charge-to-mass ratios for rays produced using different materials supported the existence of a common subatomic constituent, the electron. Millikan subsequently measured the elementary charge using charged oil drops, allowing the electron mass to be inferred from charge-to-mass measurements. These experiments distinguished an electron from the electrically neutral atom as a whole.

Rutherford interpreted the scattering of alpha particles in Geiger–Marsden experiments as evidence for concentrated positive charge. Most particles passed through thin foil with relatively little deflection, while a small fraction were scattered through large angles. A diffuse positive charge model could not readily account for these rare events. A small nucleus containing nearly all the atomic mass explained both the frequent small deflections and the rare large ones. Chadwick later established the existence of the neutron. Atomic dimensions are commonly on the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m, while nuclear dimensions are on the order of 10⁻¹⁵ m; neither number is a universal radius. Most of the atom's spatial extent is the region described by electronic wavefunctions.

Classical physics predicted that an accelerating charged electron would radiate energy. A planetary model could not satisfactorily explain the stability of atoms or hydrogen's discrete emission lines. Bohr's 1913 model introduced allowed quantised energy states, successfully describing much of the hydrogen spectrum, but its fixed-orbit picture could not be generalised satisfactorily to many-electron atoms and chemical bonds. Electron diffraction, the wave nature of matter and quantum theory led to the modern orbital description. Notice how each historical model answered a question raised by experimental evidence but also had boundaries. Atomic scattering probes concentrated nuclear charge; spectroscopy probes differences between energy states. Neither experiment in isolation reveals the complete atom.

3. Atomic identity, isotopes, ions and atomic mass

Write a nuclide as AZX with signed ionic charge q in units of the positive elementary charge e. Z is the number of protons; A is the total number of protons and neutrons. Hence N = A − Z gives neutrons and Z − q gives electrons. The sign matters: a negative q increases electron number. The proton carries +e, electron −e and neutron zero net charge, with e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Protons and neutrons each have mass near one dalton; the electron mass is approximately 1/1836 of the proton mass. Neutral hydrogen-1 is the exception to the statement that every nucleus has a neutron.

For ²⁴Mg²⁺, Z = 12, A = 24 and q = +2. There are twelve protons, twelve neutrons and ten electrons. Neon also has ten electrons, but its nucleus has only ten protons. Equal electron counts define isoelectronic species, not identical chemical behaviour. An ion forms through electron loss or gain; ordinary chemical reactions do not change the proton number. A formal monatomic P³⁻ ion would have eighteen electrons, but free phosphide is not the form of phosphorus normally present in living cells. Phosphate is a bonded polyatomic ion.

Isotopes share Z but differ in neutron count. Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 have six, seven and eight neutrons respectively. Isobars share A but differ in Z; isotones share N. Isotope substitution often retains the basic electronic bonding possibilities while changing mass-dependent behaviour. Carbon-13-labelled glucose can be followed through metabolism because its carbon atoms participate in ordinary carbon chemistry but carry a measurable mass signature. Isotope substitution is not entirely chemically invisible: vibrational frequencies and zero-point energies can change. For instance deuterium substitution can influence the rate of a reaction involving cleavage of a hydrogen-containing bond. Isotope effects are not changes in nuclear charge or valence.

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable isotopes; carbon-14 is radioactive. Carbon-13 can be studied using mass spectrometry or nuclear magnetic resonance, whereas carbon-14 can act as a radioactive tracer. Phosphorus-32 may label phosphate-containing compounds. A tracer follows the labelled atoms, not necessarily the original intact molecule. Detailed radiolabelling methods and safety are covered in Unit 14. Mass number is a count and should not be confused with measured isotope mass. Nuclear binding energy and the different masses of protons and neutrons mean most nuclide masses are not exact integers in daltons. The dalton is one twelfth of the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state at rest.

If fᵢ is fractional abundance and mᵢ is isotope mass in Da, then mean mass m̄ = Σ fᵢmᵢ, with Σ fᵢ = 1. To derive it, consider n atoms: n fᵢ have mass mᵢ, and the total mass divided by n gives the weighted mean. Relative atomic mass is dimensionless, obtained by dividing the mean mass by 1 Da. Natural abundances can vary, so standard atomic weights for some elements are intervals rather than fixed exact values.

Worked example: carbon abundance

For 98.9% ¹²C (12.000 Da) and 1.1% ¹³C (13.00335 Da), the mean is 0.989(12.000) + 0.011(13.00335) = 12.01103685 Da. Conversely, if only two isotopes exist and the heavier fraction is x, then m̄ = (1 − x)m₁ + xm₂, so x = (m̄ − m₁)/(m₂ − m₁). A mean of 12.011 Da yields x ≈ 0.01096 or 1.10%. A single average cannot independently determine several unknown masses and abundances.

4. Energy levels and the wave description of electrons

Hydrogen emits or absorbs specific wavelengths because its bound electronic states have discrete energies. Electromagnetic radiation obeys c = λν and photon energy E = hν = hc/λ. Here c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ is the speed of light in vacuum, λ is wavelength in metres, ν frequency in s⁻¹ and h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. An upward electronic transition requires energy; a downward one can emit a photon whose positive energy equals the magnitude of the energy difference. Real systems can also exchange energy nonradiatively. An absorption line is evidence of an allowed energy difference, not a measurement of an electron's exact trajectory.

For a one-electron hydrogen-like ion, a simple energy formula is Eₙ ≈ −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ Z²/n² J per atom, neglecting finite nuclear mass and relativistic corrections. The negative sign refers to a bound state relative to electron and nucleus separated at infinity. As n rises, energy approaches zero. The energy needed to ionise ground-state hydrogen is approximately 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J or 13.6 eV. This formula cannot be applied without modification to neutral helium, carbon or other multielectron atoms because electrons interact. It can be applied approximately to He⁺, Li²⁺ and other one-electron ions.

Worked example: the hydrogen Balmer line

For n = 3 → 2, ΔE = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸(1/4 − 1/9) = −3.028 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The photon energy is +3.028 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Substituting in λ = hc/E gives 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m = 656 nm. The atom's negative energy change and the photon's positive energy are consistent. Reversing the transition requires absorption of the same energy in this approximation.

De Broglie associated wavelength with momentum through λ = h/p, where p is momentum in kg m s⁻¹. Electron diffraction demonstrates wave-like behaviour. Heisenberg's uncertainty relation ΔxΔpₓ ≥ ℏ/2, with ℏ = h/(2π), sets a lower bound on the product of position and momentum standard deviations in one direction. This is not merely an error arising from imperfect instruments. Assigning an exact simultaneous position and momentum as in a classical trajectory does not describe a quantum bound state.

For a stationary one-electron state, the Schrödinger equation Ĥψ = Eψ relates the Hamiltonian operator (kinetic plus potential energy), wavefunction ψ and energy E. The probability density is |ψ|², not ψ itself. Normalising the wavefunction means integrating its probability density over all space gives one. A small volume contains probability equal to the integral over that volume. Wavefunctions can have signs or complex phases; phase is not electrical charge. Electron density in a many-electron atom accounts for all electrons. The orbital model therefore represents permitted states and spatial distributions, not an electron moving along the boundary of a drawn orbital.

5. Quantum numbers, orbital shape and nodes

The principal quantum number n is a positive integer and specifies the shell. The angular momentum quantum number l takes integers 0 to n − 1, defining subshells s, p, d, f for l = 0, 1, 2, 3. For a given l, magnetic quantum number mₗ ranges from −l to +l in integer steps. Thus there are 2l + 1 spatial orbitals. Electron spin projection mₛ is +½ or −½. Spin is intrinsic quantum angular momentum, not classical rotation of a tiny charged sphere. The four quantum numbers describe a spin-orbital in the usual atomic model.

Each spatial orbital accommodates at most two electrons with opposite spin projections. A subshell has capacity 2(2l + 1). For the nth shell, summing 2l + 1 over l = 0 to n − 1 gives the first n odd integers, whose sum is n². The maximum capacity is 2n². The third shell therefore has 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 spatial orbitals and eighteen places. This is a capacity calculation, not a claim that the third shell must fill completely before the fourth begins.

In hydrogenic orbitals, total nodes = n − 1, angular nodes = l, radial nodes = n − l − 1. A node is a surface where the wavefunction vanishes. An s orbital is spherical; a 2s orbital has one radial node. A 2p orbital has one angular nodal plane but no radial node. A 3p orbital has one radial and one angular node. The common pₓ, pᵧ and pᶻ drawings show real directional orbitals; pₓ and pᵧ are combinations of magnetic quantum-number eigenfunctions. In an isolated spherically symmetric atom the three p orbitals have the same energy in the absence of splitting. Common real d orbitals have four lobes except dᶻ², which has two lobes and a toroidal region. An angular node may be a plane or a cone. Drawn surfaces of fixed wavefunction magnitude are not hard boundaries enclosing every electron.

To check a proposed quantum-number set, test restrictions in sequence. (3, 2, −2, +½) is valid for a 3d spin-orbital; (2, 2, 0, −½) is invalid because l cannot equal n. (3, 1, 2, +½) is invalid because |mₗ| exceeds l. An electron spin projection of zero is also invalid. These restrictions give the answer even when the orbital is unfamiliar.

6. Electronic configuration and valence electrons

Hydrogen's energy depends principally on n in the elementary model. In a multielectron atom, attraction to the nucleus, electron–electron repulsion and exchange affect energies. Inner electrons shield outer electrons from nuclear attraction. Penetration is the extent to which an orbital has density close to the nucleus even when its average radius is large. Within a shell, s orbitals generally penetrate more strongly than p, and p more strongly than d. This helps explain why subshell energies differ in multielectron atoms.

The Aufbau principle guides ground-state filling of low-energy orbitals. The empirical n + l rule usually puts lower n + l first, with lower n resolving ties. Thus neutral atoms begin filling 4s before 3d in the standard sequence; this does not establish an unchanging orbital energy ladder for every ion. Pauli's exclusion principle states that two electrons cannot share all four quantum numbers. Two electrons in one spatial orbital must have opposite spin projections. Hund's first rule favours maximum total spin within a degenerate subshell for ground-state configurations. In simple orbital diagrams, fill equivalent orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing. It is not permissible to pair two electrons with parallel spin in one spatial orbital.

Carbon's six electrons give 1s²2s²2p². Two p electrons occupy distinct orbitals in the standard ground-state diagram. Nitrogen has 2p³, with three singly occupied p orbitals, and oxygen has 2p⁴, with one pair and two singles. Fluorine has 2p⁵ and neon 2p⁶. These isolated-atom configurations explain similarities in periodic families, but an isolated carbon atom's two unpaired electrons do not restrict carbon to forming only two molecular bonds. The electronic state reorganises in the bonded molecular environment.

Chromium and copper are familiar exceptions to a naive filling sequence: Cr is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, Cu [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Near-degenerate orbital energies and electron interactions determine the total energy; a simplistic rule that all half-filled or filled subshells must always be preferred is unreliable. Transition-metal cations generally lose outer ns electrons before (n − 1)d electrons. Fe is [Ar]3d⁶4s², Fe²⁺ [Ar]3d⁶, and Fe³⁺ [Ar]3d⁵. Transition-metal valence involves both ns and d orbitals. An unpaired electron contributes paramagnetism; systems with all electrons paired are diamagnetic in the simple classification. Free Fe³⁺ has five unpaired d electrons in the elementary Hund-rule picture, but a low-spin d⁵ complex can have only one because ligands split the d levels. Configuration alone does not determine every biological metal's spin state. Zn²⁺ is generally diamagnetic and acts as a Lewis acid in many enzymes without undergoing ordinary redox cycling.

7. Periodic trends: the logic behind the table

Across a main-group period, nuclear charge increases while added valence electrons occupy the same principal shell and do not fully cancel the increased attraction. We can represent effective nuclear charge qualitatively as Zₑff = Z − S, where S is a shielding parameter. This is an approximate model, not a single exact constant for all orbitals. Increased attraction generally draws valence electrons inward, reducing covalent radius. Down a group, an additional shell usually increases size despite rising Z. Atomic radius is not uniquely defined because orbitals have no hard edges; covalent, van der Waals and ionic radii refer to different measurement conventions.

A cation is generally smaller than its neutral parent because electron loss reduces repulsion and may empty a whole outer shell. An anion is usually larger because extra electrons increase repulsion. In an isoelectronic series O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, each has ten electrons, while proton count increases from eight to twelve. The bare electron cloud generally contracts with rising nuclear charge. Free oxide is not stable in ordinary water, and hydrated radius need not follow bare-ion radius. A small highly charged ion may bind a substantial hydration shell. This is why ion selectivity and mobility cannot be predicted from a single size value.

First ionisation energy is the energy required for X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻ from the gaseous ground-state atom. It is positive, generally increases across a period, and decreases down a group. Exceptions are revealing: boron's 2p electron is easier to remove than beryllium's 2s electron; repulsion between paired 2p electrons helps explain why oxygen has a lower first ionisation energy than nitrogen. Successive ionisation energies rise; a pronounced jump occurs when removal reaches a core shell, as after removal of magnesium's two valence electrons. Electron affinity is the energy change for adding an electron to an isolated gaseous species. When reported as addition enthalpy, an exothermic addition is negative; some tables instead report positive energy released. Chlorine's first electron addition is more exothermic than fluorine's because the compact 2p shell of fluorine has greater added-electron repulsion.

Electronegativity measures attraction for shared electron density in a bond. It is not the same as isolated-atom ionisation energy or electron affinity. Its general trend is increasing across a period and decreasing down a group. Main-group valence patterns arise from outer configurations: alkali metals often form +1 ions, alkaline-earth metals +2 ions and halogens −1 ions or one covalent bond. Valence, formal charge, oxidation state and partial charge are distinct. In H₂O, oxygen forms two bonds, has formal charge zero, oxidation state −2, and partial negative charge. Oxidation state assigns shared electrons to the more electronegative atom by an idealised convention. Formal charge divides shared electrons equally. Partial charges describe electron distribution through a chosen model and need not be integers. These differences are central to interpreting phosphate and metal-containing biomolecules.

8. The energy of bonding: ionic and covalent limits

Bring two initially separated atoms closer together. Each nucleus attracts both its own and the other atom's electrons, while nuclei repel one another and electrons repel one another. In a stable bonded state the total energy has a minimum at a finite separation. At very short distances repulsion rises sharply; at large distances the interaction fades. The equilibrium bond length is the separation at the minimum, not a fixed distance at every moment: bonded atoms vibrate. A bond dissociation enthalpy describes the specified cleavage process, often homolytic gas-phase dissociation in kJ mol⁻¹. Homolysis gives each fragment one electron of a shared pair; heterolysis assigns both electrons to one fragment, yielding ions for a neutral starting species. Context determines which is favoured. Forming a bond releases energy relative to corresponding separated fragments, while breaking that bond costs energy. Net biochemical reaction energy depends on all changes, not one bond.

At the ionic limit, electron transfer yields oppositely charged ions. This is not adequately explained by imagining distant sodium and chlorine atoms exchanging an electron for free: sodium ionisation costs energy, electron attachment releases or consumes energy, and the ionic lattice provides strong collective stabilisation. NaCl is an extended network. The electrostatic energy for ideal point charges q₁ and q₂ at distance r in vacuum is U = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r). Here ε₀ is vacuum permittivity, q values are signed charges in coulombs, r is separation in metres and U is joules per pair. Opposite charges give negative energy relative to infinite separation; like charges positive energy. In a simple uniform medium divide by relative permittivity εᵣ. Real solvent structure and other ions complicate the result.

Worked example: attraction between two ions

For +e and −e at 0.30 nm, with k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.988 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², U = −ke²/r ≈ −7.69 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per pair. Multiplying by 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ gives approximately −463 kJ mol⁻¹. For εᵣ = 80, the ideal continuum estimate is −5.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Neither is the full lattice energy or free energy of binding in a protein. The first omits other ions, the second omits molecular hydration and desolvation.

Covalent bonds involve shared electron density. In H₂ two electrons occupy a bonding combination of atomic 1s orbitals; they interact with both nuclei, lowering the total energy at suitable separation. A Lewis line describes a shared electron pair without confining electrons to a literal line. Real bonding spans a continuum: polar covalent C–O involves unequal electron sharing, whereas an ideal ionic model treats charges as separated. Greater covalent character in some ionic compounds can be rationalised by Fajans' qualitative rules: a small, strongly charged cation may distort a large polarisable anion. No universal electronegativity threshold divides all ionic and covalent bonds. Metallic bonding involves delocalised electrons in an extended metal; a coordinated Fe ion in haem is not a piece of metallic iron. Biological metal sites require a donor–acceptor coordination description.

9. Lewis structures, formal charge and coordinate bonds

Begin a Lewis structure by summing valence electrons, adding one per negative charge and subtracting one per positive charge. Draw a reasonable skeleton, with hydrogen terminal. Allocate two electrons per single bond, complete terminal valence shells where appropriate, and distribute the remaining electrons. If the central atom lacks a suitable valence arrangement, form multiple bonds by representing terminal lone pairs as shared pairs. Check the electron total and formal charges. The octet rule describes a useful main-group pattern: outer s and p subshells hold eight electrons. Hydrogen instead has a duet. It is not a universal law of chemical stability.

Formal charge FC = V − L − B/2, where V is neutral valence electron number, L is nonbonding electron count (not number of pairs), and B is the number of electrons in bonds to the atom. Equal sharing means each bond electron is counted exactly once across both atoms. Therefore the sum of formal charges equals the net charge. Prefer reasonable contributors with reduced unnecessary charge separation and negative charge on more electronegative atoms, but do not disregard experimental evidence.

For CO₂, 4 + 2(6) = 16 valence electrons. The O–C–O skeleton uses four electrons. If both oxygens receive three lone pairs, carbon has only four shared electrons. Represent one lone pair from each oxygen as a further bond: O=C=O. Carbon has eight shared electrons, each oxygen two lone pairs and a double bond, and all formal charges are zero. In bicarbonate, 1 + 4 + 3(6) + 1 = 24 electrons. A major contributor is HO–C(=O)–O⁻. Hydroxyl oxygen has two single bonds and two lone pairs; the nonprotonated singly bonded oxygen has three lone pairs and formal charge −1. A second major contributor exchanges double bond placement between the two nonprotonated oxygens. Moving the proton would change nuclear connectivity and is not a resonance operation.

NH₄⁺ has 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 valence electrons. Four N–H bonds use all eight, leaving no nitrogen lone pair. FC(N) = 5 − 0 − 8/2 = +1. NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ illustrates a coordinate covalent bond: nitrogen supplies both electrons of the newly formed shared pair. After formation, the four N–H bonds in isolated ammonium are equivalent; one does not retain a different physical nature because of its origin. Proton transfer in water involves real donors and acceptors rather than a stable pool of naked H⁺.

A Lewis base supplies an electron pair and a Lewis acid accepts one. Mg²⁺ accepts electron density from ATP phosphate oxygens and water without necessarily changing its oxidation state. Haem iron coordinates to porphyrin nitrogen atoms and axial ligands. Histidine nitrogen, carboxylate oxygen and cysteine sulfur are frequent donors in proteins. Coordination number counts directly attached donor atoms rather than ligand molecules or oxidation state. A bidentate ligand supplies two donor atoms. Ligand binding may change electronic energies or spin without changing the formal oxidation state. This distinction explains why Zn²⁺ and Mg²⁺ can have the same charge and different catalytic functions.

Octet exceptions include electron-deficient BF₃, odd-electron NO and hypervalent descriptions such as PF₅. Do not invent electrons to force an octet on an odd-electron species. Modern treatments of hypervalency do not require extensive participation of high-energy d orbitals in main-group bonding. Phosphate Lewis drawings with P=O or charge-separated alternatives are representations of a strongly polar delocalised system, not proof of literal d-orbital hybridisation. Use Lewis structures for bookkeeping, then select other models for actual geometry and electron distribution.

10. Sigma and pi bonds, hybridisation and geometry

Lewis structures count electrons but cannot by themselves explain bond angles. Valence-bond theory adds the concept of constructive orbital overlap. A sigma bond has shared density along the internuclear axis, with rotational symmetry in the simple two-centre model. It can arise through s–s, s–p or head-on p–p overlap, or through hybrid orbitals. A pi bond is formed by lateral overlap, commonly of parallel p orbitals, with a nodal plane containing the internuclear axis. Rotation around a double bond disrupts lateral overlap. This explains why alkenes have restricted rotation compared with most carbon–carbon single bonds. A single bond normally contains one sigma bond; a double contains one sigma and one pi; a triple contains one sigma and two perpendicular pi components. For comparable atom pairs higher bond order usually correlates with shorter and stronger bonds, though double bonds are not universally twice as strong as single bonds.

Hybridisation is a mathematical re-expression of atomic orbitals on one centre into directional combinations suitable for a local bonding description. It is not a separately observed physical step in which a free atom first promotes and then mixes its orbitals. Methane is described by four equivalent sp³ hybrids on carbon pointing approximately towards tetrahedral corners and overlapping hydrogen 1s orbitals. Each ideal hybrid has 25% s character and 75% p character. The ideal angle is 109.47°. Ethene's carbons each use three sp² hybrids in a plane with one remaining perpendicular p orbital. Its five sigma bonds form the skeleton, while lateral p overlap produces one pi bond. Its atoms are approximately coplanar. Ethyne's carbons each use two oppositely directed sp hybrids and two remaining perpendicular p orbitals. It contains three sigma and two pi bonds and is linear.

The s character increases from sp³ (25%) through sp² (33⅓%) to sp (50%). Greater s character tends to hold electrons nearer carbon's nucleus and stabilises negative charge on an sp carbon compared with analogous sp² or sp³ carbon. This contributes to the greater acidity of terminal alkynes than alkenes or alkanes, although all are weak compared with many biological acids. The whole molecular environment matters. Hybrid labels are useful approximations, especially for localised main-group bonding, and should not override delocalisation. Amide nitrogen is approximately planar because its lone pair participates in the carbonyl pi system; simply counting four apparent electron groups can give the wrong impression.

11. Predicting molecular shape with VSEPR

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion theory treats regions of electron density around a central atom as domains that tend to separate. Draw the Lewis structure before counting domains. A multiple bond counts as one domain, and each lone pair counts as another. Electron-domain geometry includes lone pairs; molecular geometry describes only positions of atoms. In AXₘEₙ notation A is the centre, X represents bonded atoms and E represents lone pairs. The steric number m + n gives the domain arrangement. VSEPR predicts shape but is not a complete quantum theory of bond formation.

All three molecules CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O have four electron domains around the central atom. In methane all are bonding; ammonia has one lone pair and water has two. A lone pair is generally more spatially demanding near the centre than a shared pair because the electron density is not pulled towards another nucleus. The qualitative repulsion order is lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair. This helps account for the decreasing bond angle, from 109.5° through about 107° to 104.5°. These are observed values, not numbers that follow quantitatively from counting lone pairs. Other factors also influence angles.

In a trigonal bipyramid, axial domains have three 90° contacts while equatorial domains have two. A lone pair usually occupies an equatorial position. Two lone pairs in an octahedral domain arrangement tend to occupy opposite sites, yielding square-planar XeF₄. Transition-metal geometry often requires ligand-field reasoning; a d⁸ metal complex may be square planar despite having four donor atoms. Do not assign sp³d or sp³d² hybridisation as literal proof of d orbital involvement in main-group hypervalency. Geometry also underlies stereochemistry: a tetrahedral carbon with four different substituents can form non-superimposable mirror-image arrangements. A carbon with two identical groups does not qualify on this basis. Enzymes distinguish such structures because molecular interactions occur in three dimensions.

12. Electronegativity, bond polarity and molecular dipoles

An electronegativity difference shifts shared electron density, creating partial negative δ⁻ and partial positive δ⁺ regions. Neither symbol denotes a full ionic charge. For an ideal pair of charges +q and −q separated by distance r, dipole magnitude μ = qr in C m; 1 debye (D) ≈ 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. Chemistry bond dipole arrows commonly point towards the negative end with a crossed positive tail, while the physical electric dipole vector points from negative to positive. The convention must be consistent when adding vectors.

For two equal bond contributions of magnitude μ separated by angle θ, transverse components cancel and each contributes μcos(θ/2) along the bisector; the resultant is 2μcos(θ/2). Thus linear CO₂ has polar bonds but no permanent molecular dipole because its equal opposite contributions cancel. Bent H₂O has a nonzero molecular dipole, as does pyramidal NH₃. Methane's four equivalent bond contributions cancel by tetrahedral symmetry; chloromethane's do not because its substituents differ. The total molecular electron and nuclear distribution determines the actual dipole. For charged species the dipole depends on the choice of origin, so neutral-molecule vector rules should not be transferred uncritically to polyatomic ions.

Worked examples: vector addition and units

Two hypothetical equal bond contributions of 1.50 D at 104.5° yield 3.00cos(52.25°) ≈ 1.84 D in the simplified model; at 180° the result would be zero. A charge separation of 0.20e across 0.10 nm gives (0.20 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)(1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 3.204 × 10⁻³⁰ C m ≈ 0.96 D. Neither calculation is a direct measurement of a real molecule's full electron density.

Polarity creates reactive regions in biomolecules. Hydroxyl oxygen is electron-rich; a carbonyl bond creates an electrophilic carbon and electron-rich oxygen. Amines can donate a lone pair or accept a proton, while amides are less basic because of lone-pair delocalisation. Carboxyl groups can ionise to resonance-stabilised carboxylates. Thiolate sulfur is an important biological nucleophile, and cysteine thiols can be oxidised to disulfides. Protonation depends on pH, pKₐ and local environment; these are treated in 1.A.03. Solubility is not determined by bond polarity alone. A polar head and long nonpolar chain can coexist in a lipid, and salt dissolution depends on lattice stability, solvation and entropy. These distinctions prepare us for water and molecular interactions in 1.A.02.

13. Resonance, delocalisation and biomolecular structure

Some measured bond lengths contradict a single localised Lewis drawing. Carbonate CO₃²⁻ contains 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24 valence electrons. One contributor has one double and two single C–O bonds with two formal negative charges on oxygen. Three equivalent drawings put the double bond at different oxygen sites. These are not three species rapidly interconverting. They are alternative representations of a single electronic state with delocalised electron density. For symmetric isolated carbonate, a simple formal average gives (2 + 1 + 1)/3 = 4/3 for each bond. This is a model-based bond order, not a universal experimental index. The equivalent bonds and trigonal planar geometry support delocalisation.

Acetate has two equivalent principal contributors and a formal average C–O bond order of 1.5. The negative charge is distributed over the carboxylate group. In a protein or metal site the two oxygens can experience unequal environments, so their bond lengths need not be perfectly identical. The neutral carboxylic acid has different hydroxyl and carbonyl oxygen environments. To draw resonance contributors, preserve nuclear positions, connectivity, total electron count and charge, moving electron pairs or pi electrons rather than atoms. Valid contributors also respect the relevant spin state. Contributors with complete second-row octets, less unnecessary charge separation and appropriate charge placement commonly have greater importance. Unequal contributors should not be averaged mechanically. A resonance arrow is not an equilibrium or reaction arrow.

A peptide linkage is an amide, O=C–NH. The nitrogen lone pair interacts with the neighbouring carbonyl pi system. One contributor has a neutral carbonyl and amine-like nitrogen; another has O⁻–C=N⁺H character. The peptide C–N bond has partial double-bond character, restricting rotation and favouring a near-planar peptide group. Rotation around adjacent backbone single bonds is still possible within steric limits. This chemistry is the foundation for phi and psi angles and Ramachandran plots. Amide nitrogen is less basic than an otherwise comparable amine because using its lone pair for protonation disrupts favourable delocalisation. Actual protonation sites depend on conditions.

Aromatic rings provide another example. Benzene has a continuous ring of overlapping p orbitals and six pi electrons; its C–C bonds are equivalent in the isolated molecule. Hückel's 4n + 2 rule applies to planar, cyclic, continuously conjugated systems with appropriate pi-electron counts, where n is a nonnegative integer. Six electrons satisfy n = 1, but a count of six alone does not guarantee aromaticity. Nucleic acid bases and aromatic amino acid side chains use conjugated electron systems, influencing planarity and ultraviolet absorption. A conjugated chain can exhibit delocalisation without aromaticity; detailed biomolecular structures follow in subunits 1.B and 1.C.

14. Molecular orbital theory and the oxygen paradox

Localised valence-bond and Lewis models are useful for connectivity and geometry. Molecular orbital theory instead combines atomic orbitals into states extending over multiple nuclei. Two 1s atomic orbitals yield a bonding combination with enhanced internuclear density and an antibonding combination with a node between nuclei. The two electrons of H₂ occupy the bonding sigma orbital with opposite spin. For an elementary MO diagram, bond order = (Nᵦ − Nₐ)/2, where Nᵦ counts bonding and Nₐ antibonding electrons; nonbonding electrons are excluded. H₂ has order 1, H₂⁺ order 0.5. A simple He₂ ground-state diagram fills both bonding and antibonding orbitals, giving zero ordinary covalent bond order. Weak helium dimers arising from dispersion are a distinct phenomenon.

Oxygen provides an experimental test. A single O=O Lewis diagram depicts paired electrons, yet oxygen is paramagnetic. The standard valence MO configuration for O₂ is σ2s² σ*2s² σ2p² π2p⁴ π*2p². Hund's rule distributes the two last electrons singly across degenerate antibonding pi orbitals. Eight bonding and four antibonding electrons give bond order 2; the two unpaired electrons explain paramagnetism. Core contributions cancel. The relative sigma and pi energy ordering can differ in lighter homonuclear diatomics, so this ordering is not a universal diagram.

Removing one electron from O₂ removes a pi antibonding electron, producing O₂⁺ with order 2.5 and one unpaired electron. Addition of one electron produces superoxide O₂⁻ with order 1.5 and one unpaired electron. Addition of two gives peroxide O₂²⁻ with order 1 and paired electrons in this elementary model. The series predicts a trend towards longer and weaker O–O bonds as antibonding occupation increases. Stability in cells depends on protonation, solvent and reaction partners as well. Redox biology later uses this reasoning to distinguish electron transfer from changes in nuclear identity.

15. Bringing the chemistry back to the cell

Return to the carbon atom in glucose. Its nucleus remains unchanged in ordinary metabolism while an enzyme reorganises its electronic environment and bonds. Electrophilic carbonyl carbon and nucleophilic oxygen, nitrogen or sulfur help explain reactions. Metal ions coordinate to donors without necessarily undergoing electron-transfer chemistry. Covalent linkages create connectivity; noncovalent interactions stabilise many possible conformations. Recognising the difference is necessary to understand molecular function rather than simply memorise structures.

A glycosidic linkage commonly includes a C–O–C connection; an ester contains C(=O)–O; peptide bonds are amides; a phosphodiester joins two oxygen-containing groups through phosphate; a disulfide contains an S–S covalent bond. A hydrogen bond between DNA bases is not the phosphodiester bond of the backbone. Protein denaturation commonly disrupts stabilising noncovalent interactions without hydrolysing peptide bonds. Disulfide formation is an oxidation of thiols, not hydrogen bonding. ATP hydrolysis is often called breaking a high-energy phosphate bond, but breaking a bond itself costs energy. Net reaction free energy includes new bonding, solvation, entropy, concentration and electronic redistribution. Thermodynamics and bioenergetics will examine those terms explicitly.

The measurements discussed here connect models to observations. Isotope mass differences can identify labelled atoms; spectroscopic transitions probe energy differences; diffraction constrains bond lengths and shapes; magnetism exposes unpaired electrons. Choose the right model for the question: Lewis accounting for electron count and formal charge, VSEPR for many main-group geometries, valence-bond theory for local overlap, MO theory for delocalisation and magnetic behaviour. No single simplified drawing answers every question. With these foundations established, water-dependent forces can be understood in the next node without mistaking them for changes in atomic identity or covalent structure.

16. Original CSIR-NET and SET reasoning practice

These questions are original practice, not reproduced official past questions. Use the reasoning before opening each answer.

Part B-style conceptual questions

1. Which has 16 neutrons and 18 electrons? A. ³¹P³⁻ B. ³²S C. ³⁵Cl⁻ D. ³⁹K⁺.

A. P has 15 protons, 16 neutrons and 18 electrons. B has 16 neutrons but 16 electrons; C has 18 neutrons; D has 20. This formal count does not imply free phosphide in water.

2. Which orbital has one radial and two angular nodes? A. 3d B. 4d C. 4p D. 3p.

B. l = 2, n − l − 1 = 1, so n = 4. 3d has zero radial nodes, 4p has two radial and one angular node, and 3p has one of each.

3. Free Fe³⁺ configuration? A. [Ar]3d³4s² B. [Ar]3d⁵ C. [Ar]3d⁶ D. [Ar]3d⁴4s¹.

B. Remove 4s² before one 3d electron from neutral Fe. A and D retain 4s; C is Fe²⁺. Formal d count does not by itself specify the spin of a ligand-bound ion.

4. For isolated NH₄⁺, which is correct? A. N retains a lone pair B. one bond is necessarily longer because it formed by donation C. tetrahedral and N formal charge +1 D. N oxidation state +1.

C. Four equivalent bonds use all eight valence electrons. No lone pair remains. The nitrogen oxidation state is −3, distinct from its +1 formal charge.

5. Which molecule contains polar bonds but has zero permanent dipole by symmetry? A. H₂O B. NH₃ C. CO₂ D. CH₃Cl.

C. Equal opposite bond vectors cancel in linear CO₂. Bent, pyramidal and unsymmetrically substituted tetrahedral structures do not have this cancellation.

6. Why is an amide linkage approximately planar? A. purely ionic C–N B. tetrahedral nitrogen C. partial double-bond character from delocalisation D. absence of a C–N sigma bond.

C. The nitrogen lone pair interacts with the carbonyl pi system. The covalent sigma bond remains, and the nitrogen environment is approximately planar.

7. Which simple MO prediction applies to superoxide? A. order 2.5, no unpaired electrons B. order 1.5, one unpaired electron C. order 1, two unpaired electrons D. order 2, two unpaired electrons.

B. Addition occupies pi antibonding, decreases order by 0.5 and pairs one electron. D describes neutral oxygen. C gives peroxide order but wrong spin; A does not describe superoxide.

8. Which statement is correct? A. formal charge always equals partial charge B. formal charge assigns both bond electrons to the more electronegative atom C. oxidation state and formal charge are different conventions D. both change nuclear identity.

C. Formal charge equally divides bond electrons; oxidation state assigns them ionically. Partial charge reflects an electronic distribution model. No nuclear change occurs.

Part C-style calculations and interpretation

9. Three isotopes of masses 24, 25 and 26 Da have fractions 0.79, 0.10 and 0.11. Find mean mass and the effect of shifting 0.04 fraction from mass 24 to 26.

24(0.79)+25(0.10)+26(0.11) = 24.32 Da. Replacement adds 0.04(26−24) = 0.08 Da, giving 24.40 Da. Nuclear proton number is unchanged. The masses are rounded illustrative values.

10. Calculate the wavelength emitted by He⁺ for n = 4 → 2, and compare hydrogen.

|ΔE| = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 4(1/4 − 1/16) = 1.635 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. λ = hc/E ≈ 1.215 × 10⁻⁷ m = 121.5 nm. Hydrogen has one quarter the transition energy and four times the wavelength, about 486 nm. Neutral helium is not a one-electron ion.

11. Two equal bond dipoles of 2 D have resultant 2 D. Find their angle in the simplified model.

2 = 4cos(θ/2), so θ/2 = 60°, θ = 120°. This does not uniquely determine the full molecular geometry; additional electron density and atoms can contribute.

12. Hypothetical carboxylate bond lengths are 1.25 and 1.26 Å, while neutral acid lengths are 1.21 and 1.35 Å. Interpret.

The near-equivalent carboxylate lengths support delocalisation, whereas acid lengths support differentiated carbonyl and hydroxyl bonds. Small differences may reflect asymmetric surroundings. A bond length is not a direct measure of formal charge. 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m; data are illustrative.

13. Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ both have ten electrons. Why can they behave differently in a protein?

Mg²⁺ has twelve protons versus eleven in Na⁺. It has a generally more contracted bare electron cloud, higher charge density and stronger interactions with many oxygen donors. Hydration and protein geometry modify selectivity. Isoelectronic does not mean chemically identical.

14. Does coordination of a neutral bidentate ligand to iron necessarily change its oxidation state?

No. Two donor atoms may occupy two coordination sites without formal electron transfer. Coordination number, spin state and oxidation state are separate descriptors. The ligand may affect orbital splitting without changing formal oxidation state.

15. Assertion: breaking a high-energy ATP bond alone releases energy. Reason: dissociation of a stable bond costs energy. Assess.

Assertion false, reason true for the corresponding dissociation. Hydrolysis involves formation of new bonds and all thermodynamic contributions; a favourable reaction is not caused by releasing energy solely through bond cleavage.

16. Count sigma and pi bonds in CH₃–C≡C–CH=CH₂ and assign local carbon hybridisation.

Four C–C sigma plus six C–H sigma = ten sigma. Triple contributes two pi and double one, total three pi. Carbon labels left to right sp³, sp, sp, sp², sp². A multiple bond is one VSEPR domain.

17. Consolidation and the next learning step

For atomic problems, begin by separating Z, A and ionic charge; then use allowed quantum numbers, orbital capacity, electron filling and ionisation order. Explain periodic trends through nuclear attraction, shielding and penetration. For molecular problems, count valence electrons, draw a plausible structure, check formal charges, examine resonance, count domains and predict shape. Only then assess molecular polarity. When Lewis theory cannot explain a property such as oxygen magnetism, examine molecular orbital occupation. When dealing with a metal centre, distinguish ligand coordination from redox. Each step builds on the previous one.

Atomic structure explains the capacity for chemical bonding; bonding explains molecular connectivity and shape; geometry and electron distribution explain many reactive and interactive properties. The next chapter, 1.A.02 — Water and Molecular Stabilising Interactions, considers the environment in which these molecules operate, including hydration, electrostatic screening, hydrogen bonding, dispersion and hydrophobic association. These are the next conceptual layer, not a replacement for covalent structure.
