1. Sound as a mechanical stimulus

Hearing begins before a receptor cell encounters a stimulus. A vibrating object alternately pushes and withdraws against the surrounding medium. When a loudspeaker diaphragm moves forward, it compresses nearby air; when it retreats, the pressure in that region falls. The resulting local disturbance causes neighbouring molecules to move and transfer energy to their neighbours. The disturbance propagates outward even though individual air molecules generally oscillate near their equilibrium positions rather than travelling from the speaker to the listener. This distinction between energy propagation and bulk transport of matter is fundamental to understanding the mechanical events in the ear.

For a simple sinusoidal sound, the pressure at a fixed position fluctuates periodically about atmospheric pressure. Frequency, measured in hertz, specifies the number of cycles per second. Period is the time required for one complete cycle, so T = 1/f. The wavelength is the spatial distance between equivalent points of successive cycles. The propagation speed c equals frequency multiplied by wavelength: c = fλ. These relationships concern an ideal travelling sound wave and do not assert that every mechanical structure excited by sound has the same wavelength or propagation velocity. The slowly travelling displacement of the cochlear partition must be distinguished from the rapid propagation of a compressional pressure wave in cochlear fluid.

In room-temperature air the approximate speed of sound is 343 metres per second. A 1,000 Hz tone therefore has a wavelength of 0.343 m, whereas a 250 Hz tone has a wavelength of 1.372 m. A sound transmitted across an interface generally retains its source frequency, while wavelength changes if the speed changes. This is why an air-to-fluid transition does not automatically convert a low-pitched tone into a high-pitched tone. The mechanical filtering of an ear, however, can change the relative amplitudes of components of a complex sound.

Pressure, force and wave geometry

Amplitude describes the size of an oscillation; pressure amplitude is measured in pascals. For sinusoidal pressure p(t) = Ppeak sin(2πft), the root-mean-square pressure is Ppeak divided by the square root of two. Root-mean-square pressure provides a convenient measure of effective oscillatory pressure even though the mean signed pressure perturbation over a complete cycle is zero. Intensity, by contrast, describes time-averaged acoustic power passing through a unit area, measured in watts per square metre. For a progressive plane wave in a uniform medium, intensity I = prms²/(ρc), where ρ is the equilibrium density of the medium and c its sound speed. This equation does not apply without qualification to an arbitrary standing wave or near-field acoustic condition.

Pitch is the perceptual attribute associated with frequency; it is not identical to frequency. A complex periodic sound can produce a pitch corresponding to a fundamental frequency even when the spectral component at that fundamental is weak or absent. Similarly, loudness is not simply acoustic intensity: sensitivity varies with frequency, duration and listener. Two sounds of the same physical intensity can have unequal perceived loudness. The auditory apparatus initially deals with physical variables; perception requires subsequent neural processing.

Natural sounds are rarely pure sinusoids. A vocalisation contains multiple frequency components that vary over time. The relative amplitudes and phases of these components determine its waveform. The cochlea creates a spatially distributed mechanical representation of frequency content, although this representation is neither a perfect mathematical Fourier transform nor sufficient by itself to account for all auditory perception. The first major task of the ear is to collect sufficient energy from the environment; the second is to distribute that energy appropriately to specialised receptor cells.

A useful way to connect the mathematical definition of pressure with the biological stimulus is to imagine a small area on the tympanic membrane. Pressure is force divided by area. An excess pressure of 1 Pa acting uniformly over an area of one square centimetre produces a force of 0.0001 N. The calculation is elementary, but the distinction between force and pressure is indispensable: a larger exposed area experiences a greater net force for the same pressure difference. The membrane's shape, attachment and displacement determine how much of that force drives the ossicles. The pressure is not itself a displacement and does not determine a displacement without information about the mechanical system. A stiff membrane and a compliant membrane subjected to identical pressure differences can move by very different amounts.

Sound in ordinary air is principally a longitudinal mechanical wave. The particle velocity is parallel to the direction of propagation for a simple plane wave. This statement should be distinguished from the motion of a membrane that vibrates transversely. An airborne longitudinal sound wave may drive a transverse displacement of the tympanic membrane, which then drives rotational and translational motions of the ossicles. It is not necessary for every mechanical component to move in the direction that the external wave travels. The orientation of displacement changes as energy is transferred through interfaces. Thus a diagram showing a longitudinal direction of sound propagation outside the ear and a vertical deflection of the basilar membrane inside the cochlea is entirely consistent.

For a sinusoidal acoustic pressure waveform it is helpful to add spatial information. One possible expression is p(x,t) = P sin(2πft − 2πx/λ), where x is position along the direction of propagation in metres, t is time in seconds, f is frequency in hertz and P is the peak pressure perturbation in pascals. At a fixed x the waveform oscillates with period T; at a fixed t it repeats in space over distance λ. If x increases by one quarter of a wavelength, phase changes by one quarter of a cycle, or 90 degrees. For a 1 kHz tone, a quarter cycle occupies 0.25 milliseconds. The expression is an idealised model, not a full description of acoustic reflections inside the ear canal or the displacement of a coupled biological membrane.

Consider a sound that starts abruptly and lasts only two milliseconds at 500 Hz. Its nominal period is also two milliseconds, so the stimulus contains about one complete cycle. In contrast, a two-second tone at that frequency contains a thousand cycles. Both have the same nominal oscillation rate during stimulation, but their frequency content is not equally narrow. A sharply truncated signal contains additional frequency components because its onset and offset are rapid. This is biologically important: an auditory organ must convey the timing of short transient events as well as the frequency content of sustained sounds. The duration of a signal can influence how clearly its pitch is perceived. Mechanical frequency selectivity does not mean that the cochlea waits indefinitely for a mathematically perfect periodic tone.

The source frequency can often be understood through the physical mechanism that produces vibration. A stretched string, air column or vocal fold has mechanical properties that determine its tendency to oscillate. Animal calls may change their frequency content over time as the sound-producing structures change tension, length or airflow. The auditory organ encounters the resulting waveform rather than receiving direct information about the source anatomy. Multiple sources can produce overlapping waves; their pressures at a given position combine by superposition when sound amplitudes are in the ordinary linear acoustic range. The ear must therefore process a combined stimulus. Its mechanical frequency separation helps distinguish components, while later processing is needed to infer which components belong together.

A wave that spreads from a compact source in an ideal unbounded medium distributes its power over a progressively larger surface. If the source radiates equally in all directions and absorbs negligible energy during propagation, average intensity at radius r is Ptotal/(4πr²). The variable Ptotal is acoustic power in watts, and r is distance in metres. This is an ideal free-field spherical spreading model, not a guarantee about sound in a room, near a large source or close to reflecting boundaries. Doubling distance reduces intensity to one quarter and therefore lowers intensity level by about 6 dB. In a far-field plane-wave approximation the corresponding rms pressure is halved. An animal moving farther from a caller receives a weaker stimulus even if the caller maintains the same source frequency and power. The biological detection problem becomes one of sensitivity and distinguishing the signal from environmental noise.

The propagation speed in air changes with temperature. Warmer air generally supports a greater sound speed, so a fixed-frequency sound has a somewhat longer wavelength in warmer air. In water, sound usually propagates much faster than in air. A 1 kHz tone travelling at an illustrative speed of 1,500 m s⁻¹ has a wavelength of 1.5 m, compared with approximately 0.343 m in room-temperature air. This changes spatial relationships among an animal's auditory structures and the incoming wave. It does not imply that the animal's auditory system has to translate one thousand cycles per second into some different frequency simply because the sound is underwater. The receptor detects the local consequences of the wave's interaction with anatomy.

There is also a useful distinction between the size of an environmental wavelength and the dimensions of a sensory organ. A human cochlea is far shorter than the wavelength of a low-frequency sound in air. It can nonetheless analyse such frequencies because the physical mechanism is not the formation of an external air wave of identical wavelength along the cochlea. Instead, stapes motion drives a coupled fluid and membrane system whose mechanical properties determine the spatial distribution of displacement. Confusing the wavelength of the incident airborne pressure wave with the spatial pattern of cochlear partition displacement leads to the false conclusion that a cochlea is too small to detect long-wavelength sounds. The relevant dimensions are those of the driven mechanical system, not simply the wavelength of the input in another medium.

2. Quantifying acoustic intensity and pressure

Acoustic intensity covers a wide range, making a logarithmic scale useful. An intensity level is defined as L_I = 10 log10(I/I0) decibels, with the conventional reference intensity I0 = 10⁻¹² W m⁻². The ratio is dimensionless. The reference is a conventional standard, not an assertion that every person hears every frequency at that intensity. Zero decibels therefore does not mean zero physical sound. Negative intensity levels can occur when the measured intensity is below the reference.

Sound pressure level is defined as L_p = 20 log10(prms/p0), where the conventional airborne reference p0 = 20 µPa. The factor of twenty follows from the square relationship between pressure and intensity for progressive waves in a fixed medium: if pressure doubles, intensity increases fourfold, and 10 log10(4) equals 20 log10(2), approximately 6.02 dB. The two expressions must not be interchanged casually. A tenfold increase in intensity raises intensity level by ten decibels, whereas a tenfold increase in root-mean-square pressure raises sound pressure level by twenty decibels.

Consider two unrelated noise sources producing equal intensities at a measurement position. If their pressure fluctuations are uncorrelated, their time-averaged intensities add. The combined intensity is twice the intensity of one source, and the level is about 3.01 dB higher. In contrast, two perfectly coherent equal-amplitude waves arriving in phase produce twice the pressure amplitude and four times the intensity under the plane-wave assumption. If they arrive in opposite phase they can interfere destructively. Adding the two decibel numbers would be incorrect in all three cases.

For a worked example, suppose root-mean-square pressure increases from 0.002 Pa to 0.020 Pa, with no change in reference. The first sound has p/p0 = 100 and therefore Lp = 40 dB SPL. The second has p/p0 = 1,000 and Lp = 60 dB SPL. The pressure ratio is ten, and the intensity ratio is one hundred if the medium and propagation conditions remain the same. A student should identify the quantity being compared before selecting the logarithmic multiplier.

Sound pressure reference conventions depend on the medium. Underwater acoustics commonly uses a reference pressure of one micropascal rather than twenty micropascals. Consequently equal numerical sound pressure levels quoted in air and water do not necessarily correspond to equal absolute pressures, equal energy flux or equal biological stimulation. When comparing auditory adaptations of fish and terrestrial animals, the physical stimulus and reference must both be specified. This prevents an apparently quantitative comparison from becoming a comparison of incompatible conventions.

Using levels in quantitative comparisons

The logarithm used in a decibel calculation has a clear mathematical purpose. Ratios that multiply in a series of transmission steps can be represented by level differences that add. Suppose a simplified external filter changes pressure by a factor of two and a following mechanical stage changes the resulting pressure by a factor of five, with compatible pressure definitions at the measured locations. The combined pressure ratio is ten. The contributions are approximately 6.02 dB and 13.98 dB, giving a combined pressure-level difference of 20 dB. This is legitimate because the levels express successive ratios of the same physical quantity. It differs fundamentally from adding the levels of two simultaneously present, independent sound sources. In that second case one must return to linear intensities before summing.

For example, two independent sounds of intensity levels 60 and 70 dB relative to the same reference have intensities 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁵ W m⁻². Their combined intensity is 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ W m⁻². The combined level is 10 log10(1.1 × 10⁷), or about 70.41 dB. The stronger source dominates because its intensity is ten times greater. Even though 60 and 70 are close as written numbers, their physical intensities differ substantially. This reasoning helps when an animal detects a faint signal in a louder background, although a real auditory system can distinguish sounds that differ in frequency or timing even when total intensity is dominated by noise.

A decibel reading is meaningless without its reference or measurement convention. Sound pressure level is a relative physical pressure measure, while hearing level used in audiometric contexts employs frequency-dependent reference values derived from typical hearing thresholds for standard equipment and conditions. They are not interchangeable. For instance, a threshold expressed as zero decibels hearing level does not imply an absolute airborne rms pressure of 20 µPa at every test frequency. The distinction illustrates the difference between a standardised biological sensitivity measure and a purely acoustic measure. Detailed clinical testing is beyond the present chapter, but the terminology prevents common interpretive errors when reading experimental threshold graphs.

For a simple numerical conversion, take a progressive plane wave with rms pressure of 0.2 Pa in air and approximate specific acoustic impedance of 412 Pa s m⁻¹. Its intensity is (0.2 Pa)² divided by 412 Pa s m⁻¹, giving approximately 9.7 × 10⁻⁵ W m⁻². The pressure level is 20 log10(0.2/0.000020) = 80 dB SPL, whereas the intensity level relative to 10⁻¹² W m⁻² is approximately 79.9 dB. The small difference comes from the approximate characteristic impedance and the conventional reference choices. A student must not conclude that a pressure measurement always yields an identical intensity level; that correspondence depends on the propagation conditions and reference standards.

The dynamic range of sound creates a physiological challenge. A threshold-level displacement must be large enough to evoke a detectable receptor response, whereas more intense sound can drive larger motion and engage nonlinear mechanisms. A purely linear amplifier designed to enhance every tiny input by the same enormous factor would risk excessive output at high input levels. In the living cochlea, amplification is particularly prominent for weak inputs, and the growth of near-characteristic-frequency mechanical response can be compressive over part of its operating range. The pressure-level calculations describe input stimulus magnitude; they do not by themselves specify the magnitude of receptor displacement or neural firing. Those require measurements or a biological model of the system's response.

3. Comparative solutions to sound detection

The sensory problem is shared across animal groups, but the anatomical solution differs. Sound can be detected through fluctuations of pressure, displacement of a body surface or the oscillatory motion of particles in a medium. The relevant stimulus depends on the receptor and how the animal couples its environment to that receptor. A mammalian external ear, three ossicles and coiled cochlea represent one evolutionary solution, not the definition of an auditory organ.

Many insects use specialised chordotonal sensory organs containing mechanosensory neurons. In tympanal organs, an external thin membrane responds to sound, and mechanical coupling transfers movement to the receptor-bearing structure. Tympanal organs occur in different anatomical positions in different insect lineages. For example, the foreleg tibiae of many crickets contain tympanal organs, whereas many moths detect ultrasound with tympanal organs elsewhere on the body. Such sensitivity may help an animal respond to communication signals or the approach of predators. A tympanal membrane in an insect is functionally comparable to a vibrating sound-collecting membrane but is not part of a mammalian ossicular chain.

Other insects detect particle motion through mobile antennae or sensory hairs. A mosquito antenna can respond to air particle motion, with the Johnston's organ in the antennal pedicel detecting the resulting movement. This provides a contrasting example to a pressure-sensitive tympanum. Mechanical displacement must reach the sensory structure for transduction, and the geometry of the coupling apparatus determines which range of signals drives that structure effectively.

Vertebrate auditory organs typically use hair cells as specialised mechanoreceptors. In many fishes, dense otoliths move differently from the surrounding body tissues during particle motion. Relative motion between the otolith-associated structures and the sensory epithelia deflects receptor bundles. In some fish, gas-filled structures such as the swim bladder improve sensitivity to pressure fluctuations by converting them into mechanical motion that reaches the inner ear. Certain fishes have additional anatomical specialisations that enhance such coupling. It would nevertheless be wrong to assume every fish relies on the same accessory pressure-detection mechanism.

Aquatic hearing and otolith motion

Terrestrial vertebrates face a different transfer problem because their sensory epithelia are situated in fluid-filled internal spaces while airborne sounds propagate through a low-impedance gas. Many amphibians and reptiles and birds possess a middle-ear apparatus involving a columella or stapes, whereas mammals have a three-ossicle chain. Mammalian hair cells are organised in the organ of Corti; other vertebrate auditory sensory epithelia do not all possess an identical cochlear architecture. These differences illustrate how mechanical coupling and frequency selectivity can evolve without requiring the same structures in every lineage.

Comparison is most informative when the same functional questions are applied to each group. First, what aspect of a sound field causes motion of the detector? Second, what anatomical structure couples the stimulus to the receptor? Third, is the receptor a mechanosensory neuron or a separate specialised sensory cell? Finally, how can different frequencies be distinguished? These questions reveal functional analogies without presupposing anatomical homology. For an insect tympanal ear, an oscillating membrane transfers movement to a chordotonal receptor organ. For a mammal, the tympanic membrane drives a middle-ear chain and the ultimate mechanosensory cells lie within the cochlea. The similar name of the first membrane does not imply that the entire chain of structures evolved as a single shared auditory system.

A chordotonal organ consists of mechanosensory units associated with accessory cells and attachments that transmit mechanical strain. These organs are also involved in proprioception in many insects, for instance in detecting movements or positions of joints. The ability to respond to periodic mechanical motion makes such receptors suitable starting points for the evolution of auditory functions. A sound-detecting structure must provide coupling and tuning appropriate to the relevant sound field; the presence of a mechanoreceptor alone does not guarantee that airborne sound is detected efficiently. This reasoning links sensory physiology to adaptive evolution without requiring a detailed developmental or genetic account of every insect auditory organ.

The cricket provides a useful comparison with a mammalian pinna. Its tympanal structures are located in the forelegs rather than beside the brain. This placement is not inherently disadvantageous because the important criterion is whether sound-driven motion reaches the receptors, not how close the membrane is to a central nervous system. Signals can travel through sensory axons from the legs to neural centres. The arrangement also permits comparisons between sound inputs on the two sides of the animal. The mechanics of insect directional hearing can involve internally connected air spaces and are not simply miniature versions of human outer ears. At the present level, the important principle is that position and mechanical coupling can contribute to direction sensitivity before central integration.

In some insects, the frequency band of the receptor system corresponds to the biological signals most relevant to survival or reproduction. The acoustic structures of a species responding to the calls of conspecifics may have a different mechanical response from those of a species sensitive to ultrasonic cues. Sensitivity to ultrasound should not be interpreted as a universal property of insects. Likewise, absence of a mammalian cochlea does not imply absence of frequency discrimination. The mechanical resonances of a membrane, the arrangement of receptor units and subsequent neural processing may all contribute to discrimination. The relevant question is whether differences in the stimulus generate distinguishable patterns of receptor activity.

In fish, consider a body immersed in water undergoing sound-induced particle oscillation. Much of the body's soft tissue moves with the surrounding medium, but the dense otolith has different inertial properties. As a consequence the otolith can move relative to the sensory epithelium. Hair bundles respond to the relative motion rather than simply to the absolute displacement of the entire fish. This is analogous to other inertial sensory arrangements, in which a structure with different mechanical properties from its surroundings produces a local deformation. An observer who measures only the displacement of the fish's body may therefore miss the mechanically relevant stimulus at the receptor.

A gas-filled swim bladder changes volume in response to fluctuating pressure more readily than surrounding water-rich tissues. Its movement can act as a secondary source of mechanical motion within the body. In certain teleosts such as goldfish, the Weberian apparatus provides a specialised anatomical linkage between the swim bladder and the inner ear. Removing or impairing this coupling is predicted to affect sensitivity to pressure-mediated signals more strongly than the underlying ability of otolithic organs to respond to particle motion. However, such predictions must account for the detailed acoustic conditions and species under study. Different fishes possess different connections, and some lack a gas-filled accessory structure altogether.

An illustrative experimental design compares the responses of a fish with an accessory gas-filled structure to those of another species without such a structure. Investigators must measure or control both particle motion and pressure in the experimental sound field. A tank can produce reflections and spatial variations that disrupt the simple proportionality between pressure and particle velocity expected for a far-field progressive wave. If only pressure is measured, apparent differences in sensitivity may reflect differences in the local particle-motion stimulus rather than inherent differences in receptor biology. This example highlights a general principle of sensory physiology: the physical stimulus should be measured as close as practicable to the receptor-relevant variable, not merely according to the settings of the stimulus generator.

Across tetrapods, the middle ear is especially informative as an adaptation to airborne sound. A bird or many reptiles may use a single principal ossicle to transfer tympanic motion to the inner ear. Mammals use three ossicles, with the malleus and incus providing additional mechanical coupling. The number of ossicles is a structural distinction; it cannot by itself establish which species has superior hearing at all frequencies. Acoustic ecology, body size, cochlear or auditory epithelial properties and neural processing also influence sensitivity. Comparative anatomy should explain the relation between a structure and its function, not become a ranking of animals according to anatomical complexity.

4. External ear: collecting and filtering sound

The external ear of a typical human consists of the auricle or pinna and the external acoustic meatus, commonly called the auditory canal. The pinna collects incoming sound and modifies the frequency spectrum reaching the canal. Its folds reflect and diffract incoming waves in a direction-dependent manner, providing spectral information useful for distinguishing sound sources at different elevations and for resolving some front-back ambiguities. This is a mechanical prerequisite to later neural analysis, not a complete mechanism of localisation.

The auditory canal terminates at the tympanic membrane. Its skin, hairs and ceruminous secretions contribute to protecting the passage. The canal behaves in part like a tube that is open at one end and acoustically terminated at the other. A simple quarter-wavelength approximation predicts a first resonance near c/(4L). With a nominal effective length of 0.025 m and speed of 343 m s⁻¹, the estimated frequency is approximately 3,430 Hz. A real canal differs from an ideal uniform tube because its cross-section is irregular, its termination is compliant and its entrance has an end correction. Consequently the calculation demonstrates a principle rather than predicting an exact resonance for every human ear.

The tympanic membrane separates the external canal from the air-containing middle-ear cavity. It is a thin, mechanically supported structure rather than an active contractile sound generator. An incoming pressure fluctuation creates a force distribution across its surface, and membrane motion transfers energy to the attached handle of the malleus. The two sides of the membrane need not experience identical instantaneous sound pressure; it is the pressure difference and the mechanical load that drive movement.

In addition to responding to acoustic fluctuations, the tympanic membrane is affected by slower differences in static pressure between the canal and middle-ear cavity. A large sustained pressure difference shifts the membrane from its normal operating position and can alter its mobility. Therefore normal hearing depends not only on an intact vibrating membrane but also on the condition of the air space behind it. The auditory tube provides a mechanism for restoring middle-ear pressure toward ambient pressure.

Damage to the external conducting structures reduces the mechanical input reaching the cochlea without necessarily damaging receptor cells. This illustrates why an animal can retain functional sensory transduction yet have impaired responses to environmental sounds: the stimulus may fail to reach the sensory surface at sufficient amplitude.

Resonance, spectral filtering and measurement

The auricle can be considered an acoustic collector whose effects depend on the wavelength and direction of incoming sound. Long-wavelength sounds diffract readily around structures of the dimensions of the pinna, whereas shorter-wavelength components interact more strongly with its ridges and cavities. Reflected components can combine constructively at some frequencies and destructively at others. The resulting spectral pattern reaching the tympanic membrane contains information about direction. The spectrum depends on anatomy, so a generic transfer function does not describe every individual's external-ear response. At the level of this chapter, one need only distinguish such mechanical spectral filtering from the neural mechanisms that interpret binaural and monaural cues.

The external canal is not merely a hollow tube through which a stimulus travels without change. In a simplified tube with an approximately pressure-release open end and a rigid closed end, the first resonance corresponds to a quarter of a wavelength because the pressure oscillation has a minimum near the opening and a maximum near the closed end. Setting L ≈ λ/4 and substituting c = fλ gives f ≈ c/(4L). A shorter effective canal has a higher predicted first resonance under the same assumptions. If effective length is reduced from 25 to 20 mm, the predicted resonance increases from about 3.43 to 4.29 kHz. In the real ear, the tympanic membrane is not perfectly rigid, and losses and geometry make the response broader and more complex than the ideal model.

This example also illustrates a distinction between resonance and energy production. At a resonant frequency, repeated excitation can maintain a relatively large oscillation because of the relationship between the drive and the mechanical response. A passive external ear can enhance sound pressure at the tympanic membrane compared with the incident pressure measured at a reference location. It does so by collecting and redistributing acoustic energy, not by creating new power. A frequency-selective enhancement does not guarantee that the same frequency is perceived as loudest, because the subsequent middle-ear, cochlear and neural responses also affect hearing sensitivity.

The tympanic membrane is held within a supporting annular region. Most of its area comprises the relatively taut pars tensa, while a smaller pars flaccida region has different mechanical properties. The handle of the malleus is attached to the membrane and follows its motion. The resulting vibration can involve complex patterns rather than uniform piston motion, especially as frequency increases. The concept of an effective area in the ideal middle-ear model refers to the portion of the membrane's motion that contributes to mechanical transfer in the simplified description. It should not be mistaken for the area of a flat circular membrane calculated from its outer diameter.

One can test the function of an upstream mechanical element by holding the acoustic stimulus constant and measuring an immediately downstream response. Suppose ear-canal pressure is constant across two conditions, but measured malleus displacement falls after the membrane becomes stiffer. The result supports a reduction in pressure-to-motion coupling at the membrane or its load. It does not directly establish hair-cell damage. To distinguish a change in the membrane from an altered ossicular load, a second measurement such as stapes velocity or the pressure dependence of motion is needed. Such experimental reasoning is central to understanding the auditory system as a series of mechanically connected stages.

5. Middle-ear mechanics and impedance matching

The middle ear houses the malleus, incus and stapes. The malleus is coupled to the tympanic membrane, the incus connects the malleus to the stapes, and the footplate of the stapes occupies the oval window of the inner ear. Their articulations and supporting ligaments permit sound-driven movements while maintaining anatomical alignment. The chain mechanically couples pressure-driven motion of a relatively large membrane to motion of a smaller interface with cochlear fluid.

Why is a coupling system needed? An acoustic medium presents an opposition to oscillatory motion. For a progressive plane wave, its characteristic specific acoustic impedance is Z = ρc, with units Pa s m⁻¹. The characteristic impedance of water-like fluid is much greater than that of air because both density and wave propagation properties differ. At an ideal boundary between two lossless, uniform media with normal incidence, the reflected intensity fraction is R = [(Z2 − Z1)/(Z2 + Z1)]². If Z2 is much greater than Z1, R approaches one, and only a small fraction of incident power enters the second medium. The ear is not a plane-wave interface, but this elementary relation identifies the physical reason that direct coupling from air into fluid is inefficient.

The middle ear helps reduce this transfer mismatch. A simplified transformer model uses the effective vibrating area of the tympanic membrane and the smaller area of the stapes footplate. If pressure p1 acts over effective area A1, the associated force is p1A1. With an ideal ossicular lever force ratio r, the output force is r p1A1, and the output pressure is approximately p2 = r p1 A1/A2. Take illustrative effective areas of 55 and 3.2 square millimetres and a lever ratio of 1.3. The area ratio is about 17.2, and multiplication by the lever ratio gives a pressure ratio of about 22.3. Expressed as a pressure-level ratio, this is about 27 dB. These are illustrative nominal values, not a universal measured transfer function.

Higher pressure does not imply that the passive ossicles create energy. In an ideal lever, a gain in force is accompanied by a reduction in displacement for a given input movement. Similarly, concentrating force over a smaller area raises pressure, while the corresponding volume displacement depends on the smaller moving surface. Mechanical work depends on force multiplied by displacement, and average power depends on the relationship between force and velocity. The tympanic membrane and ossicular chain can improve the fraction of available acoustic energy delivered to the inner ear without violating conservation of energy. Living cochlear amplification, which uses cellular energy, is a separate phenomenon.

Real middle-ear transfer depends on frequency because masses, elastic supports, air cavities and damping all contribute to its mechanical response. At relatively low frequencies the stiffness of the system may restrict displacement; at high frequencies inertial effects and complex structural motion become important. A single static area ratio cannot predict the full frequency-dependent pressure or velocity response. Experimental measurements of stapes velocity relative to ear-canal sound pressure can reveal this transfer function more directly than a simple anatomical estimate.

The Eustachian or pharyngotympanic tube connects the middle ear with the nasopharynx. It is typically closed for much of the time but opens transiently, particularly with swallowing, allowing pressure equilibration. Its ordinary function is not to convey the external auditory stimulus into the cochlea. Fluid accumulation in the middle ear or poor pressure regulation can impair the movement of the tympanic membrane and ossicles. In either case the transmission problem lies upstream of the sensory epithelium.

Frequency-dependent loading and regulation

The stapedius and tensor tympani muscles alter mechanical properties of the middle-ear chain when activated. In humans the stapedius is the principal effector of the ordinary sound-evoked acoustic reflex. The reflex can attenuate some transmitted sounds, particularly lower frequencies, but its latency means that it cannot reliably protect against every abrupt intense impulse. Its existence shows that even a predominantly mechanical transmission apparatus is subject to neural regulation.

The ossicles can be understood as a linked system of moving bodies rather than three independent conductors of sound. The malleus receives motion from the tympanic membrane, and its mechanical linkage to the incus permits the chain to transfer that motion toward the stapes. The stapes footplate is supported at the oval window by an annular ligament, allowing motion relative to the surrounding bony boundary. In the simplest low-frequency mechanical approximation the linked ossicles rotate about an effective axis and the footplate moves approximately like a piston. At other frequencies, rotations and additional modes of motion contribute. An anatomical diagram tells the reader which elements are connected, while a mechanical model predicts how they move under specified assumptions. Neither should be mistaken for a direct measurement of the full response.

The lever principle can be derived by balancing torques about an ideal pivot. If an input force F1 acts at perpendicular distance l1 from the pivot and output force F2 acts at distance l2, then in a static lossless approximation F1 l1 = F2 l2. The force ratio is l1/l2. An angular displacement θ produces approximate arc displacements x1 = l1θ and x2 = l2θ for small rotations. Consequently the output displacement ratio is x2/x1 = l2/l1, the inverse of the force ratio. Multiplying force and displacement demonstrates that the ideal input and output work are equal. This derivation is helpful because it identifies the physical tradeoff behind the commonly quoted lever advantage. In the moving middle ear, inertial forces, elastic forces, and energy dissipated through damping mean that the simple equality is only a starting approximation.

Effective area introduces a second transformation. Imagine an input pressure of 0.1 Pa acting uniformly over an effective membrane area of 55 mm². Converting the area to square metres gives 55 × 10⁻⁶ m², so the estimated force is 5.5 × 10⁻⁶ N. Multiplying by a nominal force ratio of 1.3 gives an estimated output force of 7.15 × 10⁻⁶ N. If this force acts normally over a footplate of 3.2 mm², the output pressure is about 2.23 Pa. The output-to-input pressure ratio is about 22.3. These calculations deliberately omit the frequency-dependent behaviour of real tissue and fluid loading. Their purpose is to show how anatomical dimensions and mechanical leverage can produce a much larger pressure at a smaller surface.

Notice that the pressure gain can be calculated without knowing the actual frequency in the ideal area-lever model, whereas a realistic displacement response cannot. A question that specifies an ideal lever and uniform areas invites a direct ratio calculation. A question that describes an increase in the stiffness of an ossicular joint and asks for the resulting high-frequency transfer cannot be answered from those area ratios alone. The frequency response depends on how that change interacts with the masses and damping of the chain. A good physiological explanation distinguishes a simple ideal estimate from a prediction that would require measurements or further assumptions.

The impedance mismatch can also be quantified approximately. Take characteristic impedances of 412 Pa s m⁻¹ for air and 1.5 × 10⁶ Pa s m⁻¹ for water. The ideal normal-incidence intensity transmission coefficient is 4Z1Z2/(Z1+Z2)², the complement of the reflected fraction for two lossless media. With these illustrative values, the transmitted fraction is about 0.00110, or 0.11 percent. The remaining incident energy is reflected in the ideal model. A direct interface therefore poses a severe transfer problem. This estimate should not be used as the measured hearing loss of an animal without ossicles, because its anatomy provides other coupling routes and its actual boundary conditions differ from the model.

An important nuance is that pressure amplitude can increase at a high-impedance boundary even if relatively little power is transmitted. Incident and reflected pressure waves can add at the boundary, producing a substantial total pressure, but the transmitted particle velocity may remain small. For a progressive wave, power flux depends on both pressure and particle motion; a high pressure in a nearly immobile medium does not guarantee large acoustic power. This is why the middle ear should be described as improving energy transfer into a mechanical load, rather than simply making pressure as large as possible. The output pressure must be accompanied by appropriate motion of the footplate and cochlear fluid.

A frequency-dependent system can be understood qualitatively using the forces on a driven mass attached to an elastic element. For harmonic displacement of peak magnitude X at angular frequency ω, the magnitude of an elastic force is proportional to kX, an inertial force to mω²X, and a viscous damping force to bωX. Here k is stiffness in N m⁻¹, m is effective mass in kg and b is a damping coefficient in N s m⁻¹. Their relative phases differ. At low frequencies, elastic properties can dominate the relationship between applied force and displacement; as frequency increases the inertial contribution becomes larger. Damping dissipates energy and limits excessive resonant responses. The middle ear has multiple masses, joints and cavities, so this elementary one-degree-of-freedom model is useful for trends, not for predicting a complete audiogram.

Suppose the chain becomes stiffer without a corresponding large change in effective mass. For a sufficiently slow oscillating force, displacement is approximately force divided by stiffness. Doubling stiffness under the same low-frequency force then halves displacement. At higher frequencies the relationship is more complicated, because inertial and damping forces cannot be neglected. Similarly, adding effective mass can reduce movement where inertial effects are important. These contrasting manipulations help students interpret why different mechanical defects need not produce the same pattern of frequency-dependent transmission loss. A measured response curve offers more mechanistic evidence than the statement that hearing is weaker.

The middle-ear cavity itself is an air-filled mechanical environment. Motion of its boundaries changes the volume available to the enclosed gas, and pressure variations in that space contribute to the load on the tympanic membrane. Its connection to the nasopharynx does not function as a continuously open sound duct. Instead, the auditory tube helps equalise slowly developing pressure differences across the membrane. During a change in atmospheric pressure, the external canal communicates with the environment more directly than the middle-ear cavity. Until pressure equilibration occurs, the tympanic membrane may be displaced toward the side of lower pressure. This altered static configuration can affect its response to the much smaller rapid oscillations associated with sound.

In a biological system it is useful to distinguish a mechanism that maintains operating conditions from a mechanism that performs sensory transduction. The auditory tube is part of the former category: it helps maintain conditions for efficient transmission. The stapedius reflex is another regulatory mechanism, but it acts through neural feedback and changes the mechanical state of the ossicular chain. The receptor cells of the organ of Corti perform the initial conversion of mechanical stimulation into electrical responses. Each participates in hearing, yet their roles are not interchangeable. A disturbance in pressure regulation can affect hearing despite intact receptor channels and normal neural conduction.

A sound-evoked middle-ear muscle contraction is evidence of a sensorimotor loop. Acoustic information reaches neural circuits, which activate motor pathways to muscles associated with the ossicles. Contraction changes the mechanical transfer of subsequent acoustic stimulation. The loop requires time for sensory detection, transmission, synaptic processing and contraction. A stimulus with a very abrupt onset can therefore begin reaching the cochlea before the feedback changes the middle-ear response. The reflex also does not attenuate all frequencies equally. Its physiological significance is best expressed as a regulated change of transmission, not as an infallible protective barrier.

Mechanical measurement can separate transfer properties from sensory thresholds. If ear-canal pressure is measured in pascals and stapes velocity in metres per second, their ratio has units of m s⁻¹ Pa⁻¹. It is a transfer function magnitude, not an acoustic impedance. Plotting this ratio against frequency under a fixed set of conditions can reveal a frequency-selective mechanical effect. If stapes velocity is lower in one condition while canal pressure is unchanged, the reduced velocity indicates altered coupling or loading. However, stapes motion is influenced by both the middle ear and the cochlear load, so the finding does not prove that the abnormality is confined to one ossicle. In an integrated mechanical system a downstream change may be reflected back to the input.

This last principle is important when interpreting cause and effect. A window that becomes less mobile can increase the load presented to the middle ear, and an apparently upstream displacement measurement may change in response. The anatomical sequence gives the ordinary direction of energy transfer, but it does not imply that each element is mechanically isolated from later elements. A complete physical model couples the components. For examination purposes the simplified serial pathway is useful for tracing the first consequence of a lesion, provided the student remembers that the magnitude and frequency dependence of the final response may involve the rest of the system.

6. Cochlear compartments and the mechanical input

The cochlea is a spiral structure within the bony labyrinth of the inner ear. Uncoiling it conceptually makes the direction of sound-driven mechanical events easier to follow. The basal end is adjacent to the middle-ear windows; the apical end is farther along the spiral. The cochlear duct, or scala media, lies between two perilymphatic spaces, the scala vestibuli and scala tympani.

In a cross-section of the cochlear duct, Reissner's membrane separates scala vestibuli from scala media, and the basilar membrane contributes to the partition between scala media and scala tympani. The organ of Corti rests on the basilar membrane. The tectorial membrane overlies its sensory epithelium. The lateral wall of scala media includes the stria vascularis, which contributes to maintaining the unusual ionic environment and electrical potential of endolymph. These are functionally distinct structures; the membranes are not interchangeable merely because each separates or overlies an anatomical space.

Three-dimensional anatomical organisation

Scala vestibuli and scala tympani contain perilymph, with an ionic composition broadly resembling extracellular fluid. Scala media contains potassium-rich endolymph. At the cochlear apex, a passage called the helicotrema provides communication between scala vestibuli and scala tympani. It does not open into scala media. The compartment boundaries are essential because normal hair-cell function depends on maintaining different ionic environments across different surfaces of the receptor epithelium.

When the stapes moves inward at the oval window, it displaces cochlear fluid. The round window, which communicates with scala tympani, provides a compliant boundary that can move in response to this displacement. A simplified volume-balance picture predicts that inward stapes motion is accompanied by outward round-window motion. The fluid is nearly incompressible over the conditions of interest, so a second compliant boundary allows the imposed volume displacement without demanding a large compression of the fluid enclosed by rigid bone.

This does not mean that a parcel of fluid must travel around the entire cochlear spiral from one window to the other on each acoustic cycle. Local oscillatory fluid motion and pressure differences across the cochlear partition are the relevant mechanical quantities. A rapid pressure disturbance propagates in the fluid, while the coupled displacement wave of the partition develops much more slowly. At very low frequencies the helicotrema can provide an effective pressure-equalising route between the two perilymphatic spaces, reducing the pressure difference that drives partition motion.

Mechanical boundaries and the sensory epithelium

If the round window were made much less compliant while all other structures remained unchanged, the normal mechanical load seen by the stapes would increase and the pattern of cochlear motion would be altered. The outcome depends on frequency and on other available compliance, but the exercise reveals why the two windows have complementary rather than redundant functions. One provides the usual driven interface, the other a pressure-relief and volume-displacement boundary.

The spatial arrangement of the cochlea is clearer if its longitudinal and transverse views are considered together. In the longitudinal view, the scalae extend along the spiral from base toward apex. In a transverse view at a particular position, the scala media lies between the two perilymphatic compartments. These are two views of the same structure, not alternative anatomical descriptions. The basal and apical labels describe position along the spiral, whereas upper and lower labels in a cross-sectional schematic identify relative placement at one position. A high-frequency response near the base is therefore not equivalent to excitation of the upper scala. The frequency map lies primarily along the length of the sensory epithelium.

The cochlear duct is part of the membranous labyrinth enclosed by the bony labyrinth. The central bony axis of the cochlear spiral is the modiolus, which is associated with the spiral ganglion and the course of auditory nerve fibres. The osseous spiral lamina projects from the central region and contributes to support of the cochlear partition. Laterally, the basilar membrane is supported near the spiral ligament. Thus the partition is not a loose strip suspended without attachments in an open fluid chamber. Its elastic properties depend on the geometry, materials and boundary conditions of the supporting structures. The longitudinal changes in these properties contribute to place-dependent mechanics.

The scala media has a distinct epithelial boundary system. Reissner's membrane forms its roof relative to scala vestibuli, and the basilar membrane and associated organ of Corti form its floor relative to scala tympani. The lateral wall is specialised in maintaining endolymph. Hair-cell apical bundles project into the endolymphatic environment, whereas other portions of the sensory epithelium are maintained in a different extracellular environment. Tight junctions at the apical surface contribute to the separation of these fluid environments. The precise epithelial organisation therefore links mechanical anatomy to ionic physiology: deflection must occur at a receptor surface that is exposed to the appropriate ions, and the compartmental barriers help preserve the electrochemical driving conditions.

To understand why fluid separation matters, compare the mechanical and chemical descriptions of a compartment. Perilymph and endolymph are both liquids, and either can participate in transmitting mechanical forces. Their ionic compositions, however, differ substantially. Perilymph is relatively rich in sodium and low in potassium, whereas endolymph in the mammalian cochlear duct is rich in potassium and relatively low in sodium. The stria vascularis helps maintain a positive endocochlear potential in addition to the concentration gradients. These properties are important for later hair-cell transduction. They should not be confused with the rapid oscillatory acoustic pressure differences that drive the partition. Electrical potential is measured in volts, mechanical pressure in pascals, and ion concentration in units such as millimoles per litre.

The round window and oval window are located at the basal end of different perilymphatic spaces. The oval window couples the stapes to scala vestibuli, while the round window membrane bounds scala tympani. The helicotrema is an apical communication between scala vestibuli and scala tympani. Together these features define a mechanical pathway, but the traditional diagram of arrows running through both scalae can be misleading if interpreted as a one-way circulation of liquid. The stimulus is alternating. When the direction of stapes motion reverses, the direction of local fluid movement and pressure changes also reverses. The relevant forces are time-varying, and no continuous net flow from the external ear through the cochlea is required.

It is possible to express the volume displacement associated with a moving boundary using a simple approximation. If the stapes footplate has effective area As and moves a small distance xs perpendicular to its surface, the displaced volume is approximately Asxs. If As is 3.2 mm² and peak motion is an illustrative 10 nm, the volume displacement is 3.2 × 10⁻⁶ m² multiplied by 10⁻⁸ m, or 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ m³. This is 32 picolitres. The magnitude is extremely small compared with the volume of the inner-ear spaces, yet its periodic action can produce measurable partition motion. The calculation treats the stapes as a uniformly translating piston; actual footplate movement may involve additional components and the value is a hypothetical numerical example.

If the only other compliant outer boundary is the round window, and fluid compression is neglected, the outward round-window volume displacement should approximately balance the inward stapes volume displacement. This is a volume constraint, not a statement that the displacements of the two membranes measured in metres are equal. Their areas and shapes differ. A larger moving round-window area could accommodate the same volume change with a smaller average displacement. The partition can also change how volumes are distributed internally without creating net fluid volume. Separating overall volume conservation from local partition motion helps explain why one cannot infer the displacement of the basilar membrane from the stapes amplitude alone.

A local difference between pressure on the two sides of a compliant partition supplies a force that can deform it. If both sides experienced identical acoustic pressure at every instant and the structure were otherwise undriven, that differential component of force would vanish. In the actual cochlea, the perilymphatic and duct spaces and their boundaries contribute to a spatially and temporally varying pressure field. The basilar membrane is coupled to the surrounding fluids, so its movement changes local fluid motion, which in turn influences pressure distribution. The wave arises from this interaction. It is not adequately explained by treating one scala as a rigid water pipe in which an independent sound wave simply travels to the apex before returning through the other scala.

This interaction explains why a sound can produce a strong mechanical response far from the point of input without requiring a large net transport of fluid. Energy can propagate through an oscillatory coupled system. The displacement wave is much slower than ordinary acoustic compression waves in the fluid because its propagation depends on the fluid's interaction with elastic partition motion. Its effective wavelength and speed are also position- and frequency-dependent. The fact that pressure signals can reach distant parts of the fluid rapidly does not eliminate the slower development of a travelling mechanical response. Measurements of partition phase along the cochlea reveal the latter behaviour directly.

The helicotrema becomes particularly relevant at very low frequencies. A low-frequency pressure fluctuation changes slowly enough that communication between perilymphatic compartments can reduce the pressure difference across the partition. This can reduce the effectiveness of extremely slow volume oscillations in driving the normal auditory mechanical response. The effect is not equivalent to a valve that opens only for one particular frequency. The degree of shunting depends continuously on the mechanical impedance of the passage and surrounding structures. At ordinary auditory frequencies the partition response is governed by the combined mechanical system, with frequency-dependent pressure differences developing along its length.

A different type of boundary disturbance can occur when an abnormal compliant opening is introduced elsewhere in the bony labyrinth. Conceptually, such an opening can divert some volume velocity away from the usual path and change the pressure differences driving the sensory partition. The detailed clinical manifestations depend on geometry, location and stimulus route, and are beyond the scope here. The principle is that a fluid-filled mechanical system is strongly influenced by its boundary conditions. Two specimens with identical sensory cells and nominally identical basilar membranes may produce different responses if their external mechanical boundaries differ.

The tectorial membrane is another mechanically important structure, but it is not an external pressure-relief membrane. It is an extracellular matrix structure extending over the organ of Corti and associated medially with the spiral limbus. Its position and mechanical properties influence how movement of the sensory epithelium produces relative displacement near the hair bundles. Reissner's membrane, the basilar membrane, the tectorial membrane, and the round-window membrane therefore have distinct locations and roles. In an examination, confusing them often leads to a plausible but incorrect mechanism because each is described as a flexible anatomical structure. The correct approach is to identify what each separates, what it supports, and what motion it transmits.

Finally, the cochlea and vestibular apparatus are neighbouring parts of the inner ear but do not have identical sensory tasks. The cochlear partition is arranged for auditory frequency analysis. The vestibular sensory organs detect head movement and orientation through different mechanical coupling arrangements. Both employ hair cells and depend on the specialised environment of the membranous labyrinth. Their shared cellular principle does not make the semicircular canals a route by which sound must travel before reaching the cochlea. The next chapter considers the vestibular organs as a functional comparison after establishing how sound reaches the cochlear receptor cells.

7. The travelling wave and mechanical frequency analysis

A cochlea must respond to different frequencies without requiring every receptor cell to detect the complete waveform independently. Its longitudinal mechanical organisation makes spatial separation possible. Near the base, the basilar membrane is relatively narrow and stiff; toward the apex it becomes broader and more compliant. The exact mechanics involve the entire cochlear partition, surrounding fluids and active processes, so width and stiffness alone should not be treated as a complete quantitative model.

A useful preliminary analogy is a mass attached to an elastic spring. For an ideal undamped oscillator with mass m and spring stiffness k, its natural frequency is f0 = (1/2π)√(k/m). Increasing stiffness increases the natural frequency if mass is unchanged, whereas increasing effective mass lowers it if stiffness remains unchanged. For example, increasing k fourfold at constant m doubles the natural frequency. This relationship explains why a gradient of mechanical properties can support different preferred frequencies, but the cochlea is not a row of independent uncoupled oscillators. The fluid and partition couple longitudinal positions, damping affects the response, and the outer hair cells contribute active feedback.

When a steady pure tone stimulates the ear, a displacement wave develops near the basal end and propagates apically. The wave amplitude grows toward the region that responds most strongly to the stimulus frequency, then declines rapidly beyond that region. High-frequency waves reach their maximal response near the base; lower-frequency waves reach maxima farther toward the apex. Even a low-frequency wave begins its propagation near the basal input. It does not enter the cochlea directly at the apex. The location of maximal displacement is the distinguishing feature, not the starting point.

The relationship between location and preferred frequency is called tonotopy. Frequency selectivity is therefore established mechanically before the pattern of activity in auditory nerve fibres is interpreted by the central nervous system. A particular region is most sensitive to a range around its characteristic frequency rather than responding only to an absolutely unique frequency. Overlapping responses across nearby positions permit population representations of complex sounds.

Consider a hypothetical displacement experiment in which a 4 kHz stimulus produces a peak near one-third of the distance from base to apex, and a 500 Hz stimulus produces a peak near four-fifths of that distance. These invented positions illustrate the ordering expected from the mechanical gradient; they are not calibrated human cochlear locations. If the stimulus changes from 4 kHz to 500 Hz without other changes, the maximal displacement moves toward the apex. If sound level rises at fixed frequency, amplitudes and the breadth of excitation may increase, but the change cannot be interpreted as if the tone had become a different frequency.

A travelling wave exhibits a progressive phase change along its path. If measurements at two positions reveal delayed oscillation at the more apical location, the phase information supports propagation rather than simultaneous movement of the entire membrane. One must compare phases consistently, since a sinusoidal phase can wrap through 360 degrees. A spatial envelope that marks maximum amplitudes at successive positions is not a photograph of the instantaneous wave shape. In an examination graph, the envelope shows how strongly each position can vibrate over a cycle, whereas an instantaneous curve can show positive and negative displacements along the partition at one time.

The mechanical response measured in a living sensitive cochlea differs from that in a passive or damaged preparation. Outer hair cells can enhance small mechanical responses and sharpen frequency tuning. At low stimulus levels, active amplification is especially important. As level increases, the relationship between input and displacement near the characteristic frequency becomes compressive rather than simply proportional over all levels. Such nonlinear behaviour is a property of the biological cochlea, not evidence that the external sound has changed its frequency. The molecular explanation involving hair-cell electromotility and its role in neural encoding belongs to the next chapter.

The term stiffness refers to the force required to produce a given displacement, not simply to the thickness of a structure. In a simple linear approximation a stiffness k relates force to displacement according to F = kx. Compliance is the reciprocal, 1/k, when the same displacement and force definitions are used. A highly compliant structure moves more under a given slow force than a stiff structure. The basal and apical regions of the cochlear partition differ in their mechanical properties, including their effective stiffness, dimensions and interaction with the surrounding fluid. The overall gradient is a biological design feature that helps make the mechanical response frequency dependent. A description based only on the visual width of the basilar membrane, however, is insufficient to infer the exact natural frequency of every longitudinal position.

To understand the oscillator analogy more carefully, imagine releasing a displaced mass attached to an ideal spring. The elastic restoring force accelerates the mass back toward equilibrium. The mass passes through equilibrium because it has acquired momentum, and the spring then exerts a restoring force in the opposite direction. Newton's second law yields m d²x/dt² = −kx. A sinusoidal solution has angular frequency ω0 satisfying mω0² = k. Dividing angular frequency in radians per second by 2π gives the ordinary frequency in cycles per second. This calculation is relevant to the intuition that stiffness and inertia compete to determine mechanical frequency preference. It does not describe the basilar membrane as a collection of isolated piano strings. Each location is coupled to its neighbours through the pressure and movement of fluid and other tissue structures, so a wave can propagate and energy can be redistributed along the partition.

If damping is present in such an oscillator, some mechanical energy is converted into heat during each cycle. A driven oscillator must receive additional energy to maintain steady motion. When the driving frequency is near its preferred range, its motion may be large compared with motion at less favourable frequencies. Greater damping reduces the sharpness of that preference and generally limits the response peak. In a passive cochlear preparation, damping and the spatially changing mechanical load help shape the envelope. In a sensitive living cochlea, active feedback can partly offset mechanical energy losses over an appropriate operating range. These are distinct phenomena: the longitudinal organisation can support frequency-dependent displacement even when active cellular feedback is absent, while normal biological sensitivity depends strongly on the presence of that feedback.

A useful thought experiment is to consider a uniform mechanical strip with identical properties at every point and then compare it with a strip whose properties gradually change along its length. A drive at one end of the uniform strip may generate propagating disturbances, but identical local mechanical properties do not supply the same basis for a systematic longitudinal frequency map. Introducing a gradient changes which regions respond strongly to different frequencies. In the cochlea, the geometry and supporting structures vary progressively, and fluid coupling makes the responses collective rather than independent. This analogy clarifies why both propagation and spatial variation are important. A membrane could support a wave without providing the particular orderly frequency analysis characteristic of mammalian hearing.

A frequency–position relationship can be described in two complementary ways. One can hold stimulus frequency fixed and measure displacement as a function of position. The graph then shows a spatial amplitude envelope, often with a maximum at a particular location. Alternatively, one can hold measurement position fixed and vary stimulus frequency, producing a tuning curve at that position. The characteristic frequency is associated with strong sensitivity or maximum response according to the specified measurement convention. The two plots probe related aspects of the same system but have different horizontal axes. In examination questions, confusing position with frequency can cause a student to interpret the slope of a curve incorrectly. A graph labelled in millimetres from the cochlear base describes spatial distribution; one labelled in hertz describes the response as stimulus frequency changes.

These distinctions matter when interpreting stimulus level. At a fixed position, a larger input may increase the mechanical response at multiple frequencies and change the apparent sharpness of the tuning curve. A threshold tuning curve records the minimum input required to attain a specified response at each frequency; on such a graph, greater sensitivity corresponds to a lower threshold. An amplitude tuning curve at a fixed input records the output amplitude; greater sensitivity often corresponds to a higher response. Thus the characteristic frequency can appear at a minimum in one type of graph and a maximum in another. This is not a contradiction. The plotted dependent variable determines which feature indicates the most sensitive response.

The basal-to-apical frequency map is not a simple arithmetic scale. The location corresponding to one frequency cannot generally be predicted by assuming that doubling frequency moves the peak by twice the distance. Mammalian cochlear frequency maps are often approximately related to logarithmic frequency over much of the hearing range, with species-specific details and deviations. At this level the important relationship is ordinal: higher characteristic frequencies occur more basally and lower characteristic frequencies more apically. Quantitative interpolation requires a measured or specified mapping. An examination may supply a hypothetical table of position and characteristic frequency; the correct approach is to use that table rather than import a universal formula that the problem does not specify.

Imagine that a narrow cochlear region is best stimulated at 2 kHz. A 1.8 kHz tone may still produce motion there because nearby tuning curves overlap. A 12 kHz tone will generally peak much closer to the base, but the exact motion at the original site depends on the response envelope and stimulus level. Tonotopy is therefore a spatial ordering of frequency preference, not a set of perfectly isolated mechanical switches. In an analogous sensory system, a photoreceptor with maximal sensitivity at one wavelength can still respond to nearby wavelengths. The analogy is limited because visual spectral sensitivity depends on pigment absorption while cochlear tuning depends on coupled mechanics; the shared principle is overlapping sensitivity rather than one stimulus exclusively activating one receptor.

Complex sounds illuminate the functional value of this map. In an approximately linear passive system the response to a combination of two sinusoidal inputs can be represented as the sum of their individual mechanical responses. If a 500 Hz and a 6 kHz component are both present, their maxima occur at different positions. At a location near the high-frequency maximum, the high-frequency component may dominate displacement; nearer the apex, the low-frequency component may dominate. The separation is not absolute because each component has a spatial envelope, and their effects can overlap. In a living cochlea, nonlinear interactions and active feedback mean that exact linear superposition is an approximation. Nevertheless, the elementary picture explains how complex input becomes a distributed pattern of mechanical stimulation.

The same concept can be illustrated using a simple two-tone experiment. Record displacement at two longitudinal sites under three conditions: tone A alone, tone B alone and both together. If the system is linear over the tested range, the instantaneous displacement during combined stimulation is predicted by adding the two individual displacement waveforms, with their phases properly aligned. Failure of that prediction indicates either nonlinearity or a violation of experimental assumptions, such as inconsistent stimulus level or changes in the preparation. It does not automatically identify the molecular source of the nonlinearity. A student must distinguish observation of nonlinear interaction from proof of a particular cellular mechanism.

Temporal behaviour is as important as the steady spatial envelope. At sound onset the mechanical system has not yet attained its steady response. Energy must propagate, and local oscillations develop over time. More apical responses can exhibit longer mechanical delays than basal responses under comparable conditions because of their propagation path and local mechanics. Mechanical delay is not synonymous with neural conduction delay. A recording of nerve activity includes additional time associated with receptor transduction, synaptic transfer and spike initiation. If the goal is to isolate cochlear mechanical propagation, direct displacement or pressure measurements are more informative than a total latency from sound onset to a neural response.

Phase can be measured by comparing a local sinusoidal displacement to the reference stimulus. Suppose two locations show a phase difference of 90 degrees for a 1 kHz tone, and the more apical displacement lags behind. A quarter cycle at 1 kHz is 0.25 ms. If the unwrapped phase lag is indeed one quarter cycle, it corresponds to that delay for this sinusoidal comparison. However, a measured phase difference modulo 360 degrees cannot distinguish 90 degrees from 450 degrees without additional information. Nor can a single-frequency phase measurement necessarily predict the onset delay of a broadband sound. The calculation demonstrates how phase information supports an interpretation of propagation while showing why careful measurement conventions matter.

A mechanical amplitude response can also be normalised to the input to separate sound transmission from local gain. For example, partition displacement divided by stapes displacement is a dimensionless ratio if both displacements are measured in the same units and at the same frequency. If sound pressure alone is used as the input, the resulting ratio has units of displacement per pascal and includes the effects of the outer and middle ears as well as the cochlea. A comparison of these two normalisations helps locate a physiological change. An intervention that reduces stapes displacement by half and partition displacement by half may leave the partition-to-stapes ratio unchanged. If the same stapes motion is accompanied by a substantial change in partition displacement, the local mechanical response or load is a more likely source of the difference.

The longitudinal map has consequences for selective damage. A lesion restricted to a basal region tends to compromise mechanical or receptor responses associated with the relatively high-frequency region more than responses associated with a distant apical region. Conversely, a local apical impairment may preferentially affect lower-frequency responses. This reasoning must remain qualified: injury can influence the mechanical coupling of neighbouring regions, and central auditory processing can alter the observed perceptual consequence. A spatially restricted cochlear lesion does not necessarily produce a perfectly sharp boundary in an audiogram. Nonetheless, the prediction of frequency-dependent impairment follows directly from the place-dependent mechanical organisation.

The crucial experimental distinction is between a frequency-dependent location of maximum movement and a model that treats every membrane element as independently resonating at its characteristic frequency. Von Békésy's observations provided direct evidence for a propagating pattern with changing amplitude and phase. The ability of later models to account for those observations depends on the mechanics of the coupled system. The fact that a local oscillator analogy predicts a stiffness-dependent preference does not prove that local resonance alone causes the travelling wave. A well-supported teaching model should retain the observed spatial pattern, acknowledge coupling and distinguish the simplified intuition from the complete mechanism. This is the level of understanding required to solve problems about frequency analysis without relying on unsupported mechanical analogies.

8. Organ of Corti: from partition displacement to receptor stimulation

The organ of Corti contains inner and outer hair cells together with specialised supporting cells. In the usual mammalian arrangement there is one row of inner hair cells and three rows of outer hair cells along much of the cochlear duct. Hair cells possess apical bundles of stereocilia, which are actin-rich protrusions. The word stereocilium is historical; these structures are not ordinary motile cilia driven by an axonemal motor. Their geometrical organisation permits direction-dependent responses to displacement.

The basilar membrane, reticular lamina and tectorial membrane do not move as a perfectly rigid unit. Sound-driven deformation of the cochlear partition produces relative mechanical motion and local fluid movement near receptor bundles. Outer hair-cell bundles are mechanically coupled to the overlying tectorial membrane in the mature mammalian cochlea. Inner hair-cell bundles are generally not embedded in it in the same way and are stimulated by relative motion and fluid movement. Thus one should not assume that the tectorial membrane simply presses identically on every hair cell.

The primary route by which information leaves the cochlea is through sensory output from inner hair cells to afferent neurons of the spiral ganglion. Outer hair cells have a complementary role in adjusting the mechanics that stimulate the sensory system. Their activity increases sensitivity and frequency selectivity in a healthy living ear. Damage to outer hair cells may consequently reduce mechanical gain and broaden tuning even when surviving inner hair cells retain some ability to transduce displacement.

The distinction between receptor stimulation and neural signalling is important. The travelling wave is a mechanical phenomenon. Deflection of a hair bundle is also mechanical. A receptor potential follows when mechanically sensitive channels alter ion conductance, and sensory afferent signalling follows transmitter release. These successive stages should not be collapsed into a claim that the basilar membrane itself sends action potentials. The ionic mechanism, direction selectivity and neural coding will be developed in the subsequent node.

A causal prediction follows from this separation. If ossicular coupling is impaired but receptor cells and cochlear mechanics are intact, a given external air-pressure stimulus produces a weaker mechanical input. If outer hair-cell function is disrupted, the mechanical response inside the cochlea changes even when ossicular input is maintained. If inner hair-cell synaptic output is selectively impaired, mechanical vibration can persist while afferent activity is reduced. Measurements at multiple stages of the system can therefore help locate a functional defect.

The sensory epithelium is mechanically specialised because a hair bundle must experience a relative displacement, not simply accompany the motion of every neighbouring structure. Consider two surfaces that translate in the same direction by exactly the same distance at all times. An object bridging them experiences no relative shear from that common movement. If one surface moves differently from the other, their relative motion can deform the object. In the cochlea, the basilar membrane, supporting cells, reticular lamina and tectorial membrane form a complex coupled arrangement, so their motions are not generally identical. Their relative movement and the resulting fluid motion provide the immediate mechanical stimulus to the hair bundles.

The reticular lamina is the apical surface formed by hair cells and supporting cells. Its organisation contributes to the separation of the endolymphatic space from the extracellular environment around other portions of the sensory epithelium. The tectorial membrane lies above this surface. The shape and attachment of the hair bundles determine how relative motion is translated into bundle deflection. The inner and outer hair cells have different geometries, attachments and mechanical roles, so a single diagram of a hair cell rigidly embedded in the tectorial membrane should not be used indiscriminately for both types. The distinction becomes particularly important when considering how motion near the inner hair-cell bundle is influenced by the local fluid environment.

Supporting cells are essential to the arrangement. They maintain the architecture of the epithelium and provide mechanical paths through which forces can be transmitted. The pillar cells, for example, contribute to the structure surrounding the tunnel of Corti between inner and outer regions of the organ. Other supporting cells are associated with the hair cells and their surrounding spaces. The sensory epithelium cannot be described adequately as hair cells floating independently in endolymph. Its organisation constrains motion, supports receptor cells and helps preserve the environment necessary for transduction. The detailed identities of every supporting cell are less important at this stage than understanding why receptor orientation and mechanical coupling are indispensable.

Although the receptor cells are called hair cells, they should not be confused with hair-follicle receptors in skin. A cochlear hair cell is a specialised epithelial sensory cell with a bundle of stereocilia. The sensory afferent neuron is a separate cell with a peripheral process contacting the receptor. The structural arrangement thus exemplifies a secondary sensory receptor cell, introduced in the foundations of sensory physiology. Mechanical motion produces a graded electrical response in the receptor, and synaptic signalling influences the afferent neuron. The auditory nerve carries neural spikes rather than a mechanically oscillating fluid signal. This division of labour is a direct link between the mechanics considered here and the general principles of sensory transduction.

The relative numbers of the two major hair-cell types do not directly indicate their relative contributions to sensory afferent output. There are multiple rows of outer hair cells but one principal row of inner hair cells, and yet inner hair cells provide the predominant afferent output. The outer cells exert feedback on the mechanical system. Their arrangement permits them to influence the motion of neighbouring components and thereby the stimulation received by the inner cells. An anatomical count is therefore insufficient to infer functional dominance in information transmission. It is necessary to identify how each cell type is connected and what its activity does to the mechanical or neural stages of the pathway.

The cochlear amplifier is not an additional ossicle or an amplification of acoustic power by the pinna. It is an active component of cochlear mechanics involving outer hair cells. These cells can convert electrical changes associated with stimulation into mechanical effects. Proper timing of mechanical feedback allows energy to augment the response near the relevant frequency. This requires viable cellular physiology and depends on the conditions that sustain receptor function. The middle ear's passive pressure transformation and the cochlea's active amplification should therefore be described separately. The first improves the mechanical coupling between media; the second modifies local motion through a living cellular mechanism.

To illustrate why active gain may vary with input, consider a hypothetical experiment at one cochlear location. A weak tone produces a displacement of one arbitrary unit in a passive condition and ten units in a sensitive active condition. A stronger tone produces a displacement of one hundred units passively and two hundred units actively. The active-to-passive ratio decreases from tenfold at weak input to twofold at strong input. The exact values are invented for teaching; the point is that a gain that is greatest for weak inputs can enhance sensitivity while limiting growth of the output over a broad range. The relation between input and active displacement is not necessarily represented by a single straight line.

The same reasoning can be expressed as a logarithmic input-output slope. If input pressure increases tenfold and displacement increases only about threefold over a selected range, the output has grown less than proportionally. The log–log slope is log10(3)/log10(10), approximately 0.48. A linear proportional response would instead have slope one. The example concerns mechanical displacement at a specified location and frequency, not the subjective scale of loudness. The interpretation also depends on comparing like quantities and measurement conditions. Compression of the mechanical input-output relationship is one reason that simple pressure gain calculations cannot account for the response of a living cochlea across its entire operating range.

9. Experimental evidence and interpretation

Georg von Békésy investigated the mechanics of the cochlea directly, rather than inferring membrane vibration only from subjective reports of pitch. His observations of cochlear preparations and physical models demonstrated that stimulation generates a wave whose peak location depends on frequency. The Nobel Prize awarded to him in 1961 recognised discoveries concerning the physical mechanism of stimulation within the cochlea. The conceptual importance of these experiments was the identification of a spatially organised, propagating mechanical response.

A basic experiment compares motion at several longitudinal positions during stimulation at controlled frequencies. Optical observation or modern motion-sensitive measurement methods can estimate vibration amplitude and phase. The independent variable is stimulus frequency, with stimulus level and input conditions controlled. The dependent variables include partition displacement at each position and phase relative to a reference. Repeating measurements at different positions produces the spatial amplitude envelope for each tone. A high-frequency envelope should peak more basally than a low-frequency envelope in a typical mammalian cochlea.

There are important limitations to interpreting early measurements. Preparations that lack normal living cochlear amplification can display broad, predominantly passive mechanical responses. Measuring a travelling wave in such a preparation supports passive frequency-dependent propagation but does not establish the full sensitivity or sharpness of a healthy living cochlea. Later studies using more sensitive optical measurements in living preparations demonstrated sharper tuning and nonlinear responses. The two types of observation are complementary because they address different physiological states of the same system.

A simple illustrative comparison helps distinguish mechanics from sensation. Suppose that, for two equal-level tones, the measured peak displacements occur at basal fractions of 0.20 and 0.78 of the uncoiled cochlear length. The peak at 0.20 is likely associated with the higher frequency. If a second manipulation reduces all measured displacements while retaining their approximate peak positions, reduced transmission or amplification is possible; the observations alone do not determine which. Recording stapes motion in addition to partition motion would help separate changes in mechanical input from changes in the response to that input.

Sound conduction can also be compared using air-conducted and bone-conducted stimulation. Bone conduction can excite the cochlea through several mechanical routes involving the vibrating skull, and can partially bypass a problem in the external or middle-ear air-conduction route. This distinction is a useful conceptual experiment, but bone conduction is not a perfectly isolated direct stimulation of one compartment. A pattern of relatively impaired air conduction with better bone conduction supports a conducting deficit, whereas reduced sensitivity by both routes can indicate a cochlear or neural deficit. Real clinical interpretation is more complex and is outside the scope of this chapter.

Controlled experiments across transmission stages

A rigorous investigation of auditory mechanics begins with the question of which stage of the system is being measured. An acoustic microphone near the tympanic membrane measures a pressure variable. A measurement of the stapes records the motion of the mechanical input boundary of the cochlea. Optical or related measurements of cochlear tissue can quantify displacement at specific locations. A neural recording measures electrical activity after receptor stimulation and synaptic transmission. These observations are causally related but not interchangeable. An experimental result at a later stage may arise from a change in any earlier stage, and a specific conclusion requires controls that establish which earlier stages remain intact.

Imagine two hypothetical specimens tested using the same ear-canal pressure and tone frequency. Specimen A produces a stapes displacement of 10 arbitrary units and a local partition displacement of 20 units. Specimen B produces stapes displacement of 5 units and partition displacement of 10 units. Both specimens show the same local partition-to-stapes displacement ratio of two. A plausible interpretation is that the major difference in this experiment lies in coupling from ear-canal pressure to stapes motion. If instead specimen B produced stapes displacement of 10 units and partition displacement of only 5 units, the cochlear mechanical response relative to input would differ. Neither comparison on its own identifies the precise damaged structure. They show how measurements at two stages narrow the possible explanations more effectively than one output measurement alone.

Experimental controls must include frequency, level, measurement site and preparation state. A displacement recorded at the basal region for a high-frequency tone cannot be compared without qualification to a displacement at the apex for an unrelated low-frequency tone. The differences may arise from normal tonotopy rather than an intervention. A response at a given site should be compared before and after manipulation under the same stimulus conditions, or across specimens using appropriately matched anatomical sites and calibrated stimuli. Repeated observations help establish whether an apparent change is reproducible. At examination level it is especially important to understand how a poorly controlled comparison can generate an apparently plausible but mechanistically unsupported conclusion.

One can distinguish the passive mechanical organisation from active enhancement by comparing responses in different physiological states. The passive travelling-wave pattern can remain recognisable under conditions that impair active feedback, whereas sensitivity and tuning may change. This is stronger evidence than the disappearance of a neural response alone because neural activity can fail for reasons unrelated to cochlear mechanics. Conversely, the persistence of a broad mechanical response does not show that the auditory receptor system is functioning normally. The proper inference is specific to the measured variable: propagation can persist, normal amplification may be impaired, and subsequent transduction may or may not be intact.

A response graph may show the level required to produce a fixed displacement at a single cochlear site. If after a manipulation the minimum of the threshold tuning curve shifts upward, more input is required for the same measured motion. If the curve also broadens, frequency selectivity has changed. An alternative graph may hold sound pressure fixed and plot displacement amplitude. In that representation a smaller peak indicates reduced response, while a broader peak indicates weaker selectivity. The direction of a graphical change must be interpreted in relation to the plotted dependent variable. A student who memorises that healthy tuning has a sharp peak without reading the axis may mistakenly identify the most sensitive region of a threshold plot.

Another conceptual experiment addresses the complementary roles of the two cochlear windows. Under controlled sound stimulation, measure motion at the oval and round windows. The simplest volume-displacement model predicts opposite directions of movement during a low-frequency cycle when other compliant pathways are negligible. A manipulation that changes the compliance of the round-window boundary can alter the mechanical load and partition response. The experiment should include a measurement of stapes motion because a change in downstream loading may also influence the input boundary itself. Thus the result tests the mechanical importance of the boundary without assuming that the ossicular chain is independent of its load.

Classical observations, elementary models and modern measurements answer different questions. A historical direct observation can establish the existence of a travelling displacement pattern. A simple mass-spring model can explain why changing stiffness or mass alters a frequency preference. A controlled comparison of living and passive preparations can reveal a contribution of active processes to sensitivity. None of these individually specifies every force acting on every component of the cochlear partition. Scientific understanding emerges when the observed patterns are connected with a model whose assumptions and limitations are explicit. The mechanical analysis of hearing provides a particularly useful example of how an anatomical structure, a physical stimulus and a biological response can be integrated without confusing any one of them with the others.

Answer: B. Particle motion must be considered before attributing differences to sensory biology

Fish otolithic receptors respond to relative motion, so equal pressure alone does not ensure equivalent stimuli. A and D assume the missing control; C contradicts fish sensory physiology.

52. Part C — An inner hair-cell synaptic manipulation leaves partition motion intact but reduces auditory afferent firing. Which inference follows?
  1. The basilar membrane is absent
  2. The mechanical and synaptic stages can be dissociated
  3. Outer-ear resonance must have disappeared
  4. Sound frequency increased

Answer: B. The mechanical and synaptic stages can be dissociated

The mechanical input persists but transmission to afferents is impaired. The other alternatives concern unmeasured or inconsistent mechanisms.

53. Part C — A preparation is compared at identical stimulus frequency and stapes displacement. In state X, local displacement is 12 units; in state Y it is 4 units. What does this show directly?
  1. The cochlear mechanical transfer differs threefold
  2. The auditory nerve fires threefold more
  3. The pinna is absent in Y
  4. Frequency differs threefold

Answer: A. The cochlear mechanical transfer differs threefold

The measured partition-to-input ratio is three times higher in X. Neural output, external-ear anatomy and different frequency are not established by these measurements.

Misconceptions and reasoning corrections

  1. Sound is a stream of air molecules from the source. Sound propagates energy through oscillatory particle motion; bulk transport is not required. Reasoning: Students may imagine wind rather than an oscillation. If a stem describes particle displacements, ask whether they are oscillatory around equilibrium or a net bulk flow.
  2. Frequency is the same quantity as pitch. Frequency is physical; pitch is perceptual and depends on auditory processing. Reasoning: Everyday language uses pitch and frequency interchangeably. When a problem compares a physical spectrum to a reported perception, identify the physical measurement separately from the listener's response.
  3. Pressure and intensity decibels both use 10 log10 of the respective ratio. Use 20 log10 for pressure ratios and 10 log10 for intensity ratios. Reasoning: The two logarithmic formulas look similar. Determine whether the given ratio is pressure or power per unit area; use the factor of twenty only for the pressure definition.
  4. Zero dB means complete silence. It means a measured quantity equals its stated reference. Reasoning: Zero is often read as absence. Substitute a ratio of one into the logarithm, and distinguish reference level from zero pressure or intensity.
  5. Two equal independent 60 dB sources produce 120 dB. Their intensities add, yielding about 63 dB. Reasoning: Ordinary numbers are added directly, but logarithmic levels are not linear quantities. Convert each level into intensity before addition and state the independence assumption.
  6. A higher sound speed automatically raises frequency. At an ordinary stationary interface, the source frequency stays fixed; wavelength changes. Reasoning: Students may confuse wave speed and oscillation rate. At a stationary interface use continuity of frequency, then calculate wavelength from the new speed.
  7. All auditory animals have three ossicles. Animals possess varied coupling structures; the three-ossicle arrangement characterises mammals. Reasoning: The mammalian example is often memorised as a universal template. Identify the animal lineage and the actual mechanical coupling structure before inferring anatomy.
  8. The middle ear generates extra acoustic energy. Passive mechanical transformation improves energy transfer and trades displacement for force. Reasoning: Pressure gain sounds like power gain. Check the force–displacement tradeoff and distinguish passive coupling from active cellular feedback.
  9. The Eustachian tube normally carries sound to the cochlea. Its major role is middle-ear ventilation and pressure equilibration. Reasoning: The tube's anatomical communication can be mistaken for the main auditory path. Trace external sound from canal to tympanum and distinguish slower pressure regulation.
  10. Scala media contains perilymph. It contains endolymph; both surrounding scalae contain perilymph. Reasoning: The similar names of the scalae obscure their contents. Label scala media first, then place perilymph in both adjacent scalae.
  11. The helicotrema joins scala media to scala tympani. It connects scala vestibuli and scala tympani at the apex. Reasoning: Students may assume all three fluid spaces communicate apically. The apical communication joins the perilymphatic spaces and preserves the distinct endolymphatic duct.
  12. The oval and round windows have identical mechanical roles. The stapes normally drives the oval window; the round window supplies a compliant boundary. Reasoning: Both are named windows, which can suggest redundancy. Locate the stapes-driven boundary and the complementary compliant boundary in separate scalae.
  13. Low-frequency travelling waves originate at the apex. Both high- and low-frequency waves originate near the base, with different peaks. Reasoning: A graph showing an apical peak is confused with the direction of travel. Read the horizontal axis and distinguish point of entry from position of maximal amplitude.
  14. The basilar membrane fires action potentials. Its motion is mechanical; receptor cells and afferent neurons mediate electrical signalling. Reasoning: The phrase frequency analysis may sound like neural computation. Separate partition displacement, receptor potentials and afferent action potentials as successive stages.
  15. Inner and outer hair cells do the same job. Inner hair cells are the primary sensory output; outer hair cells shape cochlear mechanics. Reasoning: The greater number of outer cells suggests greater neural output. Distinguish sensory afferent connectivity from feedback on local mechanics.
  16. A measured passive cochlear response establishes living sensitivity. Living outer hair-cell amplification can sharpen and enhance the response. Reasoning: An early mechanical observation may be overgeneralised to a living organ. Identify preparation state, stimulus level and measured variable before drawing conclusions.

Revision and active recall

Sound is an oscillatory mechanical stimulus. External and middle-ear structures shape and transmit it. Cochlear fluid displacement drives a frequency-dependent travelling wave; hair-cell stimulation and active mechanical feedback connect this spatial response to neural signalling.

  1. Distinguish pressure amplitude, rms pressure and intensity.
  2. Derive the two decibel expressions and specify their reference quantities.
  3. Compare independent and coherent sound sources.
  4. Explain why airborne sound requires impedance matching.
  5. Trace mechanical transmission from pinna to oval window.
  6. Explain the force-displacement tradeoff of the ossicles.
  7. Identify three scalae, their fluids and their boundaries.
  8. Explain why the round window matters.
  9. Sketch the amplitude envelopes of high- and low-frequency travelling waves.
  10. Distinguish outer and inner hair-cell function.
  11. Describe what von Békésy's observations established and what passive preparations cannot establish.

Mastery: explain the sound path, distinguish pressure from intensity, calculate basic acoustic quantities, identify cochlear compartments and predict mechanical responses when frequency or transmission changes.
