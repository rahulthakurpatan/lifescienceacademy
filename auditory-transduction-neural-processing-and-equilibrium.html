1. From a mechanical vibration to a neural representation

Hearing begins when an oscillation of air pressure is delivered through the outer and middle ear to the liquid-filled cochlea. Yet neither pressure nor liquid displacement is a signal that an ordinary axon can transmit to the brain. The conversion requires a biological structure that changes its electrical activity when subjected to force. This is a sensory hair cell, an epithelial receptor specialised to translate movement into membrane current. Its apical bundle is the mechanically sensitive part, while its basal region communicates with an afferent neuron. The electrical and mechanical compartments of the receptor are arranged in series, so failure of any one step can separate an otherwise normal acoustic stimulus from its neural representation.

A vibrating object creates alternating compression and rarefaction of the surrounding medium. At the cochlea, the stapes produces pressure differences between fluid compartments, establishing a travelling wave on the cochlear partition. The wave does not normally move the entire basilar membrane as a single rigid plate. Its pattern depends on frequency and on the changing mechanical properties along the length of the cochlea. Near the location where motion is largest, the organ of Corti undergoes deformation that bends hair bundles. Consequently, the auditory system begins to analyse frequency before any action potential leaves the ear. A useful distinction is between the mechanics that choose which population of cells will be strongly stimulated and the electrical transduction that converts their movement into a signal.

The ability to detect a quiet sound is not simply the result of an amplifier placed in the middle ear. Middle-ear impedance matching, cochlear frequency selectivity, active feedback and the electrical responsiveness of the sensory cells all contribute. To understand these contributions, imagine two identical sounds delivered to two preparations. One has normal cochlear mechanics but lacks functional transduction channels; the other has functional channels but has no active outer hair-cell feedback. The first cannot convert motion into the normal receptor current. The second still transduces sufficiently strong motion but has a higher threshold and poorer frequency resolution. In both preparations a hearing deficit is possible, but the underlying cause is fundamentally different.

Balance poses a related but distinct problem. The head may rotate, translate or change its orientation relative to gravity. Such movement is not normally encoded by sound-sensitive cochlear hair cells, even though the vestibular sensory cells are close relatives of those cells. A specialised mechanical apparatus attached to each vestibular receptor population selects the relevant motion. The semicircular ducts respond to rotation through motion of their enclosed fluid and the cupula. The otolith organs use a dense mineral-loaded membrane to sense linear acceleration and gravitational orientation. Thus the same general receptor design can be used to encode very different physical variables.

Throughout this chapter it is important to identify the variable at each level. The stimulus can be pressure, displacement, acceleration or a combination of these. The initial cellular response is a graded membrane potential, not necessarily a regenerative spike. The neural response is an ensemble of timed action potentials. A percept, such as pitch or the impression of movement, is an interpretation constructed by central circuits. Equating the stimulus with the perception obscures both experimental reasoning and the explanation of sensory illusions.

A sensory hair cell differs from a primary afferent receptor ending such as a cutaneous mechanoreceptor in that, in the mammalian inner ear, the cell is separate from the sensory neuron's soma and axon. The cell has no conventional long axon travelling to the brain. Instead, transmitter is released onto a peripheral process of a ganglion neuron. The ganglion neuron's central process carries the information to the brainstem. Developmental hair cells can show electrical activity distinct from the mature adult receptor, but for ordinary adult mammalian auditory physiology the important chain is graded hair-cell voltage, synaptic release and afferent action potentials. This division of labour makes it possible to specialise the receptor for analogue detection and the nerve fibre for long-distance signalling.

2. Hair-bundle geometry and the conversion of force into channel opening

A hair bundle is formed by a set of stereocilia projecting from the upper surface of a sensory cell. The name can be misleading because the projections are not equivalent to the motile cilia found on respiratory epithelium. Stereocilia contain densely organised actin filaments, are essentially enlarged specialised microvilli and do not use the familiar axonemal dynein mechanism to beat. Their shape and taper near the base permit the bundle to deflect under an applied force while its individual elements maintain their structural integrity. Their arrangement is generally graded in height, with the tallest row marking a privileged direction of sensitivity.

The polarity of this staircase organisation is crucial. Displacement toward the taller stereocilia tends to open the transduction channels. Movement toward the shorter edge tends to close them. A perpendicular displacement produces much less direct change in the tension applied along the direction of polarity. This does not mean that every off-axis force gives precisely zero current, since the detailed motion of a real bundle depends on its mechanical attachments and the geometry of the stimulus. It means that the response has a preferred vector rather than depending only on the magnitude of displacement.

The individual stereocilia do not simply push a pressure sensor in the cell body. Fine extracellular connections called tip links run obliquely between a shorter stereocilium and its taller neighbour. In mammalian auditory hair cells, cadherin 23 contributes the upper portion of the link and protocadherin 15 the lower portion. During a positive displacement the separation between relevant attachment points increases, raising force transmitted through this linkage. This changes the probability that mechanically gated ion channels associated with the lower end of the tip-link machinery are open. The channel complex includes TMC proteins, especially TMC1 in mature cochlear hair cells, together with associated proteins that help transmit force and maintain the molecular assembly.

An essential conceptual distinction is between the tip link and the channel. A tip link is an extracellular mechanical connection; the channel is a membrane protein complex through which ions can cross the lipid bilayer. Their functions are complementary but not interchangeable. A mutation that disrupts a link can prevent the normal mechanical gate from receiving tension without necessarily destroying the intrinsic ability of the channel to conduct ions. Conversely, a mutation in a pore-forming component can alter ion permeation even if the bundle retains an apparently normal external staircase arrangement. A morphological image alone therefore does not establish physiological function.

Mature mammalian cochlear hair cells no longer possess the kinocilium characteristic of many vestibular hair cells. During development the kinocilium participates in organising polarity; in the adult cochlea the orientation of the bundle is still evident from the stereociliary rows. In vestibular organs the kinocilium remains a useful structural landmark: displacement toward it is ordinarily excitatory. An examination may show a bundle with a single true cilium standing beside its longest stereocilia and ask which direction increases firing. The answer depends on identifying the kinocilium and the receptor polarity, not on whether the arrow happens to point toward the top or right side of the page.

At rest some mechanically gated channels are already open. The resting open fraction is not a fixed universal number for all hair cells. It depends on operating conditions and cell type. This resting conductance gives the receptor scope to signal movement in both directions. If all the channels were closed before any movement, a stimulus in the closing direction could not reduce current below zero. An intermediate resting open probability permits both an increase and a decrease in inward current, and hence an increase or decrease in transmitter release. In vestibular physiology, this design is especially useful because movements to either side of a reference orientation must be distinguished.

The relationship between displacement and channel opening is often represented by a sigmoid curve. Near its steep part a small change of position produces a large change in open probability. At large negative positions nearly all the channels are shut, and at sufficiently positive positions the probability approaches its maximum. This saturation is expected because there are only finitely many available channels. It limits the range over which displacement can be faithfully represented by a proportional change in current. It also means that doubling the force need not double the receptor response. Even if mechanical displacement were proportional to force, channel gating alone would introduce nonlinearity.

A simplified teaching model expresses open probability as P = 1 / [1 + exp(−(x − x½)/s)]. Here x is bundle displacement along its sensitive axis, x½ is the displacement at which half the channels are open and s is a positive displacement scale reflecting the width of the transition. All three displacements must use the same unit, for example nanometres. The exponential argument is dimensionless. This is a convenient phenomenological representation rather than a complete molecular description of a functioning bundle. At x = x½ the exponent is zero and P equals one half. At a displacement larger by s, P is about 0.731; at a displacement smaller by s, P is about 0.269. The sensitivity to displacement is greatest near the midpoint, as follows by differentiating the expression: dP/dx = P(1 − P)/s. The maximum derivative is 1/(4s), attained when P = 0.5. This derivative quantifies why a small shift in the operating point can appreciably alter detection sensitivity even with the same number of functional channels.

To test these predictions, consider an isolated receptor subjected to calibrated bundle displacements while its membrane voltage is controlled. A voltage clamp prevents the transduction current itself from appreciably altering the membrane potential; the recorded current then more directly reports changes in conductance. Reversing the direction of the mechanical stimulus should reverse the change in current around the resting baseline. Increasing the positive displacement should eventually produce a plateau. Such an experiment helps distinguish mechanically controlled channels from a voltage-gated current that is activated only after a receptor potential has developed. A control deflection perpendicular to the sensitive axis, and careful monitoring of mechanical stimulus magnitude, help establish that the direction effect is not merely a difference in the strength of the stimulus.

Classical work on hair-cell receptor currents and adaptation provided evidence for direct mechanical gating; subsequent molecular studies identified candidate components of the complex. A key feature of this evidence is the short delay between bundle movement and current onset. A mechanism requiring transcription or a long cascade of diffusible messengers could not plausibly account for a sufficiently rapid response to acoustic oscillation. Short latency by itself does not prove the exact molecular identity of the pore. Combining physiological current measurements with structural observations, genetics and rescue of defective function supplies stronger evidence than relying on any one approach. The precise molecular coupling and adaptive processes remain active research questions, so elementary descriptions should present the established gating principle without claiming that all details are settled.

3. The unusual ionic battery of the cochlea

In a typical neuron, the intracellular potassium concentration is higher than the extracellular potassium concentration. Opening a potassium conductance in such a cell at its usual resting potential frequently drives potassium out of the cell and tends to hyperpolarise it. The auditory receptor presents an important exception because its apical bundle is not bathed in ordinary extracellular fluid. The scala media contains endolymph, which is rich in potassium and relatively poor in sodium. The basal surface of the cell is exposed to a low-potassium extracellular environment resembling perilymph. The apical and basolateral membranes therefore encounter very different electrical and chemical driving forces.

The specialised lateral wall of the scala media includes the stria vascularis, whose transport systems generate and maintain the unusual ionic composition and positive endocochlear potential. The endocochlear potential is measured with respect to the surrounding perilymph; in a healthy mammalian cochlea it is often around positive eighty millivolts, although the actual value depends on species, physiological state and recording location. This extracellular potential should never be confused with the receptor cell's own membrane potential. A hair cell whose interior is negative forty-five millivolts relative to perilymph has an apical intracellular potential of negative one hundred and twenty-five millivolts relative to endolymph at positive eighty millivolts.

A convenient way to analyse this configuration is to use the Nernst relation. For potassium across a membrane, EK = (RT/F) ln([K+]out/[K+]in), where R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature in kelvin and F is the Faraday constant. The relation assumes an idealised ionic activity approximated by concentration and a membrane selectively permeable to potassium. At body temperature, RT/F is approximately 26.7 millivolts when natural logarithms are used. Importantly, EK is the equilibrium value of the intracellular voltage relative to the local extracellular compartment being considered. If the extracellular reference changes from endolymph to perilymph, both the applicable concentration ratio and the reference potential must be reconsidered.

Suppose endolymph contains 150 millimolar potassium and the hair-cell cytosol contains 140 millimolar potassium. The chemical equilibrium voltage across the apical membrane is 26.7 ln(150/140) = about positive 1.84 millivolts. If the cell is negative 45 millivolts relative to perilymph and endolymph is positive 80 millivolts, the local apical membrane voltage is negative 125 millivolts. The difference Vm − EK is approximately negative 126.84 millivolts. For a positive potassium conductance, an outward-positive current convention gives a negative current, meaning net inward positive charge. The large voltage gradient is therefore sufficient to drive potassium into the cell even though the concentration gradient is modest.

For the basal side of the same illustrative cell, assume extracellular potassium is 5 millimolar. The potassium equilibrium voltage is then 26.7 ln(5/140), approximately negative 89.0 millivolts. The basal membrane voltage is negative 45 millivolts relative to the same perilymph reference. Now Vm − EK equals approximately positive 44 millivolts, favouring outward potassium current when the basal conductance is open. This paired calculation explains how potassium can enter across one region of a hair cell and leave from another region without violating the fundamental laws of electrochemistry.

The mechanically gated transduction conductance is not exclusively selective for potassium. It is a cation conductance that also permits other ions, including calcium. Potassium is especially important to the inward current in vivo because of the composition of endolymph and the endocochlear voltage. A current calculation using only EK is therefore an instructive approximation for that ionic component, not an exact prediction of the reversal potential of the whole mixed conductance. In recordings made with different experimental bathing solutions, sodium or other ions may carry a greater share of the transduction current. The external solution in a question must be read before deciding which ion dominates.

Tight junctions between the epithelial cells help preserve the separation between endolymph and perilymph. A loss of this separation could reduce the positive voltage and alter local ion concentrations. The consequences would extend beyond the hair cells directly injured: neighbouring receptors could receive a diminished electrical driving force even if their channels and tip links remained structurally intact. Thus the tissue surrounding sensory cells is part of their physiological mechanism rather than merely packing material. Maintaining the cochlear ionic battery requires metabolic energy, although the opening of an individual mechanosensitive channel allows passive ion movement down an electrochemical gradient.

A simple equivalent circuit for the receptor combines an apical variable conductance, a basal potassium conductance and membrane capacitance. The capacitance represents the capacity of the lipid bilayer to store separated electric charge. The variable apical conductance connects the interior electrically to a relatively positive effective reversal potential; the basal conductance connects it to a more negative equilibrium potential. Opening the apical channels changes the balance of currents, and the membrane voltage moves toward a new equilibrium. When the bundle returns to its resting position, the balance shifts back. The voltage response is limited by the capacitance and the total conductance, so its time course need not precisely match every detail of the current waveform.

Consider reducing the endocochlear potential by 40 millivolts without changing endolymph potassium concentration or hair-cell intracellular voltage initially. The local apical membrane voltage becomes negative 85 millivolts rather than negative 125 millivolts. The potassium driving force is then about negative 86.8 millivolts instead of negative 126.8 millivolts. For unchanged open probability and conductance, the initial potassium current magnitude would be about 68 percent of its previous value. The later receptor voltage may change as the other currents rebalance, so this estimate must not be applied as a complete prediction of the steady-state hearing threshold. It isolates the direct influence of losing part of the electrical battery.

The vestibular labyrinth has a similar compartmental design, with potassium-rich endolymph over the bundles and a low-potassium basal fluid. However, it is incorrect to assign the large cochlear endocochlear potential unchanged to the vestibular end organs. Vestibular extracellular voltages differ and the mechanical demands on the receptors are different. The shared principle is that the unusual apical ionic environment promotes inward cation current. The magnitude of the driving force is an organ-specific physiological property. This distinction is important when a numerical example drawn from the cochlea is used to reason about an otolith hair cell.

4. From the receptor potential to a train of afferent spikes

When the bundle is bent in its excitatory direction, the increase in inward current makes the hair-cell membrane potential less negative. This is depolarisation. Depolarisation changes the probability of opening voltage-sensitive calcium channels near the basal region, where the receptor communicates with nerve endings. In mature mammalian inner hair cells, L-type calcium channels, particularly CaV1.3, play a major role in coupling the graded potential to release of glutamate. Calcium entry at the synaptic active zone triggers the fusion of transmitter-containing vesicles with the plasma membrane. Glutamate then acts on receptors on the afferent ending and contributes to generation of action potentials in the afferent fibre.

The location of these events matters. Mechanical gating at the apical bundle is not the same as voltage gating at the basal synapse. Calcium entering through the mechanotransduction channels can affect the local bundle and its regulation; calcium entering through voltage-gated channels at the active zone is especially relevant to rapid transmitter release. A question that states a calcium channel blocker has been selectively applied at the basal synapse does not necessarily imply that the mechanically evoked current will disappear. Instead, the receptor potential may persist while transmitter release and subsequent afferent activity are substantially diminished. This is a powerful example of separating sequential events by a selective intervention.

The ribbon synapse is a specialised feature of many sensory receptors that maintain sustained graded transmission. A protein-rich ribbon helps organise vesicles near the active zone, enabling transmitter release to follow changing receptor voltage over time. In an auditory inner hair cell, a single receptor can contact multiple spiral ganglion afferents, with individual synaptic contacts capable of differing in operating characteristics. The phrase ribbon synapse does not mean that a solid ribbon physically conducts an electrical signal to the neuron. It describes a presynaptic organisation that assists the chemical release process. The synaptic cleft and postsynaptic ion channels remain essential.

At the afferent ending, excitatory postsynaptic current changes membrane voltage. Once the excitation reaches spike threshold at a suitable initiating region, an action potential travels along the auditory nerve fibre. Spike amplitude is not the main variable used to signal loudness. Instead, increasing stimulus strength can change the probability, frequency and temporal organisation of spikes, as well as the number of recruited fibres. This is the same broad principle encountered in neural excitability, but with a distinct graded receptor stage preceding the spike-generating neuron.

A resting mechanotransduction current and resting transmitter release may produce spontaneous afferent firing even without a deliberately applied sound. Consequently, excitatory bundle displacement can increase discharge above baseline, while inhibitory displacement may reduce it. Spontaneous firing must be accounted for in experimental measurements. A frequency threshold curve may define threshold as the sound level needed to elevate the firing rate detectably above that baseline. A neuron showing spontaneous spikes is not necessarily responding to an unrecorded external acoustic stimulus; baseline activity is an expected feature of some sensory afferents.

One can predict the consequences of different lesions by arranging the stages in causal order. Destruction of tip links tends to reduce mechanically controlled current. Loss of the endocochlear voltage diminishes its driving force. Blocking basal calcium entry interferes with the link between receptor voltage and transmitter release. Blocking postsynaptic glutamate receptors impairs excitation of the neuron despite presynaptic release. Interfering with axonal sodium channels can prevent action-potential propagation even when an initial postsynaptic response persists. If a problem reports a measurable receptor current but no neural discharge, the evidence excludes complete failure of the initial current under those conditions but does not identify a unique downstream lesion. Additional observations are required to distinguish release, receptor activation and nerve conduction.

The transfer from bundle motion to afferent spikes is dynamic rather than a static relay. The receptor membrane acts in part like a resistance-capacitance circuit. In an elementary model with resistance Rm and capacitance Cm, the time constant is τ = Rm Cm. If Rm is measured in ohms and Cm in farads, τ is in seconds. A 100-megaohm resistance and a 10-picofarad capacitance would give a time constant of 1 millisecond. A simple single-pole passive membrane would have a corner frequency of 1/(2π τ), approximately 159 hertz in this hypothetical example. A time-varying transduction current at frequencies well above that value would produce a smaller alternating voltage component than a low-frequency current of the same amplitude. Real hair cells have voltage-dependent conductances, different properties along the cochlea and complex coupling to the synapse. This calculation explains the filtering principle, not a universal numerical cutoff for hearing.

The existence of membrane filtering does not mean that high-frequency sound is inaudible. The cochlear partition selects the high-frequency region of the organ, and the mean and envelope components of receptor voltage and firing can still encode stimulus features. At higher frequencies, a single auditory nerve fibre does not have to reproduce each acoustic cycle. The location of the activated receptors and the population response remain informative. This separation between carrying the existence of a frequency and copying the detailed waveform becomes central when examining temporal and place coding.

5. Adaptation, saturation and the operating range of transduction

A sensory response often changes during a constant stimulus. In a mechanical step experiment, a hair bundle can be deflected rapidly and held at the new position while the resulting current is measured. A common observation is a rapid change of current followed by partial relaxation toward its earlier value. Because the applied displacement is maintained, a decrease in current cannot automatically be attributed to the stimulus being removed. It can reflect adaptation within the transduction apparatus, although mechanical relaxation of the preparation must also be considered and controlled. The exact time course varies considerably across hair-cell types and experimental conditions.

Adaptation is valuable because a cell that has moved far into saturation loses sensitivity to additional changes. Suppose the resting position places the channel system near the centre of its activation curve. A large positive displacement opens nearly all available channels. A small additional displacement can now cause little increase in open probability. If an adaptive process alters the effective operating position so that the sustained stimulus is again represented on a steeper part of the curve, small variations around that stimulus can become detectable. The receptor has not simply become less sensitive to everything; it has changed the range of input over which its sensitivity is useful.

A classical mechanistic picture invokes tension adjustment in the hair bundle, in which motor-associated elements modify the effective tension transmitted to the channels. Calcium influx and calcium-dependent regulation have been important in many experimental models of hair-cell adaptation. However, fast and slow processes do not have identical mechanisms in every hair cell, and the requirement for calcium in some forms of rapid mammalian cochlear adaptation has been debated. At NET and SET level it is appropriate to understand adaptation as a dynamic adjustment of the mechanical-electrical operating point, supported by physiological evidence, while avoiding an unjustified claim that a single calcium-triggered motor mechanism accounts for all observations.

A simplified current record may be described by I(t) = I∞ + (I0 − I∞) exp(−t/τa), where I0 denotes the current immediately following a step, I∞ the long-time current and τa the adaptation time constant. This is a phenomenological model useful for analysing a monotonic exponential relaxation; it does not assume that all molecular events obey a single first-order process. If an inward current is represented by its positive magnitude, and the initial magnitude is 180 picoamperes while the sustained magnitude is 60 picoamperes, the excess current is initially 120 picoamperes. At t = τa, it is 120/e, approximately 44.1 picoamperes, so the total magnitude is about 104 picoamperes. At two time constants the excess has fallen to about 16.2 picoamperes. If a plotted current uses the standard outward-positive sign convention, the direction of the curve will appear reversed. Students must distinguish a change in signed current from a change in its magnitude.

Adaptation at the bundle is distinct from adaptation at the receptor's synapse or the auditory nerve's firing machinery. A stable receptor potential might still be accompanied by a decline in spike frequency because of reduced vesicle availability or properties of the afferent. Conversely, an adaptation of the transduction current can influence firing without a primary change in synaptic machinery. Simultaneous recording at several levels can help assign the site of adaptation. If receptor current is recorded under voltage clamp while afferent firing is also measured, a current decline under a mechanically maintained input offers evidence for an upstream contribution. If that current remains unchanged, but firing falls while synaptic output declines, a downstream contribution becomes more plausible.

Another important limitation on dynamic range is the maximum possible firing rate of a single neuron. After each spike, the refractory period limits how soon the neuron can generate another one. Even if the hair cell continues to release more transmitter, the afferent cannot increase firing indefinitely. Similarly, a mechanotransduction channel cannot become more than fully open. The auditory system manages these limitations using different fibre thresholds, different spontaneous firing levels, recruitment of additional fibres and active mechanical compression. Together these mechanisms allow a much wider range of acoustic intensities to be represented than a single linear receptor model would predict. None removes saturation altogether; the biological system distributes the coding burden across multiple mechanisms and cells.

6. Inner and outer hair cells: two cooperating roles

A cross-section through a representative mammalian organ of Corti contains a single row of inner hair cells and usually three rows of outer hair cells. Although both classes possess mechanically sensitive bundles, their contributions are not identical. Inner hair cells supply most of the primary auditory afferent information. Outer hair cells supply a major active mechanical contribution that improves the stimulation of the sensory apparatus. They are therefore not merely redundant copies of the principal receptors. Their position, mechanical attachments and specialisation of the lateral membrane allow them to influence the moving cochlear partition.

The reason to distinguish the two populations becomes apparent when considering low-intensity sounds. Passive mechanics would already produce a frequency-dependent travelling wave, but passive vibration alone would not account for the full sensitivity and sharp tuning of a healthy living cochlea. In mammals, outer hair cells convert changes in their membrane potential into changes of cell length through a motor protein called prestin. Prestin is abundant in the lateral membrane. With the normal operating polarity, depolarisation produces shortening and hyperpolarisation produces elongation. Because these length changes couple to surrounding structures, a precisely timed cellular mechanical response can feed energy into cochlear vibration.

The direct voltage-dependent change in outer hair-cell length is called electromotility. It does not require the same actin–myosin cross-bridge cycle as skeletal muscle contraction. This is a mechanistically significant distinction. A question may contrast an isolated outer hair cell held at different membrane voltages with a myofibre stimulated by a motor neuron. The outer hair cell can change length through prestin-associated membrane events; the skeletal muscle fibre relies on excitation-contraction coupling and sarcomeric cross-bridges. Both transform electrical signals into mechanical work, but their molecular machinery differs.

For the feedback to amplify the cochlear travelling wave, it must supply mechanical work with an appropriate phase relationship. If a force acts in the same direction as the velocity of a moving structure, it can transfer positive mechanical power to that structure. If the force opposes its velocity, it removes energy. Consequently, it is not enough that an outer hair cell moves; the timing and coupling of the movement within the organ of Corti determine whether it contributes constructively to the wave. This reasoning explains why observing a voltage-dependent change of length in an isolated cell establishes an important capability but does not by itself measure the full cochlear amplifier in vivo.

Active amplification is strongest for relatively small inputs and tends to become less effective as input level increases. At high stimulus levels, mechanical response grows more slowly than a proportional extrapolation from low intensities would suggest over an important part of the operating range. This compressive behaviour gives weak sounds a comparatively large boost while limiting the amplification of intense ones. A healthy cochlea thereby combines sensitivity with useful dynamic range. It also produces nonlinear effects, including distortion components that would not be generated by a perfectly linear mechanical system.

Suppose a measured basilar-membrane displacement increases from 0.10 to 0.20 nanometres when stimulus pressure doubles at one level, and from 2.0 to 2.5 nanometres when pressure doubles at a higher level. These are hypothetical values used to illustrate the response, not an actual published measurement. The first relation has a displacement ratio of two; the second has a ratio of 1.25. The difference implies that the system's incremental gain is smaller in the second regime. If both recordings are taken at the same characteristic frequency, and the physical stimulus and measurement location are controlled, they would be consistent with compression. They would not identify prestin by themselves. Molecular and cellular interventions are needed to attribute a mechanical nonlinearity to a particular component.

The comparison between functioning and impaired outer hair cells helps separate threshold from acuity. If a small tone at the characteristic frequency normally generates considerable movement with active feedback, loss of this feedback means that greater external pressure is needed to generate comparable movement. Threshold rises. Moreover, the sharp peak of the active mechanical response is broadened, so neighbouring frequencies may excite less distinct receptor populations. A listener might experience difficulty separating simultaneous sounds even when a sufficiently intense tone remains detectable. Merely increasing acoustic intensity cannot fully restore the lost frequency selectivity.

Evidence for prestin's motor role includes the work by Zheng and colleagues published in Nature in 2000. They identified a protein expressed in outer hair cells and showed that expression in a heterologous cell system conferred characteristic voltage-dependent electrical and motile properties. The relevant inference is that prestin provides a molecular basis for somatic electromotility. The result is stronger than a correlation between protein expression and motility because introducing the protein changes the behaviour of another cellular system. However, a heterologous cell is not a complete organ of Corti: the magnitude and timing of amplification in a living cochlea depend on mechanical architecture, ionic driving force and feedback interactions. The mechanistic explanation therefore combines molecular sufficiency for a cellular property with tissue-level evidence for physiological importance.

Descending neural fibres can alter cochlear function. The medial olivocochlear efferent system acts prominently on outer hair cells, and its influence can reduce cochlear amplifier gain. In the simplest physiological interpretation, this enables central control over the mechanical sensitivity of the peripheral organ. Efferent signalling is different from the ascending output of inner hair cells; it is an example of feedback rather than the main sensory information route. This distinction explains why a nerve pathway heading toward the organ of Corti can alter incoming hearing signals without carrying those signals toward the auditory cortex.

7. Place coding: how the cochlea represents frequency

A sequence of pressure fluctuations has no physical label indicating its pitch. The auditory system must extract useful regularities from the incoming vibration. A nearly sinusoidal sound is characterised by its repetition frequency, whereas a complex sound contains many frequency components. Cochlear mechanics separates these components spatially because the mechanical properties of the cochlear partition vary along its length. This spatial arrangement is called tonotopy. The basal region, near the stapes, has a relatively narrow, stiff basilar membrane and is most responsive to high frequencies. The apical region is relatively broad and compliant and is most responsive to low frequencies. Both high- and low-frequency travelling waves begin near the base; their peak displacements occur in different regions. A low-frequency wave does not enter the cochlea directly through the apex.

The place principle is not a claim that only one location vibrates in response to a tone. At any given frequency the travelling wave has a spatial envelope, rising toward a peak and declining beyond it. Receptors situated near the peak receive the largest effective stimulus, while others may receive weaker motion. Because many neighbouring receptors and their afferent fibres respond to similar but nonidentical frequencies, the neural representation of a tone is a distributed pattern. A fibre's characteristic frequency is the frequency at which a sound can evoke a response at the lowest threshold, under the particular measurement conditions. This definition depends on comparing thresholds across stimulus frequencies rather than comparing raw firing rates at arbitrary sound levels.

An auditory nerve tuning curve is usually plotted with frequency on the horizontal axis, often on a logarithmic scale, and the minimum effective sound level on the vertical axis. The low point of a normal sharply tuned curve identifies the characteristic frequency. If threshold at 2 kilohertz is 15 decibels and threshold at 1 kilohertz is 45 decibels, this fibre responds more sensitively to the former frequency. A horizontal line at 25 decibels intersects only the frequencies whose thresholds are at or below that value. At 60 decibels the range of potentially effective frequencies is wider. Thus the same fibre may respond to several frequencies, particularly at higher levels. Tonotopy is an organised preference, not a collection of perfectly exclusive frequency labels.

The sharpness of tuning can be described using a bandwidth measured at a specified level above minimum threshold. A common index of sharpness in physiological studies is Q10, defined as characteristic frequency divided by bandwidth measured ten decibels above the lowest threshold. The units of frequency cancel, so Q10 is dimensionless. For an illustrative fibre with characteristic frequency 4 kilohertz and a ten-decibel bandwidth from 3.6 to 4.4 kilohertz, the bandwidth is 0.8 kilohertz and Q10 is 5. If the same characteristic frequency is retained but bandwidth broadens to 2 kilohertz, Q10 becomes 2. A smaller value indicates broader tuning by this particular measure. Q10 is not a general measure of how loud the stimulus feels, and comparison between different experiments requires the same threshold and bandwidth conventions.

The cochlear amplifier influences the fine shape of the tuning curve. In a healthy ear the tip near the characteristic frequency is often particularly sensitive. Reduction of outer hair-cell function can elevate threshold and broaden the response near this tip. A graph of threshold as a function of frequency therefore carries information about both sensitivity and selectivity. An upward displacement of the entire curve with no appreciable widening and a selective loss of its sharp tip are not the same change. A loss of sound transmission before the cochlea could elevate external thresholds without the same direct change in cochlear selectivity. Although real measurements require control of the sound reaching the cochlea, this comparison shows why a single threshold number does not characterise all aspects of hearing.

The place pattern continues in the auditory nervous system because afferents with similar characteristic frequencies terminate in organised regions of many central structures. Such organisation is not a literal spatial map of where the sound source lies. A low tone on the left and the same low tone on the right can activate similar frequency regions of the left and right cochleae. The location of the source requires comparison of signals between the ears and use of other spatial cues. A frequency map and a map of external space represent different variables, even though both can be described using the word place.

The distinction also clarifies the limits of interpreting pitch solely in terms of the most active cochlear region. A complex periodic waveform can contain harmonics of a fundamental frequency while the physical component at the fundamental is absent. Human listeners can often perceive a pitch corresponding to that missing fundamental. The cochlea has not created a large low-frequency vibration at the missing component simply because the percept has that pitch. Instead, relations among the higher harmonics and their temporal regularity can support a perceptual estimate of periodicity. The phenomenon demonstrates that central processing of the pattern matters and that the perceptual attribute of pitch should not be identified without qualification with the displacement peak of a single pure tone.

8. Temporal coding, phase locking and the volley principle

The alternative to identifying frequency purely by the place of activation is to use the time structure of the neural response. Consider a slowly oscillating bundle. During the excitatory part of each cycle the inward current increases, the receptor depolarises and the probability of transmitter release rises. In the opposite part of the cycle release is less likely. Consequently, the nerve fibre has a preferred phase of the stimulus cycle at which it tends to fire. Repeated presentation of the same sound can reveal that the spikes are not distributed randomly in time relative to the stimulus. This statistical synchronisation is called phase locking.

Phase locking does not mean that the nerve produces one action potential during every cycle of a sound. At low rates a fibre may sometimes fire on consecutive cycles, but at higher frequencies its refractory period, synaptic variability and other constraints prevent reliable firing once per cycle. Nevertheless, the spikes that do occur may remain concentrated near a particular phase. If a sound has a frequency of 500 hertz, its period is 1/500 second, or 2 milliseconds. A fibre could fire on the first, third and sixth cycles at nearly the same phase each time. Its interspike intervals would then be near multiples of 2 milliseconds even though its average spike rate is well below 500 spikes per second. The difference between spike rate and the temporal structure of the intervals is essential.

The volley principle extends this argument from one fibre to a population. Suppose several afferents respond to the same low-frequency tone. One fibre may fire on a particular cycle when another does not, and on later cycles the second fibre may fire instead. Across the population, many cycles can be represented even though no single fibre fires on all of them. This is possible because the relevant information is the distribution of spike timing across fibres, not the demand that every axon reproduce the complete acoustic waveform. The volley principle does not remove all limits on timing information: receptor membrane filtering, synaptic kinetics and neural timing variability constrain phase locking as frequency increases.

A simple way to display temporal coding is a period histogram. A researcher presents a stable tone repeatedly and assigns every spike to its phase within one stimulus period. Spikes occurring just after the beginning of different cycles are placed in the same phase bin, regardless of how many intervening cycles were skipped. A nearly uniform histogram indicates weak phase preference. A narrow peak indicates stronger synchronisation. The total number of spikes still measures something different from their distribution around the cycle. Two preparations can have similar mean firing rates while exhibiting very different phase locking.

One quantitative index of this concentration is vector strength. If the phase of spike j is θj, expressed in radians, represent it by a unit vector pointing at angle θj. Add the cosine components, add the sine components, and divide the magnitude of the resultant by the number of spikes N. The resulting measure is VS = √[(Σ cos θj)² + (Σ sin θj)²]/N. Values are between zero and one. If all spikes occur at exactly the same phase, the vectors point in the same direction and VS equals one. If the phases are evenly distributed around the circle, opposing components cancel and VS approaches zero. Finite sample size can produce a nonzero value even in the absence of genuine phase preference; a statistical interpretation requires appropriate sampling and comparison.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose four spikes occur at phases zero, zero, π/2 and 3π/2. The cosine sum is 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 2, and the sine sum is 0 + 0 + 1 − 1 = 0. The resultant magnitude is 2; division by four gives a vector strength of 0.5. If the four spikes instead occurred at zero, π/2, π and 3π/2, both sums would be zero and vector strength would be zero. The two recordings contain the same number of spikes, so their mean rates may be identical for the same observation duration. Their phase information is not identical. This is why spike counting alone does not reveal whether a fibre carries useful temporal information.

Temporal phase locking is strongest at relatively low frequencies in mammals, and the upper range depends on species, auditory nerve properties and the definition used to assess synchronisation. Human auditory perception of interaural fine-structure timing declines strongly with increasing frequency. It is not appropriate to assign a single sharp phase-locking limit to every mammal or all neural levels. Envelope timing can remain useful when the individual high-frequency carrier cycles are not faithfully represented. For example, a high-frequency carrier whose amplitude changes slowly can produce a neural response that follows its envelope more clearly than the carrier itself. The carrier frequency and envelope frequency are different physical quantities.

At the examination level the two forms of frequency coding should not be regarded as mutually exclusive rival theories. A low-frequency tone can be represented both by the region of the cochlea that responds most sensitively and by the phase distribution of afferent spikes. A high-frequency tone relies especially on place-related information because reliable fine-structure phase locking is limited. The auditory system can combine information from these routes according to the properties of the stimulus. A change in frequency might shift the spatial excitation pattern, alter the temporal regularity, or do both. This complementarity is an important general principle of neural coding.

9. Intensity coding and the distinction between sound level and loudness

The strength of an acoustic stimulus is ordinarily described by a physical quantity such as sound pressure or acoustic intensity. Loudness, by contrast, is a perceptual attribute influenced by frequency, duration, context and the state of the auditory system. The same acoustic intensity at two frequencies may not be judged equally loud. Likewise, the difference between a very quiet detectable tone and one that sounds twice as loud cannot generally be predicted from a simple doubling of pressure or firing rate. The relationship between external stimulus and internal perception is mediated by the mechanical, electrical and neural stages already considered.

Sound pressure level is calculated as Lp = 20 log10(prms/pref), where prms is the root mean square sound pressure and pref is a specified reference pressure. In air, a common reference is 20 micropascals. A doubling of pressure therefore raises sound pressure level by 20 log10(2), about 6.02 decibels. For intensity level, LI = 10 log10(I/Iref). If intensity is proportional to squared pressure under unchanged acoustic conditions, doubling pressure quadruples intensity, giving 10 log10(4), the same numerical change of about 6.02 decibels. The two expressions are compatible but cannot be applied interchangeably to arbitrary quantities. Decibels are a logarithmic representation of ratios, and the reference must be supplied to interpret absolute values.

As the level of a tone rises, movement near its characteristic cochlear region generally increases. A stronger effective deflection can increase the average inward transduction current, change the receptor potential and increase transmitter release at an inner hair-cell synapse. Afferent discharge typically rises over an operating range before approaching saturation. This relation can be displayed as a rate-level function for one fibre. The threshold of the fibre is the point at which firing reliably differs from its baseline according to the chosen criterion. The slope of the rate-level function describes how strongly firing changes with level in that range. A plateau means that the fibre has little further rate-coding capacity for the particular stimulus and conditions.

The diversity of auditory nerve fibres extends the represented intensity range. Some fibres discharge spontaneously at relatively high rates and are sensitive to weak signals; others have lower spontaneous rates and different thresholds and dynamic ranges. This classification is useful but the properties are distributions, not perfectly separated categories in every species. At increasing sound levels additional fibres can be recruited, including fibres with less sensitive thresholds. The total activity across a population can therefore continue to carry information after some individual fibres have saturated. In addition, stronger sound can spread excitation across a wider range of nearby characteristic frequencies, adding a spatial dimension to intensity coding.

Consider a hypothetical experiment at a single frequency. Fibre A has a baseline rate of 40 spikes per second, rises to 100 at a low level and saturates near 150 at moderate levels. Fibre B has a baseline rate of 5, remains near baseline at the low level, then rises to 80 and later to 160 as the stimulus level increases. At moderate levels fibre A may have reached its plateau while fibre B still carries a graded rate signal. Neither fibre alone encodes the full range, but their combined activity does. The central nervous system has access to labelled populations and their relative timing, not merely a single scalar total. Two patterns with equal total rate could arise from different frequencies or stimulus levels.

The number of spikes in a short time window is also affected by stimulus onset and duration. Many fibres show a strong onset response followed by a lower sustained discharge during a long tone. A rate measured exclusively during the first few milliseconds is not directly comparable to one measured after hundreds of milliseconds. In an experiment, the analysis window and the duration and rise time of the stimulus must be specified. This is particularly important when the objective is to distinguish transient sensitivity from sustained intensity coding. A strong onset response can convey timing information even when sustained firing adapts.

Compression at the mechanical stage reduces the growth of displacement relative to pressure over part of the dynamic range, while recruitment at the neural stage adds information from additional fibres. These mechanisms are complementary. Loss of normal active cochlear compression may lead to a much steeper growth of perceived loudness over the remaining audible range, even when quiet sounds are harder to detect. This phenomenon illustrates why the deficit cannot be described simply as an equal attenuation of all sounds. The exact perceptual outcome depends on the nature of the impairment, and the physiological distinction is more important than memorising a clinical label.

When a question asks whether stimulus intensity is coded by action-potential amplitude, the correct approach is to identify the level at which the measurement is made. The graded receptor potential and transmitter release may vary in amplitude. An individual propagated action potential in an axon is approximately all-or-none under comparable conditions. Mean spike rate, recruitment, temporal pattern and the activity of different fibres carry the relevant neural information. A compound recording from many fibres can have a larger extracellular waveform when more fibres contribute, but that is not evidence that each individual spike has become larger in proportion to loudness.

10. From the spiral ganglion to auditory cortex

The peripheral processes of spiral ganglion neurons contact cochlear hair cells, and their central processes form the cochlear division of the eighth cranial nerve. The ganglion is named for its organisation along the spiral of the cochlea. These bipolar neurons carry information from the receptor to the central nervous system. The distinction between the spiral ganglion and the cochlear nucleus is important: the former contains sensory neuron cell bodies within the peripheral apparatus, whereas the latter is a central relay in the brainstem. A lesion of the cochlear nerve occurs before the major central redistribution of auditory information.

The central processes of the spiral ganglion neurons enter the brainstem and terminate in the ipsilateral cochlear nuclei. Dorsal and ventral divisions contain neuronal populations that analyse different features of incoming activity. At this level information is already divided into parallel streams rather than passed through a single uniform succession of identical relays. Some neurons preserve precise timing; others emphasise onset, spectral patterns or changes in firing rate. At the broad physiological level required here, the crucial feature is that the first central synapses receive information from the cochlea of the same side, and that many onward connections distribute this information to both sides of the brainstem.

The superior olivary complex is a major early site where information from the two ears can converge. Its subdivisions include the medial and lateral superior olives, important for binaural comparisons. Inputs from the two sides need not contribute in the same manner: some are excitatory and others inhibitory, and their timing and relative strength help represent differences between the ears. Ascending fibres then travel in pathways associated with the lateral lemniscus, reaching the inferior colliculus of the midbrain. Some fibres relay in nuclei of the lateral lemniscus, and some routes do not pass through every named nucleus. A list of structures is therefore a map of important stages rather than a compulsory itinerary for each individual axon.

The inferior colliculus is a major site of convergence of ascending auditory information. It combines contributions associated with frequency, temporal patterns and spatial cues. Its output reaches the medial geniculate body of the thalamus, which in turn projects to the auditory cortex through auditory radiations. This thalamic relay is the auditory analogue, at a broad organisational level, of the lateral geniculate nucleus in the visual pathway. The two geniculate structures must not be exchanged in an examination answer: medial is associated with audition, lateral with vision. Both thalamic and cortical stages contain further parallel and reciprocal connections that modify the representation of sound.

The primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe contains an orderly representation of sound frequency, although its organisation is more complex than a single uninterrupted replica of the basilar membrane. Adjacent and higher auditory regions contribute to analysis of complex sound patterns, recognition and spatial interpretation. Cortical response is influenced by experience and behavioural context. This means that the final neural representation is not a passive photographic copy of the acoustic wave. The nervous system extracts features relevant to detecting sources, distinguishing communication sounds and guiding behaviour.

An important consequence of bilateral representation is that a unilateral central lesion above the cochlear nuclei usually does not reproduce the complete ipsilateral deafness expected from interruption of the corresponding peripheral auditory nerve. Because much information from each ear reaches both sides, the other central pathway can still transmit auditory input. However, higher lesions can impair localisation, sound discrimination and other integrative functions. The outcome depends on the precise anatomical level and extent of damage, and one should not conclude that central lesions are physiologically insignificant merely because both ears retain some hearing. Bilaterality confers redundancy for basic detection while supporting special computations across the two ears.

A helpful reasoning exercise is to compare three interventions. In the first, all output of the right cochlea is prevented from reaching the brainstem. Information derived from that ear is absent from both central pathways, reducing the availability of binaural comparisons. In the second, a pathway on the right side above the first bilateral projections is interrupted. Some information from both ears may be lost, but alternative central routes remain. In the third, a focal cortical disturbance affects processing of a restricted class of complex sounds while peripheral thresholds are preserved. These distinct patterns reveal that detection, integration and recognition are separable functions. They also show why a gross anatomical label alone cannot specify the exact perceptual consequences.

Auditory processing is not exclusively ascending. Descending projections from higher regions can influence activity at lower relays, and the olivocochlear system provides a direct example of regulation of the peripheral amplifier. The same sound may be behaviourally relevant in one context and relatively unimportant in another. Central mechanisms of attention and modulation can change how auditory signals are processed without changing the physical pressure waveform. The detailed neural basis of attention lies beyond the required scope, but recognition of bidirectional communication prevents the mistaken view that the auditory system is a one-way electrical cable.

11. Localising a sound in space: binaural and monaural cues

The retina receives a spatially organised image, but the two ears do not possess an equivalent two-dimensional receptor surface onto which each source is directly projected. Instead, the auditory nervous system must infer location from how acoustic waves arrive at the two sides of the head and from the way the head and external ears alter their spectra. The same tone can be produced at many different positions. The cochlear tonotopic map alone cannot determine which position generated it. Spatial hearing is therefore a computation using multiple cues, rather than a direct reading of the basilar membrane location.

For a sound arriving from the left, the wave usually travels a shorter distance to the left ear than to the right ear. The resulting difference in arrival time is an interaural time difference, or ITD. The physical principle can be illustrated using a simplified far-field model. Let d be the distance between two idealised receiving points, c the speed of sound, and θ the azimuth measured from the straight-ahead direction toward one side. Neglecting diffraction and the shape of the head, the path difference is approximately d sin θ, and ITD is approximately d sin θ/c. The expression has units of seconds because a length is divided by a speed. For a sound arriving directly from the front, θ = 0 and the path difference is zero. In the simplified geometry the difference increases as the source moves toward one side.

For an illustrative separation of 0.18 metres, a sound speed of 343 metres per second and a source at 30 degrees, the path difference is 0.18 × 0.5 = 0.09 metres. Dividing by 343 gives 0.000262 seconds, or approximately 262 microseconds. At 90 degrees the same model gives about 525 microseconds. A real head is not transparent to sound, so actual interaural delays also depend on diffraction around its surface and are not exactly predicted by this formula. Nevertheless, the calculation conveys the small temporal differences that auditory circuits must distinguish. A neural integration period measured in milliseconds is not automatically incompatible with detecting a much smaller difference between two correlated inputs.

At low sound frequencies the timing of a periodic waveform can be represented by phase-locked activity. The interaural delay creates a corresponding interaural phase difference. For a tone of frequency f and delay Δt, the phase difference is 2π f Δt radians, considered modulo 2π. A delay of 250 microseconds at 500 hertz represents 0.125 of a period, corresponding to π/4 radians or 45 degrees. At 2 kilohertz the same delay represents half a period, or 180 degrees. With sufficiently high frequencies, the same measured phase difference can correspond to several delays separated by whole periods. This phase ambiguity, combined with the limits of fine-structure phase locking, explains why a simple periodic timing cue is less useful at high frequencies.

The medial superior olive in mammals is strongly associated with comparisons of binaural timing, particularly for low-frequency sounds. Excitatory inputs from both sides can interact so that the probability of firing is higher when they arrive within an effective temporal window. Inhibition and cellular membrane properties also contribute to timing selectivity; a coincidence detector is a useful teaching abstraction, not a full description of every mammalian circuit. A classical delay-line model illustrates how axonal conduction delays can offset differences in arrival times and make coincidence strongest at particular stimulus delays. Such explicit delay-line arrangements are particularly well illustrated by specialised avian systems. Mammalian spatial coding must not be reduced to the claim that an identical anatomical delay-line map exists in all species.

A second cue arises because the head interferes with transmission of sound to the farther ear. This creates an interaural level difference, or ILD. The effect is generally more pronounced at higher frequencies, whose shorter wavelengths make acoustic shadowing by the head important. At low frequencies the wavelength is large relative to head dimensions and diffraction can reduce the shadow. For a 500-hertz tone in air at 343 metres per second, wavelength is about 0.686 metres. For a 5-kilohertz tone it is about 0.0686 metres. The latter is much smaller than a typical human head width, which helps explain why the strength difference can be more useful at higher frequencies.

If the root mean square pressure at the nearer ear is 0.040 pascals and at the farther ear is 0.010 pascals under the same measurement conditions, their pressure ratio is four. The interaural level difference is 20 log10(4), approximately 12.04 decibels, favouring the nearer ear. The reference pressure cancels because the question compares two levels measured with the same reference. If the given quantities had been intensities instead of pressures, the appropriate factor would be 10. A larger interaural level difference tends to provide stronger evidence that the source lies toward one side, although exact localization also depends on frequency, the individual acoustic filtering of the head and other cues.

The lateral superior olive contributes to interaural level comparison. A useful schematic circuit receives excitation driven by the ipsilateral ear and inhibition driven by the contralateral ear through a brainstem pathway involving the medial nucleus of the trapezoid body. Consider a unit on the left. An increase in left-ear level can strengthen excitation, whereas an increase in right-ear level can strengthen the inhibitory contribution. Its net response can therefore depend on the balance of signals rather than the absolute level at one ear. Real circuits involve diverse inputs and may carry temporal information as well, so this schematic should be understood as the basic computational principle.

The low-frequency timing and high-frequency level preferences are sometimes referred to together as the duplex account of sound localisation. They are tendencies, not mutually exclusive rules. Broadband sounds may contain useful timing and level cues simultaneously, and timing of the envelope can help when fine-structure timing is unavailable. The auditory system also uses filtering by the pinnae, head and torso. Such filtering varies with source elevation and front-back position. The external ear can introduce frequency-dependent peaks and notches, which provide learned cues about direction even when one ear alone is available. Head movements can help disambiguate positions that give similar instantaneous binaural differences.

A cone of confusion is a useful geometric concept: different positions can generate similar binaural differences, especially in simplified geometrical descriptions. For example, a source directly in front and one directly behind may both produce nearly equal arrival times and levels at the ears. Spectral cues due to the external ear and changes in binaural differences when the head turns can help distinguish the alternatives. If both ears receive equal intensity, therefore, it is not safe to conclude that the sound must be directly in front. Equality constrains one aspect of geometry, but does not uniquely specify location.

A classic experimental way of separating these cues is to deliver controlled signals to the two ears through calibrated transducers. One can vary the timing of two otherwise similar waveforms while holding their levels fixed, and then vary their levels while holding timing fixed. If perceived lateral position changes with the controlled difference, the result supports the use of that cue. A careful design controls spectral content and the onset of signals, because differences in onset can provide timing information even when fine structure is ambiguous. Such a laboratory manipulation isolates individual variables more successfully than simply presenting sounds from loudspeakers at arbitrary positions. Its limitation is that it may omit the natural pinna filtering and head movements used in everyday localisation.

12. Evidence from audiometry, emissions and evoked activity

The study of hearing depends on measurements at different levels of the auditory pathway. No single method reveals every stage. A behavioural threshold indicates that a listener can reliably detect a specified stimulus under specified conditions. A measurement of mechanical vibration reveals how sound drives the cochlear partition. A record of a hair-cell current reveals transduction at the cellular level. A measurement from an electrode near the auditory pathway reveals a population or single-fibre electrical response depending on the recording arrangement. A sound recorded back in the ear canal can reveal active cochlear mechanics. These observations are complementary precisely because they refer to different steps.

Pure-tone audiometry estimates the minimum sound level at which tones of specified frequencies are detected by a listener. An audiogram commonly uses frequency on the horizontal axis and hearing threshold in decibels hearing level, or dB HL, on the vertical axis. In the conventional presentation increasing threshold values are displayed farther down the page. The hearing-level reference at each frequency is related to reference normal thresholds and the calibration of the transducer; it is not identical to dB SPL. Zero dB HL therefore does not mean an absence of acoustic pressure. An upward or downward line in a figure must be interpreted using the actual numerical axis rather than the reader's expectation about what higher sensitivity looks like.

Air-conduction stimulation involves the outer and middle ear before sound drives the cochlea. Bone-conduction stimulation provides a route by which skull vibration can drive cochlear mechanisms, largely bypassing the ordinary outer and middle ear transmission pathway. A difference between air-conduction and bone-conduction thresholds under appropriate calibrated conditions can support a conductive component of impairment. If both thresholds are elevated without a substantial gap, a sensorineural component may be present. These are conceptual interpretations for physiological reasoning, not a substitute for clinical audiological evaluation. Real measurements require control of crossover to the opposite ear, transducer calibration and other factors.

An illustrative audiogram can demonstrate the distinction. At one frequency, preparation A has an air-conduction threshold of 45 dB HL and a bone-conduction threshold of 10 dB HL. The 35-decibel gap suggests that the route involving ordinary sound transmission is disproportionately impaired. Preparation B has air and bone thresholds of 45 and 40 dB HL, respectively. Their similar elevation is consistent with impairment not explained by a large conductive gap. Preparation C has thresholds of 60 and 30 dB HL; both an elevation of bone threshold and a gap are present. These patterns are hypothetical and are not published data. They show how a comparison provides more mechanistic information than the air threshold alone.

Otoacoustic emissions are sounds associated with cochlear processes that can be measured in the ear canal. In a seminal study in 1978, David Kemp described acoustic responses from the human ear after stimulation. The observation supported the conclusion that the cochlea is not only a passive receiver of sound but can participate in generating mechanical energy detectable at the external ear. Modern understanding connects many types of otoacoustic emissions to the active nonlinear behaviour of the cochlea and particularly the contribution of outer hair cells. Such evidence complements direct cellular observations of electromotility.

The measurement presents a logical problem: a stimulus applied in the ear canal also produces immediate mechanical and acoustic reflections. To identify a response associated with cochlear processes, the experiment must distinguish stimulus artefact, ordinary passive reflection, ambient noise and the delayed or frequency-specific emission of interest. A microphone detects pressure in the canal; it does not directly observe a hair cell changing length. The inference is based on the characteristics of the measured response and its dependence on cochlear function. Thus an emission can serve as a noninvasive indicator of aspects of cochlear mechanics, without being an exhaustive measurement of every receptor and nerve fibre.

One useful type of nonlinear response is the distortion-product otoacoustic emission. When two primary tones at frequencies f1 and f2 are presented, a nonlinear system can generate response components at combination frequencies. A commonly measured component is 2f1 − f2 for appropriately chosen primary frequencies. If f1 is 1.0 kilohertz and f2 is 1.2 kilohertz, this component lies at 0.8 kilohertz. A strictly linear system driven by only those two frequencies does not create a new steady-state frequency component. In a real experiment, distortion generated by the stimulus equipment must be ruled out before attributing an additional frequency to the biological system. A measured emission supports an active nonlinear cochlear process but does not directly quantify hearing perception.

A present otoacoustic emission does not guarantee that inner hair-cell transmission, auditory nerve firing and central interpretation are all intact. Consider an intervention that preserves outer hair-cell motility but prevents transmitter release from the inner hair cell. The cochlea may retain measurable active mechanical behaviour while the neural response to sound is severely impaired. Conversely, a reduction of an emission could arise from impaired cochlear mechanics or from difficulties transmitting the emission back through the middle ear. It is therefore unsafe to equate absence of emission with a uniquely identified cellular lesion. Comparing methods strengthens the interpretation.

Auditory brainstem responses measure small time-locked electrical potentials generated by the auditory pathway in response to repeated acoustic stimulation. Because the response is small relative to background electrical activity, averaging repeated recordings aligned to stimulus onset can reveal reproducible components. Random background activity tends to cancel in the average more than a consistent evoked signal. This general method is an important example of signal extraction. It assesses aspects of timing and synchrony of neural responses, unlike a behavioural report or an acoustic emission. The relation between individual waveform peaks and anatomical generators is not always one-to-one; the key examination concept is the use of evoked neural activity to interrogate the pathway.

Combining a normal cochlear emission with an abnormal evoked neural response raises the possibility that the active mechanical stage is relatively preserved while neural transmission or synchronisation is disrupted. A reduced emission together with elevated thresholds may point toward impaired outer hair-cell or transmission function, but these observations alone cannot establish a unique diagnosis. An experimentally sound answer distinguishes what a result directly measures, what it supports, and what alternative explanations remain possible. This is the same principle used when comparing mechanical, electrophysiological and molecular evidence for transduction.

13. Vestibular receptors and the organisation of the labyrinth

When a person turns the head while keeping the eyes focused on a stationary object, vision remains remarkably stable. When the body accelerates forward, a sense of movement can arise even with the eyes closed. Such observations reveal that the nervous system has access to information about head motion that does not depend on vision. This information is supplied in large part by the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear. It also interacts with proprioceptive and visual signals, so equilibrium cannot be attributed to a single receptor or a single reflex. Here the focus is on the peripheral detectors and the immediate pathways that transform their signals into useful estimates of head motion.

Each membranous vestibular labyrinth contains three semicircular ducts arranged approximately in different planes and two otolith organs, the utricle and saccule. The ducts are part of an inner-ear fluid system and lie within the corresponding bony semicircular canals. Everyday descriptions sometimes use canal to refer to the entire functional organ. The mechanically important sensory epithelium of a semicircular duct lies in its expanded region, the ampulla. The otolith organs instead have sensory patches called maculae. These anatomical distinctions correspond to differences in how external motion is transmitted to the hair bundles.

Vestibular hair cells possess stereocilia and a kinocilium, and the direction of the bundle from shorter stereocilia toward the kinocilium defines its excitatory polarity. Deflection in that direction increases mechanotransduction current and tends to increase the rate of impulses in associated vestibular afferents. Deflection in the opposite direction produces the converse effect. A resting discharge in many vestibular afferents permits information to be encoded by both rises and falls in activity. This tonic activity is an essential component of the operation of paired canals: the two sides of the head can send contrasting signals when motion begins.

The presence of a kinocilium does not mean that the receptor detects motion by beating it. It provides a landmark associated with the geometry and mechanical polarity of the bundle. Displacement of the accessory structures bends the bundle, and the stereociliary mechanotransduction apparatus changes conductance. The ionic principle resembles cochlear transduction, but the accessory mechanical structures and their stimulus selectivity differ. If a problem compares a vestibular receptor to a cochlear hair cell, a sensible starting point is to identify what they share at the level of ionic and synaptic mechanisms and what differs at the level of the mechanical input.

Vestibular type I and type II hair cells have distinctive morphological and neural contact arrangements. Type I cells have a characteristic flask-like shape and can be surrounded by a calyx-shaped afferent ending; type II cells have more cylindrical profiles and receive bouton contacts. Different afferent populations have different temporal discharge properties, supporting responses across diverse motions. It is unnecessary at this level to memorise every specialised mechanism of transmission at the calyx. The key point is that the vestibular epithelium contains a heterogeneous sensory population, not one uniformly behaving receptor repeated without variation.

The vestibular division of the eighth cranial nerve carries information from these organs to the brainstem and cerebellar pathways. The cell bodies are in the vestibular ganglion, also known as Scarpa's ganglion. This is distinct from the spiral ganglion of the cochlear division. Both are associated with cranial nerve VIII, yet their peripheral inputs and principal functional tasks differ. Examination questions sometimes exploit the shared cranial nerve number to conflate sound detection with balance. The appropriate distinction is the sensory epithelium, afferent branch and target circuit, not the nerve number alone.

14. Semicircular canals: the dynamics of rotation

Imagine a circular tube containing fluid. If the tube begins to rotate about an axis approximately perpendicular to its plane, inertia causes the fluid initially to lag relative to the walls. In the vestibular apparatus, this relative motion produces a pressure difference across a gelatinous structure called the cupula, which extends across the lumen of the ampulla. Hair bundles of the crista ampullaris project into the cupula. As the cupula deflects, the bundles bend. The receptor cells thereby acquire information about the rotation of the head without directly measuring an abstract mathematical variable such as angular acceleration.

The crista is the sensory ridge, whereas the cupula is the overlying gelatinous mechanical structure. The cupula does not normally carry the dense calcium carbonate particles characteristic of the otolith organs. It is close in density to the surrounding fluid, so ordinary head tilt in a gravitational field does not produce the same sustained response as in an otolith organ. This difference helps assign rotation and linear or gravitational acceleration to different mechanical detectors. The canal's directional sensitivity also depends on the geometry of its plane relative to the axis of rotation.

The response to rotation has a characteristic temporal sequence. When the head starts turning, the relative motion of the endolymph deflects the cupula and changes hair-cell activity. If rotation continues at a constant angular velocity for a sufficiently long time, viscous coupling brings the fluid closer to co-rotation with the canal, and the cupula gradually returns toward its resting position. When the head abruptly stops, fluid inertia can produce relative motion in the opposite direction, deflecting the cupula oppositely. The transient neural response may therefore suggest movement opposite to the original rotation. These dynamics explain why the canal does not signal indefinitely the same constant rate of rotation under prolonged steady conditions.

The statement that semicircular canals detect angular acceleration is useful but requires qualification. Angular acceleration drives relative fluid motion and initiates canal responses. Over an important range of natural head movements, the mechanical dynamics and afferent properties allow the canal signal to approximate angular velocity. In a sustained constant-velocity rotation, the mechanical response eventually decays. In an extremely slow or unusual stimulus, the representation may differ from a simple velocity signal. Thus the canal is a dynamic sensor rather than an ideal mathematical instrument that measures only acceleration at all times or perfectly reports angular velocity for arbitrarily long periods.

A conceptual first-order model illustrates the return toward baseline during constant rotation. After a rapid onset, suppose cupular displacement x starts at x0 and then decays as x(t) = x0 exp(−t/τc), where τc is an effective mechanical time constant. In this teaching approximation, x and x0 have the same displacement unit and τc is in seconds. If x0 is assigned an arbitrary value of 10 and τc is 5 seconds, then after 5 seconds the displacement is about 3.68 units, and after 10 seconds it is about 1.35 units. Real semicircular canal mechanics can involve more than one time constant, and neural mechanisms may extend the functional response. The model is intended to help interpret the direction and decay of a maintained rotation response, not to provide a universal physiological time constant.

The three ducts of each labyrinth are oriented approximately so that their planes sample different components of rotation. A head movement may stimulate more than one canal if it has components in several relevant planes. The horizontal canals on the two sides form one functional pair. The vertical canals form approximately coplanar pairings between the anterior canal of one side and the posterior canal of the other. These pairings are mechanically and neurally useful because the hair cells on the paired sides have opposite directional orientations. They can provide a push-pull representation of head movement.

For a conventional head turn to the left about a vertical axis, the left horizontal canal is excited and the right horizontal canal is inhibited. This statement assumes an ordinary upright orientation and normal canal mechanics. The two changes are interpreted relative to their resting tonic rates. A simple illustrative pair might each discharge at 80 impulses per second at rest, then change to 120 and 40 during a brief turn. The difference in activity becomes 80 impulses per second even though their sum remains 160. An analysis that only added the two rates would miss the directional signal. A comparison of the rates preserves the sign of the motion. Opposite head rotation would reverse the sign of the difference.

A unilateral reduction of vestibular output can create an asymmetry at rest even without actual head rotation. The central nervous system normally uses relative activity between the paired organs as evidence about movement; loss of a tonic input can therefore resemble the activity pattern associated with motion. Adaptation and integration with other sensory signals may change the long-term response. This provides a physiologically grounded example of why sensory perception depends on patterns of neural activity, not merely the existence of an external stimulus. It also explains why one cannot infer the actual physical movement uniquely from a single afferent rate without knowing the baseline and the state of the paired system.

The mechanical response can be investigated using controlled rotations and recordings from vestibular nerve fibres. A useful experiment compares the onset of rotation, sustained rotation and stopping, rather than measuring only a single time point. Under the simple cupular model the responses at onset and stopping should have opposite signs, and the response during prolonged constant rotation should diminish. A comparison of left and right afferents would test the push-pull principle. Eye movements may persist somewhat differently from the peripheral neural signal because central processing can extend the apparent motion estimate. Hence the timing of the measurement and the recording level are important for deciding which mechanism the data support.

15. Utricle and saccule: gravity, translation and ambiguity

The otolith organs solve a different mechanical problem. Their receptor cells occupy a macula, a sensory epithelium covered by a gelatinous membrane whose superficial region contains calcium carbonate crystals called otoconia. The mineral particles make the otolithic membrane denser and more inertially significant than the surrounding fluid. When the head translates or changes orientation relative to gravity, the membrane can exert shear on the underlying hair bundles. The resultant deflection depends on both the acceleration acting on the membrane and the local direction of each receptor's polarity.

The utricular macula is oriented approximately horizontally when the head is upright, making it particularly useful for sensing horizontal linear acceleration and aspects of head tilt. The saccular macula is oriented more nearly vertically and is especially suited to detecting vertical components of acceleration. These are approximate anatomical descriptions; the surfaces are curved and their populations contain receptor polarities in multiple directions. It is therefore incorrect to assume that the utricle senses only one perfectly horizontal axis or that the saccule can detect only strictly vertical motion under all possible head orientations.

Hair-cell orientation varies across an otolith macula, with a reversal in polarity associated with a region called the striola. In the utricle many bundles are oriented toward the striola; in the saccule many are oriented away from it. This organisation gives different receptor populations complementary directional sensitivities. A single movement may excite some hair cells while inhibiting others, depending on how the shear vector projects onto their polarity directions. This pattern provides a basis for representing movement components rather than simply signalling that any acceleration occurred. A diagram should show the reversal of polarity rather than depicting all the bundles pointing identically across the macula.

A fundamental distinction between the otolith organs and the semicircular canals is the effect of sustained tilt. A head held tilted relative to gravity can exert a sustained component of force on the otolithic membrane. Unlike the cupula's transient response to a maintained rotation, this provides a persistent signal about the head's orientation relative to gravity. Similarly, a sufficiently sustained linear acceleration produces an inertial force on the otolithic membrane. Both effects can bend the bundles. The receptor cannot by itself identify which external process generated the observed shear.

The ambiguity can be expressed using vectors. Let g represent gravitational acceleration, directed downward, and a the translational acceleration of the head in an inertial reference frame. The gravito-inertial acceleration acting in head coordinates can be represented, under this convention, by g − a. A forward acceleration generates a backward inertial contribution; tilting the head changes the projection of gravity onto the sensory surface. If these effects produce the same effective vector, a peripheral otolith signal alone cannot distinguish them. Different texts may define specific force as a − g, the negative of this vector. The sign convention must be stated; the physical ambiguity is the same.

For a static tilt of angle θ from the upright orientation, the component of gravity parallel to an originally horizontal idealised macular plane has magnitude g sin θ. A tilt of 30 degrees gives 9.81 × 0.5 = 4.905 metres per second squared, in magnitude. A linear acceleration of about 4.9 metres per second squared along the appropriate horizontal direction could create an equal shear-related component in this simplified plane model. This equality does not imply that every three-dimensional component is identical or that all vestibular signals are the same, because the full gravitational vector and canal information may differ. It demonstrates the ambiguity of a single component measurement.

The brain can reduce this ambiguity by combining information from semicircular canals, vision, proprioception and expectations about movement. If the canals signal a change of head orientation and the otolith organs signal a changed gravity-related component, a tilt interpretation may be supported. If there is a strong otolith signal without matching evidence for rotation, translation may be more plausible. These are inferences that depend on the reliability and time course of the other signals. In unusual conditions the interpretations can be imperfect, explaining why vestibular perception is not a flawless physical measurement of the external world.

A useful comparison with cochlear transduction concerns the accessory apparatus. In the cochlea, vibration of the organ of Corti and associated structures bends the bundles in relation to sound frequency and intensity. In a canal, relative fluid motion bends the cupula. In the otolith organs, a dense mineral-loaded structure exerts shear in response to effective acceleration. All use direction-sensitive mechanically gated channels, but the physical relationship between the external stimulus and the displacement differs. This is a general biological strategy: a conserved cellular receptor mechanism can be coupled to specialised mechanical filters to produce different senses.
