1. Why a living cell must continually dismantle parts of itself

A cell can be alive for many years while most of the proteins that currently occupy it are much younger. This is possible because cellular identity is maintained by continuous renewal, not by retaining every original molecule. Proteins undergo chemical modification, membranes accumulate oxidized lipids and organelles can lose the ability to perform their work. Even normal metabolic reactions generate reactive oxygen species that may damage neighbouring structures. A cell that could synthesize new components but never remove old ones would gradually become crowded with material that interferes with its own metabolism.

Removal is not necessarily wasteful. A protein contains amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Hydrolysis breaks these bonds and releases smaller molecules from which new proteins can be made. Similarly, membrane lipids can be hydrolysed and their constituents reused or metabolized. The process of breaking down cellular material is called catabolism. Its products may provide substrates for anabolism, the synthesis of new cellular material, or for energy-producing reactions. The economic advantage is especially clear when extracellular nutrients temporarily become scarce: the cell already possesses internal reserves that can be mobilized.

Autophagy is a collection of regulated pathways that transfer intracellular material into lysosomes, or into the analogous lytic vacuoles of fungi and plants, for degradation. The name means self-eating, but the object being eaten is a portion of the cell, not necessarily the entire cell. The process may run at a low basal rate in well-fed cells. Its activity may change considerably during starvation, damage or differentiation. Autophagy therefore contributes to both routine maintenance and adaptation.

Why is a special delivery pathway necessary? The acidic hydrolases of the lysosome are separated from the neutral cytosol by a membrane. A cytoplasmic ribosome or damaged mitochondrion cannot simply diffuse through that membrane. If a cell released lysosomal enzymes everywhere, it would lose spatial control over digestion. Autophagy solves the transport problem by bringing cargo into the degradative compartment while maintaining separation from the surrounding cytoplasm.

The prerequisite distinction from the chapter on lysosomes is important. A lysosome provides the degradative environment; autophagy determines how material from inside the cell reaches that environment. Endocytosis also supplies lysosomal material, but its initial cargo commonly comes from the extracellular space or plasma membrane. The ubiquitin–proteasome system removes many individual soluble proteins by unfolding them and threading them through a proteolytic chamber. A whole mitochondrion cannot enter that chamber. Autophagy can handle structures far larger than a single protein, although its functions overlap with those of the proteasome.

The following chapters on cellular physiology build on the principle that viability depends on a balance between synthesis, use, damage and removal. In the present chapter the central question is how this balance is achieved by autophagy without assuming that the presence of intracellular digestion means a cell is dying. The molecular examples use canonical mammalian macroautophagy unless another organism is identified.

Prerequisite reading: Lysosomes, intracellular membrane trafficking, mitochondria, apoptosis and necrosis.

2. From microscopic observation to a genetically controlled pathway

Electron microscopy revealed membrane-bounded compartments containing recognizable cytoplasmic components, including organelles. Their appearance suggested that material already inside a cell could be delivered to a digestive compartment. Morphology alone, however, could not establish the rate of transfer or show whether the process was specifically regulated. A rare compartment might reflect a rare formation event or a very rapid disappearance event.

Yeast provided a particularly informative experimental system. Its large vacuole performs degradative functions comparable to the mammalian lysosome. Takeshige and colleagues in 1992 used Saccharomyces cerevisiae deficient in vacuolar proteinases. When nutrients were removed, membrane-bound bodies accumulated within the vacuole. In normal cells these structures are normally digested and are difficult to observe by light microscopy. Inhibiting vacuolar degradation made a transient intermediate persist long enough to be seen. The material within the bodies resembled cytoplasm, supporting delivery from the cell interior rather than synthesis of new vacuolar particles.

Notice the experimental logic: changing the disappearance rate makes an otherwise short-lived product detectable. Starvation provides the physiological stimulus; the degradation-deficient background provides a means of trapping delivered material. Comparison with nutrient-replete conditions establishes the response to nutrient availability. Comparison with cells capable of normal digestion helps explain why lack of visible bodies does not establish lack of delivery. These observations do not mean that blocking digestion is beneficial to the cell. The block is an experimental window into a dynamic pathway.

In 1993, Tsukada and Ohsumi extended this principle through genetic screening. They searched for mutations that prevented accumulation of autophagic bodies under starvation in a background where the bodies could otherwise be detected. The resulting autophagy-defective mutants also showed defects in starvation-induced vacuolar protein degradation. Complementation analysis distinguished multiple genetic groups. These studies established that the pathway required specific gene products, rather than being simply a nonspecific collapse of the cell under stress. Many of these genes were later unified under the ATG (autophagy-related) nomenclature.

A mutant lacking visible bodies need not have failed at exactly the same stage as another mutant with the same appearance. It could fail to initiate sequestration, fail to expand a membrane, or fail to deliver a completed structure to the vacuole. The screen identifies genes necessary for the measured endpoint; subsequent localization, genetic rescue and biochemical experiments distinguish their roles. Reintroduction of a functional gene is especially valuable because it links the altered phenotype to the candidate gene rather than to unrelated mutations.

The double-membrane intermediates observed before vacuolar delivery are not the same as the single-membrane bodies observed inside the vacuole. The distinction follows from membrane fusion: the outer autophagosomal membrane merges with the vacuolar membrane, releasing the inner vesicle into the lumen. Yeast and mammals share much of the core machinery, but the arrangement of initiation sites and the fate of vacuolar bodies need not look identical. These discoveries form the basis of modern flux measurements, in which formation and degradation are studied separately.

3. Three routes to a degradative compartment

In macroautophagy, a membrane grows around a selected portion of cytoplasm and eventually seals it inside a double-membrane compartment called an autophagosome. The completed carrier subsequently interacts with the lysosomal or vacuolar system. The outer membrane fuses with a degradative compartment, allowing the inner membrane and cargo to be broken down. Macroautophagy is the most commonly intended meaning of autophagy in cell biology examination questions unless the question specifies another form.

In microautophagy, cargo is taken up directly by a degradative organelle or related endosomal compartment through remodelling of its own membrane. For a simple inward invagination, the cytosolic side of the limiting membrane bends into the lumen, enclosing a small portion of cytoplasm. Pinching off the neck releases a membrane-bounded internal body that can be digested. Some microautophagic routes involve protrusions rather than a simple inward pocket. The important contrast is that a separate double-membrane carrier is not constructed in the cytosol and then delivered to the target.

Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), best characterized in mammals, solves a different problem. A soluble protein contains an accessible targeting motif resembling the sequence KFERQ (the letters represent amino acid residues). Cytosolic HSC70, a molecular chaperone, recognizes an appropriate motif and brings the protein to the lysosomal membrane. Binding to the lysosomal receptor LAMP2A promotes assembly of a translocation complex. The substrate must unfold to cross the membrane; lysosomal chaperone activity assists its translocation. Once in the lumen the polypeptide is degraded by hydrolases. No surrounding transport vesicle is needed.

Recognition of a motif is necessary for many CMA substrates but the mere presence of such a sequence does not guarantee immediate degradation. It must be accessible in the protein's conformation, and the translocation machinery must function. Modification of a protein can alter recognition. A tightly aggregated particle or intact organelle cannot use the CMA channel as an intact object. Selective macroautophagy can instead isolate large assemblies.

All three pathways share the objective of bringing intracellular material to a degradative lumen, but they should not be imagined as successive stages of one sequence. In a question describing a double-membrane cytoplasmic carrier, macroautophagy is the expected interpretation. An inward lysosomal membrane pocket suggests microautophagy; direct translocation of an unfolded HSC70-bound protein through LAMP2A suggests CMA. The pathways can compensate partly for one another in some cells, but compensation does not make their molecular components interchangeable.

4. The nutrient decision: mTORC1, AMPK and ULK

A cell that is receiving adequate amino acids and growth signals can invest resources in synthesis. A starved cell cannot maintain the same expenditure indefinitely. Nutrient availability and cellular energy status therefore influence the rate at which autophagosomes are produced. Two conserved signalling principles are especially useful: the nutrient-responsive kinase complex mTORC1 and the energy-responsive kinase AMPK.

A kinase transfers phosphate groups to a substrate, often altering its activity or interactions. mTORC1 integrates inputs including amino acid availability and growth-related signals. Under nutrient-rich conditions it promotes anabolic activity and generally restrains the canonical autophagy initiation machinery. One of its relevant substrates is ULK1, a mammalian autophagy-initiating kinase related to yeast Atg1. When mTORC1 signalling decreases during nutrient deprivation, the inhibitory influence on initiation is reduced. This is not the same as increasing the number of lysosomes instantly; it changes permission for formation of new autophagic structures.

AMP-activated protein kinase, or AMPK, responds to cellular energy stress. ATP consumption coupled to insufficient regeneration increases ADP relative to ATP; adenylate kinase can interconvert two ADP molecules into ATP and AMP, making AMP a sensitive indicator of energy imbalance. AMP and ADP influence AMPK activation and its phosphorylation state. Activated AMPK favours processes that support energy conservation and restoration. In appropriate contexts it inhibits mTORC1 signalling and directly phosphorylates autophagy-initiation machinery, including ULK1. Kim and colleagues (2011) provided evidence that mTOR and AMPK regulate ULK1 through phosphorylation. Genetic and biochemical perturbations distinguished direct kinase regulation from a mere correlation between starvation and autophagy.

ULK1 acts within a complex containing proteins such as ATG13, FIP200 and ATG101. Activation helps organize downstream machinery at a site where an isolation membrane can begin to develop. Nutrient and energy signals therefore affect a physical cellular event: recruitment and organization of the machinery that will generate a membrane around cytoplasmic material. This is why a kinase inhibitor can change the number of structures observed without itself cutting a membrane or degrading a protein.

The regulatory relationship is conditional, not a simple on/off switch. Amino acid deprivation can reduce mTORC1 activity even when ATP has not fallen catastrophically. Conversely, glucose limitation may activate AMPK even if some amino acids remain. Mild energy stress can support a recycling response, but extreme ATP depletion can prevent membrane trafficking, proton pumping and other steps necessary to complete it. AMPK activity does not establish that lysosomal digestion actually occurred.

After nutrients become available again, mTORC1 can be reactivated. Amino acids generated by degradation can themselves contribute to restoration of nutrient signals. This feedback limits excessive resource consumption and helps restore growth when conditions permit. Basal turnover can continue even when nutrients are abundant, and selective damage signals can stimulate local autophagy despite the general nutrient state. The purpose of the regulation is to adapt the balance of synthesis and removal, rather than to destroy the entire cell at the first sign of starvation.

5. Building the phagophore: initiation, PI3P and membrane supply

The first recognizable membrane intermediate of macroautophagy is the phagophore, also called the isolation membrane. It is an open, curved cisternal structure. A cisterna is a flattened membrane-bounded space; in a phagophore two closely apposed membrane surfaces meet at a curved rim. Its open mouth faces cytoplasm. The narrow space between the two surfaces is distinct from the cytoplasmic region that will become cargo. This topology explains why a mature autophagosome has two separate membranes around its cargo.

In mammalian cells, initiation commonly takes place in association with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). A local lipid signal helps specify where the next group of autophagy proteins should assemble. The class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase VPS34 phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol to generate phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate, abbreviated PI3P. In the canonical autophagy-specific complex it works with VPS15, Beclin 1 and ATG14. Beclin 1 is the mammalian counterpart of yeast Atg6. PI3P is a membrane-associated signal, not a freely diffusing cytoplasmic messenger in this setting.

Proteins capable of binding PI3P become enriched on the developing membrane. WIPI proteins are examples in mammalian cells. WIPI2 helps recruit machinery involved in attachment of ATG8-family proteins to lipids. The sequence of events matters: activation of the initiation complex establishes a site, localized lipid phosphorylation marks the membrane, and the resulting docking platform organizes expansion. A mutation that prevents PI3P production may thus allow upstream nutrient sensing but prevent construction of a normal carrier.

A growing membrane needs a supply of phospholipids. It cannot acquire enough surface area simply by bending the same small patch more sharply. The ER and other membrane sources contribute through membrane contacts, lipid transfer and trafficking. ATG9-containing membrane structures participate in the early process, and ATG2 proteins help transfer lipids into the growing membrane. These components should not be interpreted as evidence that an intact ER cisterna detaches unchanged to become every autophagosome. Membrane supply is coordinated and involves multiple contributions; the relative contribution varies by context.

Consider a perturbation in which ULK signalling is increased but VPS34 activity in its autophagic complex is inhibited. Nutrient sensing has made initiation favourable, but the PI3P-dependent recruitment step is impaired. The prediction is that successful phagophore development will be reduced relative to a cell with intact downstream machinery. VPS34 also has roles in endosomal trafficking, so a broad VPS34 inhibitor may alter other membrane compartments. A phenotype caused by such an inhibitor is not automatically attributable to the autophagy-specific complex.

A phagophore is not a lysosome and does not yet provide an acidic hydrolase-rich lumen around its cargo. Sequestration first separates material from the cytoplasm. Digestion comes later. This separation is essential for interpreting microscopy: an open isolation membrane is an early transport structure even when the cell has many perfectly functional lysosomes nearby.

6. LC3 lipidation, membrane expansion and closure

The developing membrane recruits proteins that help organize its expansion and its interaction with cargo. Two related conjugation systems are central to conventional macroautophagy. Conjugation means covalently joining one molecule to another. In these systems the protein activation and transfer logic resembles ubiquitin conjugation, but the products are not simply signals for proteasomal degradation.

In the first system, the ubiquitin-like protein ATG12 is activated in an ATP-dependent reaction by ATG7, an E1-like enzyme. ATG10 acts as an E2-like transfer enzyme and ATG12 becomes covalently attached to ATG5. This conjugate associates with ATG16L1, generating a complex that promotes the second conjugation reaction at appropriate membranes. ATG16L1 helps position the reaction, allowing membrane identity to determine where lipidation takes place.

In the second system, mammalian LC3 is a member of the ATG8 protein family. A newly synthesized LC3 precursor is processed by the cysteine protease ATG4 to expose a terminal glycine, producing the soluble form called LC3-I. ATG7 activates LC3-I, ATG3 transfers it, and the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L1 complex facilitates conjugation of the glycine to the lipid phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). The lipidated form is conventionally called LC3-II. Its PE anchor allows membrane association. In yeast the homologous protein is Atg8. GABARAP proteins are other mammalian ATG8-family members; LC3B is commonly used as a practical marker, not the only protein capable of supporting autophagy.

LC3-II is found on both the cargo-facing and cytosol-facing surfaces of the developing autophagosomal membrane. Cargo receptors can interact with membrane-associated ATG8-family proteins, thereby helping position selected material at the site of sequestration. ATG8-family proteins also contribute to membrane remodelling and later maturation. The phagophore grows around cargo until the open rim closes. Membrane closure produces a sealed compartment with an outer membrane accessible from the cytosol and an inner membrane enclosing the cargo.

The LC3 molecules on the outside of a completed carrier have a different fate from those trapped inside. ATG4 can remove lipidated LC3 from the cytosol-accessible membrane and recycle it. LC3 retained on the inner membrane is normally carried into the degradative lumen and broken down with the inner membrane. This difference explains why LC3-II abundance is sensitive not only to lipidation but also to membrane recycling and lysosomal clearance.

A loss of ATG7 in a canonical system typically impairs both conjugation pathways and markedly reduces normal LC3 lipidation. Loss of ATG3 directly interferes with the LC3 conjugation step. Loss of a closure factor may leave early membranes with LC3 but impair formation of sealed carriers. None of these manipulations is identical to neutralizing a lysosome: a late block can leave LC3 lipidation intact and allow carriers to accumulate. LC3 is also recruited in some noncanonical single-membrane processes, so observing LC3 on a membrane does not establish that the membrane is a canonical autophagosome. These distinctions will be essential when interpreting experimental results.

7. Delivery to lysosomes, membrane topology and recycling

A sealed autophagosome contains cargo but has not yet completed the main degradative task. It must encounter and fuse with a suitable endolysosomal compartment. Cytoskeletal tracks and motors contribute to movement of autophagosomes and lysosomes in many animal cells. Movement increases the opportunity for encounters, but proximity is not identical to fusion. Two fluorescent objects may touch or overlap in a microscope image without exchanging their lumenal contents.

Tethering factors help bring the membranes together. The small GTPase RAB7 and the HOPS tethering complex participate in late endolysosomal fusion in many contexts. SNARE proteins then provide a mechanism for bringing lipid bilayers into close apposition. A representative mammalian complex includes autophagosomal syntaxin 17, SNAP29 and endolysosomal VAMP8. SNARE assembly helps overcome the energetic barrier to membrane fusion. Other combinations operate in particular contexts; the named example explains the principle rather than defining every autophagosome.

Fusion joins the outer autophagosomal membrane with the lysosomal limiting membrane. The inner autophagosomal membrane does not become a second limiting membrane of the lysosome. Instead it is released inside the common lumen, still wrapped around the captured material. The resulting degradative compartment is called an autolysosome. Autophagosomes may also fuse with endosomes before reaching lysosomes, forming intermediates often called amphisomes. The key outcome is eventual access to a functional degradative lumen, not an obligatory single direct route in every cell.

Lysosomal V-type ATPases use ATP hydrolysis to transport protons into the lumen, maintaining the acidic conditions under which many lysosomal hydrolases work efficiently. Proteases, lipases, nucleases and other hydrolases can dismantle the inner membrane and its cargo. The acidic environment is a property of the degradative compartment and does not follow simply from the appearance of a double membrane. If proton pumping fails, a fused compartment may remain poorly degradative even though delivery has occurred.

Hydrolysis generates amino acids, lipid-derived products and other small metabolites. Transporters can export many of these from the lumen into the cytosol. They can re-enter anabolic reactions or energy metabolism. For instance, released amino acids can support synthesis of proteins essential for stress recovery, or some can be metabolized to provide energy substrates. Autophagy itself requires energy and is not an ATP-generating reaction in the manner of glycolysis; it makes substrates accessible for other metabolic pathways.

At the end of a prolonged starvation response, lysosomal components may be recovered through membrane remodelling and reformation of lysosomes. This helps preserve future degradative capacity. It also reinforces a critical idea: cellular recycling is a cycle of delivery, degradation and restoration of resources, rather than merely the production of vesicles. A defect in the final hydrolytic or export steps can impair the physiological benefit even if upstream structures are abundant.

The distinction between docking and fusion was experimentally clarified by Itakura and colleagues (2012), who identified syntaxin 17 on completed autophagosomes and its interaction with SNAP29 and VAMP8. Disruption of this fusion machinery impaired autophagosome delivery to endolysosomal compartments. In a teaching interpretation, the important observation is that formation of carriers can persist when a later trafficking step fails. Measuring only upstream carrier abundance would conceal that failure. The finding also explains how a cell can couple acquisition of fusion machinery to completion of a carrier, rather than delivering every open phagophore immediately to a lysosome.

8. Bulk recycling and selective removal of damaged components

When extracellular nutrients fall, cells may increase bulk sequestration of cytoplasm to obtain substrates. Bulk does not mean that every cytoplasmic molecule is equally likely to enter the phagophore. Physical proximity, molecular organization and cell type influence sampling. The term contrasts with selective autophagy, in which particular cargo is recognized and coupled preferentially to the developing membrane.

One common recognition strategy uses ubiquitin. Ubiquitin is a small protein that can be attached covalently to other proteins. Depending on its arrangement and the binding proteins present, it can act as a signal for several different cellular outcomes. A protein carrying ubiquitin is not necessarily committed to the proteasome. Autophagy receptors can recognize ubiquitinated material and simultaneously bind ATG8-family proteins on the phagophore. SQSTM1, commonly called p62, illustrates this bridging function: a ubiquitin-binding region interacts with marked cargo, while its LC3-interacting region (LIR) binds membrane-associated ATG8-family proteins. Receptor clustering can help bring several cargo molecules into one site of sequestration.

The receptor is often captured together with cargo and degraded after lysosomal delivery. Consequently, p62 may accumulate when clearance is blocked, but its abundance also depends on gene expression, protein synthesis and other processes. More p62 does not prove less autophagy without context. Likewise, a low p62 level can arise from reduced synthesis rather than increased degradation. The usefulness of p62 is greatest when it is monitored alongside LC3 turnover and independent cargo measurements.

Selective pathways are named for their cargo. Mitophagy removes mitochondria or mitochondrial portions; aggrephagy removes protein aggregates; pexophagy removes peroxisomes; ER-phagy removes parts of the ER; and xenophagy targets intracellular foreign material such as certain microbes. Lipophagy contributes to the turnover of lipid droplets in suitable tissues. These names do not denote completely unrelated mechanisms. Much of the downstream macroautophagy machinery is shared, whereas recognition and recruitment differ. Some organelle receptors bind ATG8-family proteins directly without using a ubiquitin bridge.

Why selectively remove an organelle rather than digest random cytoplasm? A damaged mitochondrion may produce less ATP and more damaging reactive species than a healthy one. Retaining it can cost resources and threaten neighbouring structures. Removing all mitochondria indiscriminately would also be harmful, because remaining cells need energy production. Selective quality control couples removal to the state or developmental role of the organelle. A cell must also balance removal with synthesis of replacement components. Thus increased mitophagy can be helpful only if sufficient functional mitochondria remain or are produced.

Autophagy and the proteasome cooperate in maintaining protein quality. Soluble damaged proteins may be amenable to proteasomal processing, whereas larger aggregates can be too difficult to unfold and translocate individually. Macroautophagy packages such material instead. The distinction is a useful examination principle, not an absolute boundary; both pathways influence each other and share some molecular signals.

9. Mitophagy: recognizing mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria maintain a proton-motive force across the inner membrane. One component of this force is the electrical potential, commonly written Δψm. The potential supports ATP synthesis and contributes to import of many mitochondrial proteins. Loss of potential is therefore a meaningful indicator of dysfunction, although it does not invariably mean that a mitochondrion or the cell is irreversibly damaged.

The PINK1–Parkin pathway illustrates a molecular link between mitochondrial condition and selective disposal. PINK1 is a protein kinase. In many healthy polarized mitochondria, newly made PINK1 is imported and proteolytically processed, followed by turnover. When mitochondrial membrane potential is lost, the normal import and processing of PINK1 are impaired. Full-length PINK1 accumulates on the outer membrane, where it can phosphorylate ubiquitin and Parkin. Parkin is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that can attach ubiquitin to outer mitochondrial membrane proteins. Phosphorylated ubiquitin assists Parkin recruitment and activation, amplifying a signal on the damaged surface.

Autophagy receptors such as OPTN and NDP52 recognize marked mitochondrial material and help recruit autophagic machinery. The phagophore then grows around the damaged organelle or a portion of it. The completed carrier must still reach a functional lysosome. PINK1 stabilization, Parkin recruitment and ubiquitination are early signs of recognition; they are not by themselves proof of actual mitochondrial degradation.

Narendra and colleagues reported in 2008 that Parkin was preferentially recruited to impaired mitochondria and promoted their autophagic removal in cultured cells. Later work demonstrated that PINK1 stabilization was important for recruitment. Comparing cells with and without functional Parkin under depolarizing conditions, and checking the fate of mitochondrial material over time, helps establish a functional connection between recruitment and removal. These experiments illustrate a general method for distinguishing signal recognition from the actual disposal of cargo. They do not establish that every form of physiological mitophagy in every tissue requires Parkin.

Other mitochondrial receptors, including BNIP3, NIX and FUNDC1, can promote selective sequestration in particular physiological settings. NIX participates in removal of mitochondria during mammalian red blood cell maturation. This developmental example is conceptually distinct from the acute removal of a damaged mitochondrion. The differentiating cell deliberately remodels its organelle population to acquire the properties of a mature erythrocyte. Other clearance mechanisms also contribute; NIX is not an all-purpose explanation for every organelle loss during differentiation.

Mutations affecting PINK1 and Parkin are associated with some inherited forms of Parkinson disease, making mitochondrial quality control biologically relevant. This association does not mean that all Parkinson disease results from a single autophagy defect or that stimulating autophagy is an established universal treatment. At examination level the important connection is that loss of a quality-control pathway can permit persistence of dysfunctional mitochondria, which may increase cellular stress over time.

A cell lacking Parkin may still have basal autophagy, functional lysosomes and receptor-mediated mitophagy through other routes. Therefore, a selective-recognition defect is not equivalent to a complete lysosomal failure. This prediction also explains why measuring overall LC3 turnover alone may fail to detect impaired disposal of a specific organelle.

Sandoval and colleagues (2008) provided a developmental test of this principle by examining Nix-deficient erythroid cells. Persistence of mitochondrial material during maturation supported a requirement for Nix in efficient mitochondrial clearance in this system. The relevant comparison is between cells at comparable maturation stages, because an immature cell naturally contains mitochondria. Detecting mitochondrial proteins alone is not sufficient to identify the stage of the defect; examination of organelle persistence and maturation together is more informative. This example also separates the signal to remove a particular organelle from a general inability to digest all autophagic material.

10. Autophagy in starvation, tissue maintenance and stress adaptation

During short-term starvation, the supply of extracellular amino acids and energy substrates may decrease before the cell's essential processes stop. By mobilizing a fraction of intracellular material, autophagy can provide substrates that support maintenance and recovery. This response is especially valuable during transitions in nutrient availability. In mammals, the period immediately after birth illustrates a physiological nutrient transition: the placental supply ends before milk feeding is fully established. Experimental disruption of core autophagy genes in mice has shown the importance of this response in neonatal adaptation. The relevant mechanism is delivery of usable metabolites, not simply the visibility of autophagic structures.

The response has a limit. If a cell lacks adequate energy to construct carriers, acidify lysosomes or transport degradation products, stimulation of initiation may not restore function. Prolonged starvation also reduces the quantity of nonessential material that can be mobilized. A cell cannot indefinitely support its energy demands by consuming its essential structures. Survival depends on the severity and duration of the stress, the tissue's metabolic reserve, the efficiency of clearance and the possibility of replenishment.

Basal autophagy is particularly important in long-lived cells such as neurons. Dividing cells can distribute some accumulated material between daughter cells, but many mature neurons do not regularly divide. Persistent aggregates and damaged organelles can interfere with transport and metabolism over long periods. Turnover of such material helps preserve the intracellular environment. Axonal transport is another reason spatial coordination matters: carriers formed in one part of a cell may need to mature as they move toward regions containing degradative compartments.

Autophagy also contributes to cellular adaptation during infection. Cytosolic bacteria or damaged pathogen-containing compartments can acquire recognition signals that lead to sequestration and lysosomal delivery. This is one component of cell-autonomous defense, meaning defense carried out within an individual cell. Some pathogens evade capture, resist degradation or exploit membrane-remodelling pathways. Thus an increase in autophagy-related markers during infection cannot automatically be interpreted as successful pathogen elimination.

Plants and fungi use lytic vacuoles rather than mammalian-type lysosomes as their principal destination for macroautophagic material. During nutrient stress or developmental remodelling, delivery to vacuoles supports redistribution and recovery of cellular resources. In plants, nutrient recycling can contribute to remobilization from older tissues. This does not imply that every senescing plant cell survives because of autophagy; recycling may serve the remaining organism during developmental transitions. The core biological idea is conserved even though organelle names and molecular details differ.

Autophagy has context-dependent relationships with cancer. In normal cells, removal of damaged organelles and proteins can restrain sources of cellular stress. In an established tumour exposed to poor oxygen or nutrient supply, an intact recycling system may instead help tumour cells survive. These are distinct biological questions asked at different stages. One cannot infer from them that autophagy is always tumour suppressive or always tumour promoting. Detailed cancer signaling belongs to a later unit; here the example demonstrates that the same homeostatic mechanism can have different consequences at the organismal level.

The neonatal study by Kuma and colleagues (2004) illustrates why a whole-animal outcome must be interpreted together with cellular metabolism. Atg5-deficient mice appeared broadly normal at delivery but failed to survive the early postnatal period. Measurements during the transition implicated loss of normal nutrient recycling. Comparing newborn mutant and control littermates at matched times helps separate an early adaptation defect from an arbitrary difference in age. The experiment supports a physiological requirement for autophagy in this model, not the conclusion that every consequence of Atg5 loss is mediated exclusively through starvation. Feeding behaviour and tissue-specific requirements can influence whole-animal survival.

In an intact tissue, release of metabolites has significance beyond the cell in which digestion takes place. A liver cell can use released carbon skeletons in intermediary metabolism; a plant leaf can help remobilize nitrogen-containing compounds during senescence. The organism still needs transport systems to move usable products from source to sink. It is therefore necessary to distinguish cellular recycling, which frees metabolites locally, from redistribution, which depends on transport and organism-level physiology. This distinction explains why autophagy can support both survival of an individual cell and resource allocation across tissues, without making every autophagic cell a dying cell.

11. Autophagy, apoptosis and necrosis are not interchangeable

Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death with characteristic molecular and morphological changes, commonly involving caspase activation, chromatin condensation and cellular fragmentation. Necrosis describes a cell death pattern characterized by loss of plasma-membrane integrity, often with swelling and release of intracellular material; some necrotic deaths are regulated. Autophagy, in contrast, is a material turnover pathway. Its usual purpose is to maintain or restore cellular function. The three concepts are therefore not simply three equivalent mechanisms for ending a cell's life.

A stressed cell may activate autophagy while retaining intact membranes and normal long-term viability. It may recover because recycling supplies metabolites or removes damaged structures. In another cell the same stress can exceed the available adaptive capacity and activate apoptosis. In a severely injured cell, loss of ion gradients and membrane integrity may dominate the outcome. Autophagic structures can be found in both surviving and dying cells because the response can be activated before the ultimate fate has been decided.

There are molecular points of interaction. Beclin 1 can interact with anti-apoptotic BCL-2-family proteins, and under some conditions this interaction limits autophagy initiation. Stress-dependent changes can release that restraint. Mitochondrial quality control can also reduce sources of signals that might contribute to apoptosis. These observations do not make Beclin 1 identical to BAX or make autophagy a branch of the caspase cascade. They show that a cell coordinates resource management and death decisions through intersecting regulatory systems.

A rise in autophagosome number in a dying cell supports the observation that autophagic compartments are present. It does not establish why the cell died. Autophagy may be protective but insufficient; it may be blocked; or under specific conditions it may contribute causally to death. The term autophagy-dependent cell death is reserved for situations in which the autophagic machinery is required for the lethal process. This requires functional evidence, not merely extensive vacuolization or LC3 accumulation.

To test dependence, compare the same stress in cells with intact autophagy and in cells with targeted disruption of essential autophagy machinery. If disabling the pathway increases death, the result is consistent with a protective role under that condition. If disabling the pathway reduces death, autophagy may be contributing to the lethal outcome, but alternative functions of the targeted proteins and other death pathways must be investigated. More than one independent genetic perturbation, rescue with a functional gene and confirmation that the intended flux change occurred strengthen the inference. A nonspecific drug that protects cells is insufficient by itself.

Death measurements must also distinguish a transient loss of metabolic activity from irreversible loss of viability. For example, starvation can reduce proliferation and metabolic assay signal in cells that will resume growth after feeding. Conversely, a membrane-impermeant dye identifies permeable cells but may not distinguish primary necrotic injury from late apoptosis. In a mechanistic experiment, markers of autophagy, markers of the proposed death pathway and a meaningful viability endpoint answer different questions. Autophagy is not automatically a programmed cell death mechanism.

12. Why autophagosome abundance is not autophagic flux

Imagine a microscope field containing ten autophagosomes per cell. After a treatment there are twenty. This increase can have two opposite explanations: carriers may be made faster, or they may disappear more slowly. A static picture cannot distinguish the two. Autophagic flux describes movement of material through the pathway over time, including successful delivery and degradation. The number of compartments present at an instant is a stock; the amount passing through during an interval is a flow.

A useful teaching model defines N as the average number of autophagosomes in a cell, F as their formation rate in carriers per cell per hour, and R as their removal rate from the counted pool in the same units. Over a short time interval Δt, the change in the stock equals newly formed carriers minus removed carriers. Hence ΔN = (F − R)Δt when the rates remain approximately constant. Dividing by Δt and considering a sufficiently short interval gives dN/dt = F − R. This equation is a bookkeeping identity, not a claim that the underlying biochemical reactions are simple.

If each carrier has an approximately constant probability of leaving the counted pool per unit time, a first-order removal model writes R = kN, where k has units of inverse hours. At steady state dN/dt = 0; formation balances removal, so F = kN and N = F/k. The ratio 1/k is the mean residence time in this idealized model. A high N can thus arise from a large F, a small k, or both. The model assumes a uniform pool with no major changes in cell volume, carrier identity or removal mechanism. Real autophagosomes have multiple maturation steps and varying cargo loads.

Worked example 1. A control cell forms 6 carriers per hour and has k = 0.5 per hour. Its predicted steady stock is 6/0.5 = 12 carriers. If starvation doubles formation to 12 per hour with the same removal coefficient, the steady stock is 24 and the removal rate is 12 per hour. If a different treatment instead halves k to 0.25 per hour while F remains 6, the stock is also 24 but removal at the new steady state is only 6 per hour. Identical counts therefore do not imply identical flux.

An even stronger late block may nearly stop removal. If F is unchanged during a short observation, N will rise approximately linearly rather than approach a finite steady state. Worked example 2. Starting at 8 carriers, a complete clearance block with formation of 5 carriers per hour yields N = 8 + 5 × 3 = 23 after three hours. If formation also shuts down after the first hour, the final count is instead 13. A drug may change both formation and removal; one cannot blindly extrapolate the initial slope indefinitely.

Loss of a counted autophagosome can reflect fusion rather than final hydrolysis. If the measurement includes only nonacidified carriers, its removal rate is a maturation rate, not necessarily the rate of cargo degradation. This is why an experimental definition of the counted pool must accompany every equation. A separate measurement of cargo loss provides information about completion of the pathway. The model is useful for understanding inference, not a replacement for biological measurements.

13. Measuring flux: complementary assays and essential controls

A classical assay measures LC3-I and LC3-II by immunoblotting, in which proteins are separated and detected with antibodies. Because of the lipid modification, LC3-II commonly migrates faster than LC3-I during standard SDS-PAGE despite having the covalently attached lipid. The intensity of LC3-II bands can be compared across samples when equal loading, linear detection and appropriate normalization are established. It is not enough to compare the darkness of bands from different exposures.

A short treatment with an effective late-stage inhibitor can help estimate LC3 turnover. Bafilomycin A1 inhibits the vacuolar-type proton ATPase and can impair acidification and autophagosome maturation or degradation. If LC3-II continues to be delivered to a pathway in which its destruction is now prevented, the lipidated marker tends to accumulate relative to an otherwise matched sample without the inhibitor. The relevant comparison uses each experimental condition with and without inhibition over the same interval. A single inhibitor-treated group without its matched untreated group cannot separate baseline formation from impaired removal.

Worked example 3. Consider normalized LC3-II signals in arbitrary units after an equal short interval: untreated control 20, control plus inhibitor 50, starvation 35, starvation plus inhibitor 95. The inhibitor-associated difference is 50 − 20 = 30 units in control and 95 − 35 = 60 units in starvation. Under the assumptions of adequate inhibition, comparable loading and detection, and no major change in formation caused by the inhibitor, the difference is consistent with increased LC3 turnover during starvation. The ratio of differences is 60/30 = 2. This is a relative marker-turnover estimate, not proof that exactly twice as much cytoplasmic mass was degraded.

Now consider a treatment producing LC3-II signals of 75 without inhibitor and 79 with it. The difference is only 4 units despite the high untreated signal. In the same assay, this is consistent with an existing late block or very low remaining LC3 turnover. It could also reflect insufficient additional inhibition, a saturated signal or altered LC3 expression. Appropriate controls and independent evidence are required. The correct exam interpretation is to favour the late-block explanation when the assumptions are explicitly supplied, not to pronounce a definitive mechanism from two bands alone.

Fluorescence microscopy provides spatial information. An LC3 reporter may appear diffuse in cytoplasm and punctate on membrane-associated structures. A tandem mCherry–GFP–LC3 reporter uses two fluorophores with different sensitivities to acidity. In a relatively nonacidic autophagosome, both GFP and mCherry can fluoresce; the merged signal appears yellow. After delivery to a sufficiently acidic compartment, GFP fluorescence is preferentially quenched while mCherry is relatively retained, producing a red-only punctum. This reports exposure to an acidic environment; it does not directly measure the disappearance of every cargo molecule.

Worked example 4. In a matched field, control cells contain 18 yellow and 12 red-only puncta per cell. A treatment produces 36 yellow and 4 red-only puncta. The red-only fraction falls from 12/(18 + 12) = 0.40 to 4/(36 + 4) = 0.10. This pattern is consistent with reduced progression to an acidic compartment, even though the total puncta count rises from 30 to 40. If the treatment neutralizes the lumen, GFP may remain fluorescent after fusion; the reporter alone cannot distinguish failed fusion from failed acidification. A lysosomal pH measurement and a fusion-sensitive assay help resolve that difference.

p62 turnover can provide a complementary cargo-receptor measure, but changes in transcription or translation can alter its abundance. Measuring degradation of a defined labelled cargo, with a matched inhibitor or genetic control, tests whether material actually disappears through the lysosomal pathway. Electron microscopy helps recognize the characteristic double membrane and its enclosed contents, but a static micrograph cannot demonstrate the rate of delivery. An adequate study combines structure, marker turnover and cargo fate wherever possible, with controls for viability, cell number, expression and imaging conditions. This experimental logic is emphasized in the international autophagy assay guidelines.

A further control addresses the difference between an inhibitor-sensitive marker and the fate of total cargo. An inhibitor can alter cellular physiology beyond its intended target, especially when exposure is prolonged. In an informative short-interval experiment, vehicle-treated controls establish the background, inhibitor-treated controls establish the normal turnover response, and matched treated groups reveal the effect of the experimental perturbation. Repeated independent cultures help distinguish a reproducible biological difference from sampling noise. If the inhibitor does not produce an expected response in an otherwise healthy control, the interpretation of every inhibitor-based comparison is weakened. If the cells lose viability during the experiment, leakage or altered protein synthesis can change measurements for reasons unrelated to autophagic traffic.

A pH-insensitive cargo marker provides a useful complementary approach to a tandem fluorescence reporter. For instance, tracking a defined labelled protein from cytoplasm to a lysosomal compartment and then measuring its disappearance can distinguish delivery from destruction. If the label persists in a compartment that contains lysosomal markers, possible explanations include ineffective acidification or defective hydrolases. If the cargo never reaches that compartment, an earlier delivery defect is more plausible. Colocalization in a two-dimensional image is suggestive rather than definitive, because objects lying above one another can appear coincident. Multiple observations along the pathway provide a stronger mechanistic answer than additional repetitions of the same ambiguous marker.

14. Interpreting perturbations and integrating the pathway

To localize a defect, follow the sequence from stimulus to nutrient regulation, initiation, membrane nucleation, expansion, sealing, delivery, acidification, hydrolysis and release of products. A lesion at an early step is expected to reduce the downstream supply of carriers, whereas a lesion at a late step can create a backlog of upstream intermediates. This general pattern is useful but not absolute: feedback, altered synthesis and cell injury can change earlier steps during prolonged experiments.

Consider three hypothetical cell lines under amino acid starvation. Line A has a defective initiation complex and produces few PI3P-positive phagophores. It also has little new LC3-II and little lysosomal cargo delivery. Line B produces PI3P-positive membranes but cannot efficiently lipidate LC3 because ATG7 is absent. Early structures may appear, yet normal sequestration and maturation are impaired. Line C has intact lipidation but poorly acidified lysosomes. Carriers and p62 may accumulate while degradation of a defined cargo decreases. These three lines all show low successful cargo recycling, but their intermediate patterns differ.

If an experiment introduces a functional copy of the defective gene into line B, restoration of LC3 lipidation together with cargo degradation is stronger evidence of a causal role than restoration of a single marker. If gene replacement restores LC3-II but not degradation, another defect may remain. Similarly, increasing ULK1 activity in line C could produce more carriers without solving the downstream acidification problem. A rational perturbation is therefore chosen according to the stage suspected to be limiting, rather than treating all increases in autophagy-related structures as improvements.

Worked example 5. A labelled mitochondrial protein starts at 100 arbitrary units in each culture. After four hours, 60 units remain in a control culture and 90 units remain in a culture with a lysosomal inhibitor. Assuming comparable cell numbers, no newly labelled protein, equal nonlysosomal losses and reliable measurements, 40 units disappeared in control and 10 in the inhibited sample. The difference, 30 units, is the inhibitor-sensitive component during the four-hour window, or 7.5 units per hour averaged over that interval. It is not necessarily all mitophagy: the protein could undergo other lysosome-dependent turnover. Evidence of delivery of the organelle, rather than merely loss of one protein, is needed to conclude whole-organelle disposal.

The most common errors in this topic arise from confusing compartments, mechanisms and measurements. A phagophore is open; an autophagosome is closed and double-membraned; an autolysosome is a degradative hybrid compartment. LC3 lipidation is an upstream molecular event, not a direct measurement of metabolite recycling. Nutrient withdrawal is a signal for adaptation, not a guarantee of completed degradation. Autophagic vesicles in a dead cell do not establish autophagy-dependent death. The three main autophagic routes deliver intracellular substrates by different membrane arrangements, rather than constituting three successive stages.

A complete explanation should be reconstructible from the biological problem. Cells require selective removal and nutrient recovery; the lysosome provides a spatially isolated degradative environment; initiation and lipid signals organize a membrane; conjugation and cargo recognition enable sequestration; fusion and acid-dependent enzymes make products available; and nutrient feedback adjusts the response. This chain explains the observations and the perturbation predictions. The next subunit turns to microbial growth and survival strategies. Microbial physiology shares the problem of resource limitation, but bacteria do not possess the canonical eukaryotic lysosomal autophagy pathway. Their growth and stress responses require distinct explanations.

15. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style practice. Answer each question before opening its explanation.

B1. A structure has two membranes and contains a region of cytoplasm. Which is most likely?

A. CMA channel; B. Autophagosome; C. Lysosome before fusion; D. Proteasome

Answer and reasoning

B. A closed double membrane surrounding cytoplasmic cargo identifies a typical autophagosome. A CMA channel translocates proteins individually; a lysosome has one limiting membrane; a proteasome is a protein complex without a lipid membrane.

B2. Which event produces LC3-II?

A. LC3 binding DNA; B. ATG4 cleavage alone; C. LC3 conjugation to PE; D. LC3 cleavage by caspase

Answer and reasoning

C. PE attachment creates membrane-associated LC3-II. ATG4 processing first exposes the glycine, producing LC3-I; DNA binding and caspase cleavage do not define this conjugation.

B3. Which is an immediate product of autophagic VPS34 activity?

A. PI3P; B. ATP; C. Cytochrome c; D. Ubiquitin

Answer and reasoning

A. The kinase phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol to PI3P, a local recruitment signal. ATP is used rather than produced; cytochrome c belongs to mitochondrial respiration and apoptosis; ubiquitin is a protein modifier.

B4. What is the direct receptor for canonical CMA at the lysosomal membrane?

A. RAB7; B. LAMP2A; C. ATG7; D. BAX

Answer and reasoning

B. LAMP2A supports substrate translocation. RAB7 participates in trafficking, ATG7 is a conjugation enzyme, and BAX promotes mitochondrial apoptosis.

B5. Which event is most directly expected after fusion of the outer autophagosome membrane with a lysosome?

A. Inner membrane becomes the outer plasma membrane; B. Cargo is released into cytosol; C. Inner vesicle enters lysosomal lumen; D. A new nucleus forms

Answer and reasoning

C. Outer membrane continuity joins the compartments and places the inner vesicle in the lumen. The cargo remains isolated until hydrolysis; the plasma membrane and nucleus are unrelated.

B6. What does a cargo receptor such as p62 accomplish?

A. Bridges marked cargo to ATG8-family proteins; B. Pumps lysosomal protons; C. Replicates mitochondrial DNA; D. Cleaves all proteins in cytosol

Answer and reasoning

A. Its distinct binding regions couple selected cargo to a forming membrane. Proton pumping belongs to V-ATPase; DNA replication and nonspecific cytosolic proteolysis are not its cargo-receptor function.

B7. Which statement about mTORC1 in the canonical nutrient-rich response is correct?

A. It directly acidifies lysosomes; B. It generally restrains ULK-dependent initiation; C. It destroys all mitochondria; D. It is the same protein as AMPK

Answer and reasoning

B. Nutrient-responsive mTORC1 generally inhibits initiation. It is a kinase complex, not a proton pump or organelle degradation enzyme; AMPK is a distinct energy sensor.

B8. Assertion: Autophagy can support survival during starvation. Reason: Lysosomal degradation can release substrates for cellular metabolism.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, no explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning

A. Recovered metabolites can sustain essential functions. B overlooks the direct causal link; C denies the known recycling outcome; D incorrectly treats autophagy as necessarily lethal.

B9. Assertion: More LC3 puncta always indicate greater completed cargo degradation. Reason: Puncta can accumulate when clearance is impaired.

A. Both true, explanatory; B. Both true, not explanatory; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning

D. Impaired clearance can increase puncta without improving degradation. A and B incorrectly accept the absolute assertion; C reverses the truth of both statements.

B10. Which distinction between apoptosis and autophagy is most accurate?

A. Both necessarily cause death; B. Autophagy is a recycling pathway, apoptosis is a regulated death mechanism; C. Apoptosis requires lysosomal acidification as its defining event; D. Autophagy always ruptures the plasma membrane

Answer and reasoning

B. Autophagy commonly supports viability. A and D misclassify turnover as death; C substitutes lysosomal digestion for the characteristic apoptotic machinery.

16. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

All numerical datasets in these questions are hypothetical teaching examples.

C1. Control cells show LC3-II values 10 without and 34 with inhibitor; treated cells show 22 and 70. Calculate the difference and infer turnover under matched conditions.

Answer and reasoning

Control difference 24; treatment 48; ratio 2. The result supports higher inhibitor-sensitive LC3 turnover, assuming comparable inhibitor effectiveness, linear signal and formation during the interval. Comparing only 22 to 10 would confuse marker abundance with turnover.

C2. Two cells each contain 30 carriers at steady state. Cell A forms 15 per hour, B forms 5 per hour. Estimate k and mean residence time for each using the first-order model.

Answer and reasoning

kA = 15/30 = 0.5 h⁻¹, residence 2 h. kB = 5/30 = 1/6 h⁻¹, residence 6 h. Equal stocks hide different turnover. It would be incorrect to assign equal clearance rates to the two cells.

C3. A cell has 9 carriers at the start of an effective complete clearance block. Formation is 4 carriers per hour for 2.5 hours. Predict the number.

Answer and reasoning

9 + 4 × 2.5 = 19 carriers. The result assumes formation remains constant and the measured pool has no other exit. Multiplying 9 by the formation rate incorrectly treats it as a fractional growth constant.

C4. A lysosomal V-ATPase inhibitor increases yellow tandem-reporter puncta and reduces red-only puncta. Does this prove membrane fusion failed?

Answer and reasoning

No. Neutralization can preserve GFP fluorescence even in fused compartments. Distinguish acidification from physical fusion by measuring pH and using an independent delivery or fusion assay; cargo degradation may be impaired in either case.

C5. Starvation causes more p62 protein, but p62 mRNA also rises. LC3-II turnover increases with an inhibitor. Is reduced autophagy established?

Answer and reasoning

No. p62 synthesis may increase at the same time that its degradation increases. LC3 turnover supports active traffic but cargo fate requires independent measurement. A p62 snapshot alone is ambiguous.

C6. A mutation prevents LC3 attachment to PE but starvation still produces PI3P-rich membranes. Which stage is most directly impaired?

Answer and reasoning

The conjugation-dependent expansion and maturation machinery is impaired downstream of lipid nucleation. Upstream PI3P formation remains demonstrable. A pure late lysosome defect would not directly abolish LC3 lipidation.

C7. A mutant lacks Parkin but has normal bulk LC3 turnover. A depolarized mitochondrial marker persists. Explain the observations.

Answer and reasoning

Selective cargo recognition can be defective despite functioning core macroautophagy. Compare with restored Parkin, monitor mitochondrial delivery and degradation, and verify damage. Normal bulk flux does not exclude a selective mitophagy defect; loss of Parkin need not block all other mitophagy routes.

C8. A labelled cargo starts at 80 units and falls to 32 after four hours; a matched late-inhibited culture retains 68. Estimate the inhibitor-sensitive disappearance.

Answer and reasoning

Uninhibited loss = 48 units; inhibited loss = 12 units. Difference = 36 units in four hours, or 9 units per hour on average. The estimate depends on equal nonlysosomal loss and cell normalization; it does not establish absolute mass unless the signal is calibrated.

C9. Stress kills 60% of wild-type cells and 85% of an ATG-deficient line. Rescue restores the wild-type phenotype and confirmed flux. What is the most plausible role?

Answer and reasoning

Under these conditions, autophagy is protective: its loss increases death by 25 percentage points, and genetic rescue supports causality. This does not prove all stress responses are protective. A second independent perturbation and viability endpoint would strengthen the result.

C10. A different stress kills 70% of control cells and 25% after inhibition of two distinct essential ATG genes, with confirmed loss of flux. Can death be labelled autophagy-dependent immediately?

Answer and reasoning

The reduction provides evidence of dependence, stronger than morphology alone. Exclude unrelated gene effects, restore gene function, examine alternative death pathways and confirm irreversible death. Autophagy may be causally contributing but the mechanism needs corroboration.

C11. An HSC70-bound motif-bearing enzyme remains soluble and is degraded in normal lysosomes but not when LAMP2A is depleted; LC3 turnover is unchanged. Which pathway best explains this?

Answer and reasoning

CMA directly transfers suitable soluble substrates through LAMP2A. Unchanged LC3 turnover is compatible with intact macroautophagy. Microautophagy may also recognize some chaperone-bound cargo, but the specific LAMP2A dependence supports CMA.

C12. A treatment increases ULK activity but decreases lysosomal protease activity. Use the stock–flow graph in Section 12 to predict what happens to recycling and interpret an LC3-II increase.

Answer and reasoning

Initiation may rise while terminal degradation falls. LC3-II may accumulate due to both increased production and reduced clearance; the actual cargo recycling rate can decline. Compare matched inhibitor conditions and cargo fate, not marker abundance alone.
