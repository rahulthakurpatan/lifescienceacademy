1. The problem of coordinated plant growth

A seedling growing beside a window usually bends toward the brighter side. At first glance this seems like movement toward a stimulus, but the plant does not relocate its entire body. Instead, cells on opposite sides of its growing organ acquire different lengths. If the side away from the window elongates faster, the tip is displaced toward the light. The ability to control differences in growth is especially important in plants because the position of an individual cell is constrained by its wall and attachment to neighbouring cells. To change the form of the organ, the plant must regulate the direction, rate and duration of cell division and expansion. A chemical signal that is made in one region and influences another can provide a link between perception of the environment and the mechanical consequences of growth.

Auxin is a family of plant growth regulators whose predominant natural representative in many flowering plants is indole-3-acetic acid, abbreviated IAA. The word hormone describes a signalling substance acting at physiologically relevant concentrations to regulate a response. It does not mean that the substance is a bulk structural material or source of energy. In an elongating cell, for example, auxin does not supply the cellulose used to build the wall, nor does it supply most of the ATP required to maintain ion gradients. Instead, it changes the activities of proteins and genes that determine whether expansion is possible. Sugar availability, water supply, wall structure, membrane transport and developmental competence all influence the eventual outcome.

A second distinction is between auxin amount and auxin response. A cell containing a particular concentration of free IAA need not respond identically to another cell with the same concentration. The receptor composition, abundance of transcriptional repressors, identity of target genes and wall properties may differ. A seedling root is often inhibited by concentrations that promote shoot elongation. A developing lateral root primordium may require a local auxin maximum even though high auxin inhibits the extension of the primary root. These are distinct processes, taking place in different cell populations on different timescales. A useful explanation must therefore identify the tissue and measured response rather than calling auxin a universal growth promoter.

Three questions recur throughout auxin physiology: where does active hormone come from, how does its distribution change, and how is the distribution translated into a biological response? The first question involves biosynthesis and metabolism. The second involves membrane transport, the physical properties of IAA and the polarity of cells. The third involves receptors, regulatory proteins, wall loosening and changes in development. Their interactions are reciprocal: auxin can influence expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters, so the signal itself can change the system responsible for making and distributing it.

2. Classical experiments: from the coleoptile to a mobile signal

The coleoptile is a protective sheath surrounding the young shoot of many grass seedlings. Its tip can perceive directional illumination while elongation in regions farther below produces curvature. Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin examined the relationship between these two regions in experiments described in The Power of Movement in Plants in 1880. Covering the tip with an opaque cap prevented normal bending toward lateral light, whereas allowing illumination at the tip while shielding the lower region permitted curvature. Removal of the tip also prevented the usual response. The essential conclusion was that light perception and the execution of differential growth could be spatially separated.

That conclusion did not identify a chemical signal. A surgical cut could interrupt several types of communication, including electrical continuity and the movement of molecules. Boysen-Jensen subsequently used permeable and impermeable barriers to investigate whether a transferable substance could cross the interface between a coleoptile tip and lower tissue. A gelatin layer could allow growth information to pass, whereas an impermeable barrier prevented transmission across the blocked region. The result was consistent with a diffusible chemical factor, although the experiment alone did not establish its molecular identity or the nature of its receptors.

The next important question was whether a growth-regulating material collected from a tip could act after separation from the original living source. In Went's coleoptile experiments, material diffusing from excised tips into agar was transferred to decapitated coleoptiles. An asymmetrically placed block containing the growth-promoting material induced curvature in the absence of the original directional illumination. A plain agar block provided a comparison. A symmetrically placed active block could stimulate elongation without imposing the same lateral asymmetry. These observations made it possible to study hormone activity through a measurable response rather than relying only on the presence of an intact illuminated tip.

The curvature assay is a biological assay: the response of living tissue is used to infer the activity of a sample. Within a calibrated range, larger amounts of biologically active material can produce larger responses. Outside that range, saturation or inhibition may occur. A bioassay is not automatically a chemical measurement. Another compound acting through the same or a different growth pathway could produce curvature. Purification and chemical identification were therefore required before the active factor could be assigned to IAA. Modern analytical methods such as chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry distinguish IAA from chemically different compounds, while genetic and reporter methods test the relationship between its distribution and the response.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which four decapitated coleoptiles receive respectively a plain agar block, an active block centred on the cut surface, an active block on the left and an active block on the right. All are maintained in darkness and measured over the same interval. The first treatment estimates growth after removal of the tip and manipulation; the second demonstrates that growth can increase without lateral curvature; the final pair tests the predicted reversal of curvature when the signal source changes sides. Identical block sizes and handling, random assignment and repeated specimens help distinguish a systematic effect from variability among seedlings. The logic is still valuable in contemporary experiments: independent manipulation of location, concentration and signalling competence is often more informative than measuring a single endpoint.

3. IAA chemistry and the identity of the active pool

IAA contains an indole ring and an acetic acid side chain attached at the third position of the ring. Its formula is C10H9NO2. The indole portion is a fused aromatic ring system containing nitrogen, whereas the side chain ends in a carboxyl group. At physiological pH, the ionization state of that carboxyl group is particularly important because neutral and charged molecules differ in their ability to cross a lipid bilayer. The compound is a weak acid, with a pKa near 4.75. The symbol IAAH denotes the protonated neutral species and IAA− denotes its deprotonated anion. The two forms interconvert rapidly according to local pH; they are not distinct auxin hormones.

Auxin is a functional classification rather than a statement that every active compound has the same molecular structure. Indole-3-butyric acid, IBA, occurs naturally in plants and can contribute to IAA supply after peroxisomal shortening of its side chain. Phenylacetic acid is another natural auxin with different transport characteristics. The synthetic regulators 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) and 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) are useful in experiments and horticulture, but they differ from IAA in uptake, metabolism and persistence. A compound that stimulates an auxin-responsive reporter need not behave like IAA in a transport assay. In particular, comparing NAA and 2,4-D can help distinguish defects in entry from defects in the common downstream response, provided their different properties are taken into account.

Free IAA is generally the relevant pool for canonical nuclear auxin perception. An analytical measurement of total IAA after hydrolysis may include IAA formerly present in conjugates. That total cannot be substituted uncritically for the free concentration in a living cell. A large inactive reserve can coexist with modest receptor occupancy, whereas release from a small local reserve can create a transient signal. Moreover, an extraction of a whole root averages cells with different functions and subcellular compartments. It can conceal steep gradients at developmental sites. To infer the mechanism of a phenotype one must distinguish which chemical species, compartment and cells were measured.

The weak-acid property can be quantified using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. For a monoprotic weak acid, pH = pKa + log10([IAA−]/[IAAH]). Rearrangement gives the neutral fraction as 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa)). If the wall space has pH 5.5 and pKa is 4.75, the anion-to-neutral ratio is 10^0.75, about 5.62; the neutral fraction is approximately 0.151 or 15.1 percent. At cytosolic pH 7.0 the ratio is approximately 178 and the neutral fraction is about 0.56 percent. These are equilibrium estimates using illustrative pH values, not measurements of a particular cell. Ionization favours passive entry of the neutral fraction from the acidic apoplast, followed by dissociation in the more alkaline cytoplasm. The anion then needs suitable export pathways for effective exit. The result anticipates the chemiosmotic model of polar auxin movement.

4. Local biosynthesis: the TAA/TAR–YUCCA pathway

The main well-established route to IAA in flowering plants connects hormone synthesis to the aromatic amino acid tryptophan. The precursor has an indole ring already present; a plant does not construct the entire auxin molecule independently from separate simple fragments at each site of growth. Tryptophan aminotransferases, including TAA1 and related TAR proteins, catalyse conversion of L-tryptophan into indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA). Like other aminotransferases these enzymes use pyridoxal phosphate chemistry to transfer an amino group to an acceptor, producing the corresponding keto acid. In the next stage, flavin-dependent YUCCA monooxygenases convert IPyA to IAA through oxidative decarboxylation, using oxygen and reducing power. These two transformations explain why mutations affecting enzymes in either stage can reduce endogenous IAA supply.

The chemical sequence is simple enough to memorise, but the central physiological issue is its spatial regulation. A leaf primordium, developing seed or root tissue can express particular members of the TAA/TAR and YUCCA families. Because different genes act in overlapping and tissue-specific domains, mutation of one gene does not necessarily eliminate all auxin synthesis. Severe defects may appear only when several related genes are lost. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of a biosynthetic enzyme can generate a local excess without reproducing the normal endogenous pattern. A biosynthetic phenotype therefore depends on gene redundancy, the availability of substrate, the site of expression and the rate of auxin removal.

Earlier descriptions often treated young shoots as the exclusive source of auxin and roots as passive recipients of a basipetal stream. Young shoot tissues are important sources, but local synthesis in roots and other organs also contributes to pattern formation and physiological responsiveness. One reason a whole-plant supply cannot substitute for every local source is that development requires appropriately timed concentrations in specific groups of cells. Transport may be limited by the arrangement of carriers; hormone delivered externally may reach the wrong tissues or be rapidly conjugated. The presence of a long-distance supply is compatible with local production controlling a separate aspect of the same gradient.

Other tryptophan-derived intermediates occur in particular pathways. For example, indole-3-acetaldoxime can arise via CYP79B enzymes in Arabidopsis and related plants; it also supplies other indolic metabolites. Indole-3-acetamide can be converted into IAA, and bacteria can use routes that are not identical to the predominant flowering-plant pathway. Proposed tryptophan-independent production must be described with appropriate qualification: evidence for its occurrence does not establish one fully characterized, universal alternative enzyme sequence. When asked to identify the principal defined two-step pathway, choose tryptophan, IPyA and IAA with TAA/TAR preceding YUCCA, not a list of all conceivable intermediates.

Genetic rescue tests distinguish a production defect from impaired hormone response. Suppose a mutant has reduced IAA in a developing hypocotyl and fails to elongate normally. External IAA at a dose that stimulates the wild type can rescue a biosynthesis-limited phenotype if the responding tissue remains competent and the hormone reaches it. Failure of rescue is consistent with a receptor or downstream signalling defect, but it is not uniquely diagnostic: poor uptake, degradation of the applied substance or irreversible developmental abnormalities may also prevent rescue. Combining hormone measurements, a biosynthetic transgene and responsiveness to applied auxin gives stronger evidence than any single treatment.

The same distinction matters in the interpretation of hormone levels in plants with altered YUCCA expression. A rise in biosynthetic enzyme abundance is not equivalent to a measured increase in the flux through the pathway. If tryptophan or IPyA availability is limiting, additional enzyme may exert only a modest effect. If feedback increases conjugation, free IAA may remain near normal while the turnover of IAA increases. Measuring the rates of incorporation of a labelled precursor into IAA, alongside the sizes of precursor and product pools, can distinguish increased formation from slowed removal. Such a measurement is most informative when sampled over an interval short enough to resolve the initial appearance of labelled product.

5. Conjugation, oxidation and homeostatic control

The free IAA pool is dynamic. A cell can increase it by synthesis, uptake or release from a conjugate, and decrease it by export, chemical modification or dilution during expansion. Conjugation means covalently linking IAA to another molecule. This can mask the free carboxyl group and reduce the direct biological activity of the hormone. An inactive conjugate is not necessarily a waste product. Some can be hydrolysed to release IAA when needed. Conversely, conjugates can act as intermediates on the way to irreversible inactivation. Whether a particular compound is a reserve or predominantly a catabolic intermediate depends on its chemical identity and the enzymes available in the tissue.

Members of the GH3 family catalyse ATP-dependent attachment of amino acids to IAA. This requires activation of the carboxyl group, commonly described through an acyl-adenylate intermediate, followed by amide bond formation. The resulting compounds include IAA conjugated with aspartate, glutamate and other amino acids. Amidohydrolases such as members of the ILR1/ILL family can liberate free IAA from appropriate substrates. IAA can also form ester conjugates with sugars; the pathways and reversibility differ among conjugates. It is important not to infer the activity of every member of the GH3 family from one example because some family members act on other hormones or substrates.

Oxidative inactivation introduces a chemically modified indole-derived structure that cannot simply be hydrolysed back to free IAA. DAO enzymes belong to the iron- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenase family. The traditional simplified scheme often shows free IAA converted directly to oxIAA, with GH3 conjugation drawn as a parallel pathway. Research in Arabidopsis published in 2021 showed that GH3 conjugation, DAO oxidation of IAA-amino acid conjugates and ILR1-mediated hydrolysis of the oxidized conjugates can form a major linked route to inactive oxIAA. IAA-Asp and IAA-Glu can therefore be subject to competing hydrolysis and oxidation rather than being universally irreversible products at the moment of conjugation. This refinement is useful because it preserves a distinction between temporary removal of free hormone and irreversible destruction of its activity.

Homeostasis is not necessarily constancy of every intermediate. A mutant impaired in an oxidative pathway may accumulate conjugates substantially while free IAA changes relatively little. Increased conjugation, altered synthesis or changes in transport can compensate for the lost pathway. Such a pattern is compatible with strong control of free hormone despite large biochemical perturbations. If only free IAA is measured at a single time, a compensating mutant could be mistaken for one with no metabolic defect. Time courses, conjugate profiling and labelled-substrate experiments provide more discriminating evidence.

A useful minimal mass balance describes the free IAA amount in a cell, N, as dN/dt = S + J_in + H − J_out − C − D, where S denotes synthesis, J_in and J_out membrane influx and efflux, H release from conjugates, C conjugation and D direct irreversible removal from the free pool. Each term has units of amount per unit time. DAO-mediated oxidation of a conjugate is not automatically included in D because it acts on the separate conjugated pool; it can alter H indirectly. At steady state dN/dt = 0, but all fluxes may be positive. For an illustrative cell making 8 femtomoles per hour, importing 4 and releasing 2 from reserves, a combined efflux, conjugation and removal rate of 14 femtomoles per hour maintains a constant free amount. If conjugation is reduced by 3 while other rates are initially unchanged, the free amount initially increases by 3 femtomoles per hour. Feedback and concentration-dependent export can subsequently change the rates, so indefinitely extrapolating that initial slope would be unjustified.

Concentration introduces another complication. If the cell volume V grows, the concentration c = N/V may fall even when N is stable. Differentiating gives dc/dt = (1/V)(dN/dt) − (c/V)(dV/dt). The second term represents dilution rather than chemical destruction. For example, 20 femtomoles in 2 nanolitres corresponds to 10 micromolar, whereas the same amount in 4 nanolitres corresponds to 5 micromolar. This hypothetical calculation is not a typical measured intracellular value; it shows why growth itself changes the relation between hormone amount and receptor exposure. An expanding tissue may need continuing input to maintain a particular free concentration.

6. Chemiosmotic transport and the directional role of carriers

A signal can move between adjacent plant cells through the apoplast and across their plasma membranes. The apoplast comprises cell-wall spaces and extracellular pathways, while the symplast comprises cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata. IAA moves through more than one route, but polar cell-to-cell auxin transport involves an especially important alternation between uptake and export across membranes. The proton gradient makes the two membrane crossings chemically different. Extracellular acidification increases the fraction of neutral IAAH, which can enter the membrane; in the cytoplasm the hormone is mostly IAA−. The low permeability of a charged species through the lipid phase makes carrier-mediated efflux an important determinant of directional transport.

AUX1 and related LAX proteins promote auxin influx. They exploit the proton electrochemical gradient and can allow entry of auxin that would otherwise be restricted by the low neutral fraction. The energy for the gradient ultimately comes from plasma-membrane proton ATPases. AUX1 is therefore not itself an ATP-powered pump in the same sense as an ABC transporter. Loss of AUX1 can produce resistance to externally supplied compounds whose entry depends strongly on that carrier, without destroying all intracellular auxin signalling. The ability of NAA to enter relatively effectively by diffusion makes it useful in interpreting certain influx-defective mutants, although the behaviour of applied analogues also depends on their metabolism and concentration.

PIN-FORMED proteins, or PINs, are important auxin efflux carriers. Many plasma-membrane PINs are asymmetrically distributed, accumulating on one face of a cell. A sequence of cells with similarly positioned exporters produces a preferential path of movement. For example, if cells along a file export IAA preferentially through their lower surfaces, each downstream neighbour receives auxin from the cell above and can export it farther downward. The bias does not arise from gravity physically pulling IAA molecules through the plant. It is encoded in the polarity of the transport machinery. If the cells are inverted as a piece of tissue, the original biological polarity need not reverse immediately relative to the organ simply because the physical orientation has changed.

Not every PIN acts at the plasma membrane. Some shorter PIN family members are associated with internal membranes and contribute to intracellular auxin distribution. Their role should not be equated with the intercellular directional effect of plasma-membrane PINs. ABCB transporters belong to a different protein family and use ATP-dependent transport mechanisms. Important members support efflux, and others have context-dependent transport properties. ABCBs can cooperate with PINs in maintaining stable streams. The names of transporters describe proteins, whereas polar transport describes a tissue-level consequence of their location, capacity and interaction. Mutating a carrier may alter the pattern of flux rather than simply eliminating every possible path of movement.

It is useful to distinguish a carrier's activity from its localization. Imagine a transporter that is equally effective on any membrane surface but is confined to the basal side of a cell. Moving the same number of transporter molecules to all sides could leave total export capacity nearly unchanged while changing the direction and destination of export dramatically. Likewise, a treatment that reduces the rate of a basally localized carrier may reduce flux but leave the preferred direction intact. Experiments using fluorescently tagged carriers reveal localization, whereas tracer movement measures the transport outcome. Combining both readouts helps identify whether a phenotype results from lower capacity or altered polarity.

Polar transport in stems is commonly basipetal, meaning from the shoot apex toward the base. In roots, a central transport stream carries auxin toward the root tip (rootward), whereas outer cell layers provide a return route toward the shoot (shootward). This organization can supply the root tip, redistribute IAA laterally after a change in orientation and deliver it to the elongation zone. Words such as basipetal and acropetal must be tied to an identified organ rather than to the top and bottom of a drawing. Phloem movement can distribute auxin over long distances as well, but bulk flow through conducting tissue is not synonymous with carrier-directed polar transport across a series of cell membranes.

An agar-block transport experiment can test the distinction between direction and diffusion. Excise a stem segment, place a donor agar block containing labelled IAA at one end and a receiver at the other, and measure label appearing in the receiver over a fixed interval. Compare otherwise equivalent segments in which donor and receiver positions are exchanged relative to the original apex and base. Include blocks without living tissue to assess background transfer and an appropriate transport-inhibitor treatment to test sensitivity. A greater transfer from apical donor to basal receiver supports a biological transport bias. Label appearing in a receiver should be chemically identified when possible, since metabolites of labelled IAA could otherwise be mistaken for intact hormone. NPA is a classic inhibitor of polar auxin transport, but inhibitor sensitivity alone does not prove that every observed effect belongs to a single PIN isoform.
