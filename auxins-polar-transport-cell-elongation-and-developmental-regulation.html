1. The problem of coordinated plant growth

A seedling growing beside a window usually bends toward the brighter side. At first glance this seems like movement toward a stimulus, but the plant does not relocate its entire body. Instead, cells on opposite sides of its growing organ acquire different lengths. If the side away from the window elongates faster, the tip is displaced toward the light. The ability to control differences in growth is especially important in plants because the position of an individual cell is constrained by its wall and attachment to neighbouring cells. To change the form of the organ, the plant must regulate the direction, rate and duration of cell division and expansion. A chemical signal that is made in one region and influences another can provide a link between perception of the environment and the mechanical consequences of growth.

Auxin is a family of plant growth regulators whose predominant natural representative in many flowering plants is indole-3-acetic acid, abbreviated IAA. The word hormone describes a signalling substance acting at physiologically relevant concentrations to regulate a response. It does not mean that the substance is a bulk structural material or source of energy. In an elongating cell, for example, auxin does not supply the cellulose used to build the wall, nor does it supply most of the ATP required to maintain ion gradients. Instead, it changes the activities of proteins and genes that determine whether expansion is possible. Sugar availability, water supply, wall structure, membrane transport and developmental competence all influence the eventual outcome.

A second distinction is between auxin amount and auxin response. A cell containing a particular concentration of free IAA need not respond identically to another cell with the same concentration. The receptor composition, abundance of transcriptional repressors, identity of target genes and wall properties may differ. A seedling root is often inhibited by concentrations that promote shoot elongation. A developing lateral root primordium may require a local auxin maximum even though high auxin inhibits the extension of the primary root. These are distinct processes, taking place in different cell populations on different timescales. A useful explanation must therefore identify the tissue and measured response rather than calling auxin a universal growth promoter.

Three questions recur throughout auxin physiology: where does active hormone come from, how does its distribution change, and how is the distribution translated into a biological response? The first question involves biosynthesis and metabolism. The second involves membrane transport, the physical properties of IAA and the polarity of cells. The third involves receptors, regulatory proteins, wall loosening and changes in development. Their interactions are reciprocal: auxin can influence expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters, so the signal itself can change the system responsible for making and distributing it.

2. Classical experiments: from the coleoptile to a mobile signal

The coleoptile is a protective sheath surrounding the young shoot of many grass seedlings. Its tip can perceive directional illumination while elongation in regions farther below produces curvature. Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin examined the relationship between these two regions in experiments described in The Power of Movement in Plants in 1880. Covering the tip with an opaque cap prevented normal bending toward lateral light, whereas allowing illumination at the tip while shielding the lower region permitted curvature. Removal of the tip also prevented the usual response. The essential conclusion was that light perception and the execution of differential growth could be spatially separated.

That conclusion did not identify a chemical signal. A surgical cut could interrupt several types of communication, including electrical continuity and the movement of molecules. Boysen-Jensen subsequently used permeable and impermeable barriers to investigate whether a transferable substance could cross the interface between a coleoptile tip and lower tissue. A gelatin layer could allow growth information to pass, whereas an impermeable barrier prevented transmission across the blocked region. The result was consistent with a diffusible chemical factor, although the experiment alone did not establish its molecular identity or the nature of its receptors.

The next important question was whether a growth-regulating material collected from a tip could act after separation from the original living source. In Went's coleoptile experiments, material diffusing from excised tips into agar was transferred to decapitated coleoptiles. An asymmetrically placed block containing the growth-promoting material induced curvature in the absence of the original directional illumination. A plain agar block provided a comparison. A symmetrically placed active block could stimulate elongation without imposing the same lateral asymmetry. These observations made it possible to study hormone activity through a measurable response rather than relying only on the presence of an intact illuminated tip.

The curvature assay is a biological assay: the response of living tissue is used to infer the activity of a sample. Within a calibrated range, larger amounts of biologically active material can produce larger responses. Outside that range, saturation or inhibition may occur. A bioassay is not automatically a chemical measurement. Another compound acting through the same or a different growth pathway could produce curvature. Purification and chemical identification were therefore required before the active factor could be assigned to IAA. Modern analytical methods such as chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry distinguish IAA from chemically different compounds, while genetic and reporter methods test the relationship between its distribution and the response.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which four decapitated coleoptiles receive respectively a plain agar block, an active block centred on the cut surface, an active block on the left and an active block on the right. All are maintained in darkness and measured over the same interval. The first treatment estimates growth after removal of the tip and manipulation; the second demonstrates that growth can increase without lateral curvature; the final pair tests the predicted reversal of curvature when the signal source changes sides. Identical block sizes and handling, random assignment and repeated specimens help distinguish a systematic effect from variability among seedlings. The logic is still valuable in contemporary experiments: independent manipulation of location, concentration and signalling competence is often more informative than measuring a single endpoint.

3. IAA chemistry and the identity of the active pool

IAA contains an indole ring and an acetic acid side chain attached at the third position of the ring. Its formula is C10H9NO2. The indole portion is a fused aromatic ring system containing nitrogen, whereas the side chain ends in a carboxyl group. At physiological pH, the ionization state of that carboxyl group is particularly important because neutral and charged molecules differ in their ability to cross a lipid bilayer. The compound is a weak acid, with a pKa near 4.75. The symbol IAAH denotes the protonated neutral species and IAA− denotes its deprotonated anion. The two forms interconvert rapidly according to local pH; they are not distinct auxin hormones.

Auxin is a functional classification rather than a statement that every active compound has the same molecular structure. Indole-3-butyric acid, IBA, occurs naturally in plants and can contribute to IAA supply after peroxisomal shortening of its side chain. Phenylacetic acid is another natural auxin with different transport characteristics. The synthetic regulators 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) and 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) are useful in experiments and horticulture, but they differ from IAA in uptake, metabolism and persistence. A compound that stimulates an auxin-responsive reporter need not behave like IAA in a transport assay. In particular, comparing NAA and 2,4-D can help distinguish defects in entry from defects in the common downstream response, provided their different properties are taken into account.

Free IAA is generally the relevant pool for canonical nuclear auxin perception. An analytical measurement of total IAA after hydrolysis may include IAA formerly present in conjugates. That total cannot be substituted uncritically for the free concentration in a living cell. A large inactive reserve can coexist with modest receptor occupancy, whereas release from a small local reserve can create a transient signal. Moreover, an extraction of a whole root averages cells with different functions and subcellular compartments. It can conceal steep gradients at developmental sites. To infer the mechanism of a phenotype one must distinguish which chemical species, compartment and cells were measured.

The weak-acid property can be quantified using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. For a monoprotic weak acid, pH = pKa + log10([IAA−]/[IAAH]). Rearrangement gives the neutral fraction as 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa)). If the wall space has pH 5.5 and pKa is 4.75, the anion-to-neutral ratio is 10^0.75, about 5.62; the neutral fraction is approximately 0.151 or 15.1 percent. At cytosolic pH 7.0 the ratio is approximately 178 and the neutral fraction is about 0.56 percent. These are equilibrium estimates using illustrative pH values, not measurements of a particular cell. Ionization favours passive entry of the neutral fraction from the acidic apoplast, followed by dissociation in the more alkaline cytoplasm. The anion then needs suitable export pathways for effective exit. The result anticipates the chemiosmotic model of polar auxin movement.

4. Local biosynthesis: the TAA/TAR–YUCCA pathway

The main well-established route to IAA in flowering plants connects hormone synthesis to the aromatic amino acid tryptophan. The precursor has an indole ring already present; a plant does not construct the entire auxin molecule independently from separate simple fragments at each site of growth. Tryptophan aminotransferases, including TAA1 and related TAR proteins, catalyse conversion of L-tryptophan into indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA). Like other aminotransferases these enzymes use pyridoxal phosphate chemistry to transfer an amino group to an acceptor, producing the corresponding keto acid. In the next stage, flavin-dependent YUCCA monooxygenases convert IPyA to IAA through oxidative decarboxylation, using oxygen and reducing power. These two transformations explain why mutations affecting enzymes in either stage can reduce endogenous IAA supply.

The chemical sequence is simple enough to memorise, but the central physiological issue is its spatial regulation. A leaf primordium, developing seed or root tissue can express particular members of the TAA/TAR and YUCCA families. Because different genes act in overlapping and tissue-specific domains, mutation of one gene does not necessarily eliminate all auxin synthesis. Severe defects may appear only when several related genes are lost. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of a biosynthetic enzyme can generate a local excess without reproducing the normal endogenous pattern. A biosynthetic phenotype therefore depends on gene redundancy, the availability of substrate, the site of expression and the rate of auxin removal.

Earlier descriptions often treated young shoots as the exclusive source of auxin and roots as passive recipients of a basipetal stream. Young shoot tissues are important sources, but local synthesis in roots and other organs also contributes to pattern formation and physiological responsiveness. One reason a whole-plant supply cannot substitute for every local source is that development requires appropriately timed concentrations in specific groups of cells. Transport may be limited by the arrangement of carriers; hormone delivered externally may reach the wrong tissues or be rapidly conjugated. The presence of a long-distance supply is compatible with local production controlling a separate aspect of the same gradient.

Other tryptophan-derived intermediates occur in particular pathways. For example, indole-3-acetaldoxime can arise via CYP79B enzymes in Arabidopsis and related plants; it also supplies other indolic metabolites. Indole-3-acetamide can be converted into IAA, and bacteria can use routes that are not identical to the predominant flowering-plant pathway. Proposed tryptophan-independent production must be described with appropriate qualification: evidence for its occurrence does not establish one fully characterized, universal alternative enzyme sequence. When asked to identify the principal defined two-step pathway, choose tryptophan, IPyA and IAA with TAA/TAR preceding YUCCA, not a list of all conceivable intermediates.

Genetic rescue tests distinguish a production defect from impaired hormone response. Suppose a mutant has reduced IAA in a developing hypocotyl and fails to elongate normally. External IAA at a dose that stimulates the wild type can rescue a biosynthesis-limited phenotype if the responding tissue remains competent and the hormone reaches it. Failure of rescue is consistent with a receptor or downstream signalling defect, but it is not uniquely diagnostic: poor uptake, degradation of the applied substance or irreversible developmental abnormalities may also prevent rescue. Combining hormone measurements, a biosynthetic transgene and responsiveness to applied auxin gives stronger evidence than any single treatment.

The same distinction matters in the interpretation of hormone levels in plants with altered YUCCA expression. A rise in biosynthetic enzyme abundance is not equivalent to a measured increase in the flux through the pathway. If tryptophan or IPyA availability is limiting, additional enzyme may exert only a modest effect. If feedback increases conjugation, free IAA may remain near normal while the turnover of IAA increases. Measuring the rates of incorporation of a labelled precursor into IAA, alongside the sizes of precursor and product pools, can distinguish increased formation from slowed removal. Such a measurement is most informative when sampled over an interval short enough to resolve the initial appearance of labelled product.

5. Conjugation, oxidation and homeostatic control

The free IAA pool is dynamic. A cell can increase it by synthesis, uptake or release from a conjugate, and decrease it by export, chemical modification or dilution during expansion. Conjugation means covalently linking IAA to another molecule. This can mask the free carboxyl group and reduce the direct biological activity of the hormone. An inactive conjugate is not necessarily a waste product. Some can be hydrolysed to release IAA when needed. Conversely, conjugates can act as intermediates on the way to irreversible inactivation. Whether a particular compound is a reserve or predominantly a catabolic intermediate depends on its chemical identity and the enzymes available in the tissue.

Members of the GH3 family catalyse ATP-dependent attachment of amino acids to IAA. This requires activation of the carboxyl group, commonly described through an acyl-adenylate intermediate, followed by amide bond formation. The resulting compounds include IAA conjugated with aspartate, glutamate and other amino acids. Amidohydrolases such as members of the ILR1/ILL family can liberate free IAA from appropriate substrates. IAA can also form ester conjugates with sugars; the pathways and reversibility differ among conjugates. It is important not to infer the activity of every member of the GH3 family from one example because some family members act on other hormones or substrates.

Oxidative inactivation introduces a chemically modified indole-derived structure that cannot simply be hydrolysed back to free IAA. DAO enzymes belong to the iron- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenase family. The traditional simplified scheme often shows free IAA converted directly to oxIAA, with GH3 conjugation drawn as a parallel pathway. Research in Arabidopsis published in 2021 showed that GH3 conjugation, DAO oxidation of IAA-amino acid conjugates and ILR1-mediated hydrolysis of the oxidized conjugates can form a major linked route to inactive oxIAA. IAA-Asp and IAA-Glu can therefore be subject to competing hydrolysis and oxidation rather than being universally irreversible products at the moment of conjugation. This refinement is useful because it preserves a distinction between temporary removal of free hormone and irreversible destruction of its activity.

Homeostasis is not necessarily constancy of every intermediate. A mutant impaired in an oxidative pathway may accumulate conjugates substantially while free IAA changes relatively little. Increased conjugation, altered synthesis or changes in transport can compensate for the lost pathway. Such a pattern is compatible with strong control of free hormone despite large biochemical perturbations. If only free IAA is measured at a single time, a compensating mutant could be mistaken for one with no metabolic defect. Time courses, conjugate profiling and labelled-substrate experiments provide more discriminating evidence.

A useful minimal mass balance describes the free IAA amount in a cell, N, as dN/dt = S + J_in + H − J_out − C − D, where S denotes synthesis, J_in and J_out membrane influx and efflux, H release from conjugates, C conjugation and D direct irreversible removal from the free pool. Each term has units of amount per unit time. DAO-mediated oxidation of a conjugate is not automatically included in D because it acts on the separate conjugated pool; it can alter H indirectly. At steady state dN/dt = 0, but all fluxes may be positive. For an illustrative cell making 8 femtomoles per hour, importing 4 and releasing 2 from reserves, a combined efflux, conjugation and removal rate of 14 femtomoles per hour maintains a constant free amount. If conjugation is reduced by 3 while other rates are initially unchanged, the free amount initially increases by 3 femtomoles per hour. Feedback and concentration-dependent export can subsequently change the rates, so indefinitely extrapolating that initial slope would be unjustified.

Concentration introduces another complication. If the cell volume V grows, the concentration c = N/V may fall even when N is stable. Differentiating gives dc/dt = (1/V)(dN/dt) − (c/V)(dV/dt). The second term represents dilution rather than chemical destruction. For example, 20 femtomoles in 2 nanolitres corresponds to 10 micromolar, whereas the same amount in 4 nanolitres corresponds to 5 micromolar. This hypothetical calculation is not a typical measured intracellular value; it shows why growth itself changes the relation between hormone amount and receptor exposure. An expanding tissue may need continuing input to maintain a particular free concentration.

6. Chemiosmotic transport and the directional role of carriers

A signal can move between adjacent plant cells through the apoplast and across their plasma membranes. The apoplast comprises cell-wall spaces and extracellular pathways, while the symplast comprises cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata. IAA moves through more than one route, but polar cell-to-cell auxin transport involves an especially important alternation between uptake and export across membranes. The proton gradient makes the two membrane crossings chemically different. Extracellular acidification increases the fraction of neutral IAAH, which can enter the membrane; in the cytoplasm the hormone is mostly IAA−. The low permeability of a charged species through the lipid phase makes carrier-mediated efflux an important determinant of directional transport.

AUX1 and related LAX proteins promote auxin influx. They exploit the proton electrochemical gradient and can allow entry of auxin that would otherwise be restricted by the low neutral fraction. The energy for the gradient ultimately comes from plasma-membrane proton ATPases. AUX1 is therefore not itself an ATP-powered pump in the same sense as an ABC transporter. Loss of AUX1 can produce resistance to externally supplied compounds whose entry depends strongly on that carrier, without destroying all intracellular auxin signalling. The ability of NAA to enter relatively effectively by diffusion makes it useful in interpreting certain influx-defective mutants, although the behaviour of applied analogues also depends on their metabolism and concentration.

PIN-FORMED proteins, or PINs, are important auxin efflux carriers. Many plasma-membrane PINs are asymmetrically distributed, accumulating on one face of a cell. A sequence of cells with similarly positioned exporters produces a preferential path of movement. For example, if cells along a file export IAA preferentially through their lower surfaces, each downstream neighbour receives auxin from the cell above and can export it farther downward. The bias does not arise from gravity physically pulling IAA molecules through the plant. It is encoded in the polarity of the transport machinery. If the cells are inverted as a piece of tissue, the original biological polarity need not reverse immediately relative to the organ simply because the physical orientation has changed.

Not every PIN acts at the plasma membrane. Some shorter PIN family members are associated with internal membranes and contribute to intracellular auxin distribution. Their role should not be equated with the intercellular directional effect of plasma-membrane PINs. ABCB transporters belong to a different protein family and use ATP-dependent transport mechanisms. Important members support efflux, and others have context-dependent transport properties. ABCBs can cooperate with PINs in maintaining stable streams. The names of transporters describe proteins, whereas polar transport describes a tissue-level consequence of their location, capacity and interaction. Mutating a carrier may alter the pattern of flux rather than simply eliminating every possible path of movement.

It is useful to distinguish a carrier's activity from its localization. Imagine a transporter that is equally effective on any membrane surface but is confined to the basal side of a cell. Moving the same number of transporter molecules to all sides could leave total export capacity nearly unchanged while changing the direction and destination of export dramatically. Likewise, a treatment that reduces the rate of a basally localized carrier may reduce flux but leave the preferred direction intact. Experiments using fluorescently tagged carriers reveal localization, whereas tracer movement measures the transport outcome. Combining both readouts helps identify whether a phenotype results from lower capacity or altered polarity.

Polar transport in stems is commonly basipetal, meaning from the shoot apex toward the base. In roots, a central transport stream carries auxin toward the root tip (rootward), whereas outer cell layers provide a return route toward the shoot (shootward). This organization can supply the root tip, redistribute IAA laterally after a change in orientation and deliver it to the elongation zone. Words such as basipetal and acropetal must be tied to an identified organ rather than to the top and bottom of a drawing. Phloem movement can distribute auxin over long distances as well, but bulk flow through conducting tissue is not synonymous with carrier-directed polar transport across a series of cell membranes.

An agar-block transport experiment can test the distinction between direction and diffusion. Excise a stem segment, place a donor agar block containing labelled IAA at one end and a receiver at the other, and measure label appearing in the receiver over a fixed interval. Compare otherwise equivalent segments in which donor and receiver positions are exchanged relative to the original apex and base. Include blocks without living tissue to assess background transfer and an appropriate transport-inhibitor treatment to test sensitivity. A greater transfer from apical donor to basal receiver supports a biological transport bias. Label appearing in a receiver should be chemically identified when possible, since metabolites of labelled IAA could otherwise be mistaken for intact hormone. NPA is a classic inhibitor of polar auxin transport, but inhibitor sensitivity alone does not prove that every observed effect belongs to a single PIN isoform.

7. Flux, concentration maxima and experimental measurements

Consider a growing organ as a collection of compartments with sources and sinks. A source produces or releases auxin; a sink can remove it by metabolism, export or redistribution. These terms are relative to a specified region and time. A cell may receive substantial auxin from a neighbour and then act as a source to another neighbour. The amount of auxin in a region is determined by the difference between incoming and outgoing flows, not by the magnitude of either flow separately. A rapidly flowing transport stream can maintain a small local concentration if the input and output rates match. In contrast, convergent streams can produce a local maximum if removal is slower than delivery. This distinction between flux and concentration helps explain why fluorescence associated with an auxin response cannot directly reveal the direction of movement.

A simple three-cell calculation makes the point concrete. Suppose cell A supplies five arbitrary amount units per hour to cell B, and B supplies five units per hour to C. If these are the only transfers involving B, B has no net accumulation. If export from B falls to two units per hour while influx initially remains five, B accumulates three units per hour. C now receives less auxin than before, even though B contains more. In a real plant feedback could increase the capacity of other efflux pathways or stimulate conjugation, so the maximum need not continue rising. A higher auxin concentration upstream and a lower concentration downstream are nevertheless entirely compatible consequences of inhibiting a directional stream.

This framework explains why blocking transport is not equivalent to blocking synthesis. When synthesis is blocked at the source, tissues receiving hormone from that source may become depleted. When export from the source is blocked, the source may accumulate hormone while the recipient becomes depleted. Both interventions may produce short roots or defective primordia. The overall phenotype is therefore insufficient to establish the location of the original defect. Chemical measurements at defined sites, suitable transport measurements and gene expression data allow the two mechanisms to be separated. The same treatment can even increase a reporter signal in one region and decrease it in another.

At the shoot apex, local convergence of transport can help establish regions in which organ initiation becomes likely. A small increase in delivery to one group of cells can lead to a more pronounced difference if the surrounding cells direct additional auxin toward that site. Adjacent sites may become relatively depleted, contributing to the separation of initiation events. It would be misleading, however, to equate a convergence point with an inevitable leaf. The cells must possess the developmental competence to initiate a primordium. Auxin influences where a new developmental program is selected; other signals and physical constraints shape the subsequent organ. A fuller account of the shoot meristem and leaf arrangement belongs to developmental biology.

Two common fluorescent approaches measure different consequences of auxin. A reporter using a synthetic auxin-responsive promoter, such as DR5, records transcriptional output. Its signal depends on active ARFs, chromatin and the accumulation and turnover of the reporter protein. A degradation-based reporter containing an auxin-sensitive Aux/IAA degron, such as DII-VENUS, tends to lose fluorescence when auxin-dependent degradation becomes more active. A degradation-resistant reference can be included to normalize differences in expression or imaging. DR5 generally increases with a suitable transcriptional response; DII fluorescence generally decreases with degron-dependent degradation. Neither is a universal direct concentration meter. Altered receptor abundance, proteasome activity or ARF activity can influence their outputs.

Suppose two sites have equal free IAA as measured by an appropriate chemical method, but the first has strong DR5 expression and the second weak expression. This is not logically inconsistent. The sites may differ in transcriptional competence or in how long they have experienced the hormone. If the DII reporter also differs, one should consider differences in receptor activity and degradation, rather than assuming the chemical measurement is wrong. If DR5 differs but the two sites have comparable DII degradation, the difference might be downstream of the degradation step. Independent markers help localize a defect by revealing which outputs change together.

For a directional tracer assay, an illustrative set of initial receiver measurements might be 24 units after transport from the original apical end toward the basal end, 4 units in the reverse direction and 2 units in a tissue-free background control. The background-corrected directional ratio would be (24 − 2)/(4 − 2) = 11. This number describes the specified assay, not an intrinsic universal constant of polar transport. If the reverse reading lies close to background, measurement uncertainty in the denominator strongly influences the ratio. Reporting both raw readings, background and replicate variability is more informative than reporting only an impressive fold change. A treatment that reduces the forward result to 8 units and leaves the reverse result at 4 has reduced the corrected directional ratio to 3. This suggests loss of preferential movement but does not distinguish transporter inhibition from broader impairment of the living tissue. Controls for viability and donor uptake remain necessary.

8. Nuclear perception: TIR1/AFB, Aux/IAA and ARF

Transport explains why a cell experiences auxin, but the hormone has no regulatory meaning without a mechanism that translates its presence into altered cellular activity. In the canonical nuclear pathway, the central switch is regulated protein degradation. A transcriptional repressor is removed when the receptor binds hormone in association with the repressor. This mechanism may initially seem unusual because many receptors are taught as proteins that change their enzyme activity or initiate a phosphorylation cascade. Auxin perception instead couples the recognition of a small molecule directly to the selection of a protein for ubiquitin-dependent turnover.

ARFs, or auxin response factors, are DNA-binding transcriptional regulators. They recognize sequence elements associated with auxin-responsive genes. Some ARFs activate transcription, whereas others have repressive roles, so a general diagram of an activating ARF should not be taken to represent every family member. Aux/IAA proteins are short-lived transcriptional regulators that can associate with ARFs and recruit corepressors such as TOPLESS. When an activating ARF is associated with a suitable Aux/IAA repressor, the target gene can remain relatively inactive even though the ARF is present. The repressor therefore provides a means of controlling transcription without requiring that the DNA-binding factor itself be destroyed.

TIR1 and related AFB proteins belong to a family of F-box proteins. An F-box protein can provide substrate recognition within an SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase, named for its SKP1, CULLIN and F-box components. The ligase cooperates with the ubiquitin activation and conjugation machinery to promote transfer of ubiquitin to a selected substrate. A polyubiquitin chain of the appropriate type can target the substrate to the 26S proteasome, where it is degraded. E1 and E2 proteins participate in ubiquitin activation and transfer; the E3 complex provides critical substrate specificity. TIR1 is not itself the protease that breaks down the Aux/IAA polypeptide.

At low auxin concentrations, the affinity between a suitable TIR1/AFB protein and the degradation motif of an Aux/IAA protein is often low enough for the repressor to persist, although basal turnover still occurs. When auxin occupies the receptor binding pocket, it helps stabilize the interface with the Aux/IAA degron. A useful term for this function is molecular glue. The auxin molecule does not create a covalent bond between the two proteins; it improves the compatibility of their interaction surfaces. The receptor and degron-containing substrate together contribute to hormone recognition, which is why the interaction is often described as a co-receptor system.

After stable recruitment of the repressor, the SCF complex promotes its ubiquitination and the proteasome degrades it. The previously restrained activating ARF can now stimulate transcription of an appropriate target. In a particular cell this can induce genes encoding regulators of growth, metabolism or further signalling. The pathway therefore contains a double negative: auxin promotes removal of a protein that inhibits transcription. If one simply remembers that auxin activates ARF, the role of the intermediate repressor can be missed. A prediction about a stabilizing mutation in the repressor then becomes difficult. The mechanistic order is hormone recognition, substrate recruitment, ubiquitination, repressor destruction and release of transcriptional activity.

Two landmark studies published in 2005 by Dharmasiri and colleagues and by Kepinski and Leyser independently established TIR1 as an auxin receptor. Experiments showing auxin-dependent interaction between TIR1 and Aux/IAA proteins supported direct hormone recognition rather than an obligatory upstream receptor that modified the substrate first. Structural work published by Tan and colleagues in 2007 subsequently showed how auxin fits into the interaction interface. The convergence of genetic, biochemical and structural evidence is more persuasive than a phenotype alone. A plant that grows abnormally after mutation of a signalling gene could have many indirect defects; direct interaction and structural information place the protein at a particular molecular step.

An important examination prediction follows from a degradation-resistant Aux/IAA protein. Imagine that its degron is altered so it can still bind an activating ARF but is inefficiently recruited by TIR1 in the presence of hormone. Such a protein can remain repressive even when free auxin is high. The phenotype can resemble auxin insensitivity, and adding more IAA is unlikely to restore the normal response if degradation remains blocked. The mutation may be dominant because the stabilized protein interferes with the action of normal ARF in a cell that also contains normal copies of the relevant genes. This differs from simple loss of a repressor that normally restrains the pathway.

Genetic redundancy complicates interpretation of receptor mutants. Loss of TIR1 alone need not abolish all responses because other members of the AFB family can contribute. A combined loss of several receptor family members may reveal a stronger phenotype. The inverse inference is equally important: a weak phenotype does not mean that a protein plays no role. Other proteins can provide overlapping function in the cells tested. Exam questions sometimes compare single mutants, combinations and complementation with a wild-type gene. The most informative observation is the pattern across genotypes under identical treatment rather than the phenotype of one mutant in isolation.

Many primary auxin-responsive genes encode components that help regulate the system itself. Aux/IAA genes can be induced, generating new repressors after the first population has been degraded. Certain GH3 genes are induced, increasing the capacity for IAA conjugation. SAUR genes can encode proteins that influence cell expansion. These outputs illustrate different consequences of the same early regulatory mechanism. Newly synthesized repressors provide negative feedback at the signalling level, conjugation provides feedback on free hormone abundance, and SAUR expression can connect transcription to wall acidification. A prolonged input may consequently produce an output that is not proportional to the initial transcriptional response.

A hypothetical time course demonstrates this distinction. A pulse of IAA might reduce a labile Aux/IAA protein rapidly while its corresponding messenger RNA increases more slowly. This is possible because the hormone affects the degradation rate of pre-existing protein and the transcription rate of the gene in opposite directions. Following removal of the pulse, newly produced repressor can accumulate and reduce transcriptional activity. Measuring only messenger RNA at the peak would not establish that the corresponding protein has increased. Separate measurements of protein and transcript are especially valuable when a hormone acts through regulated proteolysis.

Canonical nuclear signalling accounts for many auxin responses, but not all events occur on the timescale of transcription and translation. Rapid changes in membrane potential, ion transport and root elongation can occur too quickly to be explained solely by the production of new proteins. AFB1 has an important role in rapid root responses, and cell-surface signalling involving TMK kinases contributes to regulation of proton pumps in some contexts. These findings do not invalidate nuclear signalling. Rather, they establish that auxin uses more than one route to connect perception with physiological change. A NET-level explanation should distinguish the well-established canonical degradation switch from rapid signalling phenomena without assuming that one simple mechanism explains every organ and timescale.

9. Acid growth, wall mechanics and cell elongation

A growing plant cell is enclosed by a wall that resists deformation. Water entering the cell generates turgor pressure, the hydrostatic pressure of the protoplast against the wall. Yet a well-hydrated mature cell can maintain pressure without undergoing irreversible expansion. This tells us that turgor is necessary for many forms of growth but is not sufficient. The wall must be capable of yielding, and the expanding cell must continue to take up water. Auxin influences this relationship in many young shoot tissues by modifying properties of the wall and the transport processes that sustain expansion.

In primary walls, cellulose microfibrils supply tensile strength and interact with matrix polysaccharides. Cell expansion requires controlled changes in the way wall polymers constrain one another; unrestricted destruction would make the wall unable to withstand pressure. Expansins are wall-associated proteins that facilitate loosening under suitable acidic conditions without acting as ordinary polysaccharide-hydrolysing enzymes. Their action can permit stress relaxation and irreversible wall extension under turgor. The orientation of load-bearing wall structures influences whether the cell elongates predominantly along one axis or expands in several directions. Auxin-mediated loosening is thus translated into shape changes through the existing mechanical organization of the tissue.

The acid-growth hypothesis proposes that growth-promoting treatments stimulate proton extrusion into the apoplast, lowering its pH. The plasma-membrane H+-ATPase uses ATP hydrolysis to move protons from the cytosol outward. This acidifies the wall space and contributes to the electrical component of the membrane potential. Acidification favours the activity of expansins and other pH-sensitive wall-remodelling processes in an appropriate range. The outward positive current can also help drive inward movement of ions such as potassium through suitable channels, contributing to solute accumulation and the osmotic uptake of water. Wall loosening and solute uptake cooperate: the wall yields, pressure tends to relax and continuing water entry allows extension to proceed.

A mechanistic bridge between nuclear auxin perception and proton-pump activation is the SAUR–PP2C.D module. Auxin-induced SAUR proteins can inhibit a subset of type 2C protein phosphatases, the PP2C.D proteins. These phosphatases normally oppose activation-associated phosphorylation of plasma-membrane H+-ATPases. Inhibition of the phosphatases therefore favours the phosphorylated, active state of the pump. In many shoot tissues this increases proton export and promotes conditions for growth. The pathway is another example of a double negative: auxin promotes a protein that inhibits a phosphatase that would otherwise oppose pump activation. TMK-related cell-surface signalling can also promote pump activation, illustrating convergence of more than one regulatory route at the membrane.

The extracellular pH is not simply a readout of the amount of acid added. A buffered wall resists changes in pH, and proton flux can be balanced by proton-consuming processes, influx through cotransporters and diffusion. A highly active pump may therefore generate a smaller pH change in one tissue than in another. Conversely, artificially lowering the external pH can bypass part of the normal signalling system in a wall that retains suitable mechanical competence. One cannot infer identical wall responses solely from identical pump activities. Direct pH measurement, mechanical extension tests and growth observations address related but distinct parts of the mechanism.

A classical experiment compares excised elongating stem segments placed in neutral and acidic buffers. If an acidic buffer promotes extension without added hormone, this supports the proposition that acidification can act downstream of auxin. A treatment such as fusicoccin, which stabilizes activation of plasma-membrane proton pumps through interactions with their regulatory partners, can also promote proton extrusion and expansion. These interventions test sufficiency under the conditions used; they do not establish that every normal auxin response is mediated exclusively by pH. Inhibition of pump activity during auxin treatment and measurement of the wall pH provide complementary evidence concerning necessity. A living segment may be unable to sustain extension if energy supply or water availability is compromised.

Quantitative reasoning about wall extension is often expressed through a simplified relation sometimes called the Lockhart equation. The relative irreversible expansion rate may be represented as (1/V)(dV/dt) = φ(P − Y) when turgor P exceeds a yield threshold Y; otherwise the irreversible component is zero in this minimal model. Here V is cell volume, φ represents wall extensibility with units of reciprocal pressure per unit time, and P and Y have units of pressure. The equation distinguishes the driving force from the wall's ability to yield. Auxin could increase extension at a constant measured P by increasing φ, reducing Y or both. If P lies below Y, wall loosening can permit extension only if the new relation makes P exceed the threshold.

For illustration, take P = 0.6 MPa, Y = 0.4 MPa and φ = 0.05 per MPa per hour. The calculated relative rate is 0.05 × 0.2 = 0.01 per hour, or about one percent of the current volume per hour under the instantaneous model. If a growth treatment doubles φ while P and Y remain unchanged, the predicted rate becomes 0.02 per hour. If water loss lowers P to 0.35 MPa and the threshold remains 0.4 MPa, the simplified model predicts no irreversible expansion even if the wall is more extensible. The values are hypothetical and the model ignores several complexities, including tissue-level mechanical coupling and changes in solute and water transport. It is nevertheless a useful way to reason about the conditions required for growth.

Auxin concentrations that promote extension in one region may inhibit it in another. Rapid inhibition of primary-root elongation can involve changes in ion flux and extracellular pH that differ from the usual shoot acid-growth picture. Roots and shoots differ in sensitivity and the regulatory context of their growth zones. At excessive concentrations, even a shoot tissue may cease to show increased growth. Thus the statement that auxin causes acidification and elongation must be associated with appropriate tissue, dose and experimental conditions. The classical acid-growth mechanism remains useful precisely because it makes predictions in a defined physiological setting, not because it applies without exception.

A distinction between elastic and irreversible changes is also important. An increase in pressure may stretch a wall elastically; if pressure is removed the wall may return toward its original dimensions. In irreversible growth, the wall has yielded and the cell retains part of the extension after the driving stress is relieved. Researchers can investigate this difference by applying and releasing mechanical loads while monitoring the response. A short-term increase in length is not by itself proof of permanent growth. In an intact organ the sustained deposition and reorganization of wall material also matter because expansion increases surface area. Hormone-dependent transcription can help support these longer-term changes, extending the response beyond the first acidification event.

10. Phototropism and gravitropism: spatial differences in growth

Tropisms are directional growth responses to environmental stimuli. Positive phototropism means growth curvature toward a light source, whereas negative phototropism is curvature away from it. In many young shoots the familiar positive response follows perception of directional blue light by phototropins. Perception is distinct from auxin redistribution: the photoreceptor detects the stimulus, a regulatory pathway alters auxin transport or distribution, and differential growth produces curvature. Auxin is not itself the photoreceptor. Illuminating a shoot uniformly may affect its growth rate without specifying a lateral direction, because the difference between the two sides is absent.

In a simplified model of positive shoot phototropism, more effective auxin delivery or response occurs on the shaded side of the elongating organ. Cells on that side elongate more than cells on the illuminated side, and the shoot bends toward the light. The longer side becomes the outside of the curve. This geometrical relationship is worth visualizing instead of memorizing which side is longer. If two adjacent longitudinal cell files remain connected while one increases in length more than the other, the longer file cannot remain on the inside of the bend. It must occupy the convex surface, with the tip displaced toward the shorter side. The same geometry applies to gravitropic curvature even when the identity of the faster-growing side changes.

The classical redistribution model is often associated with Cholodny and Went. It provides a testable framework: asymmetric hormone distribution precedes or accompanies differential elongation. Modern fluorescent transport and response studies refine the classical picture by identifying particular cell layers, transporters and routes. Not every observed asymmetry is caused solely by auxin movement; differences in sensitivity or growth competence can contribute. The correct inference from a stronger reporter signal on one side is that the reporter pathway is more active there. A combination of reporter data, labelled hormone movement, transporter localization and growth measurements is needed to establish the relative contributions of redistribution and response sensitivity.

Gravity provides a particularly instructive comparison. Specialized gravity-sensing cells contain dense, starch-filled plastids called statoliths that can sediment after reorientation. In a primary root, important sensing cells are in the root cap columella; in shoots, appropriate endodermal statocytes contribute to gravity perception. Reorientation changes the relationship between statolith position and the cell's surfaces, and this information can trigger changes in the distribution of auxin transport machinery. Auxin is then redistributed toward the lower side of a horizontally oriented organ. The precise pathway depends on the organ, but the general sequence of perception, transport adjustment and differential growth is common.

In a young root, the additional auxin on the lower side often inhibits cell elongation relative to the upper side. Upper cells grow faster, placing the upper side on the outside of the curve; the root bends downward, displaying positive gravitropism. In many shoots, the corresponding increase in auxin on the lower side promotes elongation relative to the upper side. The lower side becomes the longer side and the shoot curves upward, displaying negative gravitropism. It is therefore not necessary to postulate that auxin moves to opposite sides in the two organs to explain opposite bending. The different dose-response relationships can convert broadly similar redistribution into opposite mechanical consequences.

The root transport circuit is particularly suitable for experimental reasoning. Auxin moves toward the root tip through central tissues and is redistributed into outer tissues that carry it shootward toward the elongation zone. PIN proteins in gravity-sensing cells can alter their membrane localization in response to reorientation; PIN2 and AUX1 contribute to effective transfer through outer layers. A defect in the return stream can prevent a lateral distribution generated near the tip from being communicated to cells that must actually change their extension rates. The plant can thus perceive gravity normally while failing to bend. Conversely, normal elongation machinery does not ensure normal bending if the sensing cells cannot create an asymmetry.

One way to separate sensing from response is to measure growth after a uniform auxin application and after a reorientation stimulus. A mutant that responds to uniform auxin but fails to bend after reorientation retains at least some downstream competence. Its defect could lie in gravity perception, redistribution, or the communication of the gradient to the growth zone. If a fluorescent PIN marker also fails to relocalize in the appropriate sensing cells, altered transport regulation becomes a stronger candidate. A normal marker pattern, however, does not prove normal transport because the tagged protein may be present but inactive. Independent hormone distribution or tracer data help close the inference.

Curvature can be estimated with simple geometry. For two parallel cell files separated by a small distance w, bending through angle θ in radians produces a difference in path length of approximately wθ if both files form arcs about the same centre. Suppose their elongation over an interval differs by 0.4 millimetres and the effective separation is 1 millimetre. The illustrative angle is 0.4 radians, approximately 23 degrees. This model ignores three-dimensional tissue stresses and assumes the files remain mechanically connected and bend uniformly. Its purpose is to relate differential elongation to curvature, not to predict the exact angle of a living root from a single cell measurement.

A useful controlled experiment follows the elongation of marked regions on the two sides of a root before and after rotation through 90 degrees. Measure cell-file extension over equal time windows, compare upright and rotated specimens, and include a treatment expected to disrupt polar transport. Curvature of the whole root and extension in the growth zone are different outputs: the first depends on differential growth, the second on the rate of expansion. A root that stops growing entirely cannot express normal tropic bending even if sensing and hormone redistribution remain intact. Therefore a failure to bend after an inhibitor is not by itself proof that the inhibitor blocked perception. Demonstrating continued overall growth and loss of lateral asymmetry is more informative.

11. Apical dominance and the regulation of shoot branching

A young shoot develops a succession of nodes, often with an axillary bud at the junction of a leaf and the stem. A bud contains a meristem that may remain relatively quiescent or grow into a branch. Apical dominance describes the suppression of axillary bud outgrowth by the growing shoot tip. It is not the same as the formation of an axillary meristem. A plant can possess many structurally recognizable buds but few elongated branches. This distinction is important in experimental descriptions: the number of buds initiated and the number of buds that begin sustained growth are separate characteristics of shoot architecture.

Removal of a shoot tip commonly releases one or more of the buds beneath it. Applying auxin to the cut stump in classical experiments can restore suppression of branching in suitable species and conditions. Such a result indicates that apically derived auxin is an important regulator of the system. It does not mean that auxin is the only property of the intact apex, or that removing the tip changes nothing except the concentration of IAA. Decapitation interrupts the polar transport stream and eliminates a growing sink for carbohydrates; it also wounds tissue and can modify the distribution of other hormones. The use of a carrier-only control, an intact control and auxin replacement helps isolate one contribution from several simultaneous effects.

Auxin from young apical tissues is exported into the stem and normally moves toward its base through a polar transport stream. An important refinement of the classical model is that the suppressive influence of stem auxin does not require the bulk of this auxin to enter the axillary bud directly. The stem can alter the environment in which the bud grows. This is a different type of signalling relationship from the one in a coleoptile whose target cells receive an applied auxin source. A treatment applied directly to a bud may have effects different from an equivalent amount delivered to the main stem because the spatial relationships of the transport networks and responding cell populations differ.

Two useful mechanistic frameworks describe how stem auxin contributes to bud suppression. In one, auxin changes the synthesis, availability or action of other regulators. In many studied systems stem auxin limits local cytokinin production and favours strigolactone biosynthesis or function. Cytokinins frequently promote bud outgrowth, while strigolactones commonly restrict branching. The net effect of auxin moving down the stem may therefore be suppression of bud activation through interactions among several hormones. These interactions depend on nutrient status, species and the developmental context. The exact biochemical pathways for cytokinins and strigolactones will be developed in their own chapters; here the important point is that indirect signalling can explain an effect in an organ that is not simply receiving the original hormone.

The other framework emphasizes competition among auxin transport streams. A growing bud produces auxin and must establish an effective route of export into the stem to sustain development. Canalization describes the progressive establishment of preferential pathways of transport, supported by reciprocal relationships between auxin flow and transport machinery. If a main stem already carries a strong transport stream from a dominant apex, a newly activated bud may face difficulty establishing its own export route. Reducing the dominant stream, or altering the stem's ability to act as a sink for bud-derived auxin, can change this competition. The transport-based model does not say that a physically full pipe is incapable of carrying another molecule; the biological capacity and direction of pathways are adjustable properties of cells.

These frameworks are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Cytokinins and strigolactones can influence properties of the bud or transport network, and altered transport can modify the distribution of hormones that regulate bud behaviour. A branch phenotype is consequently a system-level result. An examination question may describe a highly branched mutant that retains considerable IAA in the stem. It is inappropriate to infer that auxin plays no role merely because IAA is present. The plant may have altered sensitivity to a downstream inhibitor, changed cytokinin availability or an impaired competitive transport system. Likewise, reduced branching does not prove an unusually high stem auxin concentration. An informative experiment measures relevant outputs and tests targeted genetic or hormonal perturbations.

The timing of bud release is also important. A growing apex consumes assimilates, so removal of that apex can make more sugars available to axillary buds. Work by Mason and colleagues in 2014 on pea demonstrated that early bud responses can precede a substantial depletion of stem auxin at the node and highlighted the importance of sugar availability. Such evidence argues against a universal model in which local auxin depletion must be the first event in every case of bud release. Sugars serve both as substrates for growth and as regulators of signalling and gene expression. Later hormone-dependent regulation can still be crucial to sustained outgrowth and branching. Initial bud activation, subsequent elongation and long-term maintenance of a branch should therefore be considered separately.

An experiment designed to separate these phases could compare intact plants, decapitated plants receiving carrier alone, decapitated plants supplied with IAA at the stump and intact plants receiving an experimentally increased supply of sugar to a bud. Measuring bud length only after several days may obscure the first stage of release. Sampling early growth, local carbohydrate status and stem hormone levels over time can reveal whether a change in bud behaviour precedes a particular change in the stem. Wounding and application procedures must be matched as closely as possible. A sugar treatment that stimulates early growth does not by itself exclude a later role for auxin, just as auxin replacement that restricts later elongation does not establish that initial sugar redistribution was irrelevant.

The architecture of a plant has important physiological consequences. Growing upward can allow leaves to compete for light, while maintaining dormant buds provides a reserve for renewed growth after injury. Branch formation may increase light interception when resources are abundant, but multiple competing shoots require assimilates and mineral nutrients. Auxin-related regulation of branching participates in the coordination of these demands. It is not helpful to describe the main shoot as intentionally withholding hormone from the other buds; the observed pattern arises from coupled production, transport, response and resource allocation across growing tissues. This causal interpretation makes it easier to predict why changing nutrient availability or light may alter branching even in the absence of direct experimental manipulation of IAA.

12. Auxin and root formation: initiation, emergence and extension

The root system illustrates why a single description of auxin as a growth stimulant can be misleading. A primary root lengthens through the coordinated production of cells near its meristem and their subsequent expansion in the elongation zone. Lateral roots are additional organs initiated within the parent root. Adventitious roots originate from other organs, commonly stems or leaves. Each process has a different cellular basis. An increase in the number of newly initiated lateral roots can occur at the same time as a decrease in the elongation rate of the primary root. A plant with a shorter primary axis but more lateral branches has not responded inconsistently; two separately regulated components of root architecture have changed.

In Arabidopsis, many lateral roots originate from selected cells of the pericycle associated with the xylem poles. The pericycle lies inside the endodermis and surrounds the central vascular tissues. Only some of its cells acquire founder-cell identity at particular positions. Local auxin-dependent processes contribute to this selection and to the initiation of the primordium. The presence of a continuous pericycle layer does not imply that every cell will form a lateral root when exposed to auxin. Positional information, developmental timing, cell competence and interactions with adjacent tissues restrict the response.

The canonical nuclear signalling mechanism described earlier has a specific developmental implementation in this tissue. The Aux/IAA protein IAA14, also called SOLITARY ROOT, can restrain the transcriptional activity of ARF7 and ARF19. When auxin-dependent turnover removes IAA14, these activating ARFs can promote expression of downstream genes, including members of the LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES DOMAIN family such as LBD16 and LBD29. These regulators help coordinate the transition toward the early divisions associated with root initiation. A stabilized form of IAA14 interferes with this transition. The resulting phenotype is a particularly clear illustration of how altered degradation of a repressor can block an organ-specific developmental program even when hormone is available.

A lateral root primordium must do more than undergo its first division. It must organize its developing cell population and eventually emerge through tissues of the parent root. Auxin signalling also influences interactions with these overlying tissues. For example, induction of LAX3 in appropriate cells contributes to auxin-dependent changes associated with emergence. A defect in emergence is distinguishable from a defect in initiation by examining whether primordia exist below the surface. Counting only emerged lateral roots would miss this distinction. The same final root count could result from fewer initiation events, delayed primordium development or failure to cross surrounding tissues.

The location of hormone response matters in each stage. If an experiment restricts a signalling defect to the founder cells, early divisions may fail even if cells outside the primordium can respond normally. If the founder cells are competent but the surrounding tissue does not execute the necessary changes, primordia may form and remain trapped or emerge slowly. Tissue-specific complementation, in which a functional gene is restored only in selected cell layers, helps distinguish these possibilities. Rescue of initiation by restoring a component in the pericycle supports a local requirement there, while rescue of emergence by expression in an overlying layer would indicate a distinct site of action. Interpretation still depends on the actual expression pattern and whether the restored gene is functional.

For an illustrative scoring exercise, suppose a wild-type sample has twenty initiated lateral root primordia, of which fifteen have emerged at the selected time. Mutant A has six initiated primordia, five of which have emerged; mutant B has eighteen initiated primordia, but only four have emerged. The emergence fraction is 15/20 = 75 percent for the wild type, approximately 83 percent for mutant A and approximately 22 percent for mutant B. The small absolute count of emerged roots in mutant A primarily reflects fewer initiated organs, whereas the pattern in mutant B is consistent with a strong delay or block after initiation. This is a hypothetical dataset, not a published measurement. The interpretation requires similar developmental ages and appropriate replication. A time course could establish whether mutant B eventually reaches a normal fraction or remains impaired.

Adventitious rooting provides an example of applied auxin physiology. A stem cutting removed from a parent plant must establish new roots before it can efficiently absorb water and mineral nutrients from its surroundings. In many species, application of an auxin such as IBA can increase the probability of adventitious root initiation. Part of IBA's activity can arise through conversion to IAA. Local accumulation at the cut base, wound-related signals, carbohydrate supply and the identity of competent cells all affect the response. There is no universal concentration or treatment time that works for every plant species or developmental stage.

It is useful to distinguish induction from subsequent root elongation in this setting. A short auxin treatment during the period when competent cells are acquiring root identity may promote the initiation of new roots. Prolonged exposure to an excessive concentration can inhibit their later extension. A treatment that maximizes the number of initiated root primordia need not maximize root length or survival. A careful horticultural comparison would measure the proportion of cuttings forming roots, the number of roots, their lengths and survival after establishment. Counting only the longest root can conceal an increase in the number of short roots, and counting only the percentage of rooted cuttings can conceal poor subsequent development.

Auxin and cytokinin can interact in root formation. In classic tissue culture systems, a relatively high auxin-to-cytokinin balance often favours root development, while a balance shifted toward cytokinin often favours shoot formation. The relation is context dependent: it depends on species, explant identity, regulator concentration and the timing of treatment. Cytokinin can also restrict stages of lateral root initiation or change the distribution of auxin transport proteins. The hormone ratio is therefore an experimentally useful description, not a universal equation that directly predicts an organ's identity for every culture. Equal ratios at low and high absolute concentrations may have different effects because receptors and metabolic pathways are nonlinear.

A broader consequence is the adaptability of root architecture to local environments. Soil patches differ in nutrient availability, water and physical structure. Root branching allows the plant to explore favourable regions without requiring every part of the primary root to grow at the same rate. Hormone-dependent changes in local initiation and extension interact with nutrient signals and the supply of assimilated carbon. Auxin is one component of this coordination, not a substitute for nitrogen assimilation or the water-transport machinery. The physiological point is that altered growth patterns can help a rooted organism respond spatially to its surroundings while retaining the same basic cellular signalling machinery.

13. Dose, sensitivity, tissue context and time

A dose-response experiment examines a defined biological output over a range of applied concentrations. In auxin physiology the response curve is often nonmonotonic: increasing auxin over one interval promotes a process, while further increases reduce it. The existence of an optimum does not imply that one concentration is optimal for all organs. Even the same organ may show different curves for extension, gene expression, initiation of new structures and survival. The concentrations used in external treatment are also not identical to the free intracellular concentrations, because uptake, export, conjugation and diffusion intervene. The x-axis of a graph should therefore be interpreted according to what the investigator actually controlled or measured.

For a simple receptor with one ligand-binding site, the equilibrium bound fraction may be modelled as f = L/(Kd + L), where L is the free ligand concentration and Kd is a dissociation constant with the same units. At L = Kd the predicted fraction is one-half; at L = 9Kd it is nine-tenths. This elementary relation explains why equal increments in concentration need not produce equal changes in occupancy. It must not be confused with a complete model of auxin signalling. TIR1/AFB recognition involves a co-receptor interaction with an Aux/IAA substrate, and different receptor–substrate combinations have different properties. Moreover, downstream growth reflects additional nonlinear processes and feedback. The simple occupancy curve can saturate even while measured elongation later decreases for separate reasons.

Imagine two hypothetical tissues with effective Kd values of 0.1 and 1 micromolar for simplified ligand-dependent binding. At 0.1 micromolar, the first has a predicted occupancy of 0.1/(0.1 + 0.1) = 0.5; the second has 0.1/(1 + 0.1), approximately 0.091. At 10 micromolar the predicted occupancies rise to approximately 0.990 and 0.909 respectively. The difference is greatest in a range in which one tissue responds strongly while the other is far from saturation. This illustrates why altering the affinity or abundance of signalling components can change sensitivity without changing the measured extracellular hormone concentration. Actual co-receptor affinities and growth thresholds should not be inferred from these teaching values.

For growth, a second distinction is between sensitivity and maximal response. A more sensitive tissue requires a lower concentration to reach a specified response under comparable conditions. A tissue with a higher maximal response can achieve a greater effect once sufficiently stimulated. These parameters need not vary together. A receptor mutant could shift a curve toward higher concentrations without changing its maximum if other receptors still allow a complete response at sufficiently high concentrations. A defect in the mechanical capacity of the wall might lower the maximum even when hormone recognition is normal. A curve obtained over too narrow a concentration range cannot reliably distinguish a shift in sensitivity from a change in maximum.

A typical introductory graph compares root, bud and stem responses with auxin concentration on a logarithmic axis. It is often drawn with the root response shifted toward lower concentrations. This is a useful qualitative reminder that the primary-root elongation zone is frequently more sensitive to auxin-induced growth inhibition than a shoot elongation zone. However, the exact relative positions of such curves depend on tissue type, age, experimental preparation, background endogenous hormone and measured endpoint. In particular, the curve for initiation of a lateral root is not the same as that for elongation of the primary root. Examination questions should provide the relevant graph or context if they require a precise comparison.

The duration of exposure is another experimental variable. A transient auxin pulse can induce degradation of short-lived repressors, while an extended treatment can alter the amounts of newly synthesized repressors, metabolic enzymes and transporters. If free hormone subsequently falls because of induced conjugation, the biological output may return toward baseline even though a treatment was initially effective. Conversely, a change in cell identity initiated during a brief signalling window may persist after hormone concentration decreases. Continuous exposure and a short pulse with the same peak concentration therefore need not have equal developmental consequences.

One can distinguish the time-dependent inputs using a pulse-and-wash experiment. In one group, add auxin for a short interval and then transfer the tissue to hormone-free medium. In a second, maintain the same external concentration continuously. Include a mock transfer in the untreated group because mechanical handling or medium replacement can themselves influence growth. Measure a fast reporter of degradation, a slower transcriptional reporter and a final developmental outcome. If the fast reporter recovers after washing but root initiation still occurs, the result may indicate that a transient early signal was sufficient to initiate a stable developmental process. It does not prove the absence of endogenous hormone after washing, because the plant continues to make and redistribute IAA.

Interactions with other hormones and environmental conditions shift the effective response landscape. The same auxin treatment may produce different results when a tissue receives strong light, experiences limited water or has altered gibberellin or ethylene signalling. In one situation the wall can yield and water can enter; in another, the same transcriptional response may not produce expansion because a separate requirement is missing. The relationship between an early molecular response and final organ morphology must therefore be tested rather than assumed. This is especially relevant when comparing mutants that look superficially similar. A short plant can arise through defective hormone synthesis, impaired perception, reduced turgor, changes in wall structure or unrelated metabolic constraints. Mechanistic reasoning requires intermediate measurements.

14. Developmental and practical consequences beyond tropisms

The roles of auxin in vascular differentiation and organ positioning extend the same concepts beyond elongation. Developing vascular tissues establish connections among organs that differ in supply and demand. Auxin movement and response influence the specification of vascular cell files, and transport pathways may become reinforced during their formation. In wounded tissues, new connections can form between surviving vascular regions. The fact that auxin influences the position of a developing vascular strand does not imply that it alone specifies all the properties of xylem or phloem. Other regulatory factors coordinate cell fate, wall differentiation and the development of conductive cells. The detailed anatomy belongs to the developmental and transport subunits; the focus here is the contribution of hormone distribution to the choice of developmental sites.

The growth of fruits provides another illustration. Developing seeds and associated tissues can produce signals, including auxin, that promote growth of surrounding fruit tissues in many species. In some experimental systems, application of auxin or related hormonal treatments can promote fruit development without normal fertilization, a phenomenon called induced parthenocarpy. It should not be assumed that auxin treatment will universally reproduce normal fruit development or eliminate the need for every other signal. Different plants vary in the roles of gibberellins and other regulators, the responsiveness of ovary tissues and the timing of the treatment. Fruit initiation and the later processes of maturation and ripening are distinct developmental events.

Abscission is the separation of an organ at a specialized zone. Auxin supplied from a young or active leaf can influence the sensitivity of its abscission zone to ethylene. Changes in auxin supply as the leaf ages or is damaged can help alter that sensitivity and contribute to conditions favouring separation. The process should not be reduced to a rule that auxin always prevents abscission or ethylene always causes immediate separation. The state of the abscission zone, dose, timing and other environmental and hormonal signals matter. The important principle is that changing a hormone input can alter competence to respond to a second hormone without directly executing the final tissue separation.

Synthetic auxins used as herbicides provide a contrasting example of the biological consequences of sustained and excessive signalling. Some compounds elicit persistent auxin responses that disrupt normal growth regulation in susceptible plants. Their effects involve altered hormone signalling, growth patterns and interactions with other stress pathways, not simply an accelerated version of normal beneficial elongation. Differences in uptake, metabolism and sensitivity contribute to differences among plant species. For examination purposes, it is important to separate the chemical identity of a synthetic auxin from the usual developmental role of endogenous IAA, and to recognize that biological effects at unusually high exposure can differ qualitatively from effects within a physiological range.

The use of auxins in plant tissue culture is particularly useful because it permits controlled manipulation of developmental competence. A piece of an organ can be exposed to media with different hormonal compositions, and the resulting cell proliferation or organ formation can be compared. A hormone treatment may promote the formation of roots in a population of competent cells but fail in a population whose developmental history makes the same program inaccessible. This explains why the source tissue must be recorded in experiments comparing hormone ratios. An explant from a young stem and one from a mature leaf may have different internal hormone pools, different transcriptional states and different abilities to form organs. Comparing treatments on randomized pieces of a common source tissue helps reduce these differences.

Auxin-dependent patterning is also linked to the plant's capacity to respond to light and temperature. A changing environment can alter synthesis in local tissues, transport distribution or the competence of cells to respond. For example, elongation in response to altered light conditions can involve increased auxin production and changes in downstream growth regulation. The photoreceptor systems that initiate these responses are examined separately in sensory photobiology. Distinguishing perception of the external stimulus from the hormonal and mechanical execution of a response permits a useful division of the problem: an environmental sensor mutant may fail to generate an appropriate auxin change while retaining a response to experimentally applied auxin.

The common lesson from these examples is that auxin does not encode an organ's complete developmental identity in the way that a blueprint specifies every detail of a structure. Rather, its distribution and interpretation modify developmental decisions within an existing tissue context. A local maximum, a directional flux or a transient increase in concentration has meaning because particular cells possess the machinery to translate that input into a response. Consequently, the same molecular pathway can be reused in several processes while producing different outcomes. The physiological response must be interpreted with respect to the cells, developmental stage, spatial relationships and availability of other requirements for growth.

15. Integrated experiments and analytical reasoning

A convincing interpretation of an auxin-related phenotype should follow the path from hormone formation to the response of the organ. Different experiments address different links along that path. A measurement of free IAA by chemical analysis addresses the size of a particular pool. An assay for enzyme activity addresses the ability of a protein preparation to catalyse a reaction under specified conditions. An image of a fluorescent transporter addresses its location and sometimes relative abundance. A transcriptional reporter addresses the response of a regulatory system. An elongation measurement addresses the physical outcome. None of these individually identifies every intermediate cause. When two independent measurements disagree, the disagreement often identifies the part of the system that deserves investigation.

Suppose an Arabidopsis line has a short hypocotyl and reduced activation of a transcriptional auxin reporter. A plausible initial hypothesis is deficient biosynthesis, but a defective receptor could also reduce reporter activation. If carefully normalized chemical analysis shows less free IAA in the mutant than in the wild type, the biosynthesis hypothesis gains support, but increased conjugation or export could create the same result. Providing IAA externally tests whether the cells retain downstream responsiveness. If the mutant shows a normal early transcriptional response when supplied with an appropriate amount of IAA and later exhibits restored elongation, the defect is likely upstream of perception. An assay of precursor conversion and a comparison of the relevant conjugate pools can distinguish reduced formation from increased removal. Restoring the functional gene and observing recovery of the phenotype is a further test of causality.

An illustrative experiment compares four groups under identical growth conditions. The wild type contains 100 relative units of free IAA and shows a reporter response of 100 units after a standardized pulse. Mutant A contains 25 units of endogenous free IAA but gives a response of 95 units after the pulse. Mutant B contains 110 units of free IAA but gives a response of 12 units after the pulse. Mutant C has 100 units when measured across a whole seedling but exhibits a nearly uniform spatial reporter pattern where wild-type seedlings have a strongly localized response. A is consistent with reduced endogenous supply and preserved response. B is consistent with defective perception or a downstream response step. C raises a question about spatial distribution, receptor competence or both; bulk IAA alone cannot establish a transport defect. These invented values illustrate how evidence can guide the next test rather than prove the identity of an unknown gene.

The relationship between receptor abundance and substrate degradation can be explored quantitatively with a simplified kinetic model. Let R denote the concentration of a particular Aux/IAA protein, s its production rate and k the first-order rate constant for degradation. If both s and k are constant, dR/dt = s − kR, and the steady-state protein concentration is s/k. The units of s are concentration per unit time, and those of k are reciprocal time. Increasing the degradation constant lowers the steady-state concentration if synthesis does not change. This equation is not a detailed representation of proteasome capacity, co-receptor binding or transcriptional feedback, but it identifies the separate roles of synthesis and destruction. A hormone can reduce protein abundance without directly inhibiting its gene's transcription.

For example, imagine that a repressor is produced at 4 concentration units per minute and removed with k = 0.1 per minute. The initial steady state is 40 units. An auxin pulse changes k to 0.4 per minute, and synthesis is initially unchanged. The new predicted steady state, if the conditions persist, is 10 units. The degradation half-life of a labelled population whose new synthesis is excluded is ln(2)/k, which changes from approximately 6.93 minutes to 1.73 minutes. An increase in repressor transcription during the pulse could subsequently raise s and modify the new steady state. This helps explain why a degradation reporter is useful for studying an early response and why a long treatment may give results different from a brief pulse.

One approach to measuring this change is to follow the disappearance of a previously labelled repressor over time. A reporter carrying an intact auxin-sensitive degron can be compared with one in which the degron is mutated to reduce recognition. If the intact reporter declines after auxin addition while the resistant control remains stable, a specific degradation-dependent effect is supported. If both decline equally, altered reporter production, photobleaching, cell damage or imaging conditions must be considered. An inhibitor of proteasome function might reduce the loss of the sensitive reporter, but because the proteasome processes many other proteins, an inhibitor alone cannot establish the exact substrate-recognition mechanism. The genetic and biochemical controls together make the conclusion more specific.

A second type of experiment considers the source of altered auxin distribution. Take an organ with a localized response at its tip. The response disappears after an inhibitor of directional transport is added. The simplest interpretation is that transport is necessary to maintain the signal, but loss of local hormone synthesis or general decline in tissue health may also be involved. A local application of IAA to the previously responsive region can test downstream competence. A local synthesis measurement can test whether the treatment affects production. Imaging of carrier localization can show whether the directional arrangement changes. If local IAA restores the reporter in the treated region while the normal pattern across the organ remains abnormal, the downstream signalling machinery at that site is at least partially functional, whereas the distribution system remains impaired.

The notion of sufficiency must be separated from necessity. If local IAA application is sufficient to induce a reporter, it does not establish that local biosynthesis of IAA is necessary for that reporter in an untreated organism. The untreated signal might be supplied from another organ. To investigate necessity of local synthesis, the ability to produce hormone at the particular site must be selectively reduced while preserving other aspects of development. Conversely, if loss of local synthesis prevents the normal response, supplying hormone from a sufficiently well-positioned external source might rescue it. These two findings together indicate that local hormone availability matters, while leaving open whether the normal hormone must always be produced by a particular cell.

A related logical distinction arises in studies of acid growth. When externally acidified medium promotes extension of an excised segment, the result supports an ability of lowered wall pH to promote extension under the tested conditions. It does not show that IAA necessarily lowered wall pH in an intact plant. To test the sequence more directly, one can compare the time course of apoplastic pH change and growth after hormone addition, and examine whether interference with proton-pump activation reduces both. Restoring an acidic environment may rescue extension in a pump-impaired segment but cannot necessarily restore all other effects of IAA. A bypass intervention identifies a possible downstream step; it does not restore the entire upstream regulatory network.

Experiments also need appropriate normalization. Consider two roots of different lengths with six and twelve emerged lateral roots, respectively. If the first is two centimetres long and the second is six centimetres long, their densities are three and two emerged laterals per centimetre. The second root has more branches in total but fewer per unit length. If auxin treatment affects elongation strongly, density alone may also be misleading: an unchanged number of initiation events along a shorter axis would increase density without an increase in initiation rate. Counting developmental stages in a defined region of comparable age, along with primary-root extension, gives a more complete interpretation. Questions may deliberately report only counts or densities, requiring the student to identify what cannot be concluded.

In hormone transport experiments, a single endpoint integrates the rates of entry, redistribution, metabolism and exit over the entire treatment. Label recovered in a tissue after sixty minutes may include free IAA and multiple labelled metabolites. Comparing the time at which label first appears in a receiver with the total amount recovered later helps distinguish initial movement from subsequent accumulation. Chemical separation of labelled compounds is important when the goal is to follow intact IAA. When interpreting an inhibitor, one should also compare tissue viability and the amount of tracer initially absorbed. If the donor tissue absorbs only half as much tracer, a lower receiver signal is not sufficient evidence of a lower intrinsic efflux capacity.

An additional control concerns the direction of a stimulus. In tropism experiments, rotation may cause handling effects or alter the geometry of illumination in addition to changing the relationship of an organ to gravity. Uniform illumination or darkness, a sham-handled upright group and identical temperature conditions can help isolate the stimulus. Reversal of the lateral light source provides an especially direct prediction for positive phototropism: the identity of the faster-growing flank should switch after perception and redistribution adjust. Measuring two sides of the same organ also reduces some of the variability that arises when different seedlings are compared. A statistically convincing average curvature is more informative when accompanied by the corresponding changes in extension.

A well-designed experimental answer in a NET question should identify the predicted observation and at least one meaningful alternative explanation. If a mutant fails to bend but continues to grow uniformly, asymmetric signalling is a reasonable focus. If it fails to grow at all, the absence of curvature could arise without any specific defect in gravity or light perception. If the mutant responds to an artificial asymmetrical auxin application, it retains a capacity to translate the signal into differential growth, directing attention toward the upstream steps. The most informative conclusion is bounded by the observations rather than being the strongest causal statement that can be imagined.

Finally, a comprehensive interpretation should accommodate the fact that an organ is a coupled system. Altering the metabolism of auxin at one site can change the hormone available to a neighbour. A change in wall acidification can affect the proton gradient that contributes to auxin uptake. The hormone can induce regulators of its own degradation, while growth dilutes local pools and changes tissue geometry. These relationships explain why a linear sequence is a useful first model but not the complete description of a living plant. In answering an integrated problem, begin with the step directly manipulated, determine the expected immediate effect, and only then consider the slower consequences and possible feedback. This causal ordering reduces the chance of confusing a secondary response with the initial lesion.

16. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Auxin is a universal growth promoter. Root extension can be inhibited at concentrations that promote shoot extension. A question may change the tissue or endpoint; identify both before predicting a response.
  2. All auxin is made at the shoot apex. Local synthesis in roots and developing tissues also matters. The historical basipetal stream is not a proof that other cells lack biosynthesis.
  3. TAA/TAR and YUCCA are interchangeable. TAA/TAR produces IPyA; YUCCA acts afterward. Reversing the enzymes gives the wrong substrate order.
  4. Every conjugate is irreversible waste. Some conjugates can release IAA; DAO-dependent oxidation can irreversibly inactivate conjugates. Distinguish linkage from oxidation.
  5. Total extracted IAA equals active free IAA. Hydrolysis-based total measurements may include conjugated pools. Check the analytical method before inferring receptor exposure.
  6. IAA is always neutral. Its carboxyl group ionizes according to pH. Calculate the neutral fraction rather than assuming every molecule passively crosses a membrane.
  7. Gravity pulls auxin down a shoot. Polar transport reflects protein distribution and activity. A physical rotation does not automatically reverse the tissue's polarity.
  8. AUX1 is an ATP-powered efflux pump. AUX1/LAX contributes to influx using the proton gradient; ABCBs include ATP-dependent carriers. Match carrier and driving mechanism.
  9. All PIN proteins occur on the plasma membrane. Several PINs contribute to internal membrane transport. Specify the member and compartment before predicting intercellular flux.
  10. A high local concentration proves a large outgoing flux. A bottleneck can accumulate hormone upstream while downstream delivery falls. Apply a mass balance instead.
  11. Auxin destroys ARF to activate transcription. The canonical switch removes an Aux/IAA repressor. Degradation-resistant repressors may cause insensitivity.
  12. DR5 is a direct chemical IAA meter. DR5 reports transcription, DII reports degradation, and chemical analysis measures extracted hormone. Choose readouts appropriate to the claim.
  13. Expansins are ordinary cellulose hydrolases. They facilitate wall yielding without ordinary polysaccharide hydrolysis. A wall must retain structural integrity as it expands.
  14. Turgor alone is sufficient for growth. A wall must yield and continued water supply is often needed. Use the yield threshold when interpreting pressure data.
  15. Root and shoot must redistribute IAA to opposite sides. They may both accumulate auxin on the lower side; contrasting growth sensitivity explains opposite curvature.
  16. Stem auxin must directly enter the bud to inhibit branching. Indirect effects through cytokinin, strigolactone, transport competition and sugars are important. Decapitation modifies more than IAA.
  17. More emerged roots proves more initiation. Emergence depends on later progression and surrounding tissue. Count primordia and emerged organs separately.
  18. A high auxin/cytokinin ratio is a universal organogenesis law. Classic culture outcomes depend on explant, absolute concentrations, species and timing. Equal ratios need not yield identical responses.

17. Original examination practice

These original questions test the principles discussed in the chapter; they are not previous-year examination questions.

Part B — Conceptual understanding

Question 1

Which pair catalyses the principal two-step tryptophan-dependent route to IAA?

  1. TAA/TAR then YUCCA
  2. GH3 then DAO
  3. AUX1 then PIN
  4. TIR1 then ARF
Answer and explanation

A. TAA/TAR forms indole-3-pyruvate and YUCCA converts it to IAA. GH3/DAO modify the auxin pool; the other pairs participate in transport or response.

Question 2

The neutral form of IAA is most abundant, as a fraction of total free IAA, in which environment?

  1. Cytosol at pH 7.2
  2. Apoplast at pH 5.2
  3. Stroma at pH 8.0
  4. All have identical fractions
Answer and explanation

B. IAA is a weak acid; the protonated fraction increases at lower pH. The alkaline compartments favour IAA anion, and total concentration does not change this equilibrium fraction in the dilute model.

Question 3

What directly specifies the predominant export face in a polar cell file?

  1. The shape of the IAA molecule
  2. The orientation of gravity
  3. Asymmetric localization of plasma-membrane PINs
  4. The location of nuclear ARFs
Answer and explanation

C. Polar PIN localization directs the preferred exit face. Gravity may regulate localization but is not the physical cause of the flux; ARFs mediate transcription.

Question 4

Which family includes proton-gradient-dependent auxin influx carriers?

  1. AFB
  2. YUCCA
  3. GH3
  4. AUX1/LAX
Answer and explanation

D. AUX1/LAX supports influx using the proton electrochemical gradient. AFB perceives auxin, YUCCA forms IAA and GH3 conjugates it.

Question 5

Which auxin transporter family includes ATP-dependent transport proteins?

  1. ABCB
  2. TAA
  3. Aux/IAA
  4. SAUR
Answer and explanation

A. ABCB proteins are ATP-binding cassette transporters. TAA is an enzyme, Aux/IAA is a regulatory protein and SAUR is a downstream regulator.

Question 6

What is the primary role of the SCF TIR1/AFB complex?

  1. Hydrolysing cellulose
  2. Recruiting substrates for ubiquitination
  3. Synthesizing tryptophan
  4. Pumping protons into the wall
Answer and explanation

B. The E3 ligase selects Aux/IAA substrates in a hormone-dependent manner. Wall loosening, biosynthesis and proton transport are separate processes.

Question 7

What happens to an appropriate labile Aux/IAA repressor after canonical auxin perception?

  1. It is converted to IAA
  2. It becomes a PIN transporter
  3. It undergoes ubiquitin-dependent degradation
  4. It becomes a DNA polymerase
Answer and explanation

C. Auxin stabilizes association of the repressor with TIR1/AFB, allowing ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. It is not converted into a hormone or another enzyme.

Question 8

What is a key action of SAUR proteins in the classical shoot acid-growth pathway?

  1. Activate DAO directly
  2. Degrade ARF proteins
  3. Hydrolyse ATP in the cell wall
  4. Inhibit PP2C.D phosphatases
Answer and explanation

D. SAUR proteins can inhibit PP2C.D, favouring the activation-associated phosphorylation of H+-ATPases. They are not proton pumps or direct wall hydrolases.

Question 9

Which wall-associated proteins are classically associated with acid-induced wall loosening without ordinary polysaccharide hydrolysis?

  1. Expansins
  2. Rubisco
  3. Nitrate reductases
  4. Histones
Answer and explanation

A. Expansins facilitate wall yielding in appropriate acidic conditions. Rubisco and nitrate reductase are metabolic enzymes; histones organize chromatin.

Question 10

Which statement about turgor is correct?

  1. Turgor alone guarantees irreversible growth
  2. Turgor can provide the driving force if the wall yields
  3. Turgor is identical to apoplastic pH
  4. Turgor cannot influence elongation
Answer and explanation

B. A mature wall can resist expansion despite substantial turgor. Wall mechanical properties and water uptake determine whether pressure produces growth.

Question 11

Where are major gravity-sensing columella cells located in a primary root?

  1. Mature root hairs
  2. Phloem sieve plates
  3. Root cap
  4. Leaf epidermis
Answer and explanation

C. The root cap columella contains important statocytes. Root hairs and conducting tissue serve other functions; leaf epidermis is not a root tissue.

Question 12

In the common model of positive shoot phototropism, which flank typically elongates faster?

  1. Both exactly equally
  2. Illuminated flank
  3. Neither flank
  4. Shaded flank
Answer and explanation

D. Greater auxin-mediated elongation on the shaded flank makes it convex and the shoot bends toward light. Equal growth cannot generate this lateral curvature.

Question 13

A common lower-side auxin increase in a horizontal primary root typically causes what response at the growth zone?

  1. Relative inhibition of lower-side elongation
  2. Equal acceleration on both flanks
  3. Complete loss of gravity perception
  4. Preferential acceleration on the lower side
Answer and explanation

A. The lower-side auxin increase often inhibits root elongation relative to the upper side. Preferential lower-side acceleration would instead favour upward bending.

Question 14

Which statement best describes apical dominance?

  1. The shoot tip prevents initiation of every axillary meristem
  2. The growing apex suppresses outgrowth of existing axillary buds
  3. A root suppresses synthesis of all shoot hormones
  4. All buds contain identical auxin levels
Answer and explanation

B. Apical dominance concerns bud outgrowth, not necessarily bud initiation. It involves indirect hormone and resource interactions rather than identical hormone levels.

Question 15

Which combination commonly favours lateral bud outgrowth versus restriction?

  1. Strigolactone favours; cytokinin restricts
  2. IAA always favours; sugar always restricts
  3. Cytokinin often favours; strigolactone often restricts
  4. Both cytokinin and strigolactone universally promote
Answer and explanation

C. Cytokinins frequently stimulate outgrowth while strigolactones commonly suppress branching. These are context-dependent roles; sugar availability can also favour release.

Question 16

In Arabidopsis, many lateral root founder cells originate from which tissue?

  1. Epidermal pavement cells
  2. Cortex only
  3. Root cap mucilage
  4. Xylem-pole pericycle
Answer and explanation

D. Selected pericycle cells associated with xylem poles initiate many lateral roots. The other tissues may influence development but are not the typical founder-cell source.

Question 17

Which module is particularly important in lateral root initiation?

  1. IAA14–ARF7/ARF19–LBD
  2. GID1–DELLA–GA2ox
  3. ETR–CTR1–EIN2
  4. PYL–PP2C–SnRK2
Answer and explanation

A. IAA14 regulates ARF7/19 and downstream LBD transcription factors in lateral root initiation. The other modules belong to gibberellin, ethylene and ABA signalling.

Question 18

Which process can provide IAA from IBA?

  1. Nuclear translation
  2. Peroxisomal side-chain shortening
  3. Oxidation of cellulose
  4. Phosphorylation of PIN1
Answer and explanation

B. IBA can contribute to IAA after peroxisomal beta-oxidation-like chain shortening. The remaining choices do not convert IBA to IAA.

Question 19

Which statement about GH3 is most accurate?

  1. Every GH3 enzyme is a PIN carrier
  2. GH3 always converts IAA directly to oxIAA
  3. Certain GH3 enzymes conjugate IAA with amino acids
  4. GH3 is the 26S proteasome
Answer and explanation

C. IAA-conjugating GH3 enzymes form amide conjugates; other family members have different substrates. Oxidation is distinct from conjugation.

Question 20

Which method provides the most direct information about extracted free IAA chemical abundance?

  1. DR5 fluorescence alone
  2. PIN localization alone
  3. Primary-root length alone
  4. Appropriately validated LC-MS analysis
Answer and explanation

D. Chemical analysis can distinguish free IAA from other metabolites in an extract. Reporters and phenotypes are indirect functional measurements.

Part C — Analytical reasoning

Question 21

At pH 5.75 with IAA pKa 4.75, what fraction of free IAA is neutral?

  1. About 50%
  2. About 9.1%
  3. About 90.9%
  4. About 1%
Answer and explanation

B. The anion:neutral ratio is 10^(5.75−4.75)=10, so the neutral fraction is 1/11 ≈ 9.1%. The 90.9% value is the anion fraction; 50% applies at the pKa.

Question 22

A donor sends 8 units/h into a cell. The cell initially exports 6 and conjugates 2 units/h. Export drops to 3 with the other rates initially unchanged. What is the immediate net rate of free IAA accumulation?

  1. Zero
  2. −3 units/h
  3. 3 units/h
  4. 5 units/h
Answer and explanation

C. Net input is 8−3−2=3 units/h. Zero described the initial state; later feedback may change the rates but cannot be assumed at the instant of intervention.

Question 23

A mutant contains little IAA, but exogenous IAA restores reporter activity and hypocotyl growth. Which hypothesis gains strongest support?

  1. Complete loss of all auxin receptors
  2. A stable Aux/IAA unable to release ARF
  3. A total loss of turgor
  4. Reduced endogenous availability upstream of signalling
Answer and explanation

D. Both molecular and physical responses are restored, supporting preserved downstream competence. Low endogenous IAA can reflect reduced synthesis or increased removal; the experiment does not uniquely identify YUCCA.

Question 24

A mutant has normal free IAA, fails to degrade a sensitive degron reporter, and responds weakly to external IAA. What is the best next hypothesis?

  1. A defect in hormone-dependent substrate recruitment or degradation
  2. A shortage of tryptophan only
  3. Increased expansin activity only
  4. An obligatory rise in polar transport
Answer and explanation

A. Normal IAA and impaired degron loss point toward receptor/degradation machinery. A biosynthesis shortage would not alone explain the failure of the supplied hormone; wall mechanics acts farther downstream.

Question 25

A PIN fusion is equally abundant before and after a treatment, but moves from basal to lateral membranes. What might change first?

  1. IAA molecular formula
  2. Direction of preferred auxin export
  3. ARF DNA sequence
  4. Total number of cell walls
Answer and explanation

B. Relocalization changes where export occurs without necessarily changing total protein abundance. No change in molecular identity or DNA sequence is implied.

Question 26

In a directional assay, apical-to-basal receiver counts are 30, reverse counts 10, and background 5. What is the background-corrected directional ratio?

  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 5
  4. 2
Answer and explanation

C. Subtract background from both readings: (30−5)/(10−5)=25/5=5. Three is the uncorrected ratio; six and two do not apply the required correction.

Question 27

At equal free IAA, tissue X shows strong DR5 activity while tissue Y shows weak DR5. DII degradation is comparable. Which interpretation is most reasonable?

  1. X necessarily has more IAA
  2. DII proves all transcription is identical
  3. Both tissues must have equal growth
  4. The difference may occur downstream of degron turnover
Answer and explanation

D. Comparable DII response with unequal transcriptional output suggests possible differences in ARFs or transcriptional competence. Neither reporter alone measures all signalling or growth.

Question 28

A dominant Aux/IAA degron mutation preserves ARF binding. What occurs when auxin is increased?

  1. Repression can persist despite higher hormone
  2. All ARFs must be degraded
  3. IAA is converted to cytokinin
  4. The repressor automatically becomes an activator
Answer and explanation

A. The stable repressor may not be efficiently recruited for degradation and can continue restraining an activating ARF. Increasing hormone does not necessarily bypass this lesion.

Question 29

Repressor synthesis is 6 units/min; its first-order degradation constant rises from 0.2 to 0.6 min⁻¹. Assuming constant synthesis, what is the new steady level?

  1. 30 units
  2. 10 units
  3. 3.6 units
  4. 0.6 units
Answer and explanation

B. Steady abundance equals synthesis divided by degradation constant: 6/0.6=10. Thirty is the original level; the other choices multiply or confuse the units.

Question 30

After auxin addition, Aux/IAA mRNA rises but Aux/IAA protein falls. Which explanation is consistent?

  1. The measurements are necessarily contradictory
  2. Auxin prevents all transcription
  3. Protein degradation accelerates while transcription is induced
  4. The protein is a lipid
Answer and explanation

C. Transcription and degradation are independent rate processes. Early protein loss can coexist with negative-feedback transcription of new repressor.

Question 31

A segment has P=0.5 MPa, Y=0.3 MPa, φ=0.10 MPa⁻¹ h⁻¹. What is the initial relative irreversible expansion rate in the Lockhart model?

  1. 0.08 h⁻¹
  2. 0.2 h⁻¹
  3. 0.05 h⁻¹
  4. 0.02 h⁻¹
Answer and explanation

D. φ(P−Y)=0.10×0.20=0.02 h⁻¹. Adding pressures or using pressure without subtracting the yield threshold is incorrect.

Question 32

External acidic buffer restores growth of a pump-impaired segment, but DR5 remains weak. What is the supported inference?

  1. Acidification can bypass part of the growth defect without restoring transcription
  2. All nuclear auxin signalling is normal
  3. The proton pump is unnecessary in normal cells
  4. The buffer directly repairs TIR1 DNA
Answer and explanation

A. A downstream manipulation may restore wall conditions independently of upstream transcription. It does not establish that the normal pump or receptor is dispensable.

Question 33

A horizontal root retains uniform elongation after a transport inhibitor but no longer curves. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. All cell growth has ceased
  2. Directional redistribution or delivery may be impaired
  3. Gravity cannot be sensed by any plant
  4. The lower side necessarily contains no IAA
Answer and explanation

B. Continued uniform elongation argues against complete growth arrest. The absence of asymmetry is consistent with disruption of the tropic signal; direct hormone and sensing measurements are needed.

Question 34

The lower side of a shoot elongates 0.3 mm more than the upper side; effective flank separation is 0.6 mm. What angle does the simplified arc model predict?

  1. 0.18 rad
  2. 2 rad
  3. 0.5 rad
  4. 0.9 rad
Answer and explanation

C. θ≈ΔL/w=0.3/0.6=0.5 radians. Multiplying or adding lengths does not produce the required dimensionless ratio.

Question 35

Bud growth begins before stem IAA falls measurably after decapitation. Which hypothesis should be considered?

  1. IAA has no role at any later stage
  2. The bud is not a living organ
  3. The auxin receptor has disappeared
  4. Early redistribution of sugars can contribute to bud release
Answer and explanation

D. An early event need not require prior local IAA depletion; sugar redistribution is a plausible alternative. This timing does not exclude later auxin-dependent regulation.

Question 36

A branching mutant has normal stem IAA but is insensitive to an applied strigolactone analogue. Which inference is strongest?

  1. A downstream branching-regulatory defect can weaken apical suppression
  2. The plant cannot synthesize IAA
  3. All cytokinins are absent
  4. The root cap cannot perceive gravity
Answer and explanation

A. Normal stem IAA does not guarantee output if SL perception or downstream regulation is altered. The observations do not establish a biosynthetic IAA defect or unrelated root phenotype.

Question 37

WT has 16 initiated lateral roots, 12 emerged. A mutant has 15 initiated, 3 emerged. Which stage warrants priority investigation?

  1. Tryptophan synthesis exclusively
  2. Primordium progression or emergence
  3. Embryonic shoot initiation
  4. All founder-cell specification is absent
Answer and explanation

B. Initiation counts are close but emergence fractions differ greatly (75% versus 20%). A progression/emergence delay is more consistent than complete failure of founder-cell specification.

Question 38

Two samples each have eight emerged laterals. Primary root lengths are 2 and 4 cm. Which statement is correct?

  1. Both have the same density
  2. The longer has twice the density
  3. The shorter has twice the density but not necessarily more initiation
  4. The shorter must have twice as many initiated primordia
Answer and explanation

C. Densities are 4/cm and 2/cm, despite equal emerged counts. Density cannot establish initiation events without developmental-stage information.

Question 39

A treatment increases IAA-Asp while free IAA is nearly unchanged. Which conclusion is most defensible?

  1. IAA metabolism must be unchanged
  2. IAA-Asp is always irreversible
  3. No feedback can operate
  4. Altered conjugate turnover may be masked in the free pool
Answer and explanation

D. Compensating changes in conjugation, hydrolysis and oxidation can maintain free IAA. Conjugates are informative even when the free pool is stable.

Question 40

At 0.2 μM ligand, simplified binding sites have Kd 0.2 and 1.8 μM. Which occupancy pair follows the single-site model?

  1. 0.50 and 0.10
  2. 0.10 and 0.50
  3. 1.0 and 0.9
  4. 0.2 and 1.8
Answer and explanation

A. L/(Kd+L) gives 0.2/0.4=0.5 and 0.2/2.0=0.1. Kd is a concentration, not an occupancy. This is a teaching model, not a full auxin co-receptor model.

  3. Auxin is absent from every tissue
  4. A separate requirement outside or after initiation remains
Answer and explanation

D. The result supports a founder-cell requirement for initiation; the remaining phenotype warrants testing later developmental steps and surrounding tissues.

Question 47

Which experimental combination best tests whether directional transport rather than cell death is responsible for a lost tropic response?

  2. Tracer distribution, viability and growth controls
  3. Bulk IAA alone
  4. A root length without controls
Answer and explanation

B. Tracer data address movement, while viability and growth distinguish specific loss of differential transport from general tissue damage.

Question 48

Which paired observation is expected for a stronger canonical auxin response in an otherwise comparable cell?

  1. DR5 falls and DII rises in every case
  2. Both markers necessarily rise
  3. DR5 can increase while DII fluorescence falls
  4. Both markers necessarily stay constant
Answer and explanation

C. DR5 is a transcriptional output and sensitive DII fluorescence declines with degron-dependent turnover. Timing, competence and controls matter.

Question 49

An active asymmetric agar block is placed on the left of a responsive decapitated coleoptile in darkness. Which direction does it tend to bend?

  1. Right, because the left side elongates more
  2. Left, because the left side elongates more
  3. Vertically only in all cases
  4. It cannot respond in darkness
Answer and explanation

A. A longer left flank becomes convex, displacing the tip toward the shorter right flank. The assay shows that asymmetric growth can be produced without directional illumination.

Question 50

A researcher wants to separate adventitious root induction from later root elongation. Which design is most informative?

  1. One high-dose endpoint measuring only longest root
  2. Compare two species without controls
  3. Measure stem height only
  4. Compare pulse and continuous exposure, scoring root initiation and length separately
Answer and explanation

D. The design separates exposure timing and two distinct outcomes. A single endpoint or unrelated growth measure cannot distinguish induction from extension.

18. Revision and mastery

Core mechanisms

IAA supply reflects biosynthesis, release, transport, conjugation and removal. The weak-acid property of IAA and polar efflux carriers help establish spatial distributions. TIR1/AFB recognition of Aux/IAA promotes proteasomal repressor loss and changes ARF-dependent transcription. Shoot acid growth involves proton extrusion, pH-dependent wall yielding and turgor. Different organ sensitivities allow auxin asymmetry to drive opposite tropic responses. Apical dominance and rooting reflect hormone interactions and tissue competence.

High-yield equations

  • Neutral fraction = 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa)).
  • Free pool change = synthesis + influx + release − efflux − conjugation − direct removal.
  • Lockhart relation above yield threshold: relative irreversible expansion rate = φ(P − Y).
  • First-order repressor half-life = ln(2)/k; steady state with constant synthesis = s/k.
