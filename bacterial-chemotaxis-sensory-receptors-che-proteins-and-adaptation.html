1. The biological problem: moving toward a chemical opportunity

A bacterium living in water or in a hydrated environment rarely encounters an even distribution of dissolved substances. Nutrients released by neighboring organisms, consumption of oxygen, and the diffusion of metabolic products produce local variations in chemical composition. A motile cell can benefit from these variations only if it can connect information about the environment to a change in movement. Merely possessing a flagellum does not achieve this connection. A flagellum produces movement, but a sensory system determines how movement should be modified under different conditions. Bacterial chemotaxis is the process by which chemical information biases movement, producing a net tendency to approach favorable conditions or leave unfavorable ones. The term describes a directional outcome, not an obligation for every individual movement to point in the favorable direction.

Consider a population of swimming cells released at the middle of a vessel containing more attractant on the right than on the left. If all cells choose new directions at random and spend equal times moving in either direction, the population spreads. Some cells encounter more attractant simply by chance, but those encounters do not influence subsequent movement. If a sensory pathway extends movement during an improving chemical experience and increases the probability of changing direction during a worsening experience, the same collection of random movements produces a different population-level outcome. The cells on average drift toward the richer region. There is no requirement for a bacterium to identify the location of the source, construct a spatial map, or aim a flagellum directly at the source. Selective persistence of movement is sufficient.

It is essential to distinguish chemotaxis from the effects of a chemical on growth. A nutrient may produce a dense bacterial population near a source because cells reproduce rapidly there, even if they do not swim toward the source. Conversely, a nonmetabolizable attractant can induce a behavioral response without serving as a nutrient. Growth and movement can operate together in natural environments, but they are separate causal processes. Chemotaxis must be established by showing an appropriate directional bias or by linking accumulation to sensory-dependent movement while controlling for growth and other causes of redistribution.

Chemokinesis is a related but different concept. In chemokinesis, chemical conditions influence movement characteristics, such as speed or frequency of direction changes, without necessarily providing directional information. A cell that swims slowly in a particular region might remain there longer even if it has no mechanism that distinguishes an improving trajectory from a worsening one. Chemotaxis is therefore not proved simply by observing changed speed after applying a chemical. Directional statistics, appropriate controls and an understanding of the stimulus-response pathway are needed to establish the behavioral interpretation.

Attractants and repellents are defined relative to the organism and conditions under investigation. A chemical that is attractive to one species need not be attractive to another, because their receptor repertoires and ecological requirements differ. An attractant may be a nutrient, an indicator of a favorable microenvironment, or a ligand that resembles a useful substance. A repellent may indicate a damaging environment. The defining observation is the direction of the chemotactic response. It is not sufficient to classify every carbon-containing compound as an attractant or every potentially toxic molecule as a repellent at every concentration.

This chapter uses motile Escherichia coli as the named mechanistic model. Its arrangement of numerous flagella, sensory receptor clusters, histidine kinase and response regulators has been studied extensively. The model allows us to follow a complete chain from chemical binding to behavioral change. Other bacteria may use different flagellar arrangements, different motor response signs, and different adaptation mechanisms. The E. coli example is therefore a concrete explanation of chemotaxis rather than a universal description of all bacterial swimming.

2. Runs, tumbles and a biased random walk

In a suitable liquid medium, a motile E. coli cell commonly alternates between relatively persistent swimming and short episodes of reorientation. A persistent segment of swimming is called a run. Reorientation episodes are called tumbles. Several flagella occur at different locations on the surface of a typical peritrichously flagellated cell. When their rotation and filament geometry permit them to gather into a coherent propulsive bundle, their combined action drives the cell along a relatively smooth trajectory. Changes in the rotation of one or more motors can disturb the bundle and allow the cell to change its heading. When a propulsive bundle forms again, another run begins.

For the usual textbook E. coli description, counterclockwise rotation, abbreviated CCW, favors a left-handed flagellar bundle and smooth running; clockwise rotation, abbreviated CW, favors disruption of this arrangement and reorientation. The same viewing convention must be used for both directions. A tumble is not a precise backward step or a prescribed turn of a fixed angle. It provides an opportunity for a new heading to emerge, and the next run can be favorable or unfavorable with respect to the chemical gradient. Rotational diffusion and physical interactions also alter the heading during real swimming. A straight line followed by a sharp angular turn is a useful schematic representation, not an exact rendering of every trajectory.

A motor has a probability of being in its CW rotational state. This fraction of time is called CW bias. It is not the same quantity as swimming speed. A motor can rotate rapidly while spending little time in CW, and a different motor can rotate at a similar speed while switching more frequently. A whole bacterium contains multiple motors, so the relationship between single-motor CW bias and the frequency of whole-cell tumbles is not perfectly interchangeable. For elementary pathway reasoning, increased CW bias favors reorientation, whereas reduced CW bias favors longer runs. When interpreting actual quantitative experiments, the measured variable should be stated explicitly.

Imagine a gradient in which concentration increases along the positive x axis. A run toward positive x produces a progressively more favorable chemical experience for an attractant. If the average duration of such runs increases, the distance traveled during those runs increases without requiring a change in the speed of swimming. Runs that decrease attractant concentration are not similarly prolonged. Since reorientation continues to generate different headings, the cumulative displacement acquires a positive component along x. The movement remains stochastic, but its statistics are no longer symmetric. The population can therefore have a nonzero mean velocity along the gradient even though individual bacteria frequently travel in other directions.

This behavior is known as a biased random walk. Its random component provides exploration of the surroundings, and its biased component changes the residence time in favorable directions. Complete suppression of reorientation would not be ideal for persistent navigation through a changing environment. A cell that becomes locked into one heading cannot efficiently correct an initially poor direction. Frequent reorientation is also ineffective if it prevents sustained displacement. Useful chemotactic motion requires a workable balance between persistence and the opportunity to select new trajectories.

The difference between net drift and total distance traveled is especially important in numerical questions. A bacterium that runs ten micrometers to the right and then ten micrometers to the left travels twenty micrometers but has zero displacement along the axis. If a favorable run lasts twice as long as an unfavorable run at the same speed, net displacement can be positive even though equal numbers of run events occur in the two directions. Exam questions often change the durations while keeping the velocities fixed, and treating the run count as equivalent to the time spent running would give an incorrect conclusion.

In a uniform environment, wild-type cells still run and tumble. Basal motor switching is not evidence that an external repellent is present. It provides the exploratory motion upon which a sensory bias can act. The behavioral baseline is a regulated operating state, not a complete absence of signaling. The importance of this baseline becomes clear when we examine mutants that swim smoothly but fail to navigate: high persistence alone does not ensure that a population can find favorable chemical regions.

3. How a small cell compares chemical conditions over time

A chemical gradient is a change in concentration with position. If a bacterium is very small, the concentration difference across its cell body can be extremely weak even in an environment where the concentration differs substantially across a longer distance. Molecules also arrive at and leave receptors randomly, creating unavoidable fluctuations in the number of occupied receptors. Thus a single measurement at opposite ends of the body need not reliably specify a direction of increasing concentration. Motile E. coli primarily solves the navigation problem by relating chemical conditions encountered at different times as it moves.

Suppose a bacterium experiences concentration C at the beginning of a run and a larger concentration after one second. The increase indicates that its current trajectory has carried it into a region with a stronger attractant signal, provided the external concentration field is approximately stationary. If the concentration decreases during the run, a change of heading becomes more useful. The cell does not maintain an explicit record of earlier positions. Its molecular state retains information about recent stimulation, and the current stimulus is interpreted relative to that state. This is the operational meaning of sensory memory in the bacterial chemotaxis system.

A spatial and a temporal gradient can be related by a simple physical argument. Let C(x,t) be the concentration of an attractant at position x and time t. A moving cell at position x(t) experiences the total derivative dC/dt = ∂C/∂t + v ∂C/∂x, where v = dx/dt. The first term measures changes that would be observed by a stationary sensor; the second measures changes encountered through swimming. In a stationary concentration field, the first term is zero, and the sign of the experienced concentration change depends on swimming direction relative to the spatial gradient. In three dimensions the second term becomes the dot product of the velocity vector and the concentration gradient. It is positive when movement has a component toward higher concentration, negative when movement has a component toward lower concentration, and zero when the instantaneous heading is perpendicular to the gradient.

If the environment changes everywhere at once, the same sensory machinery can respond even when there is no spatial direction to follow. An experiment that increases attractant concentration uniformly around all bacteria can therefore temporarily prolong running without creating a persistent mean direction of swimming. This result separates chemical detection from spatial navigation. An initial run response to a uniform step shows that the signaling system is working, but a directional drift requires a spatial relationship between the chemical changes and the headings of moving cells.

Temporal comparison depends on two processes operating at different effective rates. One process transmits changes in receptor stimulation to the motor rapidly enough for the current run to be modified. A second process adjusts the receptor system more slowly, helping restore its previous operating state during persistent stimulation. If both occurred instantaneously, a concentration increase would be corrected before the motor could respond. If the adjustment never occurred, the sensory pathway could remain locked in an altered state after a persistent stimulus, reducing its usefulness for detecting subsequent changes. The separation of response and adaptation is consequently a central functional property of chemotaxis.

A classical study by Macnab and Koshland, published in 1972, investigated the behavior of Salmonella typhimurium following imposed temporal changes in attractant concentration. By separating a changing concentration in time from a conventional spatial gradient, the study provided evidence for temporal comparison. It is important to preserve the species identity in this historical example: the experiment addressed Salmonella, whereas the detailed signaling circuit studied in this chapter is the E. coli model. The experimental result supports the principle that changes over time can guide bacterial movement, rather than proving that every species has identical molecular machinery.

In another classic 1972 study, Berg and Brown used three-dimensional tracking to study E. coli swimming. Following individual cells rather than measuring only an endpoint population density enabled the researchers to connect motion statistics to chemotactic behavior. The resulting distinction between trajectory segments and reorientation events provides a conceptual basis for understanding how population migration can emerge from altered run statistics. Such tracking does not by itself identify the chemical modification that stores recent experience. Molecular experiments are needed to connect the observed motion to particular receptors and proteins.

4. Methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins and sensory specificity

The first molecular problem in chemotaxis is converting chemical information outside the cytoplasmic membrane into a biochemical change inside the cell. Methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins, abbreviated MCPs, perform this role for many signals in E. coli. They are called methyl-accepting because their cytoplasmic signaling regions contain specific residues that can undergo reversible methylation. This covalent modification is used during adaptation. The name does not imply that an environmental attractant must contain a methyl group or that ligand binding itself transfers a methyl group to the receptor.

A typical transmembrane MCP is a homodimer. Each subunit has membrane-spanning segments, a region that recognizes chemical information outside the cytoplasm, and an elongated intracellular signaling region. This arrangement physically connects ligand recognition to the proteins controlling the signaling kinase. Ligand binding can modify receptor conformation and shift the functional equilibrium of the receptor complex. The signaling consequence is transmitted across the membrane by protein structure rather than by obligatory passage of the ligand into the cytoplasm. A ligand that remains outside the cytoplasmic membrane can therefore influence the motor inside the cell through an intact receptor pathway.

Different receptors recognize different sets of stimuli. Tar is associated with aspartate sensing and with maltose sensing through a periplasmic binding protein. Tsr is an important serine receptor. Trg responds to certain sugars through their binding proteins, and Tap participates in sensing certain dipeptides through a binding protein. Aer is associated with monitoring cellular energetic or redox conditions and illustrates that some chemotactic inputs do not simply correspond to direct binding of an extracellular nutrient by a standard periplasmic ligand-binding domain. The details of recognition differ, but signals can feed into a common behavioral machinery.

Indirect recognition is particularly useful for interpreting receptor experiments. If a sugar must first bind a periplasmic binding protein, the absence of that protein could abolish the sugar response even when the MCP itself is present and structurally normal. Conversely, a mutation that damages ligand recognition in one receptor need not disable the entire motor control system. The cell may still respond to a ligand sensed through a different receptor. Therefore, when a bacterium fails to migrate toward one attractant, it is premature to conclude that the histidine kinase or all flagellar motors are defective.

Receptor occupancy and receptor activity are also distinct. In a simple one-site equilibrium, a higher concentration of a ligand increases the fraction of occupied sites until occupancy approaches saturation. Receptor activity, however, refers here to the ability of a receptor complex to promote kinase signaling. For canonical E. coli attractants such as aspartate, increased occupancy generally reduces CheA activity. This is a case in which binding a favorable signal decreases the activity of the downstream kinase. It is not scientifically valid to assume that ligand binding always turns a receptor-associated enzyme on.

The response to a ligand is influenced by receptor modification and interaction with other receptors. At a given external attractant concentration, two cells with different receptor methylation states can have different kinase activities. Conversely, cells at different attractant concentrations can exhibit similar basal kinase output after adaptation. This distinction between the external input and internal signaling state is necessary to understand why a bacterium can regain sensitivity after encountering a sustained chemical background. The cell is not simply reading the fraction of occupied receptors as an unchanging absolute concentration meter.

Spatial organization also contributes to signaling. Many E. coli chemoreceptors assemble in clusters, often near cell poles, together with CheA and the coupling protein CheW. These assemblies permit signals from many receptors to influence associated kinase activity. Cooperative interactions can increase responsiveness to modest changes in occupancy, although the details of cooperative signaling are more complex than a simple independent binding curve. At the examination level, distinguish ligand recognition by receptors, coupling through CheW, kinase activity of CheA and downstream response-regulator phosphorylation. None of these four functions is interchangeable.

5. CheW, CheA and the histidine-to-aspartate phosphotransfer

A receptor that has detected a ligand must influence an intracellular effector. In the E. coli chemotaxis system, CheW couples receptors to CheA, a histidine kinase. CheW contributes to the organization of signaling complexes and allows receptor conformational changes to regulate kinase activity. It should not be assigned the catalytic function of a histidine kinase, a phosphatase or a methyltransferase. A disruption of CheW can impair transmission of information even when ligand-binding receptors and CheA protein are still expressed. The presence of all individual components in an extract is not sufficient to establish the formation of a functional receptor–kinase complex in an intact cell.

CheA uses ATP as a phosphoryl donor. In its autophosphorylation reaction, a histidine residue on CheA becomes phosphorylated and ADP is formed. Histidine phosphorylation is chemically different from phosphorylation of a serine, threonine or tyrosine residue by the common eukaryotic protein kinases. The important causal distinction here is that CheA first acquires a phosphoryl group and then passes it to a response regulator. This provides a standard bacterial His-to-Asp signaling principle within a sensory pathway that controls behavior.

The major motor-directed response regulator is CheY. It receives the phosphoryl group on an aspartate residue. The phosphorylated product is written CheY-P. Phosphorylation changes the conformational and interaction properties of CheY, favoring its interaction with the flagellar motor switch. CheY-P therefore connects a receptor-associated kinase activity to a spatially separate target. The protein does not have to become part of the receptor cluster and motor simultaneously; it can diffuse in the cytoplasm. Because the receptor and motor occupy different locations, transmission through a mobile response regulator is a useful way of coordinating motor responses.

CheA also transfers phosphoryl groups to CheB, another response regulator. CheB contributes to the slower adaptation mechanism, rather than being the main switch signal sent to the motor. This branching is an important design feature of the pathway. An increase in kinase activity can simultaneously promote a rapid behavioral response through CheY and initiate a regulatory correction through CheB. Thus the signaling system carries information about its own activity into the mechanism that adjusts receptor sensitivity. The two substrates of CheA should not be confused merely because both belong to the same response-regulator family.

For a simplified response to an increasing concentration of a canonical attractant, the pathway signs are as follows: ligand recognition shifts the receptor complex toward lower kinase activity; CheA autophosphorylation and phosphotransfer flux decrease; the production rate of CheY-P decreases; the balance of production and removal shifts the CheY-P pool downward; motor CW bias falls; runs become more persistent. A stimulus need not alter the total quantity of CheY protein for this sequence to occur. The important quantity is the phosphorylated fraction, which can change rapidly through chemical modification of an existing protein.

A canonical repellent stimulus, or removal of an attractant from an adapted background, produces the opposite acute response: kinase activity increases, the CheY-P pool tends to increase, CW bias rises, and reorientation becomes more likely. The distinction between attractant addition and attractant removal is vital. A compound that remains an attractant can evoke a repellent-like motor response when its concentration decreases. The receptor is not reclassifying the chemical as toxic. The sensory system is responding to a worsening stimulus relative to recent conditions.

CheA-dependent phosphorylation consumes chemical energy because ATP supplies the phosphoryl group. However, this energy should not be confused with the direct power source for the E. coli flagellar motor. The two processes are coupled through information, not by transfer of the same phosphate to the rotating filament. The kinase regulates the probability of switching, whereas ion flow through the motor provides the mechanical work of rotation. Consequently, adding ATP alone to a cell with a collapsed proton motive force cannot be assumed to restore swimming. Similarly, a cell that retains an energized motor may remain motile after a signaling defect but lose directional control.

6. CheY-P and the flagellar switch

The flagellar motor converts an electrochemical ion gradient into rotation. In E. coli, the motor is normally driven by proton flow through stator complexes associated with the rotating machinery. The stators transmit force to the rotor, while the motor switch complex determines which rotational direction is favored. The biochemical signaling pathway influences this switch rather than directly assembling a new flagellum each time an attractant is encountered. It is therefore possible for a rapid chemical signal to change the behavior of pre-existing flagella.

The motor switch contains proteins including FliG, FliM and FliN. At a standard mechanistic level, phosphorylated CheY binds to switch components, particularly FliM-associated sites, and favors CW rotation in the E. coli system. The motor integrates the presence of CheY-P with its conformational transitions. When the concentration of CheY-P is low, the motor is biased toward CCW rotation; when it is higher, CW rotation becomes more probable. This relationship does not mean that each CheY-P molecule mechanically pushes the filament clockwise. Binding changes the probabilities of rotational states, and the energized motor supplies the actual force.

The relationship between CheY-P and CW bias is steep. A modest change in response-regulator concentration can produce a comparatively large change in rotational behavior over a suitable range of concentrations. Cluzel, Surette and Leibler reported a single-cell study in 2000 that connected signaling protein abundance and motor switching, providing evidence for a sharply responsive motor input-output relation. This observation explains how a small upstream chemical change can generate a clearly detectable behavioral output. It does not imply that all concentrations are equally effective: the motor response approaches saturation at the high and low ends of the response curve.

A simple Hill-type response function can illustrate steepness without implying a literal molecular stoichiometry. Let B represent CW bias, y represent the concentration of CheY-P, K represent the concentration at which B equals one half, and n represent an effective Hill coefficient. In the expression B = y^n/(K^n + y^n), increasing y increases B, while increasing n sharpens the transition near K. The effective coefficient describes the behavior of the input-output curve and should not automatically be equated with the exact number of ligand-binding molecules on the motor. Real motors can exhibit remodeling and other properties that are omitted from the elementary model.

One can now predict the behavior of a motor that fails to respond to CheY-P. If the defective motor remains capable of generating torque, upstream kinase activity and CheY phosphorylation might still change normally when an attractant is added. Nevertheless, the cell may fail to convert this change into the expected movement bias. An experiment measuring only phosphorylation would suggest that chemical sensing is preserved, whereas trajectory analysis would reveal defective output. This separation of measurements is a useful general strategy in pathway analysis.

A distinct failure occurs if the cell lacks the energy source for rotation. Such a cell may be unable to swim irrespective of the state of its switch. A nonmoving bacterium cannot demonstrate directional chemotaxis through flagellar swimming even when its receptors recognize chemical stimuli and its Che pathway remains partially functional. Motor activity, rotation direction and signal-dependent changes in rotational bias must therefore be measured separately when assigning a defect to the output machinery. This distinction also prevents students from interpreting every immotile phenotype as evidence of a receptor mutation.

7. CheZ and rapid termination of the motor signal

A phosphorylation-based switch is useful only if the phosphorylated state can be removed when the input changes. CheY-P is not a permanent molecular label. It can undergo hydrolysis, regenerating unphosphorylated CheY and releasing inorganic phosphate. CheZ accelerates this reaction in E. coli. The phosphatase thereby allows the motor-directed signal to track changes in CheA-dependent production with relatively little delay. CheZ is not the enzyme responsible for receptor demethylation, and CheB is not the main phosphatase responsible for removing phosphate from CheY.

The amount of CheY-P present at any moment is the result of opposing fluxes. CheA-dependent phosphotransfer replenishes the phosphorylated pool; spontaneous and CheZ-assisted dephosphorylation remove material from it. A steady level of CheY-P does not necessarily indicate the absence of biochemical reactions. In a steady state, the opposing fluxes are equal. A sudden decrease in production allows removal to dominate until a new balance is reached. Conversely, a sudden increase in production drives the amount upward until the removal rate becomes comparable to the new production rate.

This relationship explains why CheZ is important even though decreasing CheA activity is the primary acute response to attractant addition. If a cell had no efficient mechanism to remove pre-existing CheY-P, inhibiting production would not immediately erase the motor signal. A prolonged lifetime of CheY-P would blur the correspondence between recent chemical information and motor bias. The longer the signal persists after the stimulus changes, the less precisely movement can respond to the current trajectory. Rapid dephosphorylation therefore contributes to temporal resolution as well as to control of basal signal level.

In a simplified model, suppose the rate of CheY phosphorylation is proportional to the unphosphorylated CheY concentration, with effective rate constant a, and the rate of dephosphorylation is proportional to CheY-P, with effective constant b. Let YT be total CheY concentration and y the phosphorylated concentration. The rate of change is dy/dt = a(YT − y) − by. Setting the derivative equal to zero gives the steady state y* = YT a/(a + b). Both constants have dimensions of inverse time, so their ratio is dimensionless. The model assumes a fixed total CheY pool, constant effective rates and a single well-mixed compartment. It is an educational approximation rather than a detailed description of all enzyme saturation or spatial effects.

The same model predicts a characteristic response time of 1/(a + b), because rearrangement gives dy/dt = −(a + b)(y − y*). Lowering b at fixed a raises y* and, within this approximation, increases the response time. This result links a molecular defect in phosphatase activity to both an altered operating point and altered kinetics. In a real mutant, feedback, changes in protein abundance and other sources of CheY phosphorylation can modify these outcomes. Therefore, predictions based on this equation must be stated as conditional on its assumptions.

CheZ-mediated reset should not be confused with sensory adaptation. Dephosphorylation removes the motor-directed response regulator's phosphate and participates in the fast response. Adaptation adjusts the kinase-producing receptor complex over a longer period, enabling its activity to approach an earlier baseline even while the external attractant remains present. Both processes can reduce an abnormal motor signal, but they act on different molecular substrates and explain different portions of a step-response time course. A student who can locate the substrate of each enzyme will find it easier to distinguish these forms of regulation.

8. CheR, CheB and the slow sensory memory

When a bacterium encounters an attractant increase, CheA activity drops and running becomes more persistent. However, a constant attractant concentration cannot provide continuing information about whether the cell is moving in a favorable direction. If receptor signaling remained maximally inhibited for as long as the attractant was present, the bacterium could swim persistently even when it later began moving away from the source. The pathway therefore needs a mechanism to restore sensitivity to additional changes in ligand concentration. This mechanism is called sensory adaptation.

CheR is a methyltransferase that transfers methyl groups from S-adenosylmethionine to particular glutamate residues in the cytoplasmic portions of MCPs. The chemical product includes a methyl ester at the receptor side chain, while S-adenosylhomocysteine is formed from the methyl donor. Receptor modification changes the signaling properties of the receptor complex. In the standard E. coli model, increased receptor methylation tends to increase kinase-promoting activity, opposing the inhibitory effect of attractant binding. The methyl group is therefore part of a regulatory memory mechanism rather than a phosphate donor to CheY.

CheB has a receiver domain regulated by phosphorylation and a catalytic region capable of receptor modification. Phosphorylation through CheA enhances its activity in the adaptation pathway. CheB can hydrolyze receptor methyl esters, restoring the carboxyl groups at the modified residues; it can also participate in deamidation of particular receptor glutamines, creating sites that can enter the methylation cycle. For understanding short-term adaptation, the dominant conceptual distinction is that CheR adds methyl groups and CheB removes them. The enzymes act in opposite directions on the same regulatory class of receptor modification.

Following attractant addition, receptor activity decreases rapidly. Lower CheA activity also reduces the phosphorylation-dependent stimulation of CheB. The balance of receptor modification shifts toward increased methylation; the preference of CheR for less active receptor states and of CheB for more active states contributes to the direction of feedback. As methylation increases, kinase activity is pushed upward against the continued inhibitory influence of the attractant. CheY-P recovers toward its previous operating level, and motor switching resumes a pattern closer to the prestimulus behavior. The external attractant has not necessarily been consumed, removed or degraded. The system changes internally in response to continued exposure.

Now consider attractant removal after the cell has adapted to the higher concentration. The previously accumulated receptor methylation initially remains. When the inhibitory ligand signal is removed, the receptor complex becomes relatively kinase-active. CheY-P rises and the cell tumbles more often. Increased CheA activity promotes CheB phosphorylation and receptor demethylation, which tends to reduce receptor activity. The kinase and motor output then approach their earlier baseline. Adaptation after stimulus removal is thus not simply the reverse of ligand binding at the level of receptor occupancy; it also involves slower reversal of the receptor modification state.

This organization is a negative-feedback system because a change in kinase activity generates a receptor modification response that tends to oppose that change. Low activity favors conditions that raise activity, and high activity favors conditions that lower activity. Negative feedback does not mean that the cell fails to respond to stimuli. On the contrary, the rapid response occurs before the slow feedback has fully compensated for it. This temporary difference between the response and the adapted state creates the time window in which changes in the environment influence swimming.

If the output returns to its original steady value under a sustained stimulus, the response is described as perfect or exact adaptation for that measured output. A nearly complete recovery is described as near-perfect adaptation. Exact adaptation does not require receptor occupancy or methylation to return to their original values. In fact, restoration of signaling output while the ligand concentration remains elevated usually requires a different receptor modification state. It is therefore necessary to specify what is being measured: kinase activity, motor bias, methylation or ligand occupancy. They can have different steady-state changes.

The methylation mechanism has finite chemical capacity. A receptor has a limited number of modifiable residues. Extremely strong stimuli or particular receptor backgrounds can approach the limits of the modification range, preventing complete compensation. Adaptation also depends on intact enzymes and the availability of chemical substrates. Therefore, perfect adaptation is a property of certain operating regimes and outputs, not an unconditional claim that every cell restores every biochemical variable to its former value at any stimulus strength. Understanding this qualification helps connect the molecular mechanism with the limitations of simplified mathematical models.
