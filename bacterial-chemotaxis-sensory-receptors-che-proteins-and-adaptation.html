1. The biological problem: moving toward a chemical opportunity

A bacterium living in water or in a hydrated environment rarely encounters an even distribution of dissolved substances. Nutrients released by neighboring organisms, consumption of oxygen, and the diffusion of metabolic products produce local variations in chemical composition. A motile cell can benefit from these variations only if it can connect information about the environment to a change in movement. Merely possessing a flagellum does not achieve this connection. A flagellum produces movement, but a sensory system determines how movement should be modified under different conditions. Bacterial chemotaxis is the process by which chemical information biases movement, producing a net tendency to approach favorable conditions or leave unfavorable ones. The term describes a directional outcome, not an obligation for every individual movement to point in the favorable direction.

Consider a population of swimming cells released at the middle of a vessel containing more attractant on the right than on the left. If all cells choose new directions at random and spend equal times moving in either direction, the population spreads. Some cells encounter more attractant simply by chance, but those encounters do not influence subsequent movement. If a sensory pathway extends movement during an improving chemical experience and increases the probability of changing direction during a worsening experience, the same collection of random movements produces a different population-level outcome. The cells on average drift toward the richer region. There is no requirement for a bacterium to identify the location of the source, construct a spatial map, or aim a flagellum directly at the source. Selective persistence of movement is sufficient.

It is essential to distinguish chemotaxis from the effects of a chemical on growth. A nutrient may produce a dense bacterial population near a source because cells reproduce rapidly there, even if they do not swim toward the source. Conversely, a nonmetabolizable attractant can induce a behavioral response without serving as a nutrient. Growth and movement can operate together in natural environments, but they are separate causal processes. Chemotaxis must be established by showing an appropriate directional bias or by linking accumulation to sensory-dependent movement while controlling for growth and other causes of redistribution.

Chemokinesis is a related but different concept. In chemokinesis, chemical conditions influence movement characteristics, such as speed or frequency of direction changes, without necessarily providing directional information. A cell that swims slowly in a particular region might remain there longer even if it has no mechanism that distinguishes an improving trajectory from a worsening one. Chemotaxis is therefore not proved simply by observing changed speed after applying a chemical. Directional statistics, appropriate controls and an understanding of the stimulus-response pathway are needed to establish the behavioral interpretation.

Attractants and repellents are defined relative to the organism and conditions under investigation. A chemical that is attractive to one species need not be attractive to another, because their receptor repertoires and ecological requirements differ. An attractant may be a nutrient, an indicator of a favorable microenvironment, or a ligand that resembles a useful substance. A repellent may indicate a damaging environment. The defining observation is the direction of the chemotactic response. It is not sufficient to classify every carbon-containing compound as an attractant or every potentially toxic molecule as a repellent at every concentration.

This chapter uses motile Escherichia coli as the named mechanistic model. Its arrangement of numerous flagella, sensory receptor clusters, histidine kinase and response regulators has been studied extensively. The model allows us to follow a complete chain from chemical binding to behavioral change. Other bacteria may use different flagellar arrangements, different motor response signs, and different adaptation mechanisms. The E. coli example is therefore a concrete explanation of chemotaxis rather than a universal description of all bacterial swimming.

2. Runs, tumbles and a biased random walk

In a suitable liquid medium, a motile E. coli cell commonly alternates between relatively persistent swimming and short episodes of reorientation. A persistent segment of swimming is called a run. Reorientation episodes are called tumbles. Several flagella occur at different locations on the surface of a typical peritrichously flagellated cell. When their rotation and filament geometry permit them to gather into a coherent propulsive bundle, their combined action drives the cell along a relatively smooth trajectory. Changes in the rotation of one or more motors can disturb the bundle and allow the cell to change its heading. When a propulsive bundle forms again, another run begins.

For the usual textbook E. coli description, counterclockwise rotation, abbreviated CCW, favors a left-handed flagellar bundle and smooth running; clockwise rotation, abbreviated CW, favors disruption of this arrangement and reorientation. The same viewing convention must be used for both directions. A tumble is not a precise backward step or a prescribed turn of a fixed angle. It provides an opportunity for a new heading to emerge, and the next run can be favorable or unfavorable with respect to the chemical gradient. Rotational diffusion and physical interactions also alter the heading during real swimming. A straight line followed by a sharp angular turn is a useful schematic representation, not an exact rendering of every trajectory.

A motor has a probability of being in its CW rotational state. This fraction of time is called CW bias. It is not the same quantity as swimming speed. A motor can rotate rapidly while spending little time in CW, and a different motor can rotate at a similar speed while switching more frequently. A whole bacterium contains multiple motors, so the relationship between single-motor CW bias and the frequency of whole-cell tumbles is not perfectly interchangeable. For elementary pathway reasoning, increased CW bias favors reorientation, whereas reduced CW bias favors longer runs. When interpreting actual quantitative experiments, the measured variable should be stated explicitly.

Imagine a gradient in which concentration increases along the positive x axis. A run toward positive x produces a progressively more favorable chemical experience for an attractant. If the average duration of such runs increases, the distance traveled during those runs increases without requiring a change in the speed of swimming. Runs that decrease attractant concentration are not similarly prolonged. Since reorientation continues to generate different headings, the cumulative displacement acquires a positive component along x. The movement remains stochastic, but its statistics are no longer symmetric. The population can therefore have a nonzero mean velocity along the gradient even though individual bacteria frequently travel in other directions.

This behavior is known as a biased random walk. Its random component provides exploration of the surroundings, and its biased component changes the residence time in favorable directions. Complete suppression of reorientation would not be ideal for persistent navigation through a changing environment. A cell that becomes locked into one heading cannot efficiently correct an initially poor direction. Frequent reorientation is also ineffective if it prevents sustained displacement. Useful chemotactic motion requires a workable balance between persistence and the opportunity to select new trajectories.

The difference between net drift and total distance traveled is especially important in numerical questions. A bacterium that runs ten micrometers to the right and then ten micrometers to the left travels twenty micrometers but has zero displacement along the axis. If a favorable run lasts twice as long as an unfavorable run at the same speed, net displacement can be positive even though equal numbers of run events occur in the two directions. Exam questions often change the durations while keeping the velocities fixed, and treating the run count as equivalent to the time spent running would give an incorrect conclusion.

In a uniform environment, wild-type cells still run and tumble. Basal motor switching is not evidence that an external repellent is present. It provides the exploratory motion upon which a sensory bias can act. The behavioral baseline is a regulated operating state, not a complete absence of signaling. The importance of this baseline becomes clear when we examine mutants that swim smoothly but fail to navigate: high persistence alone does not ensure that a population can find favorable chemical regions.

3. How a small cell compares chemical conditions over time

A chemical gradient is a change in concentration with position. If a bacterium is very small, the concentration difference across its cell body can be extremely weak even in an environment where the concentration differs substantially across a longer distance. Molecules also arrive at and leave receptors randomly, creating unavoidable fluctuations in the number of occupied receptors. Thus a single measurement at opposite ends of the body need not reliably specify a direction of increasing concentration. Motile E. coli primarily solves the navigation problem by relating chemical conditions encountered at different times as it moves.

Suppose a bacterium experiences concentration C at the beginning of a run and a larger concentration after one second. The increase indicates that its current trajectory has carried it into a region with a stronger attractant signal, provided the external concentration field is approximately stationary. If the concentration decreases during the run, a change of heading becomes more useful. The cell does not maintain an explicit record of earlier positions. Its molecular state retains information about recent stimulation, and the current stimulus is interpreted relative to that state. This is the operational meaning of sensory memory in the bacterial chemotaxis system.

A spatial and a temporal gradient can be related by a simple physical argument. Let C(x,t) be the concentration of an attractant at position x and time t. A moving cell at position x(t) experiences the total derivative dC/dt = ∂C/∂t + v ∂C/∂x, where v = dx/dt. The first term measures changes that would be observed by a stationary sensor; the second measures changes encountered through swimming. In a stationary concentration field, the first term is zero, and the sign of the experienced concentration change depends on swimming direction relative to the spatial gradient. In three dimensions the second term becomes the dot product of the velocity vector and the concentration gradient. It is positive when movement has a component toward higher concentration, negative when movement has a component toward lower concentration, and zero when the instantaneous heading is perpendicular to the gradient.

If the environment changes everywhere at once, the same sensory machinery can respond even when there is no spatial direction to follow. An experiment that increases attractant concentration uniformly around all bacteria can therefore temporarily prolong running without creating a persistent mean direction of swimming. This result separates chemical detection from spatial navigation. An initial run response to a uniform step shows that the signaling system is working, but a directional drift requires a spatial relationship between the chemical changes and the headings of moving cells.

Temporal comparison depends on two processes operating at different effective rates. One process transmits changes in receptor stimulation to the motor rapidly enough for the current run to be modified. A second process adjusts the receptor system more slowly, helping restore its previous operating state during persistent stimulation. If both occurred instantaneously, a concentration increase would be corrected before the motor could respond. If the adjustment never occurred, the sensory pathway could remain locked in an altered state after a persistent stimulus, reducing its usefulness for detecting subsequent changes. The separation of response and adaptation is consequently a central functional property of chemotaxis.

A classical study by Macnab and Koshland, published in 1972, investigated the behavior of Salmonella typhimurium following imposed temporal changes in attractant concentration. By separating a changing concentration in time from a conventional spatial gradient, the study provided evidence for temporal comparison. It is important to preserve the species identity in this historical example: the experiment addressed Salmonella, whereas the detailed signaling circuit studied in this chapter is the E. coli model. The experimental result supports the principle that changes over time can guide bacterial movement, rather than proving that every species has identical molecular machinery.

In another classic 1972 study, Berg and Brown used three-dimensional tracking to study E. coli swimming. Following individual cells rather than measuring only an endpoint population density enabled the researchers to connect motion statistics to chemotactic behavior. The resulting distinction between trajectory segments and reorientation events provides a conceptual basis for understanding how population migration can emerge from altered run statistics. Such tracking does not by itself identify the chemical modification that stores recent experience. Molecular experiments are needed to connect the observed motion to particular receptors and proteins.

4. Methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins and sensory specificity

The first molecular problem in chemotaxis is converting chemical information outside the cytoplasmic membrane into a biochemical change inside the cell. Methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins, abbreviated MCPs, perform this role for many signals in E. coli. They are called methyl-accepting because their cytoplasmic signaling regions contain specific residues that can undergo reversible methylation. This covalent modification is used during adaptation. The name does not imply that an environmental attractant must contain a methyl group or that ligand binding itself transfers a methyl group to the receptor.

A typical transmembrane MCP is a homodimer. Each subunit has membrane-spanning segments, a region that recognizes chemical information outside the cytoplasm, and an elongated intracellular signaling region. This arrangement physically connects ligand recognition to the proteins controlling the signaling kinase. Ligand binding can modify receptor conformation and shift the functional equilibrium of the receptor complex. The signaling consequence is transmitted across the membrane by protein structure rather than by obligatory passage of the ligand into the cytoplasm. A ligand that remains outside the cytoplasmic membrane can therefore influence the motor inside the cell through an intact receptor pathway.

Different receptors recognize different sets of stimuli. Tar is associated with aspartate sensing and with maltose sensing through a periplasmic binding protein. Tsr is an important serine receptor. Trg responds to certain sugars through their binding proteins, and Tap participates in sensing certain dipeptides through a binding protein. Aer is associated with monitoring cellular energetic or redox conditions and illustrates that some chemotactic inputs do not simply correspond to direct binding of an extracellular nutrient by a standard periplasmic ligand-binding domain. The details of recognition differ, but signals can feed into a common behavioral machinery.

Indirect recognition is particularly useful for interpreting receptor experiments. If a sugar must first bind a periplasmic binding protein, the absence of that protein could abolish the sugar response even when the MCP itself is present and structurally normal. Conversely, a mutation that damages ligand recognition in one receptor need not disable the entire motor control system. The cell may still respond to a ligand sensed through a different receptor. Therefore, when a bacterium fails to migrate toward one attractant, it is premature to conclude that the histidine kinase or all flagellar motors are defective.

Receptor occupancy and receptor activity are also distinct. In a simple one-site equilibrium, a higher concentration of a ligand increases the fraction of occupied sites until occupancy approaches saturation. Receptor activity, however, refers here to the ability of a receptor complex to promote kinase signaling. For canonical E. coli attractants such as aspartate, increased occupancy generally reduces CheA activity. This is a case in which binding a favorable signal decreases the activity of the downstream kinase. It is not scientifically valid to assume that ligand binding always turns a receptor-associated enzyme on.

The response to a ligand is influenced by receptor modification and interaction with other receptors. At a given external attractant concentration, two cells with different receptor methylation states can have different kinase activities. Conversely, cells at different attractant concentrations can exhibit similar basal kinase output after adaptation. This distinction between the external input and internal signaling state is necessary to understand why a bacterium can regain sensitivity after encountering a sustained chemical background. The cell is not simply reading the fraction of occupied receptors as an unchanging absolute concentration meter.

Spatial organization also contributes to signaling. Many E. coli chemoreceptors assemble in clusters, often near cell poles, together with CheA and the coupling protein CheW. These assemblies permit signals from many receptors to influence associated kinase activity. Cooperative interactions can increase responsiveness to modest changes in occupancy, although the details of cooperative signaling are more complex than a simple independent binding curve. At the examination level, distinguish ligand recognition by receptors, coupling through CheW, kinase activity of CheA and downstream response-regulator phosphorylation. None of these four functions is interchangeable.

5. CheW, CheA and the histidine-to-aspartate phosphotransfer

A receptor that has detected a ligand must influence an intracellular effector. In the E. coli chemotaxis system, CheW couples receptors to CheA, a histidine kinase. CheW contributes to the organization of signaling complexes and allows receptor conformational changes to regulate kinase activity. It should not be assigned the catalytic function of a histidine kinase, a phosphatase or a methyltransferase. A disruption of CheW can impair transmission of information even when ligand-binding receptors and CheA protein are still expressed. The presence of all individual components in an extract is not sufficient to establish the formation of a functional receptor–kinase complex in an intact cell.

CheA uses ATP as a phosphoryl donor. In its autophosphorylation reaction, a histidine residue on CheA becomes phosphorylated and ADP is formed. Histidine phosphorylation is chemically different from phosphorylation of a serine, threonine or tyrosine residue by the common eukaryotic protein kinases. The important causal distinction here is that CheA first acquires a phosphoryl group and then passes it to a response regulator. This provides a standard bacterial His-to-Asp signaling principle within a sensory pathway that controls behavior.

The major motor-directed response regulator is CheY. It receives the phosphoryl group on an aspartate residue. The phosphorylated product is written CheY-P. Phosphorylation changes the conformational and interaction properties of CheY, favoring its interaction with the flagellar motor switch. CheY-P therefore connects a receptor-associated kinase activity to a spatially separate target. The protein does not have to become part of the receptor cluster and motor simultaneously; it can diffuse in the cytoplasm. Because the receptor and motor occupy different locations, transmission through a mobile response regulator is a useful way of coordinating motor responses.

CheA also transfers phosphoryl groups to CheB, another response regulator. CheB contributes to the slower adaptation mechanism, rather than being the main switch signal sent to the motor. This branching is an important design feature of the pathway. An increase in kinase activity can simultaneously promote a rapid behavioral response through CheY and initiate a regulatory correction through CheB. Thus the signaling system carries information about its own activity into the mechanism that adjusts receptor sensitivity. The two substrates of CheA should not be confused merely because both belong to the same response-regulator family.

For a simplified response to an increasing concentration of a canonical attractant, the pathway signs are as follows: ligand recognition shifts the receptor complex toward lower kinase activity; CheA autophosphorylation and phosphotransfer flux decrease; the production rate of CheY-P decreases; the balance of production and removal shifts the CheY-P pool downward; motor CW bias falls; runs become more persistent. A stimulus need not alter the total quantity of CheY protein for this sequence to occur. The important quantity is the phosphorylated fraction, which can change rapidly through chemical modification of an existing protein.

A canonical repellent stimulus, or removal of an attractant from an adapted background, produces the opposite acute response: kinase activity increases, the CheY-P pool tends to increase, CW bias rises, and reorientation becomes more likely. The distinction between attractant addition and attractant removal is vital. A compound that remains an attractant can evoke a repellent-like motor response when its concentration decreases. The receptor is not reclassifying the chemical as toxic. The sensory system is responding to a worsening stimulus relative to recent conditions.

CheA-dependent phosphorylation consumes chemical energy because ATP supplies the phosphoryl group. However, this energy should not be confused with the direct power source for the E. coli flagellar motor. The two processes are coupled through information, not by transfer of the same phosphate to the rotating filament. The kinase regulates the probability of switching, whereas ion flow through the motor provides the mechanical work of rotation. Consequently, adding ATP alone to a cell with a collapsed proton motive force cannot be assumed to restore swimming. Similarly, a cell that retains an energized motor may remain motile after a signaling defect but lose directional control.

6. CheY-P and the flagellar switch

The flagellar motor converts an electrochemical ion gradient into rotation. In E. coli, the motor is normally driven by proton flow through stator complexes associated with the rotating machinery. The stators transmit force to the rotor, while the motor switch complex determines which rotational direction is favored. The biochemical signaling pathway influences this switch rather than directly assembling a new flagellum each time an attractant is encountered. It is therefore possible for a rapid chemical signal to change the behavior of pre-existing flagella.

The motor switch contains proteins including FliG, FliM and FliN. At a standard mechanistic level, phosphorylated CheY binds to switch components, particularly FliM-associated sites, and favors CW rotation in the E. coli system. The motor integrates the presence of CheY-P with its conformational transitions. When the concentration of CheY-P is low, the motor is biased toward CCW rotation; when it is higher, CW rotation becomes more probable. This relationship does not mean that each CheY-P molecule mechanically pushes the filament clockwise. Binding changes the probabilities of rotational states, and the energized motor supplies the actual force.

The relationship between CheY-P and CW bias is steep. A modest change in response-regulator concentration can produce a comparatively large change in rotational behavior over a suitable range of concentrations. Cluzel, Surette and Leibler reported a single-cell study in 2000 that connected signaling protein abundance and motor switching, providing evidence for a sharply responsive motor input-output relation. This observation explains how a small upstream chemical change can generate a clearly detectable behavioral output. It does not imply that all concentrations are equally effective: the motor response approaches saturation at the high and low ends of the response curve.

A simple Hill-type response function can illustrate steepness without implying a literal molecular stoichiometry. Let B represent CW bias, y represent the concentration of CheY-P, K represent the concentration at which B equals one half, and n represent an effective Hill coefficient. In the expression B = y^n/(K^n + y^n), increasing y increases B, while increasing n sharpens the transition near K. The effective coefficient describes the behavior of the input-output curve and should not automatically be equated with the exact number of ligand-binding molecules on the motor. Real motors can exhibit remodeling and other properties that are omitted from the elementary model.

One can now predict the behavior of a motor that fails to respond to CheY-P. If the defective motor remains capable of generating torque, upstream kinase activity and CheY phosphorylation might still change normally when an attractant is added. Nevertheless, the cell may fail to convert this change into the expected movement bias. An experiment measuring only phosphorylation would suggest that chemical sensing is preserved, whereas trajectory analysis would reveal defective output. This separation of measurements is a useful general strategy in pathway analysis.

A distinct failure occurs if the cell lacks the energy source for rotation. Such a cell may be unable to swim irrespective of the state of its switch. A nonmoving bacterium cannot demonstrate directional chemotaxis through flagellar swimming even when its receptors recognize chemical stimuli and its Che pathway remains partially functional. Motor activity, rotation direction and signal-dependent changes in rotational bias must therefore be measured separately when assigning a defect to the output machinery. This distinction also prevents students from interpreting every immotile phenotype as evidence of a receptor mutation.

7. CheZ and rapid termination of the motor signal

A phosphorylation-based switch is useful only if the phosphorylated state can be removed when the input changes. CheY-P is not a permanent molecular label. It can undergo hydrolysis, regenerating unphosphorylated CheY and releasing inorganic phosphate. CheZ accelerates this reaction in E. coli. The phosphatase thereby allows the motor-directed signal to track changes in CheA-dependent production with relatively little delay. CheZ is not the enzyme responsible for receptor demethylation, and CheB is not the main phosphatase responsible for removing phosphate from CheY.

The amount of CheY-P present at any moment is the result of opposing fluxes. CheA-dependent phosphotransfer replenishes the phosphorylated pool; spontaneous and CheZ-assisted dephosphorylation remove material from it. A steady level of CheY-P does not necessarily indicate the absence of biochemical reactions. In a steady state, the opposing fluxes are equal. A sudden decrease in production allows removal to dominate until a new balance is reached. Conversely, a sudden increase in production drives the amount upward until the removal rate becomes comparable to the new production rate.

This relationship explains why CheZ is important even though decreasing CheA activity is the primary acute response to attractant addition. If a cell had no efficient mechanism to remove pre-existing CheY-P, inhibiting production would not immediately erase the motor signal. A prolonged lifetime of CheY-P would blur the correspondence between recent chemical information and motor bias. The longer the signal persists after the stimulus changes, the less precisely movement can respond to the current trajectory. Rapid dephosphorylation therefore contributes to temporal resolution as well as to control of basal signal level.

In a simplified model, suppose the rate of CheY phosphorylation is proportional to the unphosphorylated CheY concentration, with effective rate constant a, and the rate of dephosphorylation is proportional to CheY-P, with effective constant b. Let YT be total CheY concentration and y the phosphorylated concentration. The rate of change is dy/dt = a(YT − y) − by. Setting the derivative equal to zero gives the steady state y* = YT a/(a + b). Both constants have dimensions of inverse time, so their ratio is dimensionless. The model assumes a fixed total CheY pool, constant effective rates and a single well-mixed compartment. It is an educational approximation rather than a detailed description of all enzyme saturation or spatial effects.

The same model predicts a characteristic response time of 1/(a + b), because rearrangement gives dy/dt = −(a + b)(y − y*). Lowering b at fixed a raises y* and, within this approximation, increases the response time. This result links a molecular defect in phosphatase activity to both an altered operating point and altered kinetics. In a real mutant, feedback, changes in protein abundance and other sources of CheY phosphorylation can modify these outcomes. Therefore, predictions based on this equation must be stated as conditional on its assumptions.

CheZ-mediated reset should not be confused with sensory adaptation. Dephosphorylation removes the motor-directed response regulator's phosphate and participates in the fast response. Adaptation adjusts the kinase-producing receptor complex over a longer period, enabling its activity to approach an earlier baseline even while the external attractant remains present. Both processes can reduce an abnormal motor signal, but they act on different molecular substrates and explain different portions of a step-response time course. A student who can locate the substrate of each enzyme will find it easier to distinguish these forms of regulation.

8. CheR, CheB and the slow sensory memory

When a bacterium encounters an attractant increase, CheA activity drops and running becomes more persistent. However, a constant attractant concentration cannot provide continuing information about whether the cell is moving in a favorable direction. If receptor signaling remained maximally inhibited for as long as the attractant was present, the bacterium could swim persistently even when it later began moving away from the source. The pathway therefore needs a mechanism to restore sensitivity to additional changes in ligand concentration. This mechanism is called sensory adaptation.

CheR is a methyltransferase that transfers methyl groups from S-adenosylmethionine to particular glutamate residues in the cytoplasmic portions of MCPs. The product is a methyl ester at the receptor side chain, while S-adenosylhomocysteine is formed from the methyl donor. Hydrolysis of this methyl ester releases methanol and restores the glutamate carboxyl group. Receptor modification changes the signaling properties of the receptor complex. In the standard E. coli model, increased receptor methylation tends to increase kinase-promoting activity, opposing the inhibitory effect of attractant binding. The methyl group is therefore part of a regulatory memory mechanism rather than a phosphate donor to CheY.

CheB has a receiver domain regulated by phosphorylation and a catalytic region capable of receptor modification. Phosphorylation through CheA enhances its activity in the adaptation pathway. CheB can hydrolyze receptor methyl esters, restoring the carboxyl groups at the modified residues; it can also participate in deamidation of particular receptor glutamines, creating sites that can enter the methylation cycle. For understanding short-term adaptation, the dominant conceptual distinction is that CheR adds methyl groups and CheB removes them. The enzymes act in opposite directions on the same regulatory class of receptor modification.

Following attractant addition, receptor activity decreases rapidly. Lower CheA activity also reduces the phosphorylation-dependent stimulation of CheB. The balance of receptor modification shifts toward increased methylation; the preference of CheR for less active receptor states and of CheB for more active states contributes to the direction of feedback. As methylation increases, kinase activity is pushed upward against the continued inhibitory influence of the attractant. CheY-P recovers toward its previous operating level, and motor switching resumes a pattern closer to the prestimulus behavior. The external attractant has not necessarily been consumed, removed or degraded. The system changes internally in response to continued exposure.

Now consider attractant removal after the cell has adapted to the higher concentration. The previously accumulated receptor methylation initially remains. When the inhibitory ligand signal is removed, the receptor complex becomes relatively kinase-active. CheY-P rises and the cell tumbles more often. Increased CheA activity promotes CheB phosphorylation and receptor demethylation, which tends to reduce receptor activity. The kinase and motor output then approach their earlier baseline. Adaptation after stimulus removal is thus not simply the reverse of ligand binding at the level of receptor occupancy; it also involves slower reversal of the receptor modification state.

This organization is a negative-feedback system because a change in kinase activity generates a receptor modification response that tends to oppose that change. Low activity favors conditions that raise activity, and high activity favors conditions that lower activity. Negative feedback does not mean that the cell fails to respond to stimuli. On the contrary, the rapid response occurs before the slow feedback has fully compensated for it. This temporary difference between the response and the adapted state creates the time window in which changes in the environment influence swimming.

If the output returns to its original steady value under a sustained stimulus, the response is described as perfect or exact adaptation for that measured output. A nearly complete recovery is described as near-perfect adaptation. Exact adaptation does not require receptor occupancy or methylation to return to their original values. In fact, restoration of signaling output while the ligand concentration remains elevated usually requires a different receptor modification state. It is therefore necessary to specify what is being measured: kinase activity, motor bias, methylation or ligand occupancy. They can have different steady-state changes.

The methylation mechanism has finite chemical capacity. A receptor has a limited number of modifiable residues. Extremely strong stimuli or particular receptor backgrounds can approach the limits of the modification range, preventing complete compensation. Adaptation also depends on intact enzymes and the availability of chemical substrates. Therefore, perfect adaptation is a property of certain operating regimes and outputs, not an unconditional claim that every cell restores every biochemical variable to its former value at any stimulus strength. Understanding this qualification helps connect the molecular mechanism with the limitations of simplified mathematical models.

9. Ligand binding, sensitivity and receptor saturation

A receptor system is useful only if its biochemical response varies with the chemical conditions that the bacterium encounters. To understand how receptor sensitivity depends on concentration, it is helpful to begin with a deliberately simple model of ligand binding. Consider one population of equivalent, independent receptors, each of which binds one ligand molecule reversibly. Let R represent a free receptor, L the freely available ligand, and RL the receptor–ligand complex. At equilibrium, the dissociation constant Kd is defined by Kd = [R][L]/[RL], where the brackets denote molar concentrations. A small dissociation constant indicates high affinity under the conditions of the measurement because a relatively small concentration of free ligand can maintain a substantial bound fraction. This definition does not say whether the occupied receptor promotes or inhibits CheA activity.

The total receptor concentration is RT = [R] + [RL]. The fraction of receptor sites occupied is θ = [RL]/RT. Substituting [R] = RT − [RL] into the equilibrium relationship gives Kd[RL] = (RT − [RL])[L]. Collecting the terms that contain [RL] gives [RL](Kd + [L]) = RT[L]. Division by RT(Kd + [L]) yields θ = [L]/(Kd + [L]). Concentration and dissociation constant must be expressed in the same units. The ratio is dimensionless and lies between zero and one. At a ligand concentration equal to Kd, occupancy is one half. This result follows from equilibrium mass action and does not require any assumption about the behavior of the bacterial motor.

The sensitivity of occupancy to a change in ligand concentration is not uniform. At concentrations very far below Kd, most sites remain empty, and the absolute change in occupancy caused by a small proportional increase may be weak. At concentrations far above Kd, most sites remain occupied, and additional ligand has little effect on the bound fraction. In contrast, an intermediate background permits a substantial change in occupancy. For proportional rather than absolute concentration changes, differentiation with respect to ln[L] gives dθ/dln[L] = [L]Kd/(Kd + [L])², which can also be written θ(1 − θ). This expression is greatest when θ equals one half. The result helps explain why a fixed, nonadjustable receptor system would have a limited useful concentration range.

For example, if Kd is 10 micromolar, a rise in concentration from 10 to 20 micromolar changes occupancy from 0.50 to approximately 0.667, an increase of about 0.167 or 16.7 percentage points. A rise from 100 to 200 micromolar is also a doubling, but occupancy changes from approximately 0.909 to 0.952, only about 4.3 percentage points. The same proportional external stimulus does not produce the same occupancy change under all backgrounds. If an examination question instead asks for the percentage increase in occupancy relative to the initial value, the answer is a different number. For the first example it is roughly 33.3%, not 16.7%. Percentages and percentage-point differences should be distinguished.

Real E. coli chemoreceptor assemblies cannot be treated as a single population of independent binding sites under every condition. Receptors have conformational states, interact cooperatively and undergo covalent modification. The binding equation just derived is therefore a prerequisite model for understanding affinity and saturation, not a complete quantitative model of the MCP–CheA complex. In particular, half occupancy of a receptor does not establish half-maximal CheA activity or half-maximal CW bias. Those outputs depend on intervening signaling steps. The distinction between receptor binding and downstream dose-response is transferable to many signaling systems.

Adaptation does not necessarily make a ligand bind and dissociate with identical kinetics at every background. Instead, by changing the receptor's signaling state, it can restore a useful operating level of kinase activity after a persistent stimulus. The system may consequently detect new changes despite substantial differences in the absolute amount of ligand present. Receptors with different recognition properties also contribute to the range of chemical conditions the cell can monitor. Eventually, however, extreme receptor occupancy or modification limits can reduce responsiveness. It is inaccurate to say that adaptation eliminates saturation as a physical property.

At the level of experimental design, an investigator who measures binding alone should not claim to have measured chemotaxis. For a receptor mutation, a binding assay can establish whether ligand recognition is altered, while a kinase-output assay can establish whether receptor occupancy is transmitted to CheA. A motor-output experiment then tests whether that signal has a behavioral consequence. If a mutation preserves occupancy yet eliminates stimulus-dependent kinase inhibition, the defect lies in coupling or transmission rather than necessarily in affinity. If receptor binding is defective, adding a much larger concentration of ligand may partly restore occupancy for a reduced-affinity mutation but cannot be expected to rescue an absent binding site. These predictions illustrate why a sequence of distinct assays provides more informative evidence than one endpoint.

10. Signal dynamics: production, removal and the response time

The simple phosphorylation equation introduced earlier can now be used to reason about the time required for a change in receptor activity to affect motor output. Suppose the total cellular concentration of CheY is held constant over a short experiment. A sufficiently abrupt attractant step can change the effective phosphorylation rate from an initial value a0 to a lower value a1 before the slower receptor adaptation has proceeded substantially. If the effective dephosphorylation rate b is unchanged during this interval, the phosphorylated pool relaxes toward a new target value. The target is not reached instantly because molecules already present in the phosphorylated and unphosphorylated pools must undergo reactions.

For constant rates, the equation dy/dt = −(a + b)(y − y*) has the solution y(t) = y* + [y(0) − y*]exp[−(a + b)t]. To see why, define z = y − y*. Since y* is constant for fixed a and b, dz/dt = dy/dt = −(a + b)z. Separation of variables gives dz/z = −(a + b)dt. Integration yields ln|z| = −(a + b)t + constant, so z(t) = z(0)exp[−(a + b)t]. The sign of z(0) determines whether y falls or rises toward the steady state, while the positive sum of the rate constants determines how quickly the difference decays.

The time constant τ = 1/(a + b) is the time at which the initial deviation is reduced to 1/e, approximately 36.8%, of its starting magnitude. The half-time of the deviation is ln(2)/(a + b). The half-time is not necessarily the time required for the total CheY-P concentration to become half of its initial value. If the final equilibrium concentration is nonzero, the exponentially decaying quantity is the difference from that equilibrium. This distinction is a familiar one in enzyme kinetics and first-order relaxation but becomes easy to miss when the biological variable is a phosphorylation state.

Consider an illustrative cell with total CheY concentration of 10 micromolar. Initially a = 2 per second and b = 3 per second. The steady concentration of CheY-P is 10 × 2/(2 + 3) = 4 micromolar. At time zero, an attractant step reduces a to 0.5 per second without immediately changing b. The new short-time target is 10 × 0.5/(0.5 + 3) = 1.4286 micromolar. The relaxation time is 1/3.5 = 0.2857 seconds. After 0.5 seconds, the concentration predicted by this simplified model is 1.4286 + (4 − 1.4286)exp(−1.75), or approximately 1.8754 micromolar. These values are hypothetical and are not asserted to be experimentally measured E. coli concentrations or rate constants.

If b is reduced from 3 per second to 1 per second while the initial phosphorylation rate remains 2 per second, the predicted baseline increases to 10 × 2/(2 + 1) = 6.6667 micromolar. After the same reduction of a to 0.5 per second, the new target becomes 3.3333 micromolar and the response time becomes 1/1.5 = 0.6667 seconds. Thus a slower removal process changes both the baseline and the speed of response. A comparison based only on the absolute difference between initial and final concentrations would fail to capture the increased persistence of the signal.

The model assumes that CheA activity can be represented by one effective first-order rate. In reality CheA must acquire phosphate and compete among substrates; CheY-P removal may depend on CheZ concentration, localization and other biochemical features. The total CheY pool may also change across strains or during long-term experiments. The approximation is most useful for a short response interval in which protein synthesis and slow adaptation can be neglected and a simple change in effective rate is justified. Students should recognize the distinction between deriving the consequences of a specified model and claiming that the model precisely reproduces all living cells.

A different limiting case is useful when the phosphorylation flux is shut off entirely. If a is zero, dy/dt = −by, so y(t) = y(0)exp(−bt). The half-life of CheY-P under this idealized condition is ln(2)/b. If the half-life is measured as 0.2 seconds, b is approximately 3.47 per second. If another strain gives a half-life of 1.0 second under otherwise identical conditions, its effective removal rate is approximately 0.693 per second. The second strain removes the phosphate about five times more slowly in the model. However, without knowing that production has indeed ceased, fitting the decline to a zero-target exponential may give a misleading estimate of dephosphorylation.

The ability to resolve the fast signal separately from adaptation is one reason time-resolved measurements are valuable. If a phosphorylation measurement is obtained only several minutes after a sustained attractant step, the measured concentration may have recovered close to the original level. That observation alone does not show that the cell failed to respond. A sufficiently rapid series of early measurements can reveal the initial reduction, and later measurements can reveal recovery. Experimental sampling intervals must match the biological process under investigation.

11. Adaptation as a regulatory feedback problem

The slow feedback mechanism can be explained using a minimal activity-and-modification model. Let A denote a normalized kinase-promoting activity between zero and one, and m denote a variable representing the mean level of receptor methylation. For a representative E. coli attractant at a fixed methylation level, increasing attractant concentration tends to decrease A. At a fixed ligand concentration, increasing receptor methylation tends to increase A. These two influences have opposite signs, permitting a change in modification to compensate for a change in ligand input. The model concerns the functional signs of the relationships, not an assumption that every receptor species has precisely the same conformational states or number of methylation sites.

A simple phenomenological equation for the slow modification variable is dm/dt = kR(1 − A) − kB A. Here kR and kB are positive effective modification rates, with units of methylation-variable change per unit time. The first term represents net methylation favored when activity is low; the second represents net demethylation favored when activity is high. The expression is not a detailed enzyme rate law and does not explicitly track CheB phosphorylation, receptor site occupancy or substrate binding. It captures the direction of the negative feedback under suitable conditions and allows a useful analytic result to be derived.

At an interior steady state, dm/dt = 0, so kR(1 − A*) = kB A*. Rearrangement gives A* = kR/(kR + kB). In this model the adapted activity depends on the relative modification rates but not directly on the constant ligand concentration. Instead, m changes until the relation between methylation, ligand concentration and activity permits the same steady A*. This demonstrates how a feedback architecture can restore an output without restoring all its internal variables. It also explains why a mutation that changes the balance of methylation enzymes can alter the basal operating point even when the mathematical form of the feedback remains intact.

For example, if kR = 0.06 units per second and kB = 0.14 units per second, the predicted steady activity is 0.30. If a persistent attractant step initially reduces activity to 0.10, the initial rate of modification in this model is 0.06 × 0.90 − 0.14 × 0.10 = 0.040 units per second. Modification increases, raising activity. If removal of the attractant instead raises activity to 0.70, the initial rate is 0.06 × 0.30 − 0.14 × 0.70 = −0.080 units per second. Modification decreases, opposing the increase in activity. The numerical example shows why the feedback has the correct sign for both stimulus addition and stimulus removal.

A further insight is that the slow variable accumulates the difference between present activity and a preferred activity. The same equation can be written dm/dt = (kR + kB)(A* − A). Its integral over time depends on the accumulated deviation of the kinase output from A*. In a simplified control-system description, this is an integral feedback form. It is not necessary to learn specialized control theory to appreciate the biological consequence: methylation continues to change while the output remains too low or too high, and ceases to change on average when the appropriate activity is restored. The biochemical modification acts as a slowly changing record of the history of signaling error.

For this mathematical result to apply, a feasible modification state must exist for the particular ligand concentration. If the receptors are already at their maximum methylation state and the attractant still suppresses activity below A*, the equation predicts an upward tendency but the physical variable cannot increase indefinitely. A real receptor pool cannot contain more modified sites than are available. Once the boundary is reached, the assumption of an interior steady state fails, and perfect adaptation is not predicted. This is a general lesson in quantitative biology: solving a steady-state equation algebraically does not establish that the solution lies within a physically possible domain.

A classic experimental examination of robustness by Alon and colleagues, published in 1999, varied the levels of chemotaxis proteins in E. coli and compared several response properties. The important result for examination reasoning is that different aspects of the response can have different robustness: adaptation precision may be maintained even while baseline behavior or adaptation times change. It would be incorrect to translate this into a claim that the network is insensitive to every mutation or that all response features are constant. The experimental observation motivates the study of regulatory architecture, because the ability to restore an output can be less sensitive to parameter variation than the transient trajectory used to reach it.

Consider an experiment in which two strains both show an initial reduction of kinase activity from their own prestimulus baselines after an attractant step. Strain one returns almost exactly to its original output after twenty seconds. Strain two returns to its original output after sixty seconds. It is reasonable to conclude that their adaptation kinetics differ while their measured adaptation precision is similar under the chosen conditions. It is not reasonable to call strain two completely defective in adaptation merely because it is slower. Conversely, a strain returning quickly to a substantially incorrect final activity has a precision defect even if its transient time is short. Examination problems commonly use these differences to test whether students distinguish a rate from an endpoint.

The term sensory memory may sound as though the bacterium stores a list of earlier concentrations. The molecular meaning is much more specific. A receptor modification state reflects the integrated consequences of earlier stimulation and modifies the current response. Two cells exposed to the same current concentration may respond differently if their recent stimulus histories differ. Over time in a stable environment, their states can converge toward an adapted range. This dependence on recent history is an emergent consequence of biochemical feedback, not a requirement for a nervous system or a dedicated representation of the past.

12. Genetic dissection of the chemotaxis pathway

An effective way to understand a multicomponent signaling system is to ask what would happen if individual components were removed or altered. Genetic reasoning should begin by identifying the direct molecular function of the affected gene product and then tracing the expected consequences through the pathway. For example, a deletion of cheY removes the principal phosphorylated motor-directed signal. If the cell still possesses functional flagella and adequate energy, the usual prediction for E. coli is a strong smooth-swimming or CCW-biased phenotype, rather than complete immobility. Because the cell cannot properly modulate reorientation through the main CheY pathway, its directional migration is defective even though it can move.

A loss of CheA catalytic activity also reduces the normal receptor-driven supply of CheY-P. This may produce a superficially similar behavioral phenotype to loss of CheY. The molecular defects are nevertheless different. A CheA-defective cell lacks the central kinase output, whereas a CheY-defective cell may retain receptor-dependent kinase changes and phosphorylation of CheB. To distinguish the two, one could measure kinase activity directly or examine another CheA-dependent output instead of relying solely on the swimming pattern. A shared terminal phenotype is not enough to establish that two genes encode proteins with the same function.

The prediction for loss of CheZ differs. CheY-P remains capable of being produced, but its efficient removal is impaired. All else equal, the phosphorylated signal persists longer and tends to accumulate, favoring CW bias and frequent reorientation. A cheZ mutant need not exhibit an absolute and irreversible motor lock because spontaneous dephosphorylation still exists and actual behavior depends on the balance of biochemical reactions. The key examination inference is the direction of the primary kinetic perturbation. Reduced removal is not equivalent to reduced production even if both mutations ultimately disrupt chemotactic performance.

CheW mutations can compromise the coupling between MCPs and CheA. In this case the receptors may still bind ligand and CheA may still exist, yet kinase output fails to respond normally to changing receptor conditions. This type of defect is particularly useful for separating complex formation from enzymatic catalysis. One could compare ligand-binding activity, total CheA abundance, and ligand-dependent kinase regulation in the same strain. If the first two are normal but the third is defective, the data support a defect in transduction across the receptor–kinase coupling step. They would not by themselves establish the exact molecular interface disrupted by the mutation.

The adaptation enzymes produce a different class of phenotype. A loss of CheR activity prevents normal receptor methylation. In a simple model, receptors tend toward a low-methylation state, which favors lower kinase output. Such cells often exhibit an altered basal motor bias and impaired recovery following attractant stimulation. The important distinction is that an adaptation-defective strain can retain some immediate receptor-to-motor response while losing the capacity to restore the response during persistent stimulation. The actual initial response can be weak if the basal operating point is already close to a signaling limit. It is therefore unsafe to assume that every cheR mutant will show a normal-sized acute response.

Loss of CheB activity removes a major route of receptor demethylation and can also interfere with processing of receptor modification sites. Increased methylation of available sites may favor high kinase output and CW-biased behavior. However, because receptor maturation and different methylation-site states influence the phenotype, the simple prediction should be qualified rather than treated as an exact numerical or universal outcome. When comparing CheR and CheB perturbations, focus on the opposite directions of their primary catalytic actions and the corresponding tendency for receptor signaling states to change.

Suppose a mutant receptor is engineered so that it recognizes aspartate normally but cannot undergo the receptor modifications necessary for adaptation. If its initial signaling state is suitable, it may still suppress CheA when aspartate concentration rises. The motor initially responds by favoring running. Because the receptor cannot adjust its modification state, continued aspartate exposure fails to produce the normal recovery of kinase activity through that receptor's methylation-dependent mechanism. If other receptor classes remain present, network-level compensation may complicate the result. A clean exam problem must specify whether the engineered receptor is the sole relevant receptor or whether other signaling routes are intact.

An informative experiment restores a missing gene through complementation. Imagine a cheZ deletion strain that has high CW bias and a delayed return of CheY-P after a stimulus. Reintroducing functional CheZ at an appropriate expression level should improve removal kinetics if the defect is due to the missing phosphatase. An inactive CheZ variant provides a useful comparison if it is expressed and localized comparably. Failure of an inactive protein to restore the phenotype supports the requirement for catalytic function rather than merely the physical presence of CheZ. Protein abundance must be monitored because lack of rescue can also result from insufficient expression or instability of the introduced protein.

Epistasis is another useful strategy when mutations affect the same output pathway. If a cheZ deletion raises CheY-P but a cheY deletion removes the substrate entirely, the double mutant cannot accumulate the missing CheY protein in phosphorylated form. Under the assumptions of the standard motor-control model, removal of CheY suppresses the high CW bias caused by deficient CheY-P removal. This does not restore normal chemotaxis: the double mutant still lacks the main motor-directed response regulator. Suppression of one phenotype is not necessarily functional rescue of the full pathway. The distinction is especially important in Part C questions that ask which phenotype is epistatic and whether the organism regains directional navigation.

A receptor mutation that affects only one ligand can be distinguished from a defect in a shared pathway component by applying more than one stimulus. An E. coli strain that has lost the response to aspartate but still responds appropriately to serine may have a defect in the aspartate recognition route. A strain that fails to respond to both ligands while retaining the ability to swim may have a defect shared between the routes, such as kinase coupling, signal production or motor-switch recognition. Yet the two-stimulus comparison is not conclusive: unequal expression levels, altered backgrounds or independent defects can also affect results. Multiple independent measurements and genetic rescue are required to narrow the explanation.

A final genetic distinction concerns a protein that is present but unable to receive its phosphoryl group. A CheY substitution at the normal phosphoacceptor site may preserve some structural properties of CheY while preventing its standard activation. If its motor interaction depends on phosphorylation, it will not support normal chemotactic switching. A mutation that mimics an activated conformational state might instead produce elevated motor bias even without normal kinase stimulation, but one must not assume that replacing an aspartate with a negatively charged residue necessarily generates an authentic phosphorylated response regulator. A chemically modified residue can alter geometry and interactions in ways that a simple amino acid substitution does not reproduce. The phenotype of any specific mutant must be justified from the stated properties or from experimental evidence.

13. Experimental evidence: observing movement and measuring migration

Chemotaxis is an unusually clear example of the need to separate a physiological observation from its molecular explanation. An early investigator could observe cells moving toward a chemical source without knowing that receptors, a histidine kinase and response regulators mediated the response. Conversely, a modern biochemical experiment can reveal a change in protein phosphorylation without demonstrating that the bacterium successfully navigates a gradient. A coherent account requires evidence at both levels, and each kind of experiment answers a different question. At the NET and SET level, the important skill is to choose an observation that can discriminate among the proposed mechanisms.

Classical experiments by Julius Adler in 1966 examined the migration of motile E. coli within capillary tubes containing suitable chemical and energetic conditions. Visible bands of cells and their changing distributions provided evidence that motile populations could reorganize spatially in response to environmental conditions. Such an observation is not equivalent to a direct measurement of receptor binding. In a system containing growing or metabolizing cells, oxygen and nutrient concentrations can themselves change as the cells consume resources. A moving band may therefore involve both the behavior of cells and the changing concentration fields created by those cells. A student should identify this interaction rather than assume that the chemical gradient is necessarily fixed throughout the experiment.

A conceptually simple capillary accumulation assay can distinguish between a chemical-filled capillary and a control capillary containing buffer. Both are introduced into comparable suspensions of motile cells. An attractant diffuses from the test capillary into the surrounding medium, creating a concentration profile near its opening. If motile cells exhibit chemotaxis toward the chemical, more may enter the test capillary than the control. The outcome is an integrated population measurement: it combines the number of cells that encounter the gradient, the probability of entering the opening, the possibility of leaving and the conditions inside the capillary. It is informative, but it does not directly measure the duration of individual runs or the phosphorylation state of CheY.

The buffer control provides an estimate of entry that occurs without the particular attractant gradient. A motile but nonchemotactic strain provides a second useful control because its flagellar machinery can produce movement while directional sensing is defective. A nonmotile strain distinguishes behavior requiring active swimming from processes that may bring cells into the capillary passively, such as fluid movement. Equal viable cell concentrations at the start are necessary because the number of cells available to enter each capillary influences the endpoint count. Comparable incubation intervals and environmental conditions help isolate the tested difference rather than introducing variation in growth, energy supply or fluid dynamics.

Suppose that wild-type cells yield substantially more viable cells in an attractant capillary than in a buffer capillary, while a cheY mutant yields similar counts in the two. If microscopy independently confirms that the mutant is viable and able to run, these observations support a requirement for CheY-dependent control in directional accumulation. They do not prove that the ligand physically binds CheY. The response regulator lies downstream of the receptor, so the same experiment can fail when a receptor, coupling protein, kinase or motor switch is defective. A missing behavioral response establishes a functional requirement, but additional experiments are needed to locate the molecular step.

An accumulation ratio can be defined as the number recovered from an attractant capillary divided by the number recovered from a matched control. If the two counts are 18,000 and 3,000, respectively, the ratio is six. This number is dimensionless; it expresses sixfold accumulation under the chosen conditions. If another strain yields 4,500 and 3,000 cells, its ratio is 1.5. A ratio near one indicates little differential accumulation under the tested conditions, not necessarily complete absence of all chemotactic responses to all ligands. A zero control count makes the simple ratio undefined. With small counts, sampling fluctuations can substantially affect the ratio, so replicate measurements and absolute counts should also be considered.

Soft agar migration is another useful population-level assay. In a suitable semisolid medium, cells can grow, swim through aqueous spaces and respond to chemical gradients that arise through diffusion and metabolism. A motile chemotactic strain may produce an expanding pattern that differs from that of a nonmotile or nonchemotactic strain. However, the radius of an expanding colony is not a direct measurement of instantaneous chemotactic velocity. Growth rate, survival, agar properties, nutrient availability and interactions between moving cells and their surroundings all influence the pattern. If an experimentally altered strain grows more slowly, a smaller migration radius cannot be attributed to chemotaxis without additional evidence.

This limitation suggests a useful comparison between a spatial migration assay and single-cell observation in liquid. Imagine two strains with different soft agar colony diameters. When individual trajectories are recorded under matched liquid conditions, the strains have similar swimming speeds and similar directional drift in a stable gradient. The result makes a primary defect in liquid chemotaxis less likely and suggests investigating growth or movement through the semisolid environment. If the mutant instead shows normal growth and speed but fails to prolong favorable runs, the combined results support a behavioral defect. The two measurements are valuable because each removes a different ambiguity.

Single-cell tracking can quantify displacement, instantaneous or mean run speed, duration of runs, reorientation frequency and the relationship between heading and chemical gradient. It can also reveal cell-to-cell variation hidden within population averages. A population may exhibit a modest positive mean drift because all cells show a small directional bias or because a subset of cells exhibits much stronger responses. These possibilities are difficult to distinguish from a single endpoint count. By observing individual trajectories, an experimenter can assess the distribution of behavioral properties rather than reducing the entire population to a single migration value.

For a reliable interpretation of single-cell data, the measured movement should be distinguished from fluid flow. If cells are carried toward the chemical source by convection, a positive population displacement might occur even without active directional navigation. An inert tracer or a suitable nonmotile control can reveal background flow. A uniform chemical condition with otherwise comparable physical properties can establish whether the apparatus itself induces a directional bias. The absence of directed displacement under these controls strengthens the conclusion that the observed drift is related to the spatial chemical field. This reasoning is more important for an examination than memorizing any particular apparatus design.

Consider also the difference between a static field and a field that evolves through diffusion. If a chemical source is introduced only at the beginning of the observation period, the concentration profile can change as molecules spread. A bacterium's experienced concentration change may then include both the effect of its own displacement and a time-dependent increase or decrease at its current position. The derivative introduced earlier shows precisely why this matters. A spatially stable gradient simplifies the interpretation of run statistics because the temporal component due to the changing field is minimized. If the field evolves, the interpretation must account for both terms in the experienced concentration derivative.

No single measurement fully establishes the pathway. Migration assays establish consequences at the population level, trajectory measurements reveal behavioral statistics, motor measurements identify changes in rotational switching, and biochemical measurements reveal the internal signaling state. These are not competing descriptions; they examine different stages of the same causal chain. Combining them allows the investigator to connect a ligand's effects to a cellular mechanism while excluding alternative explanations based on growth, passive movement or motor damage.

14. Time-resolved signaling and molecular evidence

A central experimental challenge is to measure the rapid signaling events that occur between receptor stimulation and a motor response. The CheY phosphorylated state is chemically dynamic, so a measurement made too late may fail to capture the initial change. One approach uses a fluorescence-based reporter that reflects the interaction of CheY-P with CheZ. Sourjik and Berg's study of receptor sensitivity used fluorescently labeled CheY and CheZ and monitored changes in fluorescence resonance energy transfer during stimulation. The underlying principle is that energy transfer between suitable fluorescent molecules depends strongly on their proximity and orientation. When a signaling-dependent molecular interaction changes the relationship between donor and acceptor fluorophores, the measured fluorescence signals can change.

Such a reporter is valuable because the abundance of a fluorescently labeled protein alone does not necessarily change when that protein becomes phosphorylated. The reporter instead depends on an interaction related to the phosphorylated state. For an attractant step in the standard E. coli pathway, reduced CheA-dependent production of CheY-P should be followed by a reduction in the relevant CheY-P-dependent interaction signal, with recovery as adaptation proceeds. The exact direction of a raw fluorescence ratio depends on how the instrument and reporter are defined. A question may specify that a plotted signal increases with the amount of CheY-P; under that assumption the biological predictions are unambiguous. Without such a definition, one should not infer a phosphorylation change solely from the sign of an arbitrary fluorescence ratio.

Reporter design introduces its own controls. A protein fusion may alter the stability, localization or interaction properties of its target. Demonstrating that a reporter strain retains suitable chemotactic function helps establish that the measurement does not grossly disrupt the pathway it is intended to observe. Fluorescent signals can also depend on fluorophore abundance, bleaching and environmental effects. Comparisons using matched expression and control strains can help separate a biochemical interaction signal from unrelated changes in fluorescence intensity. An exam question that provides a validated reporter can be analyzed directly, but the broader lesson is that every reporter measures a physical proxy whose relation to the biological variable must be established.

Time-resolved measurements can separate sensing, adaptation and output. Imagine a uniform attractant step delivered to wild-type cells while kinase activity and motor CW bias are monitored. A rapid fall in kinase-related signal followed by a fall in motor bias supports the expected causal order. Slow recovery of both while the concentration remains constant supports adaptation. Repeating the experiment with a receptor methylation-defective strain could reveal an acute response but incomplete recovery. Repeating it with a motor-switch mutant could reveal a normal kinase response without the expected motor change. The three traces would localize distinct kinds of failure even if all mutant strains were unable to migrate efficiently in a gradient.

An important feature of such an experiment is knowing whether the chemical stimulus was maintained. If the attractant concentration declines during the recording because the delivery system stops or the cells consume it, recovery of kinase activity may reflect removal of the stimulus rather than sensory adaptation. To demonstrate adaptation, the experiment must establish that the signal returns toward the baseline despite continued exposure to the relevant stimulus. Conversely, recovery following deliberate ligand removal reflects a different phase of the experiment. The external concentration record is therefore as important as the intracellular signal trace when assigning a recovery process to feedback.

Motor switching can be observed independently of translational movement of the whole cell. In a suitable tethered-cell experiment, rotation of a cell attached through a flagellar structure can be used to infer motor activity. Other measurements monitor rotation using a marker associated with a flagellar component. These approaches provide information about rotation rather than relying on whole-cell displacement. The direction convention needs attention: rotation of the cell body in a tethered setup and rotation of a filament viewed from the opposite direction need not be described by the same apparent clockwise sense. For an examination, the experimentally defined CW and CCW states should be used rather than reversing a reported sign based on a drawing.

The single-motor input-output relation and the kinase response should be analyzed separately when studying amplification. Suppose a small receptor occupancy change produces a sizeable change in CheA output, and a similar fractional change in CheY-P produces an even larger change in motor bias. Amplification may then occur at more than one stage. Assigning all amplification to the receptor, or all of it to the motor, would ignore the measured relationships. The distinction can be tested experimentally by bypassing part of the upstream pathway and relating the motor response to a controlled change in the motor-directed signal. The Cluzel, Surette and Leibler study provides a classic example of examining motor sensitivity at the level of individual cells rather than only measuring migration.

Protein phosphorylation measurements can also test a proposed pathway order. Suppose a cell lacking CheA is unable to show the usual receptor-dependent change in CheY-P. Restoration of a catalytically functional CheA restores this change, whereas a variant unable to autophosphorylate does not. If receptor binding and motor structure are independently preserved, the experiment supports the requirement for CheA catalytic signaling between receptor and CheY. It does not establish that no alternative source of response-regulator phosphorylation exists in any physiological condition. The conclusion is limited to the receptor-driven response measured under the specified experimental conditions.

A receptor methylation assay can answer questions that a CheY reporter cannot. After a persistent attractant increase, the canonical adaptation mechanism predicts an increase in receptor methylation together with recovery of kinase activity. If the reporter shows recovery but the receptor modification assay reveals no change, several explanations are possible. The assay may lack sensitivity, another regulatory pathway may compensate, or the external stimulus may not have remained constant. The appropriate scientific reaction is to identify discriminating observations rather than to reject or accept the methylation model immediately. Checking ligand delivery, the modification sites and the behavior of known methylation-deficient controls would make the alternatives more testable.

The reverse experiment can be even more informative. If methylation changes are observed without restoration of motor behavior, adaptation at the receptor level might not be transmitted properly to the motor. A kinase reporter would help distinguish between failure of methylation to regulate kinase activity and failure of the downstream motor to respond to CheY-P. Again, the same overall observation of poor chemotaxis can originate from different points along the causal chain. A sequence of orthogonal measurements transforms a descriptive phenotype into a mechanistic inference.

These experimental distinctions have broad relevance beyond chemotaxis. A receptor can bind its ligand normally while its signaling coupling is defective; a biochemical output can change normally while the final physiological effector is impaired; and a regulatory feedback can restore an output while the external stimulus remains unchanged. The E. coli model is useful because the relationship between chemical recognition and behavior can be investigated at successive stages. Students should practice assigning each measurement to a stage and determining what additional observation is required before drawing a stronger conclusion.

15. Quantitative reasoning about directional motion

The effect of biased run durations can be calculated in a deliberately restricted one-dimensional model. Suppose every run points either toward increasing attractant concentration or toward decreasing attractant concentration. Assume both directions are chosen with equal probability at the start of a run, speed has a constant magnitude v, favorable runs have mean duration Tplus, unfavorable runs have mean duration Tminus, and reorientation takes a mean time Tt. The expected displacement per run–reorientation cycle is one half of vTplus minus one half of vTminus. The expected time per cycle is one half of Tplus plus one half of Tminus plus Tt. Dividing expected displacement by expected time gives a long-time mean drift V = v(Tplus − Tminus)/(Tplus + Tminus + 2Tt) under these assumptions.

This calculation is not the same as taking the mean of the positive and negative run velocities. Those velocities have equal magnitude and opposite sign, so averaging them without time weighting would give zero. The longer favorable runs occupy a larger fraction of the total observation time. The resulting drift is a time-weighted measure of displacement. It also differs from the mean speed, which is nonnegative. A bacterium can have a large mean swimming speed and a small mean drift because most of its motion cancels when projected onto the gradient direction.

For an illustrative example, suppose v = 20 micrometers per second, Tplus = 1.5 seconds, Tminus = 0.9 seconds, and Tt = 0.1 second. The predicted drift is 20 × 0.6/(1.5 + 0.9 + 0.2) = 12/2.6 = approximately 4.615 micrometers per second toward higher attractant concentration. In the absence of directional differences, Tplus and Tminus would be equal, making the numerator zero regardless of the individual run speed. Increasing the time spent reorienting reduces the drift for a fixed difference in run durations because more of the observation interval is spent in a state that the model assigns no net displacement.

Now consider a mutation that doubles both favorable and unfavorable run times, keeping speed and tumble duration unchanged. The difference in durations also doubles, and the denominator increases. With the previous values the predicted drift becomes 20 × 1.2/(3.0 + 1.8 + 0.2) = 4.8 micrometers per second. The drift does not double because the total duration of a run cycle also increases. If reorientation time were negligible, multiplying both run durations by the same factor would leave the drift unchanged in this model. Such examples encourage students to examine ratios rather than assume that doubling one measured biological quantity doubles every derived output.

The one-dimensional model deliberately neglects many real features. E. coli swims in three dimensions, not only along and against the gradient. Run direction may vary continuously, reorientation can be incomplete, and the statistics of successive runs can be correlated. In three dimensions the instantaneous component of velocity along the gradient is v cos θ, where θ is the angle between the heading and the gradient direction. A run perpendicular to the gradient can contribute substantially to total distance but has zero instantaneous projection along that direction. Thus the one-dimensional expression should be used only when the question specifies its simplifying assumptions, not as a universal formula for experimental bacterial drift.

A related mathematical quantity is the rate at which the chemical stimulus changes during a run. Assume a stationary one-dimensional gradient of 0.4 micromolar per micrometer and a cell swimming at 15 micrometers per second toward increasing concentration. The experienced rate of change is 0.4 × 15 = 6 micromolar per second. Reversing the heading while keeping the speed magnitude unchanged changes the sign to negative 6 micromolar per second. If the entire environment is simultaneously increasing at 2 micromolar per second, the forward and reverse experiences become positive 8 and negative 4 micromolar per second, respectively. The calculation demonstrates that the same spatial gradient can generate different temporal inputs depending on both swimming direction and changes in the chemical field itself.

In some questions it is useful to estimate the fractional change in concentration over a run rather than the absolute change. If a bacterium begins in a 50 micromolar background and experiences an increase of 5 micromolar, the fractional increase is 0.10. An identical 5 micromolar increase from a 5 micromolar background corresponds to a fractional increase of one. Receptor occupancy and adaptation affect how such inputs are converted into kinase activity; equal absolute changes need not produce equal outputs. It is not necessary to assume that the entire chemotaxis pathway follows an exact logarithmic sensing rule to appreciate why the baseline concentration matters.

A simple estimate of sampling noise illustrates another limit. Suppose, purely as a model, a cell observes N independent ligand-binding events during a sampling interval, with each event having a constant mean probability. Under a Poisson counting approximation, the standard deviation of the event number is the square root of its mean. If the expected count is 100, the standard deviation is 10 and the relative fluctuation is approximately 10%. At 400 events, the standard deviation is 20 but the relative fluctuation is only 5%. The relative uncertainty decreases as more independent events are sampled. Real receptor observations can be correlated because ligands may rebind and receptors interact, so this estimate is not a precise biological detection limit. It demonstrates why a larger effective sample can improve reliability.

A cell that samples for longer may gather more information, but delay carries a biological cost. During a long interval the bacterium may change direction or enter a different chemical environment. A signal averaged over too long a period can represent conditions that no longer characterize the current run. The sensing mechanism must therefore balance the benefit of averaging with the requirement to respond rapidly enough to influence behavior. CheY-P turnover and receptor adaptation contribute on different timescales to this balance. This reasoning connects statistical uncertainty with biochemical kinetics without requiring an advanced mathematical model of ligand diffusion.

The motor response can be quantified with the Hill-type relation introduced earlier. Suppose K is 3 micromolar and the effective coefficient n is four, used only for an educational example. At y = 3 micromolar, B = 0.5. At y = 1.5 micromolar, the ratio y/K is one half and its fourth power is one sixteenth, giving B = 1/17, approximately 0.0588. At y = 6 micromolar, the fourth power of y/K is sixteen and B = 16/17, approximately 0.9412. The twofold changes on either side of K produce large differences in motor bias. In contrast, a response with n = 1 would give one third and two thirds at the same concentrations. The example illustrates increased steepness rather than an experimentally fitted value for a particular motor.

When interpreting a graph, the vertical axis may represent percentage of time in CW, the proportion of motors observed in CW, or the frequency of switching events. These are not interchangeable measurements. A motor that stays in CW for long intervals can have a high CW bias but relatively few switching events per second. A motor that switches often may have equal time in both rotational directions if the dwell times are similar. If a question states the durations of both states, CW bias is obtained by dividing the total CW time by the total observed time, not by counting the number of switches. This elementary distinction often resolves apparent contradictions in data about motor behavior.

16. Comparative signaling, ecological context and biological limits

A central advantage of studying bacterial chemotaxis in E. coli is the availability of a well-defined pathway. Nevertheless, it is important not to confuse the best-known example with the only biological design. Flagellar arrangements vary among bacteria. Some possess multiple flagella distributed around their surface, while others have one or more flagella at particular locations. A change in motor rotation can therefore produce different changes in whole-cell trajectory. Depending on the organism and the mechanical arrangement of its filament, a reversal may result in backward swimming, a reorientation event, or another pattern rather than the familiar E. coli tumble. The common principle is coupling sensory information to movement, not universal use of one swimming geometry.

Bacillus subtilis provides a useful comparative example at the examination level. Its chemotaxis machinery contains homologous components that serve recognition, kinase signaling and response-regulator functions, but the relationship between attractant stimulation, kinase activity and the preferred motor behavior differs from the canonical E. coli response. In the common B. subtilis model, attractant stimulation increases CheA-dependent signaling and CheY-P promotes the smooth-swimming state. The important lesson is that a response pathway can preserve the same adaptive behavioral objective while using an opposite sign at a particular biochemical step. A student should never use the E. coli rule that attractant addition lowers CheY-P as a definition of bacterial chemotaxis in every organism.

The comparison also reveals why adaptation mechanisms should not be universalized. Other bacterial systems can contain regulatory proteins, including additional response regulators or receptor-modifying components, that are absent from the standard E. coli circuit. The presence of homologous CheA and CheY proteins does not guarantee that every reaction involving them has the same consequences for behavior. At this level it is sufficient to identify the conserved organizational theme: sensory input is transmitted through a regulatory kinase and response system, movement changes, and regulatory processes help maintain useful sensitivity. Detailed species-specific biochemical networks should be studied only when needed to explain a question explicitly about those organisms.

Chemotaxis differs from the familiar transcription-directed two-component systems even though both can employ histidine kinases and aspartate-containing response regulators. A response regulator in a transcriptional pathway often controls gene expression by interacting with DNA or associated proteins. In the E. coli motor pathway, CheY modifies the behavior of a pre-existing motor through protein interactions. It therefore produces rapid output without requiring transcription and translation after every stimulus. The difference lies in the downstream effector and temporal function, not in the absence of phosphorylation chemistry. CheB illustrates how another response regulator in the same pathway can instead control receptor adaptation through catalytic activity.

The distinction between chemotaxis and quorum sensing is equally instructive. Chemotaxis concerns movement in relation to chemical conditions, often using information about changes experienced by an individual cell. Quorum sensing concerns regulation of cellular activities in relation to extracellular signaling molecules whose concentrations can reflect population density and environmental transport. In some organisms a molecule involved in population communication can also act as a chemotactic stimulus. That overlap does not make the two processes identical. The function of a chemical must be inferred from the response under investigation: changes in migration and motor bias establish a different output from coordinated changes in gene expression. The molecular details of population-dependent communication belong to the next chapter.

In natural habitats, directed migration may help bacteria locate dissolved nutrients, favorable oxygen conditions or surfaces near which resources are concentrated. A motile soil bacterium may encounter spatial variation in compounds released near roots. In aquatic environments, a chemical patch associated with biological activity may provide a transient opportunity for a bacterium that can reach it before the molecules diffuse away. These examples explain why sensitivity and rapid response can have ecological consequences. They do not establish that any specific receptor occurs in every soil or aquatic species, nor that chemotaxis is the only process responsible for microbial colonization.

Potential biotechnology applications follow from this ecological principle. For example, bacteria able to detect particular chemical conditions may be useful as experimental biosensors when their movement can be measured. In environmental contexts, movement toward a substrate can increase encounters with it, potentially complementing metabolic processes that transform the substrate. However, detection, transport toward a compound and degradation are distinct capabilities. A bacterium might migrate toward a molecule without metabolizing it, metabolize a compound without detecting its gradient, or be limited by environmental factors even when both functions are present. An application cannot be inferred from chemotaxis alone; each required biological capability must be demonstrated.

The energetic costs of the pathway also matter. Movement requires maintenance of an electrochemical gradient and continuous motor activity. Signaling requires metabolic resources for reversible phosphorylation and receptor methylation. A bacterium that improves access to a useful resource may offset these energetic costs, but the benefit depends on environmental conditions and on whether movement leads to a better habitat. When a favorable chemical is uniformly distributed, directional navigation offers no net advantage toward a particular location, although exploratory movement may still be useful if conditions subsequently change. Not every organism or environmental state therefore requires continuous flagellar chemotaxis.

The same signaling system can be influenced by metabolic state. Changes in available energy can affect swimming speed and thereby alter how quickly the bacterium samples spatial concentration changes. Energy-sensitive receptors can also alter the sensory input itself. If an experiment exposes cells to a chemical that changes both metabolism and receptor activity, the observed movement may reflect more than one causal process. Measurements of motility in a uniform condition, receptor-dependent kinase response, and directional bias in a gradient can help distinguish these effects. Such integration of metabolism, membrane energetics and cell signaling is more useful for examination reasoning than memorizing lists of attractive chemicals.

A final conceptual limit concerns the scale of biological organization. Chemotaxis of a single bacterium does not require collective agreement among many cells. A population can display directed migration because its individual members independently bias their movement. Cell density can nevertheless affect the chemical fields, because cells release or consume molecules and thereby influence one another's local environments. Observing a population-level pattern does not automatically identify its mechanism as quorum sensing. Conversely, population-dependent gene regulation does not automatically imply that cells have moved toward a source. Distinguishing the elementary processes prevents an overly broad interpretation of chemical communication.

17. Integrating stimulus, molecular state and behavioral outcome

A useful way to solve an unfamiliar chemotaxis problem is to identify what variable the experiment changes, what variable it measures, and which intermediate reactions connect them. Consider a cell that responds poorly to an attractant but retains a normal swimming speed. The observation establishes that the motor can produce some propulsive movement. It does not establish that the motor switch responds normally to CheY-P or that the upstream sensory pathway recognizes the attractant. The next useful question is whether the receptor-associated kinase changes its activity following stimulation. If kinase signaling is normal, the investigation moves downstream. If kinase signaling is abnormal, the investigation moves toward receptor binding, coupling and the kinase itself.

A second question concerns the time interval. An immediate response after a stimulus change primarily tests rapid transduction and the operating point of the motor. The later response to a maintained stimulus tests adaptation. These intervals are not completely independent because adaptation proteins influence the basal receptor state and thereby influence the size of the acute response. Nonetheless, separating the early and late phases is a valuable analytical starting point. A cell that shows a rapid drop in kinase activity but never recovers is different from a cell that shows no initial drop, even if both eventually fail to migrate effectively in a gradient.

A third distinction is between concentration, occupancy, signaling activity and behavioral response. A higher attractant concentration increases occupancy in an elementary binding model, but for the canonical E. coli attractant this can decrease kinase activity. Lower kinase activity reduces the production of CheY-P, and lower CheY-P favors a lower CW bias. Reduced CW bias favors longer runs, which can increase displacement in a favorable direction when the concentration change was generated by the cell's own movement. Each statement refers to a different biological quantity. A graph labeled only with the word response may therefore be ambiguous unless the measured variable is specified.

A fourth distinction is between information and energy. The chemical identity and concentration of a ligand determine sensory stimulation. The phosphorylation state of CheY communicates information to the motor switch. The ion gradient drives torque. The methylation state contributes to regulation of sensory responsiveness. A molecule that changes any one of these variables may indirectly alter the final trajectory, but this does not make it a direct fuel for rotation or a direct ligand for the motor switch. Exam questions often exploit the confusion by proposing that CheA supplies mechanical energy, CheZ removes methyl groups, or CheR directly binds the motor. The correct reasoning is to assign each reaction to its actual substrate and functional role.

Imagine a hypothetical four-strain comparison. The wild type has an immediate fall in kinase activity after a maintained aspartate step, recovery over time and positive drift in a gradient. Strain A binds aspartate but shows no ligand-dependent change in kinase activity; its motor can still rotate. Strain B shows a kinase response and recovery but no change in motor bias. Strain C shows a normal initial kinase response, a corresponding motor response and incomplete recovery, followed by poor gradient navigation. The data suggest a receptor-to-kinase defect in A, an output defect downstream of kinase signaling in B, and an adaptation defect in C. These are mechanistic classes rather than definitive gene identifications, because more than one molecular lesion can generate each pattern.

The four-strain comparison illustrates why the best answer in a Part C question is often narrower than the most detailed imaginable explanation. If the data show normal ligand binding and defective kinase regulation, a coupling defect is plausible, but a kinase variant that cannot respond to receptor control is also plausible. Claiming a unique mutation in CheW would be stronger than the evidence. If the question provides an additional result that functional CheW restores signaling while an inactive control does not, the specific conclusion becomes better supported. The ability to match the strength of a conclusion to the strength of the evidence is central to biological reasoning.

When the stimulus is removed, the correct prediction depends on whether adaptation has occurred. Immediately removing a brief attractant pulse before much methylation change has occurred may return signaling near its original level with a relatively small overshoot. Removing an attractant after substantial adaptation can expose the kinase-promoting effect of accumulated receptor modification, producing a pronounced opposite transient. A stimulus protocol should therefore specify not only the concentrations but also their duration. Two cells at the same external concentration can have different internal states because their recent histories differed. This is the practical implication of a sensory memory mechanism for interpreting experiments.

Chemotaxis ultimately demonstrates a general design principle of signaling systems: a biological response does not have to encode all aspects of the environment explicitly to produce an adaptive behavior. The E. coli system converts changes in the external chemical environment into changes in the probability of movement transitions. Fast reversible phosphorylation allows the cell to respond within a run, while slower reversible receptor modification maintains sensitivity over sustained stimulation. Directional motion emerges from their combined effects on the statistics of behavior. The pathway's conceptual significance lies in this connection between molecular reactions, regulation and organism-level function, all within a single microbial cell.

Part B conceptual practice

Original examination-style questions, not official previous-year questions. All numerical examples and invented datasets are hypothetical.

1. What directly distinguishes chemotaxis from an increase in swimming speed caused by a chemical?

  1. An increase in total path length alone
  2. An increase in population growth rate
  3. A directional bias in movement relative to the chemical field
  4. The presence of a rotary motor
Answer and explanation

C. A directional bias in movement relative to the chemical field — Directional bias is the defining outcome. Speed can change without a directional bias (chemokinesis); growth and flagellar presence alone do not demonstrate navigation.

2. What is the normal effect of an acute increase in aspartate sensed through Tar in E. coli?

  1. Reduced CheA activity
  2. Increased CheA activity as a universal attractant response
  3. Direct methylation of CheY
  4. Immediate inhibition of proton flow through MotA/MotB
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced CheA activity — Aspartate inhibits receptor-associated CheA in this model. CheY is phosphorylated rather than methylated in the main output pathway; the motor is not directly deprived of its energy source.

3. Which protein principally couples MCPs to the histidine kinase?

  1. CheZ
  2. CheR
  3. CheY
  4. CheW
Answer and explanation

D. CheW — CheW mediates receptor–kinase organization; CheZ accelerates CheY-P hydrolysis, CheR methylates receptors and CheY directs motor switching.

4. Which chemical reaction is characteristic of CheA?

  1. SAM-dependent methylation of glutamate
  2. ATP-dependent phosphorylation of a histidine
  3. Hydrolysis of a receptor methyl ester
  4. Transfer of phosphate from CheY-P to DNA
Answer and explanation

B. ATP-dependent phosphorylation of a histidine — CheA is a histidine kinase. The methyl-transfer and ester-hydrolysis activities belong to CheR and CheB, respectively; CheY-P is not a DNA phosphorylation enzyme.

5. Which residue of CheY accepts the phosphoryl group in the conventional E. coli pathway?

  1. Tyrosine
  2. Serine
  3. Aspartate
  4. Lysine
Answer and explanation

C. Aspartate — CheY is a response regulator with a phosphoacceptor aspartate. The other residues are not the defining acceptor in this His-to-Asp system.

6. In E. coli, which signal favors CW rotation of the motor?

  1. Increased extracellular ATP alone
  2. Increased CheY-P
  3. Increased receptor occupancy regardless of receptor class
  4. Reduced CheY-P
Answer and explanation

B. Increased CheY-P — CheY-P acts on the motor switch to increase CW bias. Receptor occupancy acts upstream and its effect depends on stimulus identity; ATP outside the cell is not the switch signal.

7. What is the principal direct role of CheZ?

  1. Accelerate dephosphorylation of CheY-P
  2. Remove methyl groups from MCPs
  3. Bind aspartate in the periplasm
  4. Provide torque to the flagellar rotor
Answer and explanation

A. Accelerate dephosphorylation of CheY-P — CheZ accelerates hydrolysis of CheY-P. CheB demethylates receptors, Tar recognizes aspartate, and torque is generated by the motor using the ion gradient.

8. What is the methyl donor for CheR?

  1. ATP as a phosphoryl donor
  2. NADH
  3. CheY-P
  4. S-adenosylmethionine
Answer and explanation

D. S-adenosylmethionine — SAM donates the methyl group in the receptor modification reaction. ATP is used by CheA, NADH is a redox cofactor and CheY-P carries phosphate.

9. Which process tends to increase receptor methylation after a maintained attractant increase?

  1. Direct binding of ligand to receptor methylation sites
  2. The shift in net CheR/CheB modification balance toward methylation
  3. CheZ-catalyzed methyl transfer
  4. CheY-P-catalyzed ester hydrolysis
Answer and explanation

B. The shift in net CheR/CheB modification balance toward methylation — The attractant lowers kinase activity, favoring net methylation. Ligand recognition and covalent modification occur through distinct mechanisms; CheZ and CheY-P are not the methyltransferase.

10. Which statement best describes an adapted cell at a constant higher attractant concentration?

  1. Receptor occupancy must return exactly to its initial level
  2. All receptor methyl groups must be removed
  3. The cell must stop moving
  4. Kinase output can recover even though receptor modification differs from baseline
Answer and explanation

D. Kinase output can recover even though receptor modification differs from baseline — Adaptation restores selected outputs, not necessarily occupancy or methylation. Basal swimming continues; methylation may have increased.

11. Which pair correctly identifies two E. coli chemoreceptors and canonical ligands?

  1. Tar–serine; Tsr–aspartate
  2. CheZ–aspartate; CheR–serine
  3. Tar–aspartate; Tsr–serine
  4. Aer–all peptides; CheW–maltose
Answer and explanation

C. Tar–aspartate; Tsr–serine — Tar and Tsr recognize these representative amino acids. CheZ, CheR and CheW are signaling proteins, not these receptors; Aer monitors energetic/redox-related signals.

12. Which is the immediate source of mechanical energy for the ordinary E. coli flagellar motor?

  1. Proton motive force
  2. Hydrolysis of CheY-P at the motor switch
  3. Transfer of methyl groups by CheR
  4. Cleavage of ATP by the external attractant
Answer and explanation

A. Proton motive force — The motor uses proton movement down an electrochemical gradient. Phosphorylation and methylation regulate information flow, not direct torque generation.

13. What is the meaning of CW bias for a single motor?

  1. Number of flagella on the cell
  2. Distance traveled per second
  3. Number of direction changes per unit time
  4. Fraction of observation time in clockwise rotation
Answer and explanation

D. Fraction of observation time in clockwise rotation — Bias is a time fraction. Switching frequency, flagellar number and translational speed are separate variables.

14. Which observation supports temporal sensing rather than a requirement for a spatial difference across the cell body?

  1. A transient behavioral response to a uniform concentration step
  2. A large population accumulating after a day of growth
  3. A flagellum rotating without a gradient
  4. Ligand diffusion without bacterial movement
Answer and explanation

A. A transient behavioral response to a uniform concentration step — A uniform concentration step changes stimulation over time without a spatial gradient. The other observations do not distinguish temporal from spatial sensing.

15. Why does E. coli continue to reorient in a uniform environment?

  1. Every tumble is necessarily caused by a repellent
  2. Basal switching provides exploration even without a directional bias
  3. Its receptors cannot bind any ligand at equilibrium
  4. CheA is permanently inactive
Answer and explanation

B. Basal switching provides exploration even without a directional bias — Basal pathway activity produces exploratory behavior. Reorientation is not a unique indicator of repellent, and receptor binding and kinase activity can persist.

16. Which statement about an E. coli cheY-null strain is most accurate if its motors and energy supply remain functional?

  1. It necessarily lacks flagella
  2. It is unable to bind every attractant
  3. It can be motile but is usually strongly CCW biased and deficient in chemotaxis
  4. It accumulates CheY-P because CheZ is absent
Answer and explanation

C. It can be motile but is usually strongly CCW biased and deficient in chemotaxis — Deletion removes the motor-directed regulator. Flagella and receptors can remain functional; the missing protein cannot accumulate in phosphorylated form.

17. What is a major limitation of using soft agar colony radius alone to measure chemotaxis?

  1. Growth and movement through the medium can also affect the radius
  2. Soft agar never permits bacterial motility
  3. Only ligand binding determines radius
  4. The radius is always independent of nutrient availability
Answer and explanation

A. Growth and movement through the medium can also affect the radius — Soft agar spreading integrates growth, motility and environmental changes. It is not an isolated measure of directional drift.

18. Which property distinguishes the motor-directed CheY response from many transcriptional two-component outputs?

  1. It never involves protein phosphorylation
  2. It requires the ligand to enter the nucleus
  3. It regulates an existing effector without requiring new gene expression for each acute stimulus
  4. It is powered by receptor methyl groups
Answer and explanation

C. It regulates an existing effector without requiring new gene expression for each acute stimulus — Rapid signaling through existing motor proteins can change behavior without new transcription. The pathway still uses phosphorylation; bacteria lack a nucleus.

19. Why must the E. coli rotation rule be species-labeled?

  1. Every bacterial flagellum always rotates in exactly the same manner
  2. Chemotaxis cannot occur outside E. coli
  3. All bacterial response regulators are identical
  4. Other bacteria can couple CheY-P and flagellar behavior with different signs or geometry
Answer and explanation

D. Other bacteria can couple CheY-P and flagellar behavior with different signs or geometry — Different motor arrangements and signaling networks can produce different outcomes; B. subtilis is a standard sign contrast.

20. Which is an example of sensory adaptation rather than simply termination of CheY-P?

  1. Hydrolysis of one molecule of CheY-P by CheZ
  2. A maintained ligand step followed by recovery of kinase output through altered receptor methylation
  3. A reduction in motor torque caused by energy depletion
  4. Diffusion of attractant away from the assay
Answer and explanation

B. A maintained ligand step followed by recovery of kinase output through altered receptor methylation — Adaptation compensates for persistent stimulation by changing the receptor state. Hydrolysis is fast signal reset; loss of ligand is not adaptation.

Part C analytical practice

21. A strain binds aspartate normally, has normal CheA abundance, but CheA activity does not respond to aspartate. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. CheY must be deleted
  2. The flagellar motor must lack proton conduction
  3. CheR is conclusively the only mutated protein
  4. A receptor-to-kinase coupling or regulatory defect is plausible
Answer and explanation

D. A receptor-to-kinase coupling or regulatory defect is plausible — The defect is visible upstream of CheY and the motor despite preserved binding. A coupling defect or a kinase unable to respond is possible, but the data do not identify a unique gene.

22. A mutant displays normal kinase depression and recovery after attractant addition but no change in CW bias. What is the best next discriminating measurement?

  1. Measure only total DNA content
  2. Measure CheY-P and test motor response to that signal
  3. Measure growth on unrelated carbon sources as the sole test
  4. Repeat the ligand-binding assay without testing downstream steps
Answer and explanation

B. Measure CheY-P and test motor response to that signal — The upstream signal is intact. Measuring the mobile regulator and motor sensitivity distinguishes loss of downstream signal transmission from a motor defect.

23. A wild type and mutant both have positive drift. The mutant has twice the total run speed but one half the drift. Which statement follows?

  1. The mutant must have more CheA protein
  2. The mutant cannot be motile
  3. Speed alone cannot account for directional migration efficiency
  4. The mutant necessarily has more attractant receptors
Answer and explanation

C. Speed alone cannot account for directional migration efficiency — Drift depends on directional persistence and time weighting, not merely speed. The data do not establish any particular molecular change.

24. For a maintained attractant step, strain X changes normalized kinase output 0.6 → 0.2 → 0.6; strain Y changes 0.6 → 0.2 → 0.35. Which interpretation fits?

  1. Y has impaired recovery under the tested conditions despite an acute response
  2. Y cannot recognize ligand
  3. X shows no adaptation
  4. Y necessarily has a defective motor stator
Answer and explanation

A. Y has impaired recovery under the tested conditions despite an acute response — Both strains respond acutely; Y does not recover fully. This localizes the observed difference to recovery, not necessarily to a unique gene.

25. A cheZ deletion causes high CW bias. A cheY cheZ double deletion has low CW bias. What explains the suppression?

  1. CheZ now methylates receptors
  2. The substrate whose phosphorylated state accumulates in the cheZ mutant is absent
  3. The double mutant necessarily regained gradient navigation
  4. CheY deletion restored the CheZ enzyme
Answer and explanation

B. The substrate whose phosphorylated state accumulates in the cheZ mutant is absent — Removing CheY prevents CheY-P accumulation. Suppression of a bias phenotype does not restore the missing signal or normal chemotaxis.

26. A CheZ rescue construct restores normal CheY-P decay. An inactive CheZ variant expressed at the same abundance fails to rescue. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. CheZ catalytic function contributes to rapid signal reset
  2. CheZ is the attractant receptor
  3. CheZ is needed only for flagellar assembly
  4. CheR activity was directly measured by this rescue
Answer and explanation

A. CheZ catalytic function contributes to rapid signal reset — The controlled rescue supports the importance of CheZ catalytic function. It does not test receptor recognition or methyltransferase activity.

27. A receptor variant binds ligand normally, has intact signaling sites, and is the sole relevant receptor but cannot be methylated or demethylated. What is expected if its initial operating point permits a response?

  1. All acute signaling must disappear
  2. CheZ directly supplies methyl groups
  3. CheY-P necessarily remains at zero at all times
  4. An acute kinase response can persist, but methylation-dependent recovery is impaired
Answer and explanation

D. An acute kinase response can persist, but methylation-dependent recovery is impaired — Recognition and acute transduction can operate without normal receptor modification; the slow feedback mechanism is impaired.

28. A cell adapted to high attractant is abruptly transferred to its original low concentration. Which early change is expected in E. coli?

  1. Immediate complete demethylation before kinase changes
  2. A drop in CheY-P because all attractants are removed
  3. A rise in kinase output due to loss of ligand inhibition while methylation initially persists
  4. A permanent loss of flagella
Answer and explanation

C. A rise in kinase output due to loss of ligand inhibition while methylation initially persists — The adapted methylation state initially favors kinase activity. Removal exposes this influence and produces an opposite transient; demethylation is delayed.

29. Two strains both recover kinase output exactly but do so in 10 and 50 seconds respectively. What differs on these data?

  1. Adaptation kinetics, not measured final precision
  2. Ligand affinity must differ fivefold
  3. Both are completely nonadaptive
  4. The 50-second strain has zero CheA activity
Answer and explanation

A. Adaptation kinetics, not measured final precision — The endpoints match their own baselines, while the rates differ. The data do not directly quantify affinity or enzyme abundance.

30. Suppose ligand binds independently with Kd = 10 µM. What is the occupancy change from 10 to 30 µM?

  1. 0.75 (75 percentage points)
  2. 0.50 (50 percentage points)
  3. 0.20 (20 percentage points)
  4. 0.25 (25 percentage points)
Answer and explanation

D. 0.25 (25 percentage points) — Initial θ = 10/20 = 0.50; final θ = 30/40 = 0.75; difference = 0.25. Final occupancy is not the difference.

31. In the first-order model y* = YT a/(a+b), YT = 12 µM, a = 1 s⁻¹ and b = 3 s⁻¹. What is y*?

  1. 9 µM
  2. 3 µM
  3. 4 µM
  4. 12 µM
Answer and explanation

B. 3 µM — The phosphorylated fraction is 1/(1+3) = 0.25; 12 × 0.25 = 3 µM. Nine is the unphosphorylated pool.

32. If CheY-P production is stopped and its first-order decay constant is 2 s⁻¹, what fraction remains after 0.5 s?

  1. 0.50 exactly
  2. e⁻², about 0.135
  3. e⁻¹, about 0.368
  4. Zero
Answer and explanation

C. e⁻¹, about 0.368 — The exponent is −bt = −2 × 0.5 = −1. The half-life is ln 2/2, not 0.5 s.

33. A model motor has n = 2, K = 4 µM. What is its CW bias at y = 2 µM?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.80
  4. 0.20
Answer and explanation

D. 0.20 — B = 2²/(4² + 2²) = 4/20 = 0.20. At K it would be one half; 0.80 is the complementary bias.

34. For equal-probability runs at v = 10 µm/s, Tplus = 2 s, Tminus = 1 s, and Tt = 0.5 s, what is predicted drift?

  1. 5 µm/s
  2. 10 µm/s
  3. 2.5 µm/s
  4. 0 µm/s
Answer and explanation

C. 2.5 µm/s — V = 10(2−1)/(2+1+1) = 2.5 µm/s. Tumble time contributes twice in the denominator of the stated formula.

35. A gradient is +0.2 µM/µm; a cell moves at −20 µm/s; bulk concentration rises at +3 µM/s. What is its experienced rate of change?

  1. −1 µM/s
  2. +7 µM/s
  3. −4 µM/s
  4. +1 µM/s
Answer and explanation

A. −1 µM/s — dC/dt = 3 + (−20)(0.2) = −1 µM/s. Ignoring the bulk temporal change gives −4, while ignoring the heading sign gives +7.

36. For dm/dt = 0.08(1−A)−0.12A, what is the interior adapted activity?

  1. 0.60
  2. 0.40
  3. 0.20
  4. 0.08
Answer and explanation

B. 0.40 — Set 0.08 = (0.08+0.12)A; A = 0.08/0.20 = 0.40. This assumes the required modification state is feasible.

37. The same feedback model is at maximum methylation but the ligand keeps A below its predicted adapted level. What follows?

  1. The receptor must methylate without limit
  2. Ligand occupancy necessarily becomes zero
  3. The interior steady-state result may not be physically attainable
  4. The cell has proved exact adaptation
Answer and explanation

C. The interior steady-state result may not be physically attainable — Finite modification capacity invalidates the interior-state assumption. An algebraic target is not automatically reachable.

38. A capillary test gives wild type 24,000 versus 4,000 cells and mutant 6,000 versus 4,000 cells (attractant versus buffer). Which statement is supported?

  1. Accumulation ratios are 6 and 1.5; controls are still needed to assign mechanism
  2. Ratios are 20,000 and 2,000
  3. The mutant is definitely immotile
  4. The attractant is necessarily a substrate for growth
Answer and explanation

A. Accumulation ratios are 6 and 1.5; controls are still needed to assign mechanism — Divide test by matched control; differences alone are not ratios. Counts do not prove immotility or metabolism.

39. A mutant has a smaller soft agar radius, but equal directional drift and run speed in liquid. Which explanation deserves priority before diagnosing a chemotaxis defect?

  1. CheY-P is absent with certainty
  2. Growth or movement in the semisolid medium differs
  3. The mutant cannot sense any gradient
  4. The two liquid assays must be discarded
Answer and explanation

B. Growth or movement in the semisolid medium differs — The liquid evidence does not support a simple primary navigation defect. Soft agar integrates other properties, which should be investigated.

40. In a validated kinase reporter, recovery occurs after a chemical step, but the chemical concentration also gradually falls to its original level. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Perfect adaptation is proved
  2. Receptor methylation must have increased
  3. The reporter directly measures swimming speed
  4. Recovery cannot yet be attributed specifically to adaptation
Answer and explanation

D. Recovery cannot yet be attributed specifically to adaptation — Recovery could be due to stimulus removal. To establish adaptation, maintain and verify the altered stimulus.

Mixed-format and integrative practice

41. Assertion: CheR contributes to restoration of E. coli kinase output after sustained attractant addition. Reason: Increased receptor methylation tends to favor kinase-promoting activity.

  1. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion
  2. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Assertion is true but reason is false
  4. Assertion is false but reason is true
Answer and explanation

B. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion — Net receptor methylation opposes attractant-induced kinase inhibition; the reason gives the mechanistic connection.

42. Assertion: A cheY-null E. coli cell must be incapable of any swimming. Reason: CheY-P normally biases the flagellar switch toward CW.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion
  3. Assertion is true but reason is false
  4. Assertion is false but reason is true
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion is false but reason is true — CheY regulates switching rather than torque generation. A mutant can run persistently if motor and energy supply are intact.

43. Assertion: A uniform attractant step can transiently change E. coli motor behavior. Reason: The E. coli chemotaxis system responds to temporal changes in stimulation.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion
  3. Assertion is true but reason is false
  4. Assertion is false but reason is true
Answer and explanation

A. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion — A spatial gradient is not required for the immediate sensory response; temporal changes suffice, although directional drift requires a spatial relationship.

44. Which statements are correct? I. CheA phosphorylates CheY. II. CheZ accelerates CheY-P removal. III. CheR methylates MCPs. IV. CheB directly drives motor rotation.

  1. I and IV only
  2. II and III only
  3. I, II and III only
  4. I, II, III and IV
Answer and explanation

C. I, II and III only — I–III identify correct direct roles. IV is false because CheB regulates receptor modification and torque is supplied by the ion-powered motor.

45. Which statements are correct for the named E. coli model? I. Attractant addition can reduce CheA activity. II. Lower CheY-P favors CCW. III. All bacteria use the same motor response sign. IV. Sustained attractant can induce increased methylation.

  1. I and III only
  2. II, III and IV only
  3. III and IV only
  4. I, II and IV only
Answer and explanation

D. I, II and IV only — I, II and IV describe canonical E. coli signaling. III is an invalid universal generalization.

46. Which statements are correct about a simple first-order CheY-P model? I. At steady state production equals removal. II. Increasing b with a fixed lowers the phosphorylated fraction. III. The relaxation constant is a+b. IV. The concentration is always zero at steady state.

  1. I and IV only
  2. I, II and III only
  3. II and IV only
  4. III and IV only
Answer and explanation

B. I, II and III only — The balance and algebra give I–III. A nonzero steady phosphorylated pool is expected if both production and removal are active.

47. Match 1. CheW, 2. CheA, 3. CheZ, 4. CheR with P. Methyltransferase, Q. Coupling protein, R. Histidine kinase, S. CheY-P phosphatase.

  1. 1–R, 2–Q, 3–P, 4–S
  2. 1–P, 2–S, 3–Q, 4–R
  3. 1–Q, 2–R, 3–S, 4–P
  4. 1–S, 2–P, 3–R, 4–Q
Answer and explanation

C. 1–Q, 2–R, 3–S, 4–P — CheW couples, CheA phosphorylates, CheZ removes CheY-P and CheR transfers methyl groups.

48. Match 1. Tar, 2. Tsr, 3. Tap, 4. Aer with P. Serine, Q. Aspartate, R. Indirect dipeptide sensing, S. Energetic/redox sensing.

  1. 1–Q, 2–P, 3–R, 4–S
  2. 1–P, 2–Q, 3–S, 4–R
  3. 1–R, 2–S, 3–P, 4–Q
  4. 1–S, 2–R, 3–Q, 4–P
Answer and explanation

A. 1–Q, 2–P, 3–R, 4–S — These are representative recognition roles, not exclusive lists of stimuli. Tap senses through a binding protein; Aer differs from the conventional periplasmic recognition model.

49. A hypothetical motor spends 12 seconds CW, 18 seconds CCW, and switches six times in 30 seconds. What pair correctly reports bias and switch frequency?

  1. 0.60 and 0.40 s⁻¹
  2. 0.20 and 0.40 s⁻¹
  3. 0.40 and 6 s⁻¹
  4. 0.40 and 0.20 s⁻¹
Answer and explanation

D. 0.40 and 0.20 s⁻¹ — CW bias is 12/30 = 0.40. Switching frequency is 6/30 = 0.20 s⁻¹. The values measure different features.

50. A nonmetabolizable ligand suppresses CheA, prolongs runs in a uniform step and induces drift in a stable gradient. What conclusion is most appropriate?

  1. Sensory-driven navigation does not require that the ligand itself supply metabolic energy
  2. Motor torque comes from ligand catabolism
  3. Quorum sensing has been uniquely established
  4. Growth alone explains the drift
Answer and explanation

A. Sensory-driven navigation does not require that the ligand itself supply metabolic energy — The ligand functions as a sensory input without needing to be metabolized. An existing energy source can power the motor; drift is not a population-density response by itself.

51. A cell increases methylation after a maintained stimulus but motor bias fails to recover. Which measurement most directly distinguishes a failed kinase-level adaptation from a downstream defect?

  1. Count the total cells in the culture only
  2. Measure extracellular pH alone
  3. Measure whether kinase activity returns to its own prestimulus baseline
  4. Measure flagellar number without recording signaling
Answer and explanation

C. Measure whether kinase activity returns to its own prestimulus baseline — If kinase output recovers, the defect is downstream; if not, receptor modification may fail to regulate kinase appropriately. The alternative measures do not locate this transition.

52. A comparison shows attractant raises CheY-P and favors smooth swimming in species B, while lowering CheY-P and favoring smooth swimming in E. coli. What is the shared functional outcome?

  1. Both must use identical receptor-to-motor logic
  2. Both can extend favorable motion even though the molecular sign differs
  3. Species B cannot be chemotactic
  4. Only E. coli can adapt
Answer and explanation

B. Both can extend favorable motion even though the molecular sign differs — The direction of the biochemical signal is species-dependent. The shared behavioral result is altered persistence in favorable conditions.

53. A ligand recognition mutant is rescued by a second receptor that recognizes a different chemical. Why is its migration restored only toward the second chemical?

  1. The shared output is functional, but the input specificity still differs
  2. All chemical ligands bind CheY directly
  3. The motor recognizes the chemical itself
  4. CheR determines every ligand-binding specificity
Answer and explanation

A. The shared output is functional, but the input specificity still differs — A working downstream pathway can transmit signals from another receptor; it does not replace the missing recognition of the first ligand.

54. A hypothetical cell has no proton motive force but shows an attractant-induced decrease in a validated CheY-P reporter. What is the most justified interpretation?

  1. The motor necessarily rotates rapidly
  2. CheY-P directly supplies torque
  3. Upstream chemical signaling can occur despite failure of mechanical propulsion under these conditions
  4. The chemical receptor is necessarily absent
Answer and explanation

C. Upstream chemical signaling can occur despite failure of mechanical propulsion under these conditions — Reporter changes establish the measured signaling event, not functional motor energetics. Propulsion requires the appropriate ion gradient.

55. A ligand step yields a fast kinase drop in strain P and Q. P recovers while Q does not. Restoring functional CheR to Q restores recovery. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. CheR directly phosphorylates CheY
  2. CheA is absent from Q
  3. The rescue demonstrates that all species adapt through CheR
  4. CheR-dependent methylation is required for the observed recovery in Q
Answer and explanation

D. CheR-dependent methylation is required for the observed recovery in Q — The rescue connects the specific enzyme to recovery in this strain. It does not establish a universal mechanism or reassign CheR's biochemical role.

Examination traps and misconceptions

Motility equals chemotaxis
A cell can swim in random directions with no net drift; look for directional bias.
Attractant always activates its kinase
E. coli Tar-mediated attractant sensing inhibits CheA; other species can use a different sign.
CW rotation always means running
In the canonical E. coli model CW favors reorientation; use a consistent viewing convention.
CheA is the energy source of the motor
ATP-dependent phosphorylation carries information; proton motive force drives torque.
CheR phosphorylates the motor signal
CheR transfers methyl groups to receptors, whereas CheA transfers phosphoryl groups.
CheZ demethylates the receptor
CheZ accelerates CheY-P hydrolysis; CheB hydrolyzes receptor methyl esters.
Adaptation requires ligand disappearance
Recovery with maintained ligand is the defining comparison.
Adaptation restores every state variable
Output may recover while methylation and occupancy differ.
A cheY mutant must be immotile
It may swim with abnormally low reorientation and poor gradient navigation.
Half occupancy equals half motor bias
The intervening kinase and motor responses can have different input-output relations.
CW bias equals switching frequency
One is a fraction of time; the other is events per unit time.
A larger soft agar colony proves stronger chemotaxis
Growth and physical movement through agar also contribute.
One mutant phenotype identifies one gene
Different defects can produce similar behavior; use upstream and downstream measurements and complementation.
Chemotaxis and quorum sensing are synonyms
They have different functional outputs even if a chemical can participate in both.

Revision and active recall

Core sequence: chemical input → MCP/CheW → CheA → CheY-P → switch → altered motion. CheZ resets the fast signal. CheR/CheB feedback adjusts receptor activity during persistent stimulation.

Essential definitions: a run is persistent swimming; a tumble is reorientation; CW bias is the fraction of time in CW rotation; a biased random walk has nonzero mean displacement from unequal movement statistics; adaptation restores selected outputs despite persistent stimulation.

Recall without looking

  1. Explain why an attractant can lower kinase activity.
  2. Trace the two phosphate recipients of CheA and their different roles.
  3. Distinguish the power source of the motor from its switching signal.
  4. Predict the acute and adapted states after attractant addition and removal.
  5. Derive the independent binding curve and identify its assumptions.
  6. Derive the time-weighted drift expression.
  7. Distinguish defective ligand binding, coupling, motor output and adaptation using experiments.
  8. Explain why CheZ loss differs from CheY loss.
  9. Identify two confounders of capillary or soft agar accumulation.
  10. Explain why the E. coli motor sign is not universal.

Mastery checklist

  • I can predict signs without confusing kinase, ligand occupancy and motor bias.
  • I can assign the exact substrates and roles of CheA, CheY, CheZ, CheR and CheB.
  • I can read an acute versus adapted time course and check whether the stimulus persists.
  • I can solve numerical binding, kinetics, motor bias and run-duration problems with units.
  • I can design a control or rescue that distinguishes competing mechanisms.
  • I can distinguish chemotaxis from growth, chemokinesis and quorum sensing.
