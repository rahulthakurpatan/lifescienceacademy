1. From an initiated origin to a completed chromosome

A bacterium that has successfully opened its replication origin has solved only the first of several problems. The chromosome still contains millions of base pairs, arranged in two covalently continuous strands. Each parental strand must become the template for a new complementary strand. A newly exposed sequence has to be read, the correct nucleotide selected, and a bond formed before the next position is read. Meanwhile the double helix must be unwound at a matching rate, single strands must remain accessible, and two growing daughter duplexes must eventually separate. Failure at any one of these operations can prevent inheritance even when every other operation works normally. This chapter follows those operations in the model bacterium Escherichia coli, beginning with established forks and ending with two completed chromosomes.

The distinction between opening DNA and copying it is fundamental. Opening breaks noncovalent interactions between the paired bases; it does not normally break the covalent phosphodiester bonds along either template. Copying requires a chemical reaction that joins an incoming deoxyribonucleotide to the end of an existing chain. At any given time a growing fork contains parental duplex ahead, a short region of separated templates, and two daughter duplexes behind. Because the daughter DNA is made using existing parental strands, each completed daughter duplex contains one parental strand and one new strand. This molecular outcome explains semiconservative inheritance without requiring a particular mechanism of origin recognition.

A chromosome is not simply a very long version of a short linear DNA molecule. The ordinary E. coli chromosome is circular, and its DNA is constrained by proteins and its own topology. Two forks normally advance around the circle in opposite directions from oriC. They copy two portions of the same chromosome, not two separate chromosomes. The two portions are often called replichores. Each fork needs to copy both strands along its own replichore. Therefore, an origin that gives rise to two bidirectional forks produces two leading-strand regions and two lagging-strand regions, not a chromosome with one entire strand designated leading and the other entire strand designated lagging.

To visualize the distinction, imagine marking one parental strand with a color and tracing it around the circle. On one side of the origin, movement of the relevant fork exposes this strand in the orientation suitable for leading synthesis; on the opposite side, the other fork moves in the reverse direction and encounters the very same parental strand in the opposite orientation. There the strand serves as the lagging template. The designations belong to a relationship between template orientation and fork movement. They are not permanent names for the chemical strands themselves. This point resolves many examination questions in which the circle is rotated or the fork is drawn on the opposite side.

The mature chromosome must also meet a standard beyond base-pair accuracy. A daughter can contain the expected base sequence yet still retain RNA primers, unsealed backbone discontinuities, or topological links to the other daughter. Such a molecule is not equivalent to an intact independent chromosome. It is useful to distinguish sequence copying, chemical maturation, and physical separation. During much of elongation these processes overlap; for example, primers can be removed behind an advancing fork while DNA ahead of that fork remains unreplicated. At the terminus, the outstanding work must be finished. This distinction helps explain why a mutation that leaves nucleotide incorporation nearly normal can nevertheless be lethal.

For the following sections it is helpful to recall the chemistry of DNA polarity, the basic concept of a replicon and the general functions of a replication fork. These are the prerequisites developed in the earlier nodes of this subunit. We now assign those general functions to the actual bacterial proteins and examine how their coordination produces a complete genome. The mechanisms of eukaryotic chromosome-end replication and the detailed biochemical pathways of DNA repair are reserved for their own chapters. The model used here is E. coli unless another organism is explicitly identified; conserved principles can be transferred between bacteria, but enzyme names and termination arrangements must not be assumed universal.

2. Why two different modes of synthesis are necessary

The two DNA strands are antiparallel because the sugar-phosphate backbone has a direction. A free 3′ hydroxyl group on a deoxyribose can attack the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphate, whereas a free 5′ end is not the normal growing end of a DNA polymerase substrate. DNA polymerase reads its template in the 3′ to 5′ direction and extends the complementary chain in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The numbers refer to positions on the sugar rather than to the order of letters in a particular gene. A gene can be encoded on either DNA strand without altering this chemical rule.

Consider a fork traveling to the right. Draw the upper parental strand with its 3′ end on the left and its 5′ end on the right. The enzyme can follow this strand toward the right and add nucleotides at the rightward-facing 3′ end of the growing daughter. As soon as helicase exposes more template, polymerase can extend farther in the same overall direction. This is leading-strand synthesis. In an uninterrupted ideal fork it needs an initiation primer and can then proceed over a long distance without frequent re-priming. If the fork becomes damaged or interrupted, leading synthesis may also have to restart; the word continuous describes the normal arrangement, not an unbreakable promise about every chromosome.

Now draw the lower parental strand with its 5′ end at the left and its 3′ end at the right. A polymerase on this template must move toward the left. If it started at the origin and attempted to follow the fork toward the right, it would have to use an impossible direction of extension. Instead, a new RNA primer can be made near the advancing fork, where previously paired template has just become accessible. Polymerase extends that primer leftward toward the region copied previously. When additional template becomes exposed on the right, a second primer can be placed there. By making progressively newer fragments closer to the fork and extending each away from it, the cell copies the entire second template without violating its chemical directionality.

Each of these pieces is called an Okazaki fragment. The overall region occupied by fragments grows in the direction of fork travel even though the synthesis of an individual fragment proceeds away from the fork along its template. This distinction between progression of the replicated region and direction of polymerization is crucial. Draw an arrow for the movement of the fork and separate arrows for the growth of each new strand; a correct diagram shows leading growth pointing toward the fork and lagging growth pointing away along an unfolded representation. An arrow indicating the direction of an enzyme on a looped template may appear differently in three-dimensional space, but the nucleotide chemistry is unchanged.

There is no intrinsic distinction in the chemical quality of the DNA made by these two modes. Both are synthesized from deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, both depend on complementarity, and both acquire phosphodiester bonds through the same general reaction. The difference appears in the number of initiation and finishing events. Leading synthesis can continue for a large fraction of a chromosome before encountering a reason to stop. Lagging synthesis repeatedly needs primer creation, clamp assembly, extension, release, primer replacement and sealing. A full explanation of bacterial replication must therefore account not only for long-chain extension but also for an efficient assembly line for short-chain maturation.

The cell has no need to evolve a separate 3′ to 5′ DNA synthesis chemistry to overcome this asymmetry. An important chemical benefit of the existing direction is that each arriving dNTP supplies the activated phosphate for its own addition. When a proofreading enzyme removes a terminal incorrect nucleotide, the remaining chain generally retains the hydroxyl needed for subsequent extension using another activated substrate. A hypothetical reverse-direction mechanism using activation on the growing chain would have to deal with restoring that activation after proofreading. This is a useful mechanistic explanation for why a simple reversal of polymerase direction is not an equivalent alternative, rather than a direct demonstration of the evolutionary origin of today's polymerases.

Two questions should now be distinguishable. If a fork is moving right, what direction does synthesis take on the top and bottom templates? The answer follows from their marked ends. If instead a student asks which strand is leading, the answer cannot be determined from a 5′ label alone unless the direction of fork movement is known. In a replication bubble, analyze each fork independently. If the top strand is 3′ to 5′ left to right, it serves as the leading template at the right fork but the lagging template at the left fork. A correctly drawn pair of forks also shows that the new leading strands extend away from the origin, while fragments extend toward older replicated DNA.

3. How experiments revealed discontinuous DNA synthesis

A key historical problem was that identifying a DNA polymerase did not explain how an entire antiparallel molecule could be replicated. Arthur Kornberg's group isolated the enzyme now known as DNA polymerase I from E. coli in the 1950s. The enzyme could catalyze DNA synthesis in a suitable biochemical system. This was an enormous advance because copying could be investigated using purified components rather than only whole growing cells. However, a reaction in a test tube establishes catalytic capability, not necessarily the dominant physiological role. An enzyme able to extend DNA may be primarily responsible for filling gaps rather than for duplicating millions of base pairs.

The work of Reiji and Tsuneko Okazaki and colleagues addressed the strand asymmetry by examining the size of DNA made over very short intervals. Their 1968 publications investigated whether nascent DNA initially appeared in short pieces. The basic method of pulse labeling is to supply a detectable nucleotide precursor for a limited time so that material synthesized during that interval becomes preferentially marked. If the cell makes independent short intermediates before joining them, a very short pulse should reveal labeled DNA in a small-molecule fraction. After additional time, joining should transfer much of the same label into longer DNA molecules.

The experimental logic requires the investigator to distinguish chain length from the size of a complete double helix. A fragment that has already base-paired to a long parental strand may behave as part of a large complex under conditions that preserve duplexes. Alkaline sedimentation separates strands and helps assess the length of individual DNA chains. Newly synthesized labeled material can be distinguished from the far greater mass of previously existing, mostly unlabeled DNA. Short chains sediment differently from long ones, so the distribution of radioactive signal provides a measure of the relative lengths of the newly synthesized molecules rather than merely the total amount of DNA in the sample.

A pulse-chase interpretation is especially powerful. After a short period with radioactive precursor, an excess of unlabeled precursor is supplied. The original labeled nucleotides remain in DNA already made, while subsequent incorporation is largely unlabeled. If labeled short chains are joined into longer ones, the label should be recovered in larger DNA molecules as the chase proceeds. The same individual labeled atoms need not be added to DNA again. Their changed sedimentation behavior can be explained by their residence in a longer covalent chain. A loss of signal from the short fraction alone would be ambiguous because degradation might remove material. A corresponding gain in long DNA supports precursor-product conversion.

Such reasoning is also useful for interpreting an experiment in which ligase is defective. A 1968 study by Sugimoto, Okazaki and Okazaki examined short newly synthesized DNA chains in E. coli infected with ligase-defective bacteriophage T4. Under conditions impairing the relevant ligase, short chains accumulated. The experiment is an important demonstration that joining activity is required to convert short-chain intermediates into longer products. It is also an example of why the biological system must be stated correctly: an infected cell using phage replication machinery is not identical to an uninfected E. coli chromosome. The observed dependence on ligation supports the general discontinuous-synthesis mechanism; it does not directly establish the identity or exact behavior of every host chromosomal protein.

The Okazaki group subsequently investigated E. coli mutants deficient in polymerase I. Their 1971 paper reported that newly replicated short DNA in the examined polymerase I-deficient mutants was joined at about one tenth of the wild-type rate. That relative rate describes the particular strains and experimental conditions, not a universal quantitative property of all polA mutants. This result linked Pol I activity to processing intermediates rather than treating all DNA polymerases as interchangeable. In particular, a delayed conversion of short DNA to long DNA is consistent with insufficient removal of the primer or inadequate filling of the intervening sequence before ligation. The ligase itself might be perfectly functional, yet its appropriate substrate would be produced slowly. The strength of the experiment is that a genetic defect and a biochemical phenotype connect an enzyme to a stage of a pathway.

There are important interpretive limits. Detecting short nascent chains does not by itself prove that every short chain came from the lagging template, because repair-associated events can also create discontinuities. Harsh isolation could introduce breaks, and incomplete quenching could permit additional synthesis after the intended pulse. A control for bulk DNA integrity, comparison with a wild-type strain processed in the same way, and time-dependent behavior in chase experiments all improve the inference. The combination of polarity constraints, biochemical primer-dependent synthesis, genetic perturbation and joining kinetics provides a much stronger argument than any isolated short-DNA observation.

Suppose a hypothetical pulse gives 75 percent of the recovered label in the short fraction and 25 percent in a long fraction. After a chase, 15 percent remains short and 85 percent is long, while total recovered label remains approximately constant. The decrease in the short fraction is sixty percentage points, and eighty percent of the initially short-associated label has left that fraction. Under the simplified assumption that newly labeled molecules are not degraded or re-used, the result supports conversion into long chains. If a ligase-deficient sample retains 65 percent in the short fraction at the same chase time, joining is impaired. This experiment distinguishes a maturation problem from a simple failure to incorporate precursors, provided the initial pulse incorporation and recovery have been compared.

4. The molecular machine that moves with the fork

The collective assembly of proteins at a replication fork is called the replisome. A complex is valuable because its components must act on substrates that are continuously changing. The parental duplex entering the machine is converted into two single strands, and those strands become daughter duplexes. If each enzyme diffused independently to find its next substrate, exposed DNA could accumulate, enzymes could interfere with one another, and copying might lag behind unwinding. Protein interactions and DNA-binding interfaces allow the activities to remain coordinated while substrates pass through them. Some interactions are stable over many reactions, whereas others must be transient to permit repeated replacement.

At the front of the E. coli replisome is the DnaB helicase, an oligomeric ring that uses nucleoside triphosphate hydrolysis to support translocation and strand separation. DnaB encircles the lagging-strand template and moves along that strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction in the normal elongating fork. The other parental strand is excluded from the central channel. This arrangement couples directional movement on one strand to separation of the two. DnaB should not be confused with DnaA, which recognizes the origin during initiation, or DnaC, which helps load DnaB in E. coli. Once a functional fork has been established, unwinding must continue during elongation, but loading the original helicase and continuously driving it are distinct tasks.

Single-stranded DNA-binding protein, SSB, associates with exposed single-stranded DNA. Base stacking, complementary segments within one chain and nonspecific interactions with other molecules can otherwise make exposed templates unavailable or vulnerable. SSB reduces these problems and helps organize the lagging template for use by primase and polymerase. SSB is neither a polymerase nor an enzyme that forms a protective covalent modification. Its binding must also be dynamic: an enzyme cannot copy a nucleotide that is permanently inaccessible beneath a protective protein. Interaction with other replication factors and rearrangement or displacement of SSB permits protected DNA to become a productive template.

The DnaG primase makes short RNA primers complementary to DNA. A useful question is why RNA is used for a DNA copying problem. DNA polymerase cannot normally begin a new chain without an existing 3′ hydroxyl at a suitable primer-template junction. Primase, as a specialized RNA polymerase, can initiate a short RNA chain without such a primer. The RNA therefore provides a chemically usable 3′ end. DNA polymerase extends it with deoxyribonucleotides. Because the RNA occupies sequence that should ultimately be DNA, the initiation strategy necessarily creates a later processing requirement. Primase solves the starting problem but does not provide a finished backbone.

DnaG interacts with DnaB, allowing priming to be coordinated with template exposure. Primase does not need to accompany the leading polymerase at every step, since the leading strand generally continues from a primer made at initiation. The lagging template presents repeated opportunities for primer synthesis. The timing of these opportunities influences fragment length. If primers were made extremely infrequently while helicase continued to unwind, long stretches of single-stranded template could accumulate. If priming were very frequent, more short intermediates would be created and maturation enzymes would face more junctions. The actual arrangement balances these competing requirements rather than minimizing any one variable in isolation.

The E. coli DNA polymerase III holoenzyme performs the bulk of chromosomal elongation. It includes catalytic polymerase cores, DNA sliding clamps and a clamp-loader assembly. These names identify different levels of organization. A polymerase core has the catalytic capacity to add nucleotides and proofread, but the holoenzyme has additional components that allow it to remain associated with DNA and work efficiently at a replication fork. The sliding clamp is a DNA-bound ring that interacts with polymerase; the loader is an ATP-dependent machine that places the ring around DNA. A clamp is not a helicase merely because both proteins can form rings around nucleic acid. Their physical substrates and chemical tasks are different.

The association of DnaB with the tau form of the DnaX protein in the Pol III machinery helps coordinate unwinding with copying. The connection is especially important because the two daughter strands are synthesized in different chemical directions relative to the fork. A molecular assembly can retain both polymerase activities while accepting a loop of lagging template and then releasing it. The concept is not that every individual replisome protein is permanently rigidly joined to its neighbors. Some polymerase components exchange dynamically, and lagging-strand synthesis explicitly requires recurring changes in DNA and clamp engagement. What remains coordinated is the overall production of daughter DNA as the fork progresses.

A mutation in an interface can produce a phenotype even if each isolated enzyme retains catalytic activity. If an active helicase fails to coordinate with polymerases, more single-stranded DNA may be exposed than can be promptly copied. If primase can make RNA in isolation but cannot interact efficiently with the helicase, the frequency or position of priming at a fork may change. Conversely, an assay showing normal bulk polymerase activity on an artificial primed template cannot establish that a fully coupled replisome is working in cells. These are examples of the difference between molecular capability and systems-level function.

5. DNA polymerase III: the catalytic core and the holoenzyme

Consider what a polymerase must accomplish before a bacterial chromosome can be copied in a biologically useful time. It must hold a primer and template in a geometry suitable for chemistry, recognize the next complementary base, bind the corresponding nucleotide, catalyze its incorporation and move forward by one position. It must sometimes recognize an incorrect terminus and remove it. Finally, it must repeat the cycle thousands of times without repeatedly losing the template. The E. coli Pol III system divides these jobs between proteins. The distinction between catalytic functions and accessory functions is central to interpreting a mutation in any of its subunits.

A Pol III core contains three principal subunits: alpha, epsilon and theta. Alpha is encoded by dnaE and contains the principal 5′ to 3′ DNA polymerization activity. Epsilon, encoded by dnaQ, provides the major 3′ to 5′ proofreading exonuclease activity. Theta, encoded by holE, is associated with epsilon and can support its stability or function; it is not the principal polymerization catalyst. A core deficient in alpha cannot efficiently extend a primer simply because its proofreading subunit remains active. Conversely, a defective epsilon can leave considerable synthetic activity while increasing the frequency with which incorrect termini survive. A deficiency of theta must not automatically be treated as equivalent to the complete loss of epsilon.

The catalytic center of alpha uses the primer-terminal 3′ hydroxyl and the alpha phosphate of an incoming dNTP. Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium under physiological conditions, assist positioning and catalysis. The base on the incoming nucleotide must be appropriately paired with the next base of the DNA template. Formation of the new phosphodiester bond releases pyrophosphate, which contains the beta and gamma phosphates of the original nucleotide. The newly incorporated unit now contributes its own 3′ hydroxyl to the growing end, so the cycle can repeat. Subsequent hydrolysis of pyrophosphate helps pull the overall process toward polymer formation. It does not supply a separate DNA sequence-reading mechanism.

For a student, it is useful to follow the atoms rather than memorize the phrase “energy from ATP.” If the next base to be added is deoxycytidine, its immediate substrate is dCTP, not ATP. The alpha phosphate of dCTP remains in the backbone. The two other phosphates leave as pyrophosphate. If the next base is adenine, the corresponding DNA precursor is dATP. ATP also fuels many accessory reactions in the cell, including clamp loading and helicase activity, but those reactions must be accounted for separately from the activated DNA building blocks. The DNA backbone does not contain three phosphates for every nucleotide incorporated, and polymerase does not generate a phosphodiester bond by hydrolyzing an independent ATP molecule for every addition.

Accurate synthesis involves recognition of the geometry and chemical interactions of the nascent base pair. Complementary Watson–Crick pairs tend to fit the polymerase active site and permit productive alignment of the reacting groups. A nucleotide can bind transiently without necessarily being incorporated, and incorporation of an incorrect nucleotide is not impossible. The resulting abnormal primer terminus may make continued extension less favorable. It may instead be transferred to the exonuclease site associated with epsilon. Removal of the terminal nucleotide restores a 3′ hydroxyl on the preceding correctly paired unit. Extension can then resume with another incoming nucleotide. This editing cycle occurs on the growing chain and does not require removal of an entire downstream primer.

The terms 3′ to 5′ exonuclease and 5′ to 3′ polymerase describe distinct operations. A 3′ to 5′ exonuclease removes material from the 3′ end of a chain, progressing toward its 5′ end. A 5′ to 3′ polymerase lengthens the chain at its 3′ end. Both operations may concern the very same terminal nucleotide. This is why a polymerase can have a polymerization direction and an exonuclease direction that appear opposite without contradiction. Epsilon performs an error-correction function that competes with extension. The details of replication fidelity and post-replicative mismatch repair are developed in Node 3.A.07; the essential point here is that active proofreading is integrated into the replicative core.

A mispair and a nick are not equivalent substrates. A mispair involves inappropriate base pairing, potentially with a fully continuous backbone. A nick involves a missing covalent bond between adjacent nucleotides, potentially with perfect complementarity throughout. A proofreading enzyme removes a terminal nucleotide; it does not directly seal a nick. DNA ligase seals a chemically suitable nick, but it cannot in general recognize and replace an incorrectly paired internal base. Different errors in the architecture of replicated DNA therefore require different molecular activities. A student can often solve a difficult phenotype question by first describing the structure that has accumulated and then identifying the missing chemical operation.

The holoenzyme gives the core high processivity. Processivity means the number of nucleotide additions achieved during one productive association with a template, whereas rate describes how quickly additions occur. These quantities can vary independently. A polymerase might add one hundred nucleotides per second but dissociate after ten additions. Another might add the same number per second and remain associated for thousands of additions. Both have the same instantaneous synthetic rate while bound, but their effective performance on long DNA differs enormously if re-association takes appreciable time. The distinction is particularly important in a chromosome replication system, where inefficient binding would repeatedly interrupt copying.

Polymerase complexes can remain associated through interactions with the beta sliding clamp, while the clamp itself is topologically retained around DNA. The clamp does not attach to each successive nucleotide by forming covalent bonds. Instead, a ring surrounds the duplex and moves along it as the enzyme extends the new strand. Polymerase can therefore stay close to its substrate without needing to bind every new backbone position exceptionally tightly. This arrangement allows movement and retention at the same time. If the DNA molecule is linear and its ends are accessible, a freely sliding clamp could eventually move off an end; on a circular substrate it cannot simply escape through an end. Ring opening, unloading or other interactions can nevertheless regulate clamp residence.

A purified core that produces short products in the absence of the beta clamp is not necessarily catalytically damaged. Its failure to make long products may reflect frequent dissociation. To distinguish catalytic rate from processivity experimentally, one could compare initial incorporation on a short primer-template with product length in a single-binding experiment. A suitable competing DNA trap added after binding would prevent polymerases that dissociate from rejoining the labeled template, provided the trap itself does not disrupt active complexes. Addition of the clamp should increase product length under these conditions if retention is the key problem. Simply measuring more total incorporation after an hour would not identify which aspect of the reaction has changed.

6. The beta clamp and its loader: making a processive connection

The beta clamp, encoded by dnaN, is a ring assembled from two protein subunits in E. coli. Its architecture produces a central opening through which duplex DNA can pass. It contains protein-binding surfaces recognized by the replicative polymerase and by other DNA-processing factors. It is useful to picture it as a movable tether, but the analogy has limits: the ring is a molecular binding platform with specific interaction sites, not a passive mechanical washer. Its attachment to DNA and its interactions with polymerase are regulated by the availability of a suitable DNA structure and by other proteins.

A closed ring cannot be threaded directly onto the interior of a covalently closed DNA molecule without passing through the DNA backbone. The clamp-loader complex solves this topological problem by opening the ring and placing it around DNA. The loader is an ATPase-containing assembly built around products of the dnaX gene and several accessory subunits. In E. coli the DnaX products include tau and gamma forms; tau has additional domains that help connect polymerase cores and helicase. Other loader components include delta and delta prime, with chi and psi providing further interactions in the replisome. The exact subunit occupancy in cellular complexes can vary, so a simplified drawing should not imply that every complex always has one immutable composition.

ATP binding allows the loader to adopt conformations favorable for binding and opening the beta clamp and engaging a primer-template junction. DNA association activates steps in the loader's ATPase cycle. Hydrolysis and subsequent conformational changes promote release of the clamp-loader from the loaded clamp, which closes around DNA. The resulting clamp can engage polymerase. This sequence establishes directionality for the assembly process: binding a free clamp, opening it, recognizing the DNA junction, depositing it on DNA and releasing the loading machinery. The ATPase is not continually consuming one ATP per nucleotide to pull the clamp along the duplex. DNA synthesis and the conformational transitions of clamp loading are separate chemical cycles.

The appropriate loading position is a junction with a primer end paired to the template, typically at or near the transition between duplex and single-stranded DNA. A random intact duplex lacks the growing 3′ terminus needed for replicative extension. A completely single-stranded DNA region lacks the same junction geometry. By recognizing the substrate already prepared for synthesis, the loader helps couple the creation of primers to assembly of the processive polymerase. This is especially important on the lagging strand, where the clamp must be deposited at many newly created junctions. The leading strand generally does not require a new clamp at every nucleotide, although replacement and exchange are possible.

There is a division of labor even within the loader. The ATP-binding DnaX components provide major conformational driving functions, while delta participates in opening the beta ring. The tau domains contribute organization of the replication machine by contacting polymerase and DnaB. Interactions involving chi and SSB assist the management of single-stranded template and protein handoffs. This organization allows the same DNA substrate to be recognized differently at successive times. A primer coated or surrounded by proteins involved in making it must eventually become a primer available to a polymerase. The loader participates in this transition rather than simply acting on completely naked DNA.

A useful way to distinguish clamp-related mutants is to ask whether the ring reaches DNA. A clamp mutant whose interface with polymerase is defective may still load and slide but fail to support long polymerase residence. A loader mutant may bind ATP but fail to open the ring, or open a ring without depositing it productively. A mutant impaired in ATP hydrolysis might trap a loading intermediate, making the clamp inaccessible to the polymerase. These phenotypes can all reduce production of long DNA, but the physical intermediates differ. The most direct diagnostic assay would compare DNA-associated clamp abundance, ATPase activity and DNA extension rather than infer the failed step from final DNA yield alone.

For example, imagine an experiment in which a fluorescent beta clamp is used to examine its association with a primer-template DNA substrate. Wild-type loader produces substantial stable DNA-associated fluorescence after free protein is removed. A mutant loader produces little stable signal, despite comparable concentrations of clamp and DNA. An independent short-template assay shows that the isolated polymerase core extends a primer normally. This combination points to deficient clamp assembly rather than a primary defect in alpha catalysis. It would still be necessary to control for fluorescent labeling, DNA quality and the possibility that the mutant loader rapidly unloads clamps after deposition. The assay distinguishes classes of explanations rather than proving a precise amino acid mechanism.

Many textbooks introduce the replisome as two Pol III cores associated through the loader: one for the leading strand and one for the lagging strand. This is a useful minimal picture because it explains how both strands can be copied simultaneously. Experimental work also supports assemblies with three polymerase cores, and polymerase exchange at an active fork. An additional core can help provide lagging-strand capacity or participate in dynamic handoffs. The important examination distinction is between a simplified two-polymerase diagram and a claim that exactly two polymerase molecules are present at every fork at every instant. A question about the minimum functional arrangement can use two activities; a question about measured cellular stoichiometry requires evidence rather than the schematic.

7. Executing leading-strand synthesis

Imagine following a leading strand immediately after initiation. DnaG has provided a short RNA primer. The template is accessible, a beta clamp has been loaded at the primer-template junction, and a Pol III core has engaged the primer's 3′ end. DnaB ahead separates more parental DNA, permitting alpha to extend the primer into a daughter strand. Each newly exposed template position supplies the order in which the four different dNTPs should be added. Epsilon can remove occasional terminal errors. The beta clamp reduces loss of the polymerase from the duplex, and the organizational contacts between the polymerase machinery and helicase maintain a coordinated fork.

The leading polymerase does not copy DNA ahead of the helicase in the normal model. It can polymerize only when the next template base is accessible. A temporary reduction in unwinding limits how much new sequence is available for copying. A reduction in polymerase activity may instead permit uncoupling, in which unwinding and DNA synthesis are not perfectly synchronized. The amount and persistence of uncoupling depend on the molecular system and the nature of the obstruction. Single-stranded DNA and SSB occupancy may increase if helicase continues while copying is delayed. It would be too strong to assert that the helicase always stops at exactly the same instant whenever polymerase pauses, or that it always continues indefinitely without the polymerase.

A leading strand is also subject to nucleotide availability. Polymerization depends on a balanced supply of the four dNTPs. Depletion of one precursor preferentially creates problems when the corresponding complementary template base is encountered. The cell cannot simply substitute another nucleotide without generating a mismatch. In an idealized reaction with no errors, the polymerase must wait at the first position requiring an unavailable substrate. In a real cell, the concentrations and interactions of many precursors affect synthesis, fidelity and the response to stress. This connection explains why nucleotide metabolism is a prerequisite for genome duplication, rather than a separate topic with no direct relationship to the replication machinery.

The physical growth of the leading daughter remains tied to fork movement through two different molecular tracks. DnaB translocates on the lagging template, and the leading polymerase extends DNA on the other template. Thus the helicase and leading polymerase can both move in the direction of fork advance even though they follow different strands and have opposite polarity conventions for those strands. An apparent paradox arises only if the polarity of the helicase's substrate is confused with the polarity of the polymerase's substrate. In a rightward fork with the upper strand running 3′ to 5′ from left to right, leading Pol III advances rightward on the upper strand while DnaB advances rightward on the lower strand in its 5′ to 3′ direction.

An established leading strand has an origin-proximal 5′ end, initially containing RNA primer material, and a growing 3′ end near the moving fork. When the original primer is ultimately replaced, its position may become associated with a neighboring daughter strand segment made by the other fork. This is one reason why the simple statement “one primer is needed for each leading strand” does not imply that leading regions never require any finishing operations. It describes the initial organization of an uninterrupted leading chain. In a circular chromosome the completed new strand has no free end; all primer regions and relevant discontinuities must eventually be processed into continuous DNA.

A polymerase defect can be studied by asking where labeled DNA accumulates after a short pulse. An enzyme unable to perform the main extension reaction should reduce incorporation even if primers are made. In contrast, a defect that predominantly prevents joining after synthesis can leave much initial incorporation intact but produce discontinuous products. A short pulse alone does not distinguish leading and lagging products. Strand-specific labeling or probes that recognize a known genomic orientation can add that information. A DNA synthesis defect should also not be diagnosed solely by growth rate, since many unrelated failures slow bacterial growth. Combining evidence about incorporation, strand length and fork progression improves interpretation.

8. The recurring lagging-strand cycle

To understand discontinuous synthesis at a molecular level, begin with an exposed stretch of lagging template close to the helicase. The newest accessible sequence is closest to the fork, and the region already replicated lies behind it. SSB protects exposed DNA until a priming event occurs. DnaG creates a short complementary RNA. Its 5′ end lies nearer the fork than its 3′ end in the unfolded representation of the rightward-moving fork described earlier. The 3′ hydroxyl points toward previously exposed DNA, allowing a DNA polymerase to extend toward the older fragment region.

The clamp loader deposits a beta ring at this new primer-template junction. Pol III engages the ring and begins DNA extension. The polymerase copies successive template positions, generally moving away from the fork when traced along the unlooped lagging template. Meanwhile the helicase continues exposing newer template. If the leading polymerase and lagging polymerase are coordinated within a common assembly, the lagging template may form a loop that allows the polymerase to remain spatially near the fork as DNA passes through it. The loop changes the physical path followed by the DNA but does not reverse the chemistry of addition.

As extension proceeds, a newly growing fragment approaches the preceding fragment. To avoid leaving a gap, the region between the current primer and the previous fragment must be copied. The polymerase does not normally remove the next RNA primer using a 5′ to 3′ exonuclease, because Pol III does not supply that activity. A collision with an older fragment or signals related to formation of a new primer can contribute to the transition away from the existing lagging fragment. The precise recycling process can be dynamic. It is safest at examination level to understand that Pol III repeatedly releases from completed or terminated lagging-strand synthesis and is made available to engage new primed DNA, rather than assume that a single universal collision signal explains every event.

The previous beta clamp can remain associated with duplex DNA after its polymerase leaves. Other enzymes may interact with DNA-associated clamps, and the rings can later be recycled. New primers need suitable loading events; the same physical clamp need not be carried from the old fragment directly to the next primer by the departing polymerase. This is a particularly important distinction between recycling the catalytic core and unloading the old clamp. A defect preventing polymerase release can trap the enzyme on old DNA even if new primers and new rings are available. A defect preventing ring loading can produce the converse situation: available polymerase and primers but poor processive extension.

A completed newly synthesized fragment is still a composite RNA-DNA chain at this stage. Its own RNA primer is at its 5′ end. Its DNA extends toward the adjacent older segment, with a new 3′ end. The directionality of the primer is not changed when it becomes part of the fragment. Maturation uses the 3′ end of a neighboring fragment to fill sequence while replacing the intervening RNA primer. Consequently, the primer of one fragment is often processed using synthesis initiated at the 3′ end of a fragment produced subsequently. Thinking about a single fragment in isolation can conceal this relationship; a pair of adjacent fragments shows the geometry more clearly.

The size of bacterial Okazaki fragments is commonly of the order of one to two kilonucleotides in standard E. coli textbook examples. This is not a fixed count imposed by a molecular ruler, and actual distributions vary with organism, conditions and measurement. The distance covered by helicase between successful priming events provides a simple explanation for why the typical fragment size depends on the relative timing of priming and fork motion. A doubling of fork speed with an unchanged average interval between primers tends to increase the amount of template exposed per interval. A doubling of priming frequency with the same speed tends to decrease it, provided the new junctions are productively used.

The lagging-strand cycle creates a need for throughput rather than merely speed of one enzyme. Suppose primers are produced once every two seconds, and each polymerase takes nearly two seconds to synthesize its fragment. If the next primer cannot be used until the previous fragment finishes and the machinery recycles, even a short delay may become significant. Additional capacity, transient overlap of events and efficient handoffs can help maintain progress. Conversely, making more primers without increasing extension or maturation capacity may create a backlog of unfinished fragments. The behavior of a coupled system cannot be inferred from the rate of primase alone; one must identify the slowest effective stage and the available buffering capacity.

The difference between continuous and discontinuous synthesis becomes especially evident if a primase perturbation is applied after forks have already initiated. Existing leading polymerases may transiently continue from their current DNA termini. Lagging synthesis faces a recurring requirement for new primers, so unprimed template can accumulate. Eventually, coordination of the whole fork may fail and leading synthesis may also decline. The immediate differential effect and the later global effect should be distinguished. If the perturbation was applied before initiation instead, both leading and lagging synthesis may be affected because both require initial primer formation. The timing of an experimental intervention is therefore as important as its molecular target.

9. Why a loop can coordinate opposite synthesis directions

The trombone model illustrates how a single fork-associated machine can synthesize both strands without requiring the lagging polymerase to travel away from the rest of the replisome in physical space. Picture the single-stranded lagging template bending back toward the catalytic core. The DNA enters and exits the polymerase in a changing loop. As the fragment is extended, the loop can grow; when the polymerase releases and prepares for the next primer, the old loop is released. A new loop forms during the next synthesis cycle. The name comes from the repeated extension and contraction of a trombone slide, although the molecular loop is flexible and does not behave as a rigid metal instrument.

The representation is useful because a two-dimensional fork drawing can otherwise appear to require the lagging polymerase to move left while the entire replication assembly moves right. In three-dimensional space, it is possible to move DNA through a catalytic site that itself remains near the helicase. A laboratory analogy is feeding a flexible ribbon through a stationary reading head. The reader does not have to travel along the full length of the ribbon if the ribbon moves through it. The analogy describes relative motion only: DNA polymerase actually performs sequence-dependent chemistry, and the ribbon is a paired and unpaired polymer with polarity.

A simple sketch of a loop should identify its template strand, its primer 5′ end, its growing 3′ end, the helicase direction and the location of the polymerase. Without these labels, a loop can obscure rather than clarify the mechanism. In particular, the arrow drawn at the growing 3′ end must remain consistent with the sequence of template positions even if the arrow points toward the physical fork on the folded diagram. One can mentally unfold the loop to check the polarity. If the unfolded representation suggests new DNA is being extended at its 5′ end, the original diagram is wrong.

There is a balance between the stability and flexibility of the protein contacts that hold the machinery together. Very weak interactions would allow premature dissociation. Irreversibly strong interactions could prevent polymerase release and reuse when a fragment is finished. The replisome must combine persistent coordination with adjustable engagement. Thus, protein exchange and repeated binding are not signs of a defective machine by themselves. They can be part of how a functional machine manages substrates of unequal lifetimes. The leading region is relatively persistent, while each lagging fragment is a transient catalytic job.

The two-strand system also needs to manage timing differences. A lagging polymerase may finish a fragment while leading synthesis continues, then wait briefly for a suitable new primer or clamp. Alternatively a new primer can be available before an older synthesis event has fully ended. An experimentally observed pause does not automatically mean the whole fork has stopped. Measuring single-strand synthesis, total DNA incorporation and fork movement in the same experiment can help distinguish these possibilities. For examination purposes the central lesson is that the timing of the lagging cycle is linked to fork advance but the two strands need not behave as perfectly synchronized mechanical gears.

10. From RNA-DNA intermediates to DNA: polymerase I

A newly replicated lagging strand is not ready for inheritance simply because every position has been paired with a complementary base. At regular intervals it includes RNA that supplied the starting hydroxyl for DNA polymerase. At a boundary between adjacent chains there may also be a gap or a nick. A gap is an interval of missing nucleotides on one strand opposite an intact template; a nick is a missing phosphodiester bond between adjacent nucleotides without a missing base position. Both can expose the discontinuity of a daughter backbone, but they require different operations. A polymerase can fill a gap when it has a suitable primer and template. A ligase can close a nick with appropriate end chemistry. A ligase does not fill a gap by inventing the missing nucleotide sequence.

E. coli DNA polymerase I is particularly suited to the transition between these states. The polA gene encodes a protein with 5′ to 3′ polymerase activity, 3′ to 5′ exonuclease proofreading activity and a 5′ nuclease activity traditionally called 5′ to 3′ exonuclease. The last activity is of special importance for processing the downstream chain during synthesis from a neighboring upstream chain. The intact protein can both remove nucleic acid from the downstream region and extend the 3′ end of the upstream DNA. The reactions are coordinated so that the complementary sequence can be retained while its RNA component is replaced by DNA.

The terms upstream and downstream here refer to positions in the direction of synthesis along the new strand, not to transcription from a promoter. Let the new strand be drawn from 5′ at the left to 3′ at the right. At the junction, an upstream DNA fragment ends in a 3′ hydroxyl. The downstream fragment begins with a 5′ RNA segment followed by DNA. The parental template extends continuously beneath the two fragments in the 3′ to 5′ direction. Pol I can extend the upstream 3′ end rightward, using the parental sequence to determine which deoxyribonucleotides to insert. At the same time its 5′ nuclease removes obstructing RNA ahead of the extending strand, sometimes through cleavage of displaced flap structures rather than a literal one-base-at-a-time mechanism. The RNA is therefore replaced with a DNA copy of the correct sequence.

This process is often described as nick translation because the location of the backbone discontinuity moves along the strand. Before processing, the break is at the boundary between upstream DNA and downstream RNA or DNA. After the upstream DNA extends and downstream material is removed, the break is located farther along the strand. The net result is replacement of a stretch of nucleic acid without requiring the cell to discard a large correctly synthesized fragment. When replacement has proceeded sufficiently, the remaining boundary is between two DNA segments with adjacent, ligatable termini. Translation in this expression has nothing to do with ribosomes or protein synthesis.

One can reason through a concrete sequence. Suppose an upstream DNA segment ends with a correctly paired 3′ hydroxyl and the following four template positions in the direction of synthesis are 3′-A C T G-5′. An RNA primer already occupies those positions as 5′-U G A C-3′. The replacement DNA sequence is 5′-T G A C-3′. Pol I must use the parental DNA, not the excised RNA molecule, to choose the new bases. The RNA and DNA copies have matching complementarity except for the expected uracil versus thymine difference. A student should not predict that the primer is removed and replaced by an identical RNA sequence, or that the next fragment is reversed when the primer is removed. The template orientation has not changed.

The 5′ nuclease activity of full-length Pol I is separable from its other major activities. Proteolytic cleavage can produce a large fragment called the Klenow fragment, which retains polymerization and 3′ to 5′ proofreading but lacks the intact enzyme's N-terminal 5′ nuclease activity. It can therefore extend an appropriate primer, yet it cannot perform the same coupled downstream cleavage reaction as complete Pol I. Some engineered Klenow derivatives additionally lack proofreading, so an exam statement about ordinary Klenow should not be applied without qualification to every commercial variant. The distinction is a valuable example of how identifying separate catalytic domains explains an enzyme's experimentally observed abilities.

An enzyme preparation that extends a gapped DNA substrate successfully is not necessarily capable of normal primer processing. The gap assay supplies an already accessible template region and does not require the enzyme to remove an obstructing downstream RNA. A more informative comparison uses two substrates: a simple gap with a usable 3′ end and a junction containing a downstream RNA-DNA segment. Wild-type Pol I may fill the first and process the second. A nuclease-deficient but polymerization-competent derivative may perform the first reaction yet fail to generate efficient mature products in the second. If ligase is present in both assays, it still cannot compensate fully for the missing nuclease activity, because the correct DNA-only junction may not become available.

RNA degradation activities, including RNase H, can assist handling of RNA-DNA hybrids in bacteria. It is important not to infer that all RNA primers are removed by a single universally conserved protein, or that the presence of RNase H abolishes the need for other processing activities. Different nucleases recognize different structures, and removal of RNA can leave DNA gaps or residual junctions requiring further processing. At the level of the classical E. coli mechanism, Pol I combines primer removal with replacement synthesis, and DNA ligase completes the seal. This assignment explains its characteristic role in lagging-strand maturation while allowing for alternative or overlapping activities in cells.

Pol I also acts at many DNA repair intermediates, which are often small gaps or discontinuities. Its catalytic abilities make it appropriate for localized synthesis, but its normal role is not to replace Pol III as the highly processive machine at the main replication fork. This helps reconcile the historical observation that mutants defective in parts of Pol I activity can still incorporate substantial amounts of DNA with the importance of Pol I in cell survival and maturation. The precise phenotype of a polA mutation depends on which domains and functions are affected and on genetic context. Some functions can be partly compensated; complete loss of all activities is not equivalent to a mutation specifically affecting its polymerase domain.

11. DNA ligase and the chemistry of sealing

After all necessary replacement synthesis, adjacent DNA segments can be paired continuously to a template while remaining chemically discontinuous on the newly synthesized strand. The adjacent upstream nucleotide has a 3′ hydroxyl, and the downstream nucleotide has a 5′ phosphate. Formation of the missing phosphodiester bond joins these ends into one covalent chain. DNA ligase catalyzes this reaction. It does not need to read and incorporate a new base from a dNTP, because both base positions are already occupied. Its task is activation and joining of existing DNA ends.

The major essential replicative ligase of E. coli, LigA, uses NAD+ as the cofactor for its adenylation cycle. The enzyme first becomes covalently adenylated, with AMP attached to an active-site lysine; nicotinamide mononucleotide is released from NAD+. AMP is then transferred to the 5′ phosphate at the nick, generating an activated DNA intermediate. The adjacent 3′ hydroxyl attacks the activated phosphate, forming the phosphodiester bond and releasing AMP. The cofactor has supplied a means to activate the existing phosphate rather than serving as a source of nucleotides for extension. ATP-dependent DNA ligases use ATP for enzyme adenylation instead; phage T4 DNA ligase is a familiar example. Both classes converge on the activated DNA-adenylate intermediate and the same overall joining chemistry.

The requirement for an appropriate 5′ phosphate and 3′ hydroxyl matters for predicting failed reactions. A DNA fragment whose 5′ end is a hydroxyl group is not automatically a usable standard substrate for nick sealing. It first requires conversion to the appropriate phosphate state. Similarly, a 3′ end lacking the required hydroxyl cannot perform the normal attack. An end already occupied by a terminal chemical lesion may require repair before ligation. Providing more ligase cannot correct a problem of substrate chemistry by itself. In a question involving a nick, inspect the identity of both adjacent ends before predicting ligation.

There is also a structural distinction between nick sealing and joining unrelated DNA pieces. During replication, the complementary template holds adjacent DNA termini in a geometry favorable for ligation. The two new fragments need not diffuse independently until they happen to meet; they are aligned on the same parental strand. Laboratory ligases can be used to join suitable DNA ends in molecular cloning, but that application often involves separate molecules with complementary or blunt ends. It should not obscure the physiological substrate in this chapter: an aligned discontinuity in a newly copied strand. The ligase does not determine the base sequence of the completed region.

Suppose a DNA population has exactly five hundred nicks, each with correctly paired termini and the appropriate chemical end groups, and suppose LigA seals every one through its normal NAD-dependent cycle. Five hundred enzyme adenylation events are required under the simplified assumption of one cofactor molecule consumed per successful nick, corresponding to five hundred NAD+ molecules. If the enzyme were supplied in an already adenylated state, some first turnovers could occur without adding NAD+ during that particular observation interval; the full catalytic cycle nevertheless requires regeneration of activated enzyme. The distinction between initial enzyme state and steady turnover is important in biochemical reasoning.

A ligase defect produces a different immediate DNA structure from a polymerase defect. If Pol III has continued producing fragments and Pol I has replaced RNA appropriately, inhibiting LigA can leave a high abundance of DNA-only nicks. Under conditions that denature the duplex, the discontinuous new strands separate into smaller pieces even though their complementary templates are intact. The total amount of nucleotide incorporation might remain appreciable early after inhibition, but recovery of continuous long DNA is reduced. Over longer periods, persistent discontinuities can produce additional damage, interfere with subsequent replication and threaten viability. Therefore, a time-resolved experiment can separate the direct biochemical effect from indirect consequences of an accumulating defect.

A useful rescue experiment begins with purified DNA from a hypothetical ligase-impaired preparation. If adding functional ligase in vitro converts many molecules into longer continuous products without adding new dNTPs, the initial preparation contained suitable unsealed junctions. If rescue requires Pol I and dNTPs as well as ligase, more substantial processing defects may be present. A failure of rescue is not proof that a particular enzyme was absent, since isolation may have changed end chemistry or damaged the DNA. Nevertheless, substrate analysis coupled with selective addition of enzymes is a powerful way to locate a block in a multistep pathway.

12. Comparing E. coli polymerases I, II and III

The numbering of bacterial DNA polymerases reflects their discovery history, not the chronological sequence of their normal roles at a fork. DNA polymerase I was characterized first and was initially a natural candidate for the enzyme responsible for whole-chromosome replication. Identification of additional enzymes and the analysis of mutant phenotypes changed that interpretation. Pol III is the principal replicase in E. coli, whereas Pol I performs much of the local processing needed to produce mature DNA. Pol II, encoded by polB, belongs to a different polymerase family and contributes to DNA damage responses, replication recovery and certain repair-associated synthesis events. The names alone cannot tell a student which enzyme works first on a nascent fragment.

The three enzymes all have 5′ to 3′ synthetic activity, and their normal forms possess 3′ to 5′ exonucleolytic proofreading activity. They differ in their organization and in additional activities. Pol I is distinguished by its 5′ nuclease and suitability for localized replacement synthesis. Pol III core depends on association with accessory machinery for the exceptionally processive chromosome-scale reaction. Pol II can participate in synthesis when the normal replicase is obstructed or when specialized substrates arise, but it is not the dominant polymerase required for routine elongation of every E. coli chromosome. Its involvement in damage-associated synthesis does not make it identical to an inherently error-prone polymerase.

In the E. coli SOS response, expression of polB can increase after DNA damage. This provides additional polymerase capacity in conditions under which the usual replicative apparatus may be challenged. The full regulatory mechanism is outside this node and belongs with DNA damage tolerance. Here the relevant point is that cells maintain polymerases with partly overlapping substrate capabilities but different physiological tasks. Polymerases IV and V also exist in E. coli and contribute to damage tolerance in particular circumstances. Their presence does not invalidate the introductory comparison of Pol I, II and III; it does invalidate a claim that these are the only DNA polymerases in the bacterium.

Consider three simplified enzyme defects introduced into cells that already contain initiated forks. Defect A abolishes Pol III alpha catalysis. The immediate ability to extend large amounts of new DNA should fall sharply. Defect B specifically removes the 5′ nuclease of Pol I while leaving its ability to fill short gaps. Much nascent DNA may still appear during a pulse, but processing of downstream primers is compromised. Defect C reduces Pol II activity. Routine growth under conditions without strong DNA damage may be less immediately affected than in A, whereas recovery from particular replication obstacles may be impaired. The order of physiological severity is not universal because mutations can have different residual activity and backgrounds. The mechanistic predictions concern each specifically defined defect.

The enzyme comparison also illustrates why biochemical purification and genetic analysis complement each other. A purified Pol I preparation can make DNA, so a cell extract with this activity cannot establish that Pol I is the chromosome's main replicase. A mutant unable to perform normal elongation provides a different type of evidence: it connects an enzyme to a required in vivo process. Rescue with a functional gene can test whether the observed phenotype is attributable to that gene. Purified reconstitution can then ask which molecular interactions and substrates are sufficient for the specific operation. The convergence of evidence supports a mechanistic assignment more strongly than the enzyme's discovery order or relative abundance alone.

13. The topology problem ahead of a moving fork

Every complete turn of duplex DNA represents relative winding of the two complementary strands. If helicase separates successive base pairs within a constrained circular DNA molecule, the remaining parental region cannot indefinitely accommodate the rearrangement without changes in its geometry. A double helix approximately follows ten to eleven base pairs per turn under common relaxed B-DNA conditions. Opening thousands of base pairs per second at advancing forks therefore poses a substantial topological demand. Unless the system changes the relevant constraints through rotation or strand passage, accumulated torsional stress makes further separation energetically unfavorable.

For a covalently closed duplex circle, linking number Lk describes how many times one closed strand is linked with the other, with sign determined by orientation convention. It is a topological invariant in the absence of strand breakage and passage. The relationship Lk = Tw + Wr separates twist, the winding of the strands around the duplex axis, from writhe, the coiling of that axis in space. Twist and writhe can exchange through deformation while their sum remains fixed. A standard relaxed reference linking number for a particular DNA length is approximately N divided by h, where N is the number of base pairs and h is the relaxed helical repeat in base pairs per turn. Because Lk for a closed circle is an integer, this reference can be a convenient noninteger model value rather than the actual linking number of a single molecule.

Positive supercoiling means the linking number is greater than the chosen relaxed reference, whereas negative supercoiling means it is smaller. In a local constrained domain ahead of a replication fork, opening parental DNA tends to introduce positive torsional stress into the remaining duplex. One must be careful with the phrase “helicase increases linking number.” An ordinary helicase separates strands without intentionally cutting and rejoining the DNA backbone. It redistributes the winding constraints and can change local torsional state, but it does not by itself change the global topological invariant of an intact covalently closed parental DNA system. Topoisomerases provide a mechanism for actually altering topological links through controlled backbone cleavage and rejoining.

DNA gyrase is a bacterial type II topoisomerase. In E. coli it consists of GyrA and GyrB subunits, arranged in a functional A2B2 complex. The enzyme binds DNA segments, transiently creates a double-strand gate in one segment, transports another duplex segment through the gate and reseals the break. ATP-dependent conformational changes support the strand-passage cycle. In the canonical negative-supercoiling reaction, DNA is wrapped and passed in an orientation that decreases linking number by two per completed strand-passage event. Gyrase can also remove positive supercoiling ahead of a moving fork and thereby reduce the mechanical resistance to unwinding. This ability is essential to understanding why an enzyme that cuts DNA can support DNA replication rather than merely destroy it.

A controlled topoisomerase break is chemically different from a freely exposed accidental double-strand break. During the catalytic cycle, enzyme-DNA covalent linkages help retain the DNA ends so that they can be religated after strand passage. The enzyme does not require a new DNA sequence to fill the transient gate, and its rejoining operation is not the routine sealing of lagging-strand fragments by LigA. If the topoisomerase is trapped in the cleavage state, however, the intermediate becomes a source of genomic damage. Hence two inhibitors that both reduce the overall catalytic activity of an enzyme may produce different types of DNA injury depending on whether they prevent a catalytic transition or stabilize a broken-DNA intermediate.

E. coli topoisomerase I, encoded by topA, is a type IA enzyme that chiefly relaxes excessive negative supercoiling through single-strand passage. In the standard duplex topological context its catalytic action can change linking number in single-unit steps. It counterbalances gyrase in maintaining a suitable superhelical state. It is not a direct replacement for gyrase's ATP-driven negative-supercoiling activity or for the major duplex decatenation function of topoisomerase IV. This division of labor illustrates why the term topoisomerase is a functional class rather than the name of one interchangeable enzyme. Topoisomerase III is another type IA enzyme that can contribute to processing particular linked intermediates containing single-stranded regions; its role should not be generalized to the dominant removal of fully duplex chromosome catenanes.

A simple numerical illustration makes the scale of the topological challenge clearer. If one fork opens 1,050 base pairs and we approximate the relaxed helical repeat as 10.5 base pairs per turn, it exposes DNA corresponding to 100 turns of parental winding. In a simplified constrained-domain model with no fork rotation or other relaxation, this creates an effective demand to redistribute or remove approximately 100 turns. A type II relaxation step changing the relevant linking number by two would require about fifty net strand-passage events to compensate. This estimate is not a claim that precisely fifty gyrase molecules are needed. A single enzyme can catalyze multiple cycles, other topoisomerases may participate, and some winding can be transferred behind the fork. The example converts a nucleotide-scale rate into a topological workload without overinterpreting the simplified geometry.

Superhelical density expresses the relative deviation of linking number from a relaxed reference: σ = (Lk − Lk₀)/Lk₀. It is dimensionless. For example, a relaxed reference of 100 turns and an actual linking number of 94 give σ = −0.06, representing underlinking relative to the reference. A change of six units is not comparable between different DNA lengths until the reference linking number is considered. The topological state of DNA is not determined by sequence alone. Two circular DNA molecules of identical sequence can have different linking numbers and different shapes. Electrophoretic separation can therefore yield different mobility patterns for relaxed, supercoiled and nicked circular forms even when their nucleotide lengths are identical. A nick can permit rotation and relaxation of torsional constraints without necessarily creating a linear double-stranded molecule. A double-strand break, in contrast, can linearize a circular duplex. These distinctions are useful in interpreting topoisomerase and ligase assays, where migration changes may indicate altered topology rather than simply changes in the number of bases synthesized.

14. Precatenanes and physical coordination of the two daughter duplexes

Positive winding stress ahead of a fork is not the only possible topological outcome of replication. If the fork rotates relative to the unreplicated DNA, some winding can be transferred behind it as intertwining of the newly formed daughter duplexes. Such interlinks formed in a partially replicated chromosome are called precatenanes. They arise because the copying process transforms one paired parental duplex into two daughter duplexes while the geometry of the original strands still imposes constraints. The two daughters may therefore be linked around one another even when the local sequences have been copied accurately.

One can distinguish an individual duplex's supercoiling from catenation by asking which objects are interlinked. In supercoiling, the geometry of a DNA duplex's own axis changes and the linking relationship between its two strands is relevant. In catenation, two separate DNA duplex circles are topologically interlinked like two links of a chain. They cannot be pulled apart by moving the circles around in space without one passing through the other. Precatenation is the analogous problem while the molecules are not yet fully separated as completed circles. Ordinary physical separation of daughter chromosomes in a growing cell cannot resolve a true catenane by force alone; the appropriate topology must first be changed.

Topoisomerase IV is the major E. coli duplex decatenase and is another type II topoisomerase. Its ParC and ParE subunits are related to the corresponding catalytic and ATPase components of gyrase. Topoisomerase IV can transiently cleave one DNA duplex and pass another through it, then reseal the first. This reaction can remove links between sister duplexes. The enzyme can also relax certain supercoiled substrates, so its biochemical capabilities overlap with those of gyrase. Nevertheless, the normal physiological emphasis differs: gyrase is central to removing positive winding stress and establishing negative supercoiling, whereas topoisomerase IV is especially important for separating interlinked daughter DNA.

Decatenation does not have to begin only at the exact instant when the two replication forks meet. Precatenanes can be removed as replication advances, reducing the work remaining at the end. At completion, any unresolved links between covalently closed daughter circles still require removal. A chromosome may therefore pass a sequence-copying assay and fail a segregation assay. Conversely, an inhibitor primarily blocking positive-supercoil relaxation can hinder elongation so severely that the cell never reaches the stage at which full daughter catenanes form. This distinction underlies the usefulness of comparing DNA amount, topological forms and chromosome segregation rather than treating all topoisomerase defects as identical.

In a classical class of decatenation experiments, intertwined circular DNA substrates are produced through replication or site-specific recombination and their conversion into separate DNA circles is measured. Work by Zechiedrich and colleagues showed the major role of topoisomerase IV in decatenating products of recombination in E. coli and clarified how gyrase can contribute indirectly by creating favorable DNA topology. A careful interpretation distinguishes direct strand passage by topoisomerase IV from supporting effects of gyrase on substrate conformation. The two proteins may participate in the same physiological outcome without executing the same principal step. This is a general theme in molecular biology: two loss-of-function phenotypes can appear similar because the proteins act consecutively or cooperatively, not because they are identical catalysts.

15. Fork convergence and the logic of a polar termination region

In a simple chromosome with one bidirectional origin, the two forks move toward one another along complementary paths around a circle. If they start together, travel at the same speed and do not encounter long pauses, they meet approximately opposite the origin. There is no need for either fork to count a predetermined number of nucleotides. The remaining unreplicated region becomes shorter as the two machines advance. Completion requires them to resolve this final segment without leaving a gap, making an unnecessary extra copy of a region or retaining a protein assembly that obstructs later events.

The E. coli chromosome contains a series of asymmetric DNA sequences called Ter sites in its terminus region. The terminus utilization substance, Tus, is a protein that binds these sites. The complex can act as a polar replication fork barrier. A fork encountering the complex from its permissive direction can usually proceed, whereas one approaching the nonpermissive direction can be arrested. The distinction is not caused by different base composition on two chromosomes. It arises from the orientation of a particular protein-DNA complex and how the advancing machinery interacts with its two faces.

The arrangement of several Ter sites in opposing orientations produces a fork trap. To reason about the trap, draw an interval between two oppositely oriented barriers. A fork entering from the left can proceed through the left boundary but encounters a blocking face at the right boundary. A fork entering from the right can proceed through the right boundary but encounters a blocking face at the left boundary. Both can enter the interval, but each is restricted from leaving in the corresponding forward direction. The forks therefore have an increased chance of converging within the terminus region even if one advances substantially faster than the other.

This is not equivalent to a barrier that blocks motion equally in both directions. Such an obstacle could prevent a slower fork from reaching the region in which it must complete copying. A polar arrangement provides direction-dependent access. It is also not a mechanism requiring both forks to stop simultaneously at the same Ter site. One fork may reach a barrier first and be paused while the other continues through unreplicated DNA. In the usual model the remaining sequence is copied by the converging fork, and the replication intermediates are subsequently processed. The observed location of convergence depends on fork dynamics, site occupancy and the cellular context.

Tus recognizes the specific Ter sequence through protein-DNA interactions. In a commonly discussed molecular model for strong arrest at nonpermissive encounters, unwinding exposes a particular conserved cytosine (C6 at TerB in the reported structural study) that can become captured in a binding pocket of Tus, stabilizing a locked complex. The two orientations do not produce the same sequence of unwinding and binding events. This provides a structural basis for polarity and explains why an asymmetrical complex can be more difficult to displace from one direction. Details of Tus arrest can also depend on the helicase and reaction conditions, so the lock should not be treated as an exhaustive explanation of every experimental observation.

An important limitation of the textbook picture is that Tus–Ter is not the universal chemical mechanism of bacterial replication completion. Even in E. coli, cells deficient in Tus can often complete replication and grow under standard conditions. The fork trap helps constrain where forks meet and can limit certain abnormal fork movements, but it does not provide the basic chemistry of DNA synthesis, primer processing or decatenation. Two forks can converge in the absence of a dedicated polar trap. The effects of removing Tus may become more apparent when the chromosome is stressed or replication dynamics are altered. A deletion phenotype must therefore be interpreted in relation to the conditions under which it was measured.

The contrast with other bacteria clarifies what is conserved. Bacillus subtilis has a terminator system involving replication terminator protein RTP rather than E. coli Tus, and its terminator arrangement differs. Many bacteria do not have the same dedicated fork-trap system. The physical requirements that persist across organisms are the completion of daughter DNA and the resolution of relevant topological intermediates. A question asking about E. coli may specifically require the names Tus and Ter; a question asking about a universal requirement for circular chromosome replication is better answered by reference to fork convergence, maturation and separation. A named example must not be mistaken for a general definition.

In an orientation experiment, a Ter sequence can be placed on a defined DNA substrate in either orientation, and replication products can be compared in the presence and absence of Tus. A marked reduction of fork passage only when Tus is present and the site faces the fork in the nonpermissive orientation supports a polar protein-DNA barrier. If both orientations equally impede replication even without Tus, an additional sequence-dependent obstacle or some experimental artifact would need investigation. A control lacking the Ter sequence helps distinguish specific arrest from nonspecific effects of adding protein. The essential reasoning is an interaction between two variables: protein occupancy and site orientation.

16. What happens when two replication forks finally meet

Fork convergence is a geometrical event, whereas completion is a collection of chemical and physical events. As the distance between the forks decreases, the amount of unreplicated duplex ahead becomes very small. Topological stress may be particularly important when little space remains for rearrangement of the parental DNA. The replication machines must copy or otherwise resolve the remaining sequence, remove any primer material and seal residual discontinuities. The associated helicases, polymerases and clamps must ultimately be disengaged or recycled. At the same time any interlinks between daughter chromosomes must be removed. These tasks may overlap and are not required to occur in one rigid sequence at a single instant.

A final leading-strand region can approach DNA that has already been synthesized by the opposing fork. A lagging region can still contain newly generated primer-associated junctions close to the point of convergence. The two machines therefore cannot simply keep copying indefinitely as though the neighboring sequence did not exist. Intermediates depend on the relative positions of synthesis and unwinding activities and on whether the approaching forks are active or paused. It is reasonable at NET level to recognize that converging DNA ends and protein complexes must be processed; it is not justified to claim that the entire process is performed by Tus, Pol I or a single dedicated termination enzyme.

Experiments on fork convergence have shown that some conditions can generate incompletely replicated or excess DNA intermediates. For example, a replication barrier that remains strongly occupied may interfere with copying immediately around the arrest point in a particular reconstituted reaction. Such results identify a problem that cellular completion mechanisms need to solve, but they do not establish that every normal chromosome retains a gap at every Ter site. A biochemical system containing selected proteins can reveal requirements under its defined conditions, while a whole cell can also possess repair, restart and DNA-processing activities not included in the reaction. Students should separate a demonstrated intermediate from the broader model inferred from it.

Another complication is the difference between a stalled fork and a collapsed fork. A stalled fork has stopped advancing or is progressing very slowly, but may retain a structure from which synthesis can resume. A collapsed fork has lost essential functional continuity of the replication apparatus or its template arrangement and may require additional processing before restart. A simple pause at a polar barrier does not by itself prove that the fork has produced a free double-strand break. Conversely, irreversible template damage can be more serious than a transient reversible pause. Detailed restart and recombination pathways belong to later nodes; their relevance here is that fork arrest and chromosome completion are distinct outcomes.

Physical chromosome separation also requires distinguishing catenanes from chromosome dimers. Two catenated chromosomes are separate covalently continuous duplex circles that are interlinked. A chromosome dimer is one enlarged covalent circular DNA molecule formed, for example, through an odd number of homologous recombination crossovers between sister circles. Removing a topological link between two circles cannot split one covalently continuous dimer into two separate circles. In E. coli the XerCD site-specific recombination system at dif can resolve chromosome dimers. The molecular chemistry of site-specific recombination is assigned to Node 3.A.12. The connection in this chapter is that a successful DNA-amount measurement does not establish whether the cell has two physically separable chromosomes.

A useful experimental prediction follows from these distinctions. If a mutant accumulates approximately twice the original DNA amount, contains extensive daughter-length DNA but fails to separate sister chromosome loci, there may be a late completion or segregation defect rather than a complete block of synthesis. If purified DNA reveals linked duplex circles, topological processing becomes a strong candidate. If it instead reveals a single covalently continuous DNA molecule twice the normal chromosome length, a dimer-resolution problem becomes plausible. These conclusions require suitable structural assays; microscopy showing two fluorescent foci close together cannot by itself determine the covalent connectivity of the underlying molecules.

17. Quantitative reasoning from fork motion to genome completion

A basic kinematic model is useful for connecting the scale of the E. coli chromosome to the efficiency of its replisome. Let G denote the chromosome size in base pairs, and let v1 and v2 denote the speeds of the two forks in base pairs per second. During a small interval dt, the forks copy approximately v1 dt and v2 dt base pairs of previously unreplicated chromosome, respectively. Assuming that the forks do not copy overlapping regions before convergence, the remaining unreplicated length decreases at the combined rate v1 + v2. Starting with G base pairs, the remaining length after time t is G minus (v1 + v2)t. Setting this remaining length equal to zero gives the ideal completion time T = G/(v1 + v2). The formula describes the interval of elongation under the stated assumptions and does not include additional delays for initiation or final maturation.

For a hypothetical 4.6-million-base-pair chromosome and two equally fast forks moving at 1,000 base pairs per second, the total rate of covering previously unreplicated DNA is 2,000 base pairs per second. Division gives 2,300 seconds, or approximately 38.3 minutes. Each fork travels 2.3 million base pairs. If both forks instead move at 800 base pairs per second, the predicted interval is 2,875 seconds, or approximately 47.9 minutes. Both examples are biologically reasonable illustrations of the order of magnitude of bacterial chromosome replication; neither represents a fixed universal replication time. Temperature, growth conditions, strain, sequence and measurement method can affect the observed rate.

A base-pair coverage rate is not the same as the number of nucleotide incorporation reactions. Each fork copies both parental strands. Advancing by one base pair in the original chromosome creates one new nucleotide on each of two daughter strands, after primer processing is complete. Thus a total genome coverage rate of 2,000 base pairs per second corresponds to a net production rate of approximately 4,000 DNA nucleotides per second across both forks. Replication of G base pairs requires a net increase of 2G DNA nucleotides. The total biochemical incorporation count can be larger because some newly added nucleotides are excised during proofreading or processing, but the final DNA mass has increased by the amount specified. The RNA primers introduce additional temporary ribonucleotide incorporation not counted in the final DNA increase.

Unequal fork speeds change the meeting position as well as the time. With G equal to 4.6 million base pairs, v1 equal to 1,000 and v2 equal to 500 base pairs per second, unrestricted convergence would occur after about 3,066.7 seconds, or 51.1 minutes. The faster fork would cover approximately 3.067 million base pairs and the slower one approximately 1.533 million. This assumes the faster fork is not arrested by a polar termination barrier or another obstacle. If the fast fork instead stops exactly 2.3 million base pairs from the origin and waits, the slower fork must traverse its remaining 2.3 million base pairs at 500 per second. Under this deliberately simplified barrier model, completion takes 4,600 seconds, or 76.7 minutes. This example shows why an observed termination location can reflect both geometric constraints and timing rather than speed alone.

The number of lagging-strand fragments can be estimated independently of the elongation duration. Across the two forks, the total length of lagging regions in one round of chromosome replication is approximately G nucleotides: one lagging daughter position is created for each base pair of parental DNA encountered. If the mean fragment length is L nucleotides, the expected approximate number of fragments is G/L. With G equal to 4.6 million and L equal to 1,500 nucleotides, the estimate is about 3,067 fragments across both forks. It would be incorrect to use 2G/L because the other half of the total newly synthesized DNA corresponds to leading regions. This estimate treats fragment lengths as a mean and ignores boundary effects, initial leading primers and any unusual repriming events.

The frequency of lagging primer formation can also be related to fork speed. For one fork moving at 750 base pairs per second and an average fragment size of 1,500 nucleotides, an idealized interval between effective priming events is L/v = 2 seconds. If the fork continues at the same speed while the mean interval increases to three seconds, the simple prediction is an average fragment size of 2,250 nucleotides. This inference assumes that primers are used in order and that the relevant measure of speed represents exposure of new template. If some primers are skipped or multiple lagging polymerases work with overlapping intervals, observed fragment lengths need not be given exactly by the simple equation. A student should state that limitation rather than treat deviations as proof that the directional mechanism is false.

Suppose every fragment in the preceding estimate begins with an average of twelve RNA nucleotides. The approximate total temporary RNA incorporated into lagging primers is 3,067 times twelve, or 36,804 ribonucleotides. These occupy about 0.8 percent of the lagging regions, since twelve divided by 1,500 equals 0.008. Despite this relatively small fraction of positions, each primer introduces an additional processing junction. The importance of primer replacement is therefore not determined solely by the percentage of DNA initially occupied by RNA. Thousands of discontinuities must be resolved before the daughter strands are covalently continuous.

The rate of maturation can be compared with fragment production through a simple backlog model. If two forks together create 1.2 fragments per second and the effective combined processing machinery matures 1.0 fragment per second, the number of unprocessed fragments increases by 0.2 per second during the interval when both rates remain constant. After one minute the expected increase is twelve. The model uses a difference of rates and assumes no initial backlog, no loss and no change in processing capacity. In an actual cell the increasing substrate concentration may stimulate processing or alter fork behavior, so the linear prediction is only an initial approximation. The model explains why a modest deficit of a repeatedly used enzyme can accumulate into a substantial problem without completely eliminating nucleotide incorporation.

The physical length of the chromosome also gives perspective. B-DNA has an approximate axial rise of 0.34 nanometers per base pair. A 4.6-million-base-pair chromosome would have a contour length of about 1.564 million nanometers, or 1.564 millimeters, if extended along its contour. This is far longer than the micrometer-scale cell. Replication therefore takes place in a compact and dynamically organized nucleoid, not along a stretched DNA thread. The calculation does not mean the chromosome has a straight end-to-end length of 1.564 millimeters in vivo. Contour length and spatial dimensions are different quantities, and the topological challenges described earlier arise within the organized DNA of the cell.

18. Physiological context, comparative interpretation and diagnostic experiments

DNA replication time is not identical to bacterial generation time. A population can grow under conditions in which new rounds of replication initiate before a previous round has finished. This overlapping arrangement allows descendants to inherit chromosomes whose replication was initiated in an earlier generation. It does not require the polymerase to copy an entire chromosome within every short generation interval from a single newly activated origin. The difference between the replication period and the population doubling time connects the molecular mechanism in this chapter to microbial growth and cell division without repeating the complete cell-cycle regulation mechanisms of Unit 2.

An approach to observing replication in a population is marker-frequency analysis. A genomic locus near an active origin is typically copied before a locus near the terminus. In a population with ongoing replication, origin-proximal sequences may therefore be present in more copies on average than terminus-proximal sequences. A plot of normalized genomic coverage across the chromosome can reveal a broad origin-to-terminus gradient. The shape depends on initiation timing, fork speed, growth state and the population's distribution of replication stages. Coverage at one locus is not a direct stopwatch for one individual fork. In a nonreplicating population of complete chromosomes, the copy numbers of unique chromosomal loci should be more nearly equal if technical biases are controlled.

A slow fork on one replichore may generate an asymmetry in such a profile, but the exact form depends on how cells with different stages contribute to the sampled population. Repeated DNA sequences, uneven sequencing coverage and variable numbers of extrachromosomal copies can distort interpretation. Appropriate normalization, comparison to a complete-chromosome control and knowledge of the genome map are therefore important. If the scientific question is whether a mutation decreases fork speed rather than changing initiation frequency, complementary time-resolved measurements are needed. A high origin-to-terminus ratio can arise through more frequent initiation, slower progression or a combination of effects.

Pulse-labeling experiments can be designed to ask whether a block lies in extension or maturation. Compare a wild-type preparation with two hypothetical mutant preparations using equal observation intervals. In the first mutant the pulse incorporation is ten percent of the wild-type value, while the fraction of the label that eventually reaches long DNA is similar. This result favors an upstream deficiency in the production of new DNA, although uptake and nucleotide metabolism must also be considered. In the second mutant initial incorporation is ninety percent of wild type but only twenty percent of its labeled DNA appears in long covalent chains after a chase. That pattern favors a joining or processing problem. Determining whether RNA remains in the DNA and whether functional ligase alone can rescue the preparation can help distinguish Pol I-related processing from a ligase-related sealing defect.

Topoisomerase perturbations require similar precision. Quinolone antibiotics can stabilize cleavage complexes of bacterial type II topoisomerases, including gyrase and topoisomerase IV. Trapping such a complex can impede replication and generate DNA damage; this differs from a pure catalytic inhibitor that prevents a required ATPase transition without necessarily stabilizing a DNA break. The principal drug target can vary among bacterial species and compounds. In E. coli, gyrase is often a major target, but it would be incorrect to infer from the presence of a topoisomerase IV homolog that every drug in the class has exactly the same target preference. The molecular distinction is important in antimicrobial mechanism questions, not a basis for making clinical treatment decisions.

Bacterial enzymes have also provided valuable tools for studying DNA. The Klenow fragment demonstrates how a subset of catalytic activities can be isolated from an enzyme and used in DNA end-filling or labeling applications. DNA ligases illustrate the controlled formation of backbone bonds between suitably prepared substrates. Topoisomerases demonstrate that DNA topology can be experimentally altered without changing the nucleotide sequence. These applications follow directly from the enzymatic operations studied here. The practical methods, reagent choices and detailed biotechnology workflows belong to the corresponding Methods and Biotechnology units.

Finally, the E. coli replisome is a representative system, not a complete inventory of bacterial diversity. Bacillus subtilis uses a different distribution of replicative polymerase functions involving PolC and DnaE, rather than an exact duplication of the E. coli DnaE-centered arrangement. Yet the need for primer formation, directional synthesis, processive extension, primer maturation and topological management remains. Eukaryotic chromosomes introduce additional problems because they are organized around multiple origins, packaged into chromatin and often linear. The next node develops those solutions. The productive way to compare these organisms is to identify the conserved chemical problem first and then assign the distinct molecular components that solve it in each system.

  4. Nucleotide selection
Answer and explanation

A. The observed lesion is an inter-duplex topological link. Topo IV is the major E. coli enzyme responsible; extra polymerization cannot remove a catenane.

33. Part C · Analytical

In a simplified domain 2,100 bp are unwound with a 10.5 bp/turn repeat and no rotation. Estimate the number of type II relaxation steps, each removing two turns.

  1. 50
  2. 100
  3. 200
  4. 2,100
Answer and explanation

B. 2,100/10.5 = 200 turns; 200/2 = 100 net events. This is a model workload, not a direct count of enzyme molecules.

34. Part C · Analytical

A closed 1,050 bp plasmid has a relaxed reference Lk of 100 and actual Lk of 94. What is its superhelical density?

  1. +0.06
  2. −6
  3. −0.06
  4. +6
Answer and explanation

C. σ = (Lk−Lk0)/Lk0 = (94−100)/100 = −0.06. The negative sign indicates underlinking relative to the reference.

35. Part C · Analytical

Two forks together produce 1.8 fragments/s while processing removes 1.3 fragments/s. Starting at zero, what backlog accumulates after 40 seconds if rates are constant?

  1. 52
  2. 72
  3. 124
  4. 20
Answer and explanation

D. The net rate is 0.5 fragments/s, hence 20 fragments. The other values correspond to cumulative production, removal or sums rather than backlog.

36. Part C · Analytical

A 4 Mb chromosome is replicated once. How many net DNA nucleotide residues are added to all daughters combined?

  1. 8 million
  2. 2 million
  3. 4 million
  4. 16 million
Answer and explanation

A. Each of the two original template strands is 4 million nucleotides long. Net new DNA is 2G = 8 million nucleotides, excluding excised material.

37. Part C · Analytical

In an orientation assay a fork passes 95% of sites without Tus. With Tus, passage is 90% in orientation X and 5% in orientation Y. Which inference is strongest?

  1. Ter is an unconditional barrier
  2. Tus-dependent arrest is polar in this assay
  3. Tus is a DNA polymerase
  4. All bacterial chromosomes require Tus
Answer and explanation

B. The strong interaction between Tus presence and orientation supports polarity. It does not establish universal necessity or catalytic identity.

38. Part C · Analytical

After Tus deletion cells complete chromosome replication under standard conditions. What conclusion is warranted?

  1. Fork convergence is impossible
  2. Ter sequences must be copied as RNA
  3. Tus is not strictly required for completion under those conditions
  4. Topo IV is dispensable
Answer and explanation

C. Forks can meet without a dedicated trap. This result does not exclude conditional roles for Tus, nor does it establish that other completion enzymes are unnecessary.

39. Part C · Analytical

A mutant has normal bulk DNA incorporation and increased RNA-DNA hybrid material at fragment junctions. Ligase alone gives poor rescue. What is the most useful next test?

  1. Measure only colony color
  2. Add additional Tus
  3. Assume all polymerases have stopped
  4. Assay Pol I processing and test rescue with Pol I plus dNTPs and ligase
Answer and explanation

D. The intermediate points to maturation rather than initial extension. Sequential enzyme rescue can test whether primer removal and replacement precede sealing.

40. Part C · Analytical

A mutant exhibits elevated origin/terminus marker ratio. Which conclusion is valid without additional measurements?

  1. The ratio alone does not uniquely distinguish increased initiation from slow progression
  2. Fork speed is definitely doubled
  3. Every cell contains exactly two forks
  4. Pol I has been deleted
Answer and explanation

A. Both initiation frequency and replication time influence marker abundance in a population; an independent progression measurement is needed.

41. Part C · Analytical

An exonuclease-defective Pol III core extends a primer almost normally but misincorporations survive more often. Which prediction best follows?

  1. No new phosphodiester bond can form
  2. Reduced proofreading fidelity despite substantial DNA synthesis
  3. No clamp can load
  4. Tus polarity reverses
Answer and explanation

B. Epsilon editing and alpha catalysis are distinct. Downstream mismatch repair can partly compensate, so the final mutation frequency is not determined solely by this assay.

42. Part C · Analytical

A hypothetical inhibitor stabilizes a gyrase-DNA cleavage complex rather than merely blocking ATP turnover. What additional outcome is especially plausible?

  1. Direct RNA primer synthesis
  2. More complete decatenation
  3. DNA break-associated damage when forks encounter trapped complexes
  4. Loss of base complementarity in all DNA
Answer and explanation

C. A cleavage-complex poison retains DNA in a broken intermediate. A simple catalytic inhibitor can also stop replication but need not stabilize such breaks.

43. Assertion–Reason

Assertion: A nick may be present in a duplex with all base pairs intact. Reason: A nick is a missing covalent bond between adjacent nucleotides on one strand.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Base pairing and backbone continuity are distinct. A nick can remove a phosphodiester bond without removing a nucleotide or its base pair.

44. Assertion–Reason

Assertion: Both DNA strands are synthesized 5′ to 3′. Reason: Lagging synthesis requires repeated priming because its template orientation is opposite to fork movement for polymerization.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

B. Both are true, but repeated priming is a solution to the pre-existing chemical constraint; it is not the reason polymerases synthesize 5′ to 3′.

45. Assertion–Reason

Assertion: Tus is obligatory for all circular bacterial chromosome termination. Reason: Tus–Ter can arrest E. coli forks in an orientation-dependent manner.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Tus–Ter polarity is established in E. coli. The universal and obligatory claim is false; other bacteria and Tus-deficient E. coli can complete replication.

46. Assertion–Reason

Assertion: Klenow can extend a correctly primed DNA template. Reason: Ordinary Klenow retains the polymerase domain of Pol I.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. The C-terminal fragment retains polymerization; it lacks the N-terminal 5′ nuclease.

47. Assertion–Reason

Assertion: Topo IV can separate linked daughter duplex circles. Reason: A type II topoisomerase can pass a duplex through a transient double-strand gate.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Duplex strand passage changes inter-circle topology while allowing DNA resealing.

48. Multiple statements

Select the correct statements: I. DnaG makes RNA primers. II. Beta clamp is the main nuclease. III. Pol III alpha polymerizes. IV. LigA fills nucleotide gaps.

  1. II and IV
  2. I, II and III
  3. I and III
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

C. I and III correctly assign functions. Beta is a sliding tether rather than nuclease, and LigA joins adjacent termini rather than filling gaps.

49. Multiple statements

Select the correct statements: I. Leading and lagging roles depend on fork direction. II. Both daughter strands grow at 3′ ends. III. DnaB normally tracks the lagging template. IV. Pol III has the same 5′ nuclease as Pol I.

  1. II and IV
  2. I and IV
  3. All four
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

D. The first three describe fork geometry and chemistry. IV wrongly assigns Pol I's characteristic downstream nuclease to Pol III.

50. Multiple statements

Which outcomes can occur even if much DNA has already been synthesized? I. Retained RNA primers. II. Unsealed DNA nicks. III. Catenated daughters. IV. Failure to have any new DNA at all.

  1. I and II only
  2. III and IV
  3. I, II and III
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

C. Maturation and topology are distinct from total synthesis. IV contradicts the premise of substantial new DNA.

51. Matching

Match P DnaG, Q DnaN, R LigA, S ParC/ParE with 1 clamp, 2 priming, 3 decatenation, 4 sealing.

  1. P1 Q2 R3 S4
  2. P4 Q3 R1 S2
  3. P3 Q4 R2 S1
  4. P2 Q1 R4 S3
Answer and explanation

D. DnaG creates RNA primers, DnaN is the beta clamp, LigA seals nicks and ParC/ParE make Topo IV.

52. Matching

Match P Pol I, Q Pol III ε, R gyrase, S Tus with 1 negative supercoiling, 2 downstream primer processing, 3 polar barrier, 4 terminal proofreading.

  1. P4 Q2 R3 S1
  2. P2 Q4 R1 S3
  3. P1 Q3 R2 S4
  4. P3 Q1 R4 S2
Answer and explanation

B. Pol I processes downstream nucleic acid, epsilon edits a 3′ terminus, gyrase controls supercoiling and Tus binds Ter.

53. Part C · Analytical

A chromosome has 3,000 fragments with mean 12-nt primers. If each primer is fully replaced and no extra DNA is removed, how many dNTPs are incorporated specifically in replacement?

  1. 3,000
  2. 36,000
  3. 12,000
  4. 72,000
Answer and explanation

B. Each RNA position requires a DNA residue, 3,000 × 12 = 36,000. Actual processing can replace additional downstream DNA, which is excluded by the premise.

54. Part C · Analytical

Two complete chromosome copies are covalently joined into one doubled-length circle. Which operation directly resolves this structure in E. coli?

  1. Decatenation of two independent circles alone
  2. Adding a beta clamp
  3. XerCD-mediated site-specific dimer resolution
  4. Repeated primer formation
Answer and explanation

C. The product is a covalent dimer, not two linked rings. Site-specific recombination can resolve it; Topo IV strand passage alone does not split a covalent dimer.

55. Part C · Analytical

In a trap experiment, a clamp-free core adds 40 nt/s for 0.25 s per association; a clamp-bound core adds 40 nt/s for 25 s. What changes under this model?

  1. Rate increases 100-fold
  2. Rate falls 100-fold
  3. Both complexes have the same processivity
  4. Processivity increases from 10 to 1,000 nt while rate stays constant
Answer and explanation

D. Multiplying the bound lifetime by the instantaneous rate gives the expected additions per binding. The clamp increases retention, not the stipulated catalytic speed.

56. Part C · Analytical

A sample of 1,000 correctly formed DNA-only nicks is sealed through fully regenerated LigA cycles. Ignoring initially adenylated enzyme, how many NAD+ molecules are consumed?

  1. 1,000
  2. 500
  3. 2,000
  4. Zero
Answer and explanation

A. One NAD+ supplies the adenylation intermediate for each complete successful ligation cycle. The number would differ if initially activated enzyme were counted separately.

57. Part C · Analytical

A new strand has an upstream 3′-OH and adjacent downstream 5′-OH rather than 5′ phosphate. Adding LigA does not seal it. Which inference is appropriate?

  1. LigA is necessarily inactive
  2. The end chemistry is unsuitable; phosphorylation must precede ordinary nick ligation
  3. The duplex cannot contain complementary bases
  4. Pol III must add a nucleotide at the 5′ end
Answer and explanation

B. LigA needs an appropriate 5′ phosphate to form DNA-adenylate. A failed reaction using an unsuitable substrate does not establish enzyme inactivity.
