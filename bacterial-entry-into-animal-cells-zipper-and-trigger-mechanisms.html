1. Why an animal cell can engulf a bacterium

A bacterium at the surface of an intestinal epithelium is outside the host cell even when the bacterium is already inside the animal. This distinction is essential. An epithelium is a sheet of cells joined to form a selective boundary between two environments. Many of its normal functions depend on restricting the passage of large particles. Small nutrients may be carried across by membrane transport proteins, and certain extracellular molecules may enter vesicles, but a bacterial cell cannot simply pass through a nutrient transporter. Its physical dimensions exceed those of the channels and carriers that handle individual ions and small molecules. If an intact bacterium enters an epithelial cell, another cellular process must account for the movement of the much larger object. The relevant process is a reorganization of the host membrane around the particle, rather than ordinary transport through the membrane.

A useful starting point is to distinguish three questions about the location of a bacterium. First, is the bacterium associated with the host tissue or free in the surrounding environment? Second, is it attached to the outer surface of a host cell or enclosed by a host membrane? Third, if it is enclosed, does it remain in a membrane-bounded compartment or reach the host cytosol? These questions describe different physical boundaries. Attachment is a change in association, internalization is a change in membrane enclosure, and access to the cytosol is a change in compartment. The three changes need not occur together. A bacterium held tightly against a cell may remain extracellular, and a bacterium surrounded by a complete intracellular membrane may never enter the cytosol. Confusing these states obscures both the biology and the interpretation of experimental observations.

The plasma membrane is a lipid bilayer with a nonpolar interior. Its stability depends on the collective organization of amphipathic lipids in water. Membrane proteins provide selective communication and transport functions, while connections to the underlying cytoskeleton influence the shape and mechanical behavior of the membrane. A bilayer can bend without breaking, as happens during ordinary vesicle formation. Because bending the membrane can enclose a portion of extracellular material, the cell can bring large cargo into its interior without requiring that the cargo cross the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer. The membrane surface that originally faced the external medium becomes the luminal face of the internal compartment. This topological principle will remain true irrespective of which receptor or signaling pathway initiated engulfment.

Internalization therefore does not automatically require a permanent hole in the plasma membrane. An external particle may become partly surrounded by a membrane cup, the rim of the cup may advance, and closure can separate the resulting compartment from the external medium. The immediate product is an intracellular membrane-bounded compartment. Its subsequent behavior is governed by the host trafficking machinery and the biological properties of the cargo. If the cargo is an ordinary nutrient or an inert particle, the compartment may enter a degradative pathway. If the cargo is a microorganism, the outcome can include destruction of the microorganism, continued residence or another change in location. These are alternative outcomes, not necessary components of the definition of entry.

In a professional phagocyte, uptake of relatively large particles is an established cellular function. Macrophages and neutrophils use receptor-dependent responses to capture and remove microorganisms and cellular debris. A nonprofessional phagocyte, such as an epithelial cell, has a different normal repertoire of functions. It may be quite capable of endocytosis and dynamic changes in its surface, yet it is not specialized for routinely engulfing intact microorganisms. The important observation in bacterial cell invasion is that the second type of cell can sometimes display a particle-enclosing response. This is not evidence that the cell has permanently changed into a macrophage. It demonstrates that the same fundamental materials of the eukaryotic cell, particularly membrane and actin, can participate in an unusual response.

The words zipper and trigger describe two classical models of induced bacterial entry. In the zipper model, an interaction between a bacterial surface ligand and a host surface receptor couples recognition to a relatively localized engulfment response. In the trigger model, the initiating influence is delivered into the host cell by the bacterium and alters signaling associated with membrane shape. Although the resulting morphologies often differ, both models depend on host cellular capabilities. Both are best understood as examples of biological communication with an abnormal source or context. They are not different laws of membrane physics. They are different ways of engaging the host's own membrane-remodeling system.

The term invasion sometimes refers broadly to penetration of a tissue, while in a cell-biological description it may refer specifically to internalization. A useful reading habit is to identify the boundary under discussion whenever the term appears. A bacterium between two epithelial cells has passed neither of their plasma membranes simply because it has moved deeper into a tissue. An internalized bacterium, conversely, may never spread beyond the first cell. A chapter focused on bacterial entry must establish the membrane event before discussing the broader consequences of infection. The next chapter will consider how bacteria alter host-cell functions after reaching a tissue or intracellular compartment.

2. Cell-surface recognition and the logic of membrane enclosure

Cellular recognition begins with molecular contact. A ligand is a molecule or molecular region that binds a particular partner. A receptor is a cellular molecule that recognizes a ligand and may transmit information or change cellular behavior. The words describe roles rather than chemical categories: a protein can be a receptor in one context and a ligand in another interaction. At the interface between a bacterium and an animal cell, a molecule on the bacterial surface can associate with the extracellular portion of a host membrane protein. The interaction may be sufficiently selective that differences in the structure or accessibility of the host protein change how the two surfaces associate. This specificity is conceptually related to other receptor systems studied in cell signaling, even when the normal biological purpose of the receptor has nothing to do with infection.

Binding is a physical event, not by itself an instruction with a guaranteed result. The two partners can be held together by noncovalent interactions, yet remain at the original location. Whether the interaction has a cellular consequence depends on the organization of the receptor and its surrounding molecules. A receptor linked to intracellular signaling or the cytoskeleton can participate in a response that a receptor lacking these connections cannot support. Conversely, a receptor that is capable of signaling under its normal physiological conditions need not generate the same response to every possible ligand. In the context of bacterial entry, attachment and internalization should be regarded as related but separable phenotypes. This distinction is particularly important when a study reports that a bacterium is cell-associated without resolving which side of the membrane it occupies.

Normal receptor-mediated endocytosis often concerns individual macromolecules or much smaller particles. Bacterial engulfment involves a much larger object, so the cell has to coordinate membrane shape over a correspondingly greater distance. The difference is not simply a matter of the size of an endocytic vesicle. An extended region of membrane must be repositioned while the surface maintains its integrity. The membrane is also mechanically coupled to a cortex of actin and associated proteins. As a consequence, the behavior of one receptor cannot always be understood independently of its neighboring receptors or the local mechanical condition of the cortex. The geometry of the cell surface and the distribution of molecules are part of the biological explanation.

The term membrane cup describes an intermediate configuration in which the plasma membrane surrounds only part of an external object. Before closure, the fluid in the cup remains continuous with the extracellular fluid. A complete intracellular compartment has a different topology because its lumen is no longer freely continuous with the environment. A two-dimensional view of a cup can misleadingly suggest a closed vesicle when the actual opening lies outside the plane of the image. The difference between a cup and a sealed compartment is therefore a useful example of why morphology alone must be interpreted with care. A picture of membrane around a particle is not sufficient proof that the entire particle has completed internalization.

A lipid bilayer has both material and geometric constraints. Lipids can move laterally, and membrane domains can change position, but the area of a membrane does not increase indefinitely without additional membrane being supplied or existing folds being unfolded. Cells possess surface folds and membrane reservoirs that can contribute to deformation. The underlying cortex also resists and transmits mechanical forces. When membrane protrusions form, the observed shape reflects the interplay of cytoskeletal force, local membrane properties and cell geometry. A useful general model is that membrane shape is actively controlled rather than that the membrane is a passive bag into which a particle falls. This model explains why viable cells can display organized protrusions in contexts where passive lipid material could not reproduce the same behavior.

A receptor can also have different functions at different locations within a cell. In a polarized epithelium the apical surface faces the lumen, whereas lateral and basal surfaces interact with neighboring cells and the supporting tissue. The molecular composition of these regions differs. A whole-cell measurement of the amount of a receptor therefore does not reveal its accessibility to a particle outside the apical surface. This principle applies broadly to epithelial biology: anatomical orientation affects which molecular interactions are physically possible. It is important to separate the concept of molecular recognition from the geometry of a multicellular tissue. A receptor that exists somewhere in a cell is not necessarily exposed at the surface under consideration.

A host-cell response may be temporary. A membrane protrusion can emerge and recede, a cytoskeletal structure can assemble and disassemble, and a receptor can move to another compartment. A single observation may thus represent an intermediate state rather than an endpoint. The same apparent morphology can also arise during different normal activities of a cell. A membrane ruffle, for example, may accompany migration or extracellular fluid uptake in the absence of bacteria. Observing a ruffle in a bacterial interaction is consistent with remodeling but does not identify the mechanism on its own. The molecular origin of the response, the sequence of cellular events and the final location of the particle must be considered together.

3. Actin dynamics as a general cellular mechanism

The actin cytoskeleton is central to understanding the physical response of the host cell. Actin is a protein that can exist as a soluble globular subunit, called G-actin, or as part of a filament, called F-actin. An actin filament is a polarized polymer whose ends differ in their structural arrangement and kinetic behavior. The growing and shrinking of such filaments allows the cell to reorganize its internal architecture. Beneath the plasma membrane, actin contributes to the cortical network that influences cell shape. In motile cells it also supports protrusions that explore the environment, provide new contacts and participate in particle uptake.

Assembly of actin does not create a rigid permanent framework. The network is continually renewed by addition and removal of subunits and by proteins that control filament organization. In many protrusive structures the assembly of filaments near the membrane contributes to a force directed against it. The growth of a sufficiently organized network can move the membrane outward, while changes in network connectivity and contractility can help reshape it. These events use chemical energy in cellular nucleotide cycles and are influenced by the mechanical load on the network. The membrane does not move merely because actin is present: the spatial organization and dynamics of actin are what matter.

An elementary distinction is that actin polymerization and actin concentration are not the same measurement. A cell with a fixed total quantity of actin can redistribute it from soluble pools into filaments, or shift filament-rich regions from one location to another. The total amount of actin protein can remain unchanged while the shape of the cell changes substantially. A fluorescence image of filamentous actin is primarily a description of the location and relative abundance of the labeled polymer under the conditions represented. It is not a direct measurement of the rate of polymerization, of mechanical force, or of all actin protein. Those quantities require different kinds of evidence. Students who understand this distinction can avoid interpreting a bright actin-rich region as direct proof of a particular biochemical reaction.

Actin-related proteins organize filament assembly, branching, bundling and turnover. The Arp2/3 complex is a well-known example of a host protein complex that supports branched actin networks. Branching allows a collection of filaments to spread over a surface rather than all growing in a single parallel direction. This geometry is useful in broad membrane protrusions. Other actin structures contain more nearly parallel bundles and serve different mechanical functions. The same actin protein can therefore participate in several different architectures. A protrusion is not defined solely by its molecular composition; it also depends on how the polymers are arranged.

Small GTP-binding proteins are another important general component of cellular shape control. Members of the Rho family participate in regulation of actin organization. A GTPase can cycle between nucleotide-bound states with different capacities to interact with downstream partners. This provides a mechanism through which local cellular information can influence an actin structure. GTPases are not motors that physically drag the membrane, and they do not themselves constitute the actin filament. Rather, they participate in regulatory networks connecting molecular recognition to structural responses. In a normal cell these networks are controlled spatially and temporally so that a protrusion forms in an appropriate region and does not persist indefinitely.

The separation of signal, regulator and structural output is a valuable way to analyze cellular mechanisms. A receptor can provide an input, an intracellular signaling network can interpret that input, and an actin network can provide a physical response. Failure at any one of these levels may reduce the final shape change. However, similar final appearances need not imply the same initial event. A cell with impaired receptor recognition and a cell with impaired general membrane remodeling might both show little engulfment while differing in their ability to bind an external particle. This is why examination questions often give more than one observation: the combination of observations allows the affected stage to be distinguished.

Actin also participates in normal phagocytosis, cell migration, cytokinesis and the organization of cell junctions. Bacterial entry illustrates how a general cellular module can be recruited in an unusual context. It would be misleading to describe the presence of actin near a bacterial particle as a structure that is uniquely microbial. The bacterial cell and the surrounding host actin are different structures; the cortical filaments in these examples belong to the host. Moreover, although bacteria themselves possess cytoskeletal proteins of their own, those proteins are not the host actin network under discussion. Keeping the cellular origin of each component clear prevents confusion when interpreting diagrams of the interface.

4. Scientific evidence and the emergence of two classical models

The study of particle uptake began long before the molecular identity of its receptors was known. The concept of phagocytosis established that cells can internalize relatively large objects as an active biological process. Work on animal cells later connected surface movement with the actin cytoskeleton. These developments made it possible to interpret the interaction between an invasive bacterium and an epithelial cell as a problem in cell biology rather than solely a problem in microbiology. A scientist could ask whether the host membrane was rearranged, whether the surrounding actin was reorganized and whether a specific recognition event preceded the changes.

Electron microscopy was particularly useful because it could resolve relationships between the outline of a bacterium and the surrounding host membrane. Classical studies found that not all entering bacteria were associated with the same surface morphology. Some appeared to be enclosed by a membrane that closely followed the contour of the bacterial body. Others were associated with much larger, irregular membrane protrusions. The names zipper and trigger reflect these contrasting observations and their later mechanistic interpretation. The terms should not be taken literally: there is no zipper-shaped molecule pulling the membrane together, and a trigger is not a single universal bacterial protein. They are models that organize a set of observations.

An important advance was reported in 1996 by Mengaud and colleagues, who identified E-cadherin as a receptor for Listeria internalin in epithelial cell entry. E-cadherin was already recognized as an animal cell adhesion molecule, so the finding linked an established host biological function to a new context. The investigators also described the contrast between the closely associated membrane during Listeria uptake and the conspicuous surface remodeling associated with Salmonella. The receptor identification gave the zipper model a molecular basis rather than only a descriptive name. It was possible to connect a particular extracellular interaction with the physical event visible at the membrane.

The importance of Mengaud and colleagues' 1996 work is best appreciated against the two competing explanations of the compact cup. A membrane could simply conform to a particle as a passive material, or the cell could recognize a specific molecular feature and respond through its own machinery. Identification of E-cadherin as the receptor for internalin supported the second interpretation in the Listeria model. This finding establishes a molecular relationship, rather than a general claim that all particles enclosed in narrow cups use cadherins. The independent observation that E-cadherin has a normal junctional role makes the case especially informative: an interaction normally associated with tissue organization also belongs in an account of host–pathogen recognition. The membrane shape and receptor identity are complementary lines of explanation, not interchangeable forms of evidence. See Mengaud et al. (1996) and Cossart and Sansonetti (2004).

Further work brought host signaling and membrane trafficking into the explanation of receptor-dependent entry. A review by Cossart and colleagues described internalin-mediated uptake in terms of the host receptor, endocytic machinery and actin remodeling. This progression illustrates how biological explanations often develop: an initially visible phenomenon is linked to molecular recognition, then to the cellular response, and finally to the broader fate of the internalized compartment. The examination-level model is a condensed account of that progression. Its purpose is not to list every protein reported in the specialist literature but to preserve the causal logic connecting recognition to movement of a membrane.

Studies of Salmonella and related enteroinvasive bacteria provided a contrasting paradigm in which the bacterium delivers proteins into the host cell through a secretion system and the host responds with prominent surface remodeling. A major review by Cossart and Sansonetti in 2004 synthesized the comparison between receptor-driven and effector-driven models. The scientific lesson is that two distinct classes of initial interaction can converge on the same broad structural requirement: a host membrane must enclose the bacterium. Because both mechanisms use host cellular processes, an observation of actin involvement is not a sufficient discriminator between them. The origin and position of the initial signal are more informative.

Classical models are deliberately simplified descriptions of biological systems. A localized cup or an extensive ruffle is characteristic of particular situations, not an immutable morphological law that applies under every imaging condition. Cells differ in polarity, shape, signaling state and mechanical properties. The same underlying process may also appear differently at different moments. A robust classification uses several independent features: the initiating interaction, the relationship of the receptor or delivered factor to the host, the location and scale of remodeling, and the topology of the resulting compartment. These features are the foundation for the detailed comparison developed below.

5. The zipper model: recognition coupled to local engulfment

In ordinary animal-cell communication, the extracellular recognition event is often separated from the intracellular response by the plasma membrane. A membrane receptor can bridge that separation because its extracellular portion recognizes a ligand while its cytoplasmic portion participates, directly or indirectly, in an intracellular network. The zipper model uses this general principle to explain the enclosure of a comparatively large extracellular particle. The bacterium has a surface component that interacts with a host receptor, and the host responds in the region of contact. The bacterial surface and the moving host membrane remain relatively closely apposed. The term zipper emphasizes progressive contact around the particle rather than a mechanism in which an intact bacterium travels through a channel.

It is helpful to distinguish an adhesin from an invasin by considering the biological outcome rather than memorizing two completely separate chemical categories. An adhesin contributes to attachment. An invasin is a bacterial factor involved in host-cell invasion. A particular molecule may participate in both outcomes, so the classifications can overlap. If a molecule contributes only to holding a bacterium at a surface, its presence cannot by itself explain how the host membrane encloses that bacterium. If a surface interaction also engages a cellular response involved in uptake, the same molecular event can contribute to recognition and internalization. This functional distinction explains why a lesson about attachment does not fully explain induced entry even when some of the same molecules appear in both topics.

In the classical zipper picture, contact is organized around the extracellular object. A region of host membrane approaches the bacterial surface and retains relatively close association as the cup develops. The contact is not equivalent to a covalent union between two cells. It is a dynamic interface at which molecular binding, receptor organization, cytoskeletal remodeling and membrane mechanics can all influence the next state. The shape of the engulfing membrane therefore depends on properties of the host and of the external object. A compact cup may remain incomplete for some time; a cell may also contain internalized bacteria at the same time that additional particles are still attached outside. These are expected consequences of a population of events occurring at different stages.

The zipper metaphor is a description of a geometrical pattern, not a molecular motor. At an epithelial junction, adhesive proteins are laterally mobile within the membrane and their organization is influenced by the cortical cytoskeleton. A junction is normally a contact between two host cells; in the classical zipper example, the contact is instead between an external bacterial surface and host membrane. The shared concept is localized molecular association coupled to cell architecture. The bacterial envelope is a separate boundary throughout; the host membrane does not fuse with it. If a diagram depicts the two boundaries as a single line, it conceals an important difference between particle uptake and the membrane fusion mechanisms encountered later in this subunit. The geometry also explains why a narrow extracellular space may remain between the opposing surfaces before enclosure.

A further distinction concerns the position of the initiating information. In this model, the defining recognition event occurs across an extracellular interface. Information is conveyed into the host by the participation of its receptor-associated machinery. This is conceptually different from a model in which bacterial proteins are first delivered into the host cytoplasm and act there. Both systems can involve host receptors somewhere in the broader interaction. The classification does not mean that a trigger-type bacterium never contacts receptors, nor that a zipper-type event is free from intracellular signaling. The classification refers to the characteristic way in which the engulfment response is initiated.

The localized character of the response is also important. Normal cells maintain different biochemical and structural states in different regions of their surface. A receptor-rich contact may be associated with changes near that region without requiring identical changes across the entire cell. Thus a cell can exhibit an actin-rich area surrounding a bacterial particle while its remaining cortex continues to serve other functions. Localization is a general feature of biological signal transduction. If all cellular actin were reorganized in precisely the same manner at once, the cell would lose much of its capacity to maintain distinct surface functions. Understanding compartmentalized signaling helps explain how a small contact can have a distinct morphological consequence.

The mechanical work of enclosure is carried out by host-cell processes. Receptor binding stabilizes particular molecular associations, but a binding energy is not equivalent to an unlimited source of work capable of continuously deforming a living cell. The cellular response depends on pre-existing energy metabolism and regulatory processes. Membrane remodeling also has a cost associated with changing surface shape and interacting with the cortex. The biological event is thus a combination of recognition, signaling and active structure formation. Separating these components is useful because a cell can recognize a particle without completing the mechanical response. A mechanism diagram that contains only arrows from ligand to engulfment hides the very distinction needed to interpret intermediate states.

One can represent the complete entry event as a transition from an extracellular particle to an enclosed one. A bacterium undergoing zipper-like uptake is still external while an open membrane cup surrounds it. After closure, the particle occupies a compartment whose interior was derived from the extracellular side of the original membrane. The fact that the particle remains separated from the cytosol at that moment does not contradict its internalization. Membrane enclosure and subsequent cytosolic localization are distinct processes. This observation becomes particularly useful when comparing bacteria that have different intracellular fates after apparently similar entry events.

The zipper concept is not restricted to a single bacterial species. Yersinia invasin and host beta-one integrins are another classical example discussed in the historical literature. Integrins normally mediate interactions between cells and their surroundings, whereas cadherins contribute to cell-cell adhesion. The comparison reveals a shared principle: a cellular molecule involved in ordinary adhesion can also take part in an atypical host response. It does not mean that integrins and cadherins have identical structures, bind the same ligands or use exactly the same intracellular networks. The model groups the events by the logic of receptor-associated engulfment while preserving differences in the participating molecules. The Academy treatment emphasizes the Listeria example because it connects directly to the previous study of epithelial junctions.

6. Listeria and the normal cellular functions of its receptors

Listeria monocytogenes is a Gram-positive bacterial species used extensively in textbook accounts of intracellular infection. Its envelope is structurally different from the envelope of Gram-negative species such as Salmonella. A Gram-positive bacterium has a cytoplasmic membrane surrounded by a prominent peptidoglycan layer, whereas a typical Gram-negative bacterium has an additional outer membrane. These structural descriptions are useful for recognizing that the two classical entry examples are not two versions of the same bacterial surface architecture. They are distinct biological systems whose host responses can be compared. The Gram reaction by itself, however, does not predict whether an unfamiliar species is invasive or which cellular mechanism it uses.

The term internalin refers to a family of bacterial proteins, not to a single protein present in the host cytosol. In classical descriptions of Listeria entry, internalin A, usually written InlA, is associated with recognition of E-cadherin. Internalin B, abbreviated InlB, is associated with the host receptor Met. These two bacterial proteins and two host receptors must not be interchanged in a mechanism diagram. E-cadherin and Met belong to different molecular classes. Their normal functions provide two distinct starting points for understanding how a host cell responds to information at its surface. The important lesson is that one bacterial species can be associated with more than one host recognition pathway, so a simplified example should not be mistaken for the entire biology of the organism.

E-cadherin is a classical cadherin found in many epithelial tissues. Its normal function includes participation in adhesive contacts between neighboring cells. Its extracellular region consists of cadherin domains whose conformation and function are influenced by calcium ions. Its intracellular region participates in complexes involving proteins associated with the actin cytoskeleton. This organization makes E-cadherin an informative example of how extracellular adhesion and intracellular architecture are connected. It also explains why a description of E-cadherin as merely a passive attachment point is incomplete. In its normal setting the protein is part of a larger, dynamic junctional system that contributes to tissue organization.

An adherens junction is a specialized region of contact between neighboring cells where cadherin-associated adhesion connects to the actin system. The junction is not identical to a tight junction. Tight junctions contribute to the restriction of paracellular passage and to the maintenance of membrane domain organization, whereas adherens junctions are particularly associated with cell-cell adhesion and force transmission. Both are important to the organization of an epithelium, but the presence of one junctional protein does not establish that the entire junction has a particular functional state. When the name E-cadherin appears in a question about bacterial entry, the student should recall its normal role before interpreting its participation in an atypical interaction.

InlA is a bacterial surface protein with a recognition region containing leucine-rich repeats, a structural motif that can provide a surface for protein interactions. The significance of this motif in a textbook account is that a recognition protein has a three-dimensional structure suited to associating with a partner; the sequence or structural motif is not itself a complete explanation of tissue infection. Protein recognition depends on the identities and accessibility of the interacting surfaces, as well as the environment in which those surfaces meet. The discovery of the InlA–E-cadherin relationship thus connected bacterial surface recognition to a well-characterized host protein. It did not imply that every cadherin in every organism has the same relationship with the bacterial molecule.

Met is a receptor tyrosine kinase normally involved in responses to hepatocyte growth factor. Unlike a classical cadherin, it contains an intracellular kinase domain. Receptor tyrosine kinases participate in cellular networks by controlling phosphorylation-dependent associations and signaling. The details of receptor activation were studied in the cell-signaling subunit, and here that background clarifies why Met and E-cadherin should not be depicted as identical proteins with different names. The InlB–Met association represents a distinct receptor context in the broader Listeria entry literature. The two examples illustrate that different host receptor classes can converge on cellular responses involving the membrane and cytoskeleton without having identical physiological roles.

The study of receptor identity has an important consequence for interpreting specificity. A receptor name is an abbreviation for a molecular entity that may vary across species, tissues and cellular states. A scientific conclusion established in a particular cell model must not be transformed into a universal claim merely because the same receptor name is used in another organism. In addition, the amount of receptor measured in a whole tissue does not tell us its location in the individual cells that form the tissue. These issues are familiar from hormone biology, where receptor expression and cellular response are related but not equivalent. In host-pathogen biology, the same reasoning prevents a simplistic equation of receptor presence with disease susceptibility.

E-cadherin and Met belong to different protein families. E-cadherin is an adhesion molecule with an extracellular recognition region and a cytoplasmic association with catenins; Met contains an intracellular kinase domain and is a normal receptor for hepatocyte growth factor. Neither classification changes because a bacterial ligand interacts with the receptor. In an exam, a claim that every bacterial surface receptor is a tyrosine kinase is therefore false, as is a claim that all receptor-associated cellular responses are identical to normal homophilic cadherin adhesion. The distinction connects the chapter with Unit 4.A and 4.B without requiring a second account of either signaling pathway. The historical receptor examples are reviewed in Bonazzi, Lecuit and Cossart and the review of epithelial entry.

At the scale of a tissue, the cell junction is not simply a line drawn between two rectangles. Epithelial cells have several regions of their plasma membrane, cytoskeletal attachments, underlying extracellular matrix and neighboring cells. An adherens junction has a particular spatial location in this organization. As a result, a diagram of a single isolated cell cannot fully represent the relationship between an animal tissue and the molecules located in its cells. Conversely, a diagram of an epithelium does not establish a molecular interaction merely by showing that two objects occupy adjacent positions. Linking molecular biology and tissue architecture requires evidence at both scales. This is one reason why receptor identification is an important contribution to, but not a complete description of, pathogenesis.

The physiological role of E-cadherin also clarifies an apparent paradox about receptor recognition. A cadherin is a normal cellular component, not a molecule produced by the animal solely because bacteria are present. A tissue can express this protein while retaining strong epithelial organization. Conversely, an observation of cadherin in a tissue section does not establish that the particular cells have experienced an infection. Receptor expression, cell architecture and an actual biological outcome are separate observations. This prevents students from treating a receptor-expression map as if it were an infection map. The same reasoning will be important when viral receptors and tissue tropism are considered in 4.D.05.

A useful way to read a cadherin diagram is to follow the membrane topology from the extracellular domain to the cytoplasmic side. The extracellular domain participates in binding; the membrane-spanning region anchors the protein; cytoplasmic proteins connect adhesion to the cortical organization of the host. Catenins are not extracellular ligands and actin is not the membrane-spanning portion of E-cadherin. A figure with these locations reversed would be wrong even if it contained all the correct protein names. This spatial reasoning is more informative than memorizing a list of names, and it is directly transferable to the analysis of integrins, receptor kinases and immune receptors.

The term receptor-mediated uptake encompasses many different physical events. An LDL receptor interacting with its normal cargo illustrates uptake of a molecular complex in a relatively small endocytic vesicle; engulfment of a whole bacterial cell illustrates a much larger object surrounded by host membrane. The analogy is recognition linked to a cellular response, not a claim that both objects occupy vesicles of identical size or require precisely the same membrane machinery. Large-particle uptake is a useful test of whether a learner understands the distinction between membrane-bound receptor activity, membrane curvature and movement of a particle relative to the cytoplasm.

When the normal Met pathway is introduced in a cell-signaling chapter, the biological questions concern growth, motility and tissue organization. In a host–pathogen chapter, the question is instead how an external biological interaction relates to a cellular structural response. Distinguish the molecular class of the receptor, its normal function, and the disease context in which it is discussed. These categories are often collapsed in memory-based answers. The existence of a second receptor class in the Listeria literature is a useful warning against equating the entire zipper category with a single cadherin.

Receptor organization, cortical reorganization and membrane enclosure are observations made at different spatial scales. A molecular diagram can correctly display a transmembrane protein without showing whether a cell has enclosed anything. Conversely, a low-magnification photograph may show altered cell morphology but not identify a particular molecule. A textbook model is the synthesis of these scales. In the examination setting, the correct conclusion must remain at the scale of the observation supplied in the question unless an additional piece of evidence supports a more specific explanation.

7. The trigger model: intracellular signals and membrane ruffling

An animal cell can produce a membrane ruffle during normal processes such as migration and fluid-phase uptake. A ruffle is a transient, often irregular protrusion of the plasma membrane associated with a reorganized underlying cytoskeleton. It may extend over a relatively broad area of the cell surface. Some ruffles recede, while others fold or close in a way that encloses extracellular material. The geometry differs from a membrane cup that closely follows the contour of a single particle, but geometry alone does not reveal the underlying molecular cause. A photograph of a ruffle is not evidence that a bacterial secretion system is operating. A mechanistic conclusion requires independent information about the origin of the cellular response.

In the classical trigger model of bacterial entry, the bacterium delivers proteins that act inside the host cell. These proteins are called effectors because they influence cellular processes. The word effector should be distinguished from the term secretion system. The secretion system refers to the bacterial machinery associated with delivery of proteins; the effector refers to a protein that acts on a target or process. In addition, not every secreted protein is necessarily an effector involved in entry, and not every bacterial factor that affects a host cell is delivered through the same system. The distinction between the carrier of an influence and the molecular agent that exerts it is the same kind of distinction encountered elsewhere in cell biology, for example between a receptor-associated signaling complex and a downstream regulatory protein.

Salmonella enterica is a Gram-negative bacterial species whose infection biology provides a classical example of trigger-type internalization in epithelial cell models. In the textbook account, a Type III secretion system is associated with the delivery of bacterial effectors into the host cytoplasm. These effectors influence host processes linked to actin organization and membrane remodeling. Broad surface ruffles can appear, and a bacterium may become enclosed in a membrane-derived compartment. The name Type III denotes a class of bacterial secretion machinery rather than a numbered stage of host endocytosis. The bacterial machinery should not be confused with the host membrane systems that form the enclosing compartment.

The principle of effector action is different from a simple extracellular ligand binding a host receptor and remaining outside the cytosol. In the trigger model, the characteristic initiating influence on host-cell remodeling includes bacterial material acting on the intracellular side. Nevertheless, the bacterium initially exists outside the host cell. The physical boundary crossed by a delivered bacterial protein is not identical to the membrane event that internalizes the whole bacterial particle. This is an especially important conceptual distinction. Protein delivery and bacterial engulfment describe two different kinds of movement involving different molecular objects. The occurrence of the first does not logically prove that the second has been completed.

The critical topological difference from ordinary surface recognition is that the characteristic trigger signal in the classical description originates in a bacterial protein delivered to the intracellular side. This does not mean the whole bacterium passes through the protein-delivery apparatus. That apparatus is not a pore large enough for a bacterial cell; the enclosing structure is the host membrane itself. In diagram interpretation, distinguish the direction of information transfer from the direction of movement of the particle. A small arrow representing a protein and a large arrow representing engulfment depict different events. Cossart and Sansonetti's 2004 review provides the historical comparison of these enteroinvasive paradigms.

Membrane ruffles can be associated with macropinocytosis, a cellular process in which large membrane structures enclose extracellular fluid. Macropinocytic structures need not contain a bacterium, and the occurrence of fluid uptake is not identical to an infection. The term macropinocytosis-like is therefore useful in descriptions of trigger-type uptake: it expresses a resemblance to a host membrane-remodeling process without assuming that every component and every biological consequence are identical. A ruffle that closes around external material can generate a compartment containing extracellular fluid as well as particles. Its lumen, like that of other endocytic compartments, is initially separated from the cytosol by a host membrane.

The physical role of actin in this response is related to the general cellular mechanics discussed earlier. Changes in the location and architecture of the host actin network can accompany broad surface protrusions. This means that actin enrichment is expected in relevant morphological observations, but it does not establish a unique pathway because actin also participates in other types of uptake. It is useful to visualize a ruffle as a dynamic surface region rather than an independently moving sheet of microbial material. The membrane is still part of the host until enclosure occurs, and the filaments associated with it are host structures. A model that attributes the entire shape change to bacterial propulsion would not account for the observed cellular remodeling.

Several possible states can occur at a ruffling surface. A particle may be external near a protrusion, partly surrounded by membrane, or enclosed in a new intracellular compartment. A ruffle that remains open has not established a sealed compartment, even if the bacterium lies below the highest point of the protruding membrane. The exterior of the host cell cannot be defined simply by drawing a straight line through a micrograph. The membrane itself is the relevant boundary. This geometrical reasoning applies equally to ordinary endocytosis, phagocytosis and bacterial uptake. A careful observer must identify the complete boundary or recognize that the available information does not establish closure.

The two examples make an important connection with general cell signaling. In the zipper case a ligand–receptor interface is the defining starting relationship; in the trigger case the defining input is a delivered microbial factor. A membrane ruffle, however, is not a microbial structure. It is an animal-cell protrusion with normal roles in cell migration and fluid uptake. Thus the presence of a ruffle is a meaningful morphological observation but cannot identify the source of the signal without other information. The classic Francis et al. study connected this host-cell behavior with macropinocytosis, explaining why the term trigger is about altered host behavior rather than bacterial motility.

The extracellular location of the bacterial cell and the intracellular location of its delivered protein are not contradictory. A useful analogy is a receptor that transmits information across a membrane while the original ligand remains outside. In the Type III secretion example a bacterial factor itself is delivered to the host side of the interface, but the much larger bacterial cell is still outside until it is enclosed. A general schematic of the process should therefore draw the microbial envelope, the host plasma membrane and the positions of the two kinds of object separately. The biological significance of the secretion system in this chapter is its role in the origin of the intracellular influence, not the molecular architecture of its individual parts.

Macropinocytosis is distinct from passage through a transmembrane channel because the extracellular fluid is enclosed with a membrane boundary. A molecule in this fluid can therefore occupy a lumen without being mixed with cytosol. A tracer in a membrane-bound region can indicate fluid enclosure in an image, whereas a diffuse cytosolic signal would represent a different spatial distribution. Neither observation on its own identifies the origin of the initiating signal. This distinction explains how a large membrane ruffle can be associated with uptake while leaving the identity of the resulting compartment as a separate cellular question.

Normal macropinocytosis is a useful comparative process because it illustrates large-scale fluid uptake in cells without introducing a new kind of membrane material. It differs from the classic drawing of a small receptor-associated coated vesicle in the size and shape of the membrane structures involved. It also differs from the simplest particle-centered model of phagocytosis, in which the geometry of an individual target is particularly apparent. These distinctions are not absolute boundaries between every cellular protein used in the processes. The terminology emphasizes the characteristic cargo and morphology, whereas the participating molecular components can overlap. The trigger model is described as macropinocytosis-like in this sense.

8. Professional phagocytosis and induced entry into other cells

The term phagocytosis refers to the uptake of large particles by a cell through the formation of a membrane-bound compartment. Among animal cells, macrophages and neutrophils are classic professional phagocytes. Their ability to capture and process particulate material contributes directly to innate defense and tissue maintenance. They encounter not only microorganisms but also cellular debris and other material that must be removed from tissues. Their normal physiology therefore includes a coordinated set of functions associated with particle recognition, membrane enclosure, compartment processing and disposal of the captured material. Uptake is only the beginning of this larger cellular program.

Macrophages can be found in many tissues and perform functions that extend beyond immediate antimicrobial defense. Their contributions include recognition and clearance of dead cells, communication with other immune cells and the removal of particulate material. Neutrophils are another major phagocytic population with important roles in acute inflammatory responses. The two cell types differ in their lifetimes, recruitment patterns and other physiological properties, yet both illustrate why specialized uptake capacity is useful in an animal. The animal body needs a way to handle objects too large to be passed through molecular transport proteins. A professional phagocyte is equipped to perform such cellular work as part of its ordinary function.

Recognition by a professional phagocyte is not limited to one molecular relationship. Some receptors recognize features of particles directly, while others recognize host molecules that have associated with a particle. Opsonization is the coating of a particle with host recognition molecules in a way that can facilitate its uptake by cells bearing appropriate receptors. Antibody and complement components are important examples discussed in the immunology subunit. For the present chapter, the central distinction is between a receptor detecting the particle itself and a receptor detecting a host-associated coat on the particle. Both can contribute to a cellular recognition event without requiring that the captured object provide its own active invasion signal.

Antibodies illustrate the distinction between binding and downstream cellular response particularly well. The antigen-binding region of an antibody recognizes an antigen, while other regions can participate in interactions with immune cells or complement. Thus recognition of a target by an antibody and recognition of that antibody-associated target by a phagocyte can involve different molecular interfaces. The presence of an antibody at a particle surface is not equivalent to the presence of an intracellular bacterial effector. It is an example of host-derived recognition information. The biological intention of the immune response is not a property of a single binding event, but of the organized host system that processes the event.

A professional phagocyte may form a cup around a recognized particle, and the completion of enclosure yields a phagosome. The phagosome is not initially identical to a lysosome. Its membrane and internal contents have a history derived from the uptake event. Its interactions with intracellular trafficking compartments can change its molecular composition and physiological properties. In many normal defensive responses, maturation contributes to degradation or killing of captured material. This interpretation connects the membrane event studied in this chapter to the organelle functions already studied in Unit 2. The cell's ability to internalize a particle is not necessarily a measure of its ability to destroy that particle.

A nonphagocytic epithelial cell has a different set of normal responsibilities. It can selectively absorb molecules, exchange information with neighboring cells, maintain membrane polarity and regulate passage across the tissue. These functions require active membrane trafficking and cytoskeletal control. Consequently, lack of professional phagocytic specialization does not mean lack of membrane remodeling machinery. The machinery is present in a physiological context that ordinarily serves other tasks. The occurrence of an induced uptake event in such a cell illustrates the flexibility of general cell-biological processes. It does not erase the differences between epithelial cells and professional phagocytes.

Professional and nonprofessional uptake are not distinguished by whether the enclosed particle is a bacterium. A professional phagocyte can capture an inert particle or a microorganism, whereas a nonprofessional cell may internalize a particle under particular conditions. The identity of the cargo alone does not establish the identity of the host-cell mechanism. A diagram should therefore identify the host cell type as well as the origin of the recognition event. When a question states that a bacterium is observed within a macrophage, it has not yet established whether the bacterium induced a particular nonphagocyte entry pathway. Conversely, the observation of a bacterial particle inside an epithelial cell raises a different set of questions about how its membrane came to enclose the particle.

The difference between ordinary host-driven uptake and pathogen-induced uptake is also not a simple difference between an active host and a passive host. In each case the host contributes membrane and cytoskeletal machinery. In the normal defensive context, host recognition systems are organized around clearance of material. In the classical induced-entry models, the association of bacterial factors with the host contributes to the occurrence of the engulfment response. The origin of the relevant signal is different even though some of the physical materials used in the response are shared. This is why the presence of actin around a particle does not identify the biological context on its own.

Some cellular uptake events may resemble the morphology of the zipper model even in the absence of the particular bacterial ligands discussed earlier. A phagocyte can form an intimately associated cup around a recognized target. This similarity should not be interpreted as evidence that every phagocytic event is mediated by Listeria internalin. The membrane configuration reflects an underlying geometrical problem common to many systems: enclosing a particle while retaining the integrity of the cell surface. The particular molecular relationships are determined by the host and cargo involved. A comparison based on membrane geometry should therefore be accompanied by a comparison of recognition context and physiological function.

A final important distinction is between the fate of the phagocyte and the fate of the captured microorganism. A normal host cell can survive while processing captured material, but cellular stress or injury may occur in other contexts. The observation of uptake does not establish either eventual host survival or microbial survival. In a scientific description, the time at which each outcome was evaluated matters. Initial entry, subsequent localization, degradation and cell viability are separate variables. This reasoning will be developed further in the next chapter when we consider the alteration of host-cell behavior after microbial contact.

9. The immediate intracellular fate: vacuoles and cytosol

The cytosol is the aqueous portion of the cell interior outside membrane-bounded organelles. It contains soluble proteins, metabolic intermediates, ions and many macromolecular assemblies. A membrane-bounded organelle has an internal space that is physically and chemically distinguishable from the cytosol. The endoplasmic reticulum, lysosomes and endocytic compartments provide familiar examples of this principle. A membrane-bounded bacterial particle is physically within the outline of the host cell, but its immediate molecular environment is the lumen of the compartment. The phrase intracellular bacterium therefore does not specify whether the bacterium is in the cytosol or separated from it by a host-derived membrane.

The lumen of a newly formed endocytic compartment is related topologically to the extracellular environment. Its contents were enclosed during the movement of the membrane. This does not mean that the lumen continues to have the same chemical composition indefinitely. The membrane contains proteins that can transport substances, and interactions with other compartments can change both the membrane and the lumen. The chemical environment of an endocytic compartment can consequently become quite different from the extracellular medium. Topological origin and chemical state are distinct concepts. The first concerns membrane orientation and continuity, whereas the second concerns the present composition of the enclosed environment.

The word vacuole can be used broadly for a membrane-bounded compartment. In the present context it describes a compartment that contains a bacterium. It should not be confused with the large central vacuole that has characteristic functions in many plant cells. The word describes an enclosed space rather than identifying one universal organelle. Similarly, a phagosome is named with reference to the uptake process that produced it. These terms can overlap in particular descriptions, but they are not all equivalent to the term lysosome. A careful diagram specifies the stage of the compartment and the processes that are established rather than relying entirely on a generic label.

After enclosure, several different outcomes are possible. In a host defense context a captured microorganism may be degraded. In other contexts a bacterium may be observed within a membrane-bounded compartment for an extended period. Still other bacteria may be found in the host cytosol after an earlier enclosure event. These alternatives concern changes after the completion of membrane uptake. They do not define whether the preceding entry event was zipper-like or trigger-like. To infer the nature of entry from the final location alone would confuse two stages separated in time and governed by partially distinct processes.

Listeria is classically described as a bacterium that can be found in the host cytosol after an earlier vacuolar stage. Salmonella is classically associated with a membrane-bounded Salmonella-containing vacuole in many textbook descriptions. These contrasting examples are useful because they show that bacteria which initially enter within a host-derived membrane can subsequently occupy different environments. The examples are not absolute statements about every bacterium in every cell or time point. A species-level shorthand should not override actual localization evidence when a scientific question supplies more specific observations.

A bacterium that reaches the cytosol does not thereby become a constituent of the host cytoskeleton. It remains a distinct cell bounded by its own envelope. The host cytoplasm contains macromolecules and organelles that belong to the animal cell, while the microorganism has its own structures and genetic material. The spatial relationship between these two cells has changed, but their cellular identities remain distinct. Likewise, when a bacterium is enclosed in a host-derived compartment, its own envelope and the surrounding host membrane are separate barriers. Drawings that depict a single membrane around the entire association may conceal this important distinction.

It is also necessary to distinguish the bacterial envelope from the vacuolar membrane when interpreting an image of internalized material. The bacterial envelope defines the boundary of the bacterial cell. The vacuolar membrane defines the boundary between the compartment lumen and the host cytosol. An electron micrograph may show both as neighboring structures if the resolution and section plane permit. In a schematic, using different line styles or colors for the two boundaries helps avoid the impression that the bacterium itself has become an animal-cell organelle. The fact that a bacterium is close to the vacuolar membrane does not mean the two boundaries have fused.

A membrane-derived compartment can be considered at two scales. At the level of its geometry, it is a region enclosed by a membrane. At the level of its physiology, it participates to varying degrees in cellular trafficking and may contain a distinctive chemical environment. Movement from one compartment to another can change what molecules contact the bacterium and how the host recognizes the event. These considerations explain why intracellular localization is relevant to cell biology and immunology even without a detailed account of bacterial persistence. A change in localization can have physiological implications because the new compartment has different properties.

A common examination trap is to describe an internalized particle as cytosolic solely because it is surrounded on all sides by host cytoplasm in a drawing. In a two-dimensional illustration, almost any internal organelle appears surrounded by cytoplasm. What establishes the distinction is whether an additional host membrane separates the particle from that cytoplasm. A diagram that explicitly shows a continuous host-derived membrane around a bacterium represents a vacuolar bacterium, regardless of its position relative to the nucleus. Conversely, the absence of a membrane in a simplified drawing is not proof that a real membrane has disappeared unless the diagram or accompanying data establish that interpretation.

Entry and intracellular fate are logically independent variables even though particular organisms often show characteristic combinations. Imagine a question that describes a receptor-associated enclosure event followed by a membrane-bounded location. The first observation classifies an aspect of entry, while the second classifies the immediate compartment. Imagine another description in which pronounced surface remodeling accompanies uptake followed later by a change in intracellular position. Neither statement licenses the assumption that one model of entry always leads to one final location. This separation is a useful foundation for the next node, which examines broader changes in host-cell physiology and intracellular niches.

10. Comparing zipper and trigger without false dichotomies

The most useful comparison between biological mechanisms begins by identifying the question the comparison is intended to answer. The zipper and trigger models answer a question about how a bacterium becomes enclosed by an animal cell. They are not comprehensive categories of all bacteria and all infectious diseases. A bacterium may cause disease without entering an animal cell, and a microorganism may be captured by a phagocyte independently of the classical induced-entry models. The comparison becomes meaningful when the setting is an entry event into an animal cell and the evidence concerns the initiation of membrane remodeling.

The classical models can be compared on three independent axes: identity of the initiating influence, spatial extent of the host membrane response, and topology of the final compartment. These axes should not be merged into a single variable. Receptor recognition and the appearance of a tight cup are related in the zipper model, but a tight cup is not unique to that model because ordinary phagocytosis can have a similar geometry. Similarly, macropinocytic ruffles are common features of normal animal cells. In the absence of information about the initiating influence, the most defensible description is morphological rather than molecular.

Consider two schematic textbook descriptions without assigning any experimental conditions. Description A shows a particle associated with an identifiable surface receptor and a compact cup around its outline. Description B shows a broad surface deformation associated with intracellular action of a delivered bacterial factor. A comparison based on initiating influence is possible because the descriptions specify the location and nature of the relevant information. If the descriptions instead merely state that a particle became enclosed, the distinction cannot be drawn. The difference between these two questions is not a lack of biological knowledge. It is a difference in the amount of discriminating evidence provided.

There is also a useful distinction between an incomplete observation and a negative observation. If a description does not mention ruffles, the reader cannot conclude that ruffles were absent. If an image does not label an intracellular factor, its presence or absence has not been established. A negative observation requires an appropriate measurement or statement about what was not detected. An omitted detail is not a negative result. The same reasoning applies to receptors, actin and compartments. In a multiple-statement question, a conclusion may be unjustified not because it is impossible but because the provided evidence does not discriminate among alternatives.

The classical models remain valuable because they compress complex cell biology into two recognizable patterns of explanation. The limitation is that biological systems are not obliged to fit perfectly into categories created for teaching. There may be overlapping or alternative influences in actual cellular interactions, and observations can depend on the stage examined. A student should know the classical examples clearly while resisting the claim that the models are mutually exclusive descriptions of every feature of a bacterial cell. The comparison table below is a guide to characteristic distinctions, not a list of universal prohibitions.

A concise explanatory matrix separates the bacterial and host contributions. The bacterial contribution may be a recognized surface ligand or a delivered signaling influence; the host contribution is its own membrane and cytoskeletal dynamics. At the next scale, the resulting membrane-bound compartment contains an organism with its own envelope. This matrix guards against two common errors: describing bacterial movement as if the bacterial cytoskeleton itself pulled the animal membrane around the cell, and confusing the host-derived vacuole with the bacterial envelope. Both mistakes disappear when the boundaries are drawn separately.

A molecular defect and a cellular structural defect need not produce the same set of observations. Consider a hypothetical host cell that retains an external ligand–receptor association while its cortical organization is disturbed. The presence of binding and the disturbance of the cortex are compatible because the recognition interface and the structural response occur at different levels of organization. A failure to generate a final compartment would not necessarily establish that the original molecular contact failed. Conversely, absence of a specified binding event concerns the beginning of that receptor-dependent branch. This distinction provides a general method for assigning a phenotype to a stage rather than describing all changes with the single word invasion.

A receptor-dependent contact and a wide ruffle also demonstrate why scale is important in cell biology. Cell–cell adhesion is a molecular and tissue phenomenon; ruffling is a cellular mechanical response; formation of a membrane-bounded compartment is a topological event. An answer that identifies one of these events but claims that it proves all three is too strong. A good examiner may supply evidence for one axis while deliberately leaving another unknown. A strong answer identifies exactly what was observed and what remains unestablished.

The comparison also has an evolutionary dimension. Animal cells retain their membrane-remodeling and actin-associated machinery because it supports essential functions such as motility, tissue organization, nutrition and defense. The two classical bacterial examples illustrate distinct forms of interaction with these conserved host capabilities. It is misleading to say that the host developed a particular adhesion molecule for the purpose of allowing bacteria to enter. Host receptors have ordinary physiological functions, and their participation in infection-associated interactions is one context in which the molecular properties of both organisms become relevant. This is why the diversity of host cells and microorganisms must be respected even when using a small number of classical teaching models.

The medical significance of these classical models is the ability to distinguish exposure and entry from the eventual disease outcome. The presence of a bacterial particle within a cell does not establish that it remains viable, and altered cell shape does not establish clinical disease. Ordinary immune phagocytosis can be protective. The next chapter considers bacterial virulence factors and host-cell alteration; the present chapter therefore ends at enclosure and immediate compartment identity, rather than repeating detailed intracellular survival mechanisms.

11. Connecting receptors, membrane lipids and actin regulation

The preceding sections identify actin as a shared structural component of several membrane responses. A more complete understanding requires explaining how a membrane-associated signal and an actin-rich structure are related in an animal cell. The plasma membrane is not composed of a single lipid species. Its different lipids contribute distinct physical and biochemical properties, and some provide binding environments for proteins at the inner membrane surface. Phosphoinositides are phosphorylated derivatives of phosphatidylinositol that participate in membrane-associated cellular regulation. Although they represent a relatively small fraction of the total membrane lipid, their distribution can help specify where a regulatory protein associates with the membrane. Consequently, changing the identity of a membrane domain can involve biochemical changes as well as changes in shape.

A phosphoinositide is not a freely soluble equivalent of a protein hormone. The lipid remains associated with a membrane, and its exposed head group can interact with proteins or participate in enzymatic reactions. Enzymes that add or remove phosphate groups can alter the set of proteins associated with a membrane region. This provides a way for spatial organization to participate in signal transduction. A regulator that recognizes a particular molecular feature of the membrane may be preferentially recruited to a particular region, increasing its local abundance without increasing its total cellular concentration. The important distinction is between production of a signaling molecule, recruitment of a protein and the mechanical change that may follow.

Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, commonly abbreviated PI(4,5)P2, is one example familiar from studies of the plasma membrane. Its involvement in cellular regulation is not limited to the production of soluble second messengers. It also participates in membrane-associated interactions with proteins that influence membrane and cytoskeletal organization. Phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate is another signaling lipid that students may recognize from receptor-associated PI3K signaling. These lipids differ in the positions and number of phosphate groups on their head groups. They should not be treated as alternative spellings of the same molecule. In normal cellular uptake, dynamic membrane identities help coordinate changes in the associated protein machinery.

A cytoskeletal regulatory network may use GTP-binding proteins to connect a signal with changes in actin organization. Rac and Cdc42 are members of the Rho family that students encounter in descriptions of cellular shape and protrusion. Their signaling roles do not mean that every cellular protrusion is exclusively controlled by one GTPase. In general, a nucleotide-bound regulatory protein can associate differently with its downstream partners according to its state, while regulatory proteins influence the duration and distribution of that state. The switch is therefore both biochemical and spatial: the cell can have different local activities at the same time without changing the chemical identity of the entire GTPase population.

Actin itself also participates in nucleotide-associated reactions, but the nucleotide cycle of an actin subunit is not the same as the switch cycle of a small GTPase. Actin binds adenine nucleotide, whereas Rho-family GTPases bind guanine nucleotide. Their roles differ correspondingly. Actin is a structural polymer-forming protein, and the GTPase is a regulator. A diagram with both proteins should not use an identical arrow labeled activation to describe all of their biochemical changes. Activation of a regulatory protein and assembly of a structural polymer are different types of event. Understanding the roles of the molecules helps explain why the energy requirements of a living cytoskeleton cannot be reduced to a single ATP or GTP molecule per complete uptake event.

The Arp2/3 complex is an example of a cellular structure associated with nucleation of branched actin networks. Nucleation refers to the formation of a structure from which a polymer can grow; elongation is the subsequent addition of subunits to an existing polymer. Regulatory proteins can help determine the site and activity of nucleation. This distinction matters because a cell may have abundant actin yet lack a particular organized network. Providing an additional pool of monomers is not logically equivalent to generating filaments in the right orientation at the membrane. At the level of the cell, network geometry and renewal are at least as important as the amount of available structural material.

Other host proteins contribute to the turnover and mechanical organization of actin. Cofilin is a familiar example of a protein associated with actin filament turnover. Myosin family proteins can produce forces through interactions with actin, and some myosins contribute to membrane-related functions. The term myosin does not name a single protein with an identical role in every cell. A contractile network, a branched protrusive network and a parallel bundle differ in organization even when they share actin as a component. In studying a general actin-dependent uptake event, it is therefore appropriate to distinguish protrusion, organization of the cortex and later changes in the shape of the enclosing structure. The relative contributions of individual proteins depend on the cellular context.

Normal membrane trafficking includes the recruitment of proteins to membrane domains, remodeling of membrane shape and the formation of distinct compartments. Clathrin is one example of a protein that participates in many cellular endocytic processes. An important conceptual caution is that participation of a protein known from one endocytic pathway does not automatically establish that every morphological and biochemical feature of that pathway is present. A bacterial particle is much larger than the cargo typically illustrated in a small clathrin-coated vesicle. Scientific literature can describe shared cellular components without asserting that the complete large-particle response is identical to the textbook depiction of a small coated vesicle. This principle applies equally to the interpretation of proteins associated with other cellular trafficking processes.

Some receptor-associated responses involve changes in the chemical modification or localization of the receptor itself. A receptor tyrosine kinase can participate in phosphorylation-dependent signaling, while ubiquitination can have several consequences depending on the target and context. Ubiquitination is often introduced as a signal associated with protein degradation, but that is not its only role. In membrane biology, protein modification can influence interactions and sorting. The broad cell-biological lesson is that posttranslational modifications help the cell regulate the location and state of molecular components. Students should not infer proteasomal destruction from every reference to ubiquitin, just as they should not infer transcriptional activation from every reference to phosphorylation.

The classical bacterial examples fit within this general organization of the host cell. Reviews of Listeria entry describe receptor-associated endocytic and actin-remodeling processes; reviews of Salmonella emphasize effector-associated remodeling of the host surface. These associations make sense because the host membrane already contains regulatory lipids, signaling proteins and a mechanically active cortex. The recognition event is therefore not an isolated molecular binding reaction detached from the host. Nor is membrane shape an inexplicable consequence added at the end of a pathway diagram. It is an output generated by a living system capable of spatially organized biochemical and mechanical responses. The comparison remains understandable without reproducing detailed pathogen-specific factor networks beyond the examination scope.

Regulation is needed during ordinary membrane remodeling because a cellular response must have an appropriate beginning, location and end. Persistent protrusion at every surface would interfere with the organization of the cell. Continual formation of internal compartments without compensating membrane traffic would also affect the cell surface. The restoration of a functional cortex and the redistribution of membrane proteins are therefore important aspects of cellular homeostasis. Feedback, local turnover and compartmentalization are general means by which a cell can coordinate such processes. They are familiar concepts from cell signaling, now viewed through their physical consequences at the plasma membrane.

At the end of this molecular integration, the central relationships can be summarized without confusing different levels of organization. Ligand recognition or intracellular signaling provides information; biochemical regulation changes the behavior or localization of cellular components; the organized actin network contributes to mechanical force and membrane shape; the membrane creates a physical compartment when enclosure is completed. These are four distinguishable levels. In a real cell there are interactions and feedback among them, rather than a perfectly linear chain. The simplified ordering is useful for learning because it shows the kind of causal explanation needed at each level and prevents the structural outcome from being mistaken for the molecular initiating event.

12. Quantitative principles of particle enclosure and interpretation

A useful mathematical starting point is the difference between surface area and volume. A spherical object of radius r has surface area A equal to 4πr² and volume V equal to 4πr³/3. The radius must be expressed in a consistent length unit, such as micrometers. Surface area then has units of square micrometers and volume has units of cubic micrometers. The ratio A/V equals 3/r and has units of inverse length. This simple relationship explains why surface area and enclosed volume do not change in the same proportion when geometrically similar objects differ in size. It is a general principle of cell biology rather than a model for the efficiency of infection.

For a hypothetical inert spherical object of radius 0.5 micrometers, the calculated area is 4π(0.5)², or π square micrometers, approximately 3.14 square micrometers. Its volume is 4π(0.5)³/3, approximately 0.524 cubic micrometers. A geometrically similar object of radius 1 micrometer has an area of approximately 12.57 square micrometers and a volume of approximately 4.19 cubic micrometers. Doubling the radius multiplies the area by four and the volume by eight. These relationships follow from the exponents in the equations. They are not empirical measurements of the membrane requirement of a living cell, since a real compartment may have a different shape and include additional material or membrane folds.

The geometric model has several important limitations. A bacterial cell is not necessarily spherical, and a compartment can contain extracellular fluid as well as a particle. A bilayer also has thickness, and its shape is affected by associated cellular structures. The formula gives the surface area of an ideal sphere, not the total area of membrane that would be redistributed during a complex biological event. Furthermore, area alone does not specify the energetic cost of changing the shape of a cell. A physical description of membrane deformation would require additional information about tension, curvature, molecular interactions and mechanical forces. A student should therefore interpret the example as a scaling relationship, not as a prediction of the success of cellular entry.

A second quantitative principle concerns dimensional consistency. Suppose the radius is expressed as 500 nanometers instead of 0.5 micrometers. The two values represent the same length because one micrometer equals one thousand nanometers. Substitution of 500 into the area equation produces a value in square nanometers, not in square micrometers. The conversion for area is squared: one square micrometer equals one million square nanometers. For volume the conversion factor is cubed. A calculation that converts a length correctly but applies the same linear factor to an area or volume will produce a serious numerical error. Examination questions often use exactly this kind of dimensional change to test whether the student understands an equation.

The same reasoning can be applied to a membrane image. If an ideal circular section through the center of a sphere has a measured diameter of two micrometers, its radius is one micrometer. Its two-dimensional section area is π square micrometers. The total three-dimensional surface area of the sphere is four times that value. The distinction is important because microscopy frequently presents sections or projections of three-dimensional objects. The outline visible in a section is not the complete surface of the compartment. A section away from the center of a sphere will also have a smaller apparent diameter, even when the actual spherical object has not changed size. The inference from image size to physical size therefore depends on the geometry and measurement conditions.

Another set of quantitative distinctions concerns how data are summarized. An observation may count objects, cells or compartments. These are not interchangeable denominators. Suppose a conceptual image collection contains 100 animal cells. Forty cells have at least one clearly visible inert particle enclosed in a compartment. If those forty cells together contain sixty particles, the proportion of cells with at least one enclosed object is 40 percent. The average number of enclosed particles per cell across the entire collection is 0.60. Among only the cells with enclosed particles, the average is 1.50. Each value is mathematically valid, but each answers a different question. A report that simply says uptake was 40 without specifying the units or denominator is incomplete.

A percentage change is also different from a change in percentage points. In a hypothetical cell-biological dataset, suppose the fraction of cells displaying an ordinary membrane protrusion changes from 20 percent to 30 percent. The absolute change is ten percentage points. The relative change compared with the initial value is (30 − 20)/20, or 0.5, corresponding to 50 percent. A statement that the fraction increased by ten percent would be ambiguous unless it specified what was meant. The distinction matters when interpreting bar graphs of cellular behavior, because identical percentage-point differences can correspond to different proportional changes if the starting values are different.

Graphical descriptions can conceal important variation between individual cells. If two sets of cells both show a mean of two inert particles per cell, their distributions need not be the same. In one set every observed cell might contain exactly two particles. In another, half might contain none and half might contain four. The means are identical, but the biological pattern differs: one set is uniform in this simplified example while the second is heterogeneous. A mean alone cannot establish that every cell behaves in the manner represented by that mean. This is why a study of a cell population may report distributions or variability as well as an average.

The same principle applies to a time-dependent observation. A graph that records the proportion of cells displaying a particular membrane morphology at different times does not necessarily track the fate of individual objects. A decrease in the fraction of cells with visible protrusions may reflect the completion of protrusive events, their reversal or a difference in which cells are represented by the observation. It is not equivalent to a direct measure of the rate of polymerization of actin. A time axis provides information about changes in a measured variable, not automatic identification of the mechanism responsible. A graph of cellular states should therefore be read by naming the quantity on the vertical axis and asking what alternative processes could produce its change.

When describing an association between a cellular feature and a measured response, it is helpful to distinguish correlation from causation. A strong association can be consistent with a proposed mechanism, but other explanations remain possible if the two variables are influenced by a third factor. For example, two cellular markers may both become more abundant during differentiation without one directly causing the other. The biological meaning of the association depends on the nature of the markers and additional evidence. Mathematical measures of association cannot replace the need to identify the molecular and structural relationships involved. The quantitative principles used here therefore support interpretation of general cellular evidence rather than providing a numerical model of bacterial invasion.

13. Reading scientific evidence and evaluating alternative interpretations

Scientific evidence for a cellular mechanism is strongest when several observations address different parts of the proposed explanation. A structural image can describe the relationship of a particle to a membrane. Molecular evidence can identify a binding interaction. A cellular observation can reveal a change in the distribution of actin. Compartment-localization evidence can establish whether a particle is separated from the cytosol by an additional membrane. These are complementary rather than redundant observations. Each has limits, and none of them on its own describes every level of the mechanism. A student reading a research summary should identify which particular claim is supported by each type of evidence.

The 1993 study by Francis and colleagues connected Salmonella-associated surface ruffles with macropinocytic uptake. It is a useful historical landmark because it addressed the relationship between conspicuous membrane morphology and a more familiar cellular process. The ruffles were not simply decorative morphological features of the host surface. They were associated with changes in the way extracellular material could be enclosed. In a later historical comparison, the receptor-identification study by Mengaud and colleagues provided evidence for a distinct Listeria-associated entry model. Together, the two bodies of work helped establish the contrast between broad surface remodeling and localized receptor-associated engulfment. The studies are best remembered for their conceptual discoveries, not as instructions for conducting infection experiments.

When a microscopy figure contains a bright region labeled as actin, the first task is to establish the meaning of the label. It may represent a probe associated with filamentous actin, a fluorescently labeled cellular protein or an annotation added by the figure author. These are not identical sources of information. The next task is to identify the plane and orientation of the image. A region that appears adjacent to a particle in a projection may lie above or below that particle in three-dimensional space. A statement about molecular association should not be inferred solely from overlapping colors in a two-dimensional representation. Colocalization is a spatial observation and does not by itself establish physical binding or causality.

Electron microscopy can provide high-resolution information about the relative locations of membranes and cellular structures. A thin section, however, displays only a portion of the object. The appearance of two separated membrane profiles can represent a continuous structure that extends outside the section. The absence of a visible membrane in one image may reflect its orientation, the section plane or the limits of image contrast. The most defensible interpretation is one that acknowledges the type of observation being presented. For an examination question based on a simplified diagram, the accompanying labels may explicitly define the topological state. For a real image, the conclusion may require additional evidence.

Time-resolved observations offer a different kind of information. They can show a succession of structural states, such as an external particle, a partly enclosing membrane and a compartment. Temporal order is necessary for many causal explanations, since an event that occurs only after another event cannot be its initial cause. But temporal order alone is not sufficient. Two events could both arise from a common preceding signal. In addition, the resolution of a time series may be insufficient to distinguish closely spaced molecular changes. A scientifically careful interpretation therefore uses temporal observations together with mechanistic information rather than treating the earliest visible event as the complete molecular cause.

For a cell-biology image, distinguish three types of claim: a localization claim (where is an object?), an identity claim (what molecule or compartment is it?), and a causal claim (what accounts for the observed event?). A membrane outline can establish location when its continuity is clear, but not the molecular identity of a receptor. A receptor label can establish localization, but not the occurrence of uptake. A series of images may establish the order of morphological events without proving a particular biochemical interaction. Evidence for the classical models combines these levels rather than assuming they are equivalent. This is the central logic of the historical work reviewed by Cossart and Sansonetti and the general account of microbial endocytosis.

It is particularly important to distinguish necessary conditions from sufficient conditions. A necessary condition is one without which a stated outcome cannot occur within the specified model. A sufficient condition is one that establishes the outcome when the relevant background assumptions are satisfied. A host membrane is necessary for formation of a host membrane-bounded compartment, but the presence of a membrane is not sufficient to establish that a particle has been engulfed. An interaction between two proteins may be necessary for a particular association while being insufficient to establish a complete sequence of cellular events. These logical relationships apply to many biological mechanisms and are especially useful when interpreting statements about binding and signaling.

The logic of a negative result also depends on the measured endpoint. Suppose a general cell-biology question reports that a particular marker is absent from an image. This observation concerns detection of the marker under the conditions represented. It is not a direct measurement of the absence of every related protein or process. Similarly, a cell that does not show a particular morphological feature in one image might have shown it earlier. A negative observation can be informative, but it should not be extended beyond the property actually measured. This is one reason that general statements such as no signaling occurred or no actin was involved are much stronger than a report of no visible structure.

A final source of uncertainty is the biological model itself. A cultured animal cell may not reproduce all of the geometry, intercellular communication or differentiated functions of a complete tissue. An inert particle can be useful for illustrating physical enclosure but cannot represent the full biology of a living microorganism. A schematic representation can clarify topological distinctions but cannot supply direct empirical evidence for the sequence of molecular events. The type of model determines which conclusions it can support. The best examination answers preserve that relationship rather than generalizing a useful model beyond its intended domain.

A comparison with ordinary phagocytosis provides a useful independent check of the models. The review by Flannagan, Jaumouillé and Grinstein describes particle recognition, phagosome formation and maturation as related but distinguishable phases. These phases are not themselves evidence that a bacterial ligand or delivered factor initiated an event. A diagram of a macrophage clearing cellular debris can show a closed cup and subsequent maturation without depicting any bacterial virulence mechanism at all. That example explains why the structural similarity between phagocytic and induced cups does not settle a question about pathogenesis. See The Cell Biology of Phagocytosis.

The 1996 receptor-identification paper illustrates why a molecular claim and a cellular claim should be stated separately. The identification of E-cadherin as a partner for internalin addressed the molecular recognition question, while the observed epithelial-cell uptake addressed the cellular consequence. The inference from the combined work is a receptor-associated entry mechanism in the investigated model. It does not establish that every E-cadherin-containing tissue experiences the same events. The 1993 ruffle study addressed a different connection: it related an observed membrane structure to a form of fluid-associated internalization. A comparison of the two papers therefore shows how different experimental questions can contribute to a common explanation of bacterial entry without asking for identical observations.

Consider an abstract image-analysis exercise involving only inert particles and ordinary host membranes. One set of records identifies the object as external and another as surrounded by a continuous host-derived boundary. The independent observation in the second set is enclosure, not viability or growth of the cargo. A second annotation records the distribution of filamentous actin at the host cortex. Comparing the two annotations can suggest an association between membrane state and actin organization, but it cannot demonstrate direct contact between every molecular component. A student can formulate the scientific inference without knowing how the images were collected: the topology annotation answers where the object is, while the cytoskeletal annotation answers where a labeled cellular structure is located. An explanation of cause requires a different category of evidence.

Worked quantitative examples

These are hypothetical, ordinary cell-biological examples and ideal geometric models.

1. Surface-to-volume scaling

For r = 0.75 μm, A = 4π(0.75)² = 2.25π ≈ 7.07 μm² and V = 4π(0.75)³/3 = 0.5625π ≈ 1.77 μm³. A/V = 4 μm⁻¹. The ratio describes the geometry of an ideal sphere, not a measured uptake response.

2. Conditional and population averages

Suppose 25 of 100 cells contain 75 inert beads in total. The positive-cell fraction is 25/100 = 25%; the mean per positive cell is 75/25 = 3; the mean per observed cell is 75/100 = 0.75. Each denominator answers a different question.

3. Absolute and relative percentage changes

A normal cellular feature appears in 12 of 60 cells at one observation and 18 of 60 at another. The proportions are 20% and 30%. The absolute increase is ten percentage points; the relative increase is (30 − 20)/20 × 100 = 50%. These values do not establish the cause of the change.

4. Cross-section geometry versus genuine size change

An ideal spherical endocytic compartment has radius 2 μm. A section lies 1.2 μm from the center. The section radius ρ follows ρ² + d² = R² by Pythagoras, giving ρ = √(4 − 1.44) = 1.6 μm. The central section has area 4π μm²; the offset section has area 2.56π μm², or 64% of the central area. The same compartment can therefore show a 36% smaller profile without shrinking. A section is not a volume measurement.

5. Separating the particle from its compartment

For an inert ideal sphere of radius 0.5 μm enclosed in a spherical compartment of radius 1 μm, the particle occupies 4π(0.5)³/3 = π/6 μm³, while the total enclosed volume is 4π/3 μm³. The particle therefore occupies one-eighth of the volume; the remaining fluid space is seven-eighths, assuming concentric spheres and negligible membrane thickness. A host-derived membrane is not the particle envelope.

6. Choosing the correct denominator for a membrane image archive

Of 100 inert particle observations, 36 are external, 24 in open cups and 40 sealed. The sealed fraction of all observations is 40%; the fraction among cup-or-compartment observations is 40/(24+40) = 62.5%. Neither fraction measures cytosolic access, which is a different topological endpoint. A student must state the denominator and the observation being counted before interpreting a number.

Part B — Conceptual practice

Original Academy practice questions; not previous-year examination questions.

B01. Which statement correctly distinguishes bacterial adhesion from internalization?

  1. Adhesion always delivers bacteria into the cytosol.
  2. Internalization is synonymous with bacterial replication.
  3. Adhesion is surface association; internalization requires a change in membrane enclosure.
  4. Adhesion is a change in the host nucleus.
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. Surface association is not proof that a particle has crossed the boundary defined by the plasma membrane. Internalization places the object within the cell outline; cytosolic access and multiplication are separate outcomes. The alternatives confuse attachment with cytosolic access, localization with growth, or a different cellular compartment.

B02. The classical bacterial partner of E-cadherin in the Listeria zipper model is:

  1. Internalin A
  2. Complement C3
  3. Arp2/3
  4. Met
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. InlA is the bacterial surface recognition protein in the classic E-cadherin example. Met is a host receptor associated with a different internalin example. Arp2/3 organizes host actin and C3 is a host complement component.

B03. The normal physiological role of E-cadherin is primarily associated with:

  1. DNA replication
  2. ATP synthesis in mitochondria
  3. Ribosomal decoding
  4. Epithelial cell–cell adhesion
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. E-cadherin is a classical cadherin that participates in adherens junctions and links indirectly to the actin system. It is not a DNA polymerase, ATP synthase or ribosomal component.

B04. In the classical trigger entry model, the characteristic initiating influence is:

  1. A bacterial flagellum acting as a host transporter
  2. Intracellular action of delivered bacterial factors
  3. Host nuclear fusion
  4. Passive diffusion of the entire bacterium
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. The trigger model is associated with bacterial effectors acting within the host cell and resulting in membrane remodeling. Neither an intact bacterium diffusing across the bilayer nor nuclear fusion describes this mechanism.

B05. Which property is shared by classical zipper and trigger entry?

  1. Identical bacterial recognition proteins
  2. Participation of host membrane and actin remodeling
  3. A permanently open hole in the plasma membrane
  4. A necessarily cytosolic final location
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. Both models recruit host cell structural responses. The initiating molecules are not identical, enclosure does not require a permanent opening and later localization is independent of the entry category.

B06. A bacterium enclosed by a continuous host-derived membrane lies in:

  1. The host cytosol without any separating membrane
  2. The extracellular medium by definition
  3. A membrane-bounded intracellular compartment
  4. The host nucleoplasm by definition
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. A complete host-derived membrane separates the compartment lumen from the cytosol. The bacterium is intracellular even though it is not freely cytosolic. Its position relative to the nucleus is not specified.

B07. Which is a professional phagocyte?

  1. Macrophage
  2. Erythrocyte
  3. Skeletal muscle fiber
  4. Mature sperm cell
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. Macrophages are specialized for particulate uptake and processing. The other cell types have different principal physiological roles and are not classic professional phagocytes.

B08. Which distinction between a secretion system and an effector is correct?

  1. An effector is necessarily a host receptor.
  2. The terms are identical.
  3. Both are host lipid bilayers.
  4. A secretion system is delivery machinery; an effector is a factor that acts on a target.
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. Delivery and molecular action are separate roles. A bacterial protein can influence a host process without itself constituting the machinery that delivered it. A host receptor is not synonymous with a bacterial effector.

B09. G-actin differs from F-actin in that G-actin is:

  1. The globular subunit rather than the assembled filament
  2. A guanine nucleotide regulatory protein
  3. A bacterial membrane receptor
  4. A phospholipid
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. G-actin is a soluble actin subunit; F-actin is a filamentous polymer. Rho-family regulatory GTPases, membrane receptors and phospholipids belong to different molecular categories.

B10. The characteristic geometry of a classical zipper event is:

  1. An isolated mitochondrion surrounding a bacterium
  2. A permanent breach of the plasma membrane
  3. Close apposition of the engulfing membrane to the particle
  4. Fusion of two animal nuclei
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. The close-fitting cup is a characteristic depiction of zipper-like uptake. This is a membrane-enclosure event, not nuclear fusion or permanent membrane destruction.

B11. Which classical organism is associated with trigger-type membrane ruffling in the chapter?

  1. Amoeba proteus
  2. Saccharomyces cerevisiae
  3. Arabidopsis thaliana
  4. Salmonella enterica
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. Salmonella is the classical bacterial example of effector-associated ruffling. Amoeba, yeast and Arabidopsis are eukaryotic organisms and do not serve as the bacterial example in this comparison.

B12. An open membrane cup differs from a closed compartment because:

  1. An open cup contains no water.
  2. The lumen of an open cup remains continuous with the exterior.
  3. A closed compartment is necessarily a lysosome.
  4. An open cup contains no membrane.
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. Membrane topology, not the presence of water or the chemical identity of the compartment, distinguishes an open cup from a sealed compartment. A newly enclosed compartment need not yet be a lysosome.

B13. Met, the host receptor associated with the classical InlB example, is a:

  1. Ribosomal protein
  2. Actin monomer
  3. Bacterial peptidoglycan hydrolase
  4. Receptor tyrosine kinase
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. Met is the hepatocyte growth factor receptor and has a kinase domain. The other alternatives do not identify its host receptor class.

B14. Macropinocytosis is best described as:

  1. Enclosure of extracellular fluid by relatively large membrane structures
  2. Fusion of host and bacterial nuclei
  3. Synthesis of a bacterial chromosome
  4. Movement of individual sodium ions through a channel
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. Macropinocytosis is a form of fluid-phase uptake associated with membrane remodeling. It is not ion-channel transport, replication or nuclear fusion.

B15. Opsonization refers to:

  1. Loss of every host receptor
  2. Coating a target with host recognition molecules that can facilitate uptake
  3. Polymerization of all cellular actin
  4. Movement of a bacterium through a nuclear pore
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. Opsonins such as antibodies and complement components can associate with targets and be recognized by appropriate host receptors. This is distinct from the mechanical movement of the particle or destruction of receptors.

B16. Which statement about actin is accurate?

  1. All filaments are static.
  2. Actin polymerization implies synthesis of new actin molecules.
  3. The organization of actin can change without a change in total actin protein.
  4. F-actin and Rho GTPases are identical.
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. Cells can redistribute existing actin between soluble and filamentous pools. The total protein amount can remain constant. GTPases regulate structural responses but are not actin filaments.

B17. An intracellular bacterium is necessarily:

  1. In the nucleus
  2. Alive and replicating
  3. Within the host cell boundary, if the term is used literally
  4. Free in the cytosol
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. Intracellular describes location, not viability, replication or the particular internal compartment. A vacuolar bacterium is also intracellular.

B18. Which of these is a membrane-associated signaling lipid?

  1. Phosphoinositide
  2. Ribosomal RNA
  3. Histone H3
  4. Hemoglobin
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. Phosphoinositides are phosphorylated membrane lipids. Hemoglobin, histones and RNA belong to different classes and are not the signaling lipids described here.

B19. A host cell receptor is present in a polarized tissue. What is not established by that fact alone?

  1. That the tissue contains cells
  2. That it is accessible on every exposed surface
  3. That receptor is a molecular entity
  4. That receptor expression can be measured
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. Receptor abundance and surface accessibility are separate properties in polarized cells. The other alternatives either repeat the premise or are general facts.

B20. Which result is sufficient to classify an event as the classical trigger model?

  1. A bacterium is Gram-negative.
  2. Actin is present near a particle.
  3. A particle lies inside a cell.
  4. The description identifies intracellular bacterial effector action as the initiating influence on remodeling.
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. The characteristic initiating influence distinguishes the trigger model. Intracellular location, actin association and Gram classification alone are not diagnostic.

  1. The first figure proves InlA–E-cadherin recognition.
  2. The third proves bacterial trigger entry.

Answer and explanation

Correct: D. The macrophage counterexample shows that tight enclosure is not specific to a bacterial zipper model. A ruffle is also a normal host structure. A and B incorrectly infer microbial mechanisms from shape; C ignores the actin dependence of ordinary engulfment and localized cups.

Correct: A. Positive fraction is 20/80 = 25%. The conditional mean is 50/20 = 2.5. The population mean of 50/80 = 0.625 answers a different question.

  1. Zipper is molecularly proven; cytosolic access occurred.
  2. Trigger is molecularly proven; the particle was degraded.
  3. Enclosure occurred; later cytosolic access and the molecular initiating event remain unestablished.
  4. The initiating ligand is identified; replication occurred.
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. The two geometrical states establish an enclosure transition. Cup morphology is not unique molecular evidence and a closed compartment remains separated from cytosol.

C05. A textbook depicts E-cadherin at an adherens junction and Met in a receptor-kinase signaling diagram. Which statement correctly connects the two with the classical zipper model?

  2. Both are bacterial internalins.
  3. Both contain intrinsic tyrosine kinase domains.
  4. Both are soluble cytosolic actin monomers.
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. E-cadherin is a host adhesion protein and Met is a receptor tyrosine kinase. They are not bacterial ligands, both kinases, or actin. This makes receptor class distinct from the broad entry-model category.

C06. Three observations are reported: P, host receptor and bacterial ligand are identified in a molecular diagram; Q, a host membrane cup is open; R, a bacterial particle is surrounded by a closed host-derived boundary. Which inference is supported?

  1. P alone proves that R has occurred.
  2. Q is proof of a free cytosolic bacterium.
  3. P concerns recognition, Q is an intermediate morphology and R is enclosure; the observations answer different questions.

Answer and explanation

Correct: C. A molecular identity does not establish completion of uptake. An open cup retains continuity with the exterior. A closed boundary identifies enclosure but not the upstream recognition mechanism, so A, B and D overinterpret individual observations.

  1. 40%; all sealed particles are free in cytosol.
  2. 24%; the initiating receptor is known.
  3. 64%; all associated particles are sealed.
  4. 62.5%; the fraction is conditional on cup or compartment association and does not identify a bacterial entry mechanism.
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. The denominator is 24 + 40 = 64, and 40/64 = 0.625. Forty percent uses all observations. Twenty-four percent is the open-cup share of all observations and 64% is the associated share. None identifies a bacterial initiating mechanism.

C08. In a graph of normal membrane dynamics, 45% of cells display protrusions at t1 and 15% at t2. A separate panel measures neither enclosure nor particle location. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. Thirty percent of cells completed enclosure.
  2. Prevalence fell by 30 percentage points; the fate of individual protrusions is unresolved.
  3. Exactly two-thirds of protrusions sealed.
  4. The relative decline was 30%.
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. The absolute difference is 45 − 15 = 30 percentage points. The relative fall is 30/45, or two-thirds, but no enclosure endpoint was measured.

C09. In two cross-sections of a single sealed spherical compartment, one passes through its center and has radius 2 μm; another passes 1.2 μm from its center. What is the radius of the second circular section?

  1. 0.8 μm
  2. 1.2 μm
  3. 2.4 μm
  4. 1.6 μm
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. By the Pythagorean theorem the section radius satisfies ρ² = R² − d² = 4 − 1.44 = 2.56 μm², giving ρ = 1.6 μm. Subtracting radii directly gives 0.8, treating displacement as radius gives 1.2, and 2.4 exceeds the sphere radius. A smaller image profile alone does not indicate biological shrinkage.

  3. 3.375 and 2.25
  4. 2 and 3

Correct: B. Surface area scales with radius squared and total enclosed volume with radius cubed. Thus the factors are 1.5² = 2.25 and 1.5³ = 3.375. C11. A host-cell image shows actin enrichment near a particle but gives no information on the initiating interaction. Which interpretation is justified?

  2. The compartment is identical to the cytosol.
  3. Membrane transport and trafficking can change lumen composition after enclosure.
  4. The lipid bilayer must have permanently ruptured.

Correct: C. Topological origin does not fix chemical composition indefinitely. Membrane-associated transport and trafficking can change the lumen. A sealed compartment need not be chemically identical to the extracellular fluid or cytosol.

  1. Fusion of the particle's membrane with host plasma membrane.

M01. Assertion: An epithelial cell can participate in induced bacterial engulfment. Reason: Epithelial cells possess membrane-trafficking and cytoskeletal machinery even though they are not professional phagocytes.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
  3. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  4. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. The general capacity for membrane remodeling is a cellular prerequisite for induced enclosure. Epithelial cells do not become professional phagocytes simply because they undergo such an event.

M02. Assertion: A bacterium inside a host-derived vacuole is freely cytosolic. Reason: The host-derived membrane separates the lumen from the host cytosol.

  1. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
  2. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  4. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. The assertion contradicts the stated membrane boundary. The bacterium is intracellular but not freely cytosolic while an intact enclosing membrane separates it from the cytosol.

M03. Match each term to its normal cellular role: (i) E-cadherin, (ii) Met, (iii) actin, (iv) opsonin. Roles: P, adhesion; Q, receptor kinase; R, structural polymer; S, recognition coat.

  1. i–P, ii–Q, iii–R, iv–S
  2. i–Q, ii–P, iii–S, iv–R
  3. i–R, ii–S, iii–Q, iv–P
  4. i–S, ii–R, iii–P, iv–Q
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. E-cadherin is an adhesion molecule, Met is a receptor kinase, actin forms filaments and an opsonin associates with material in a way that facilitates immune recognition. The other pairings interchange distinct molecular roles.

M04. Evaluate the statements. I. A closed endocytic lumen initially derives from extracellular space. II. Its composition can subsequently change. III. Every such compartment is a lysosome. IV. Every particle enclosed within a host cell is freely cytosolic.

  1. I, II, III and IV
  2. I and II only
  3. II and IV only
  4. I and III only
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. I and II distinguish topology from later chemistry. III confuses enclosure with organelle identity and IV ignores the membrane separating the compartment from cytosol.

M05. Refer to the figure showing the open cup and the closed compartment. If only the open cup is observed, which label is justified?

  1. Incomplete enclosure
  2. Sealed phagosome
  3. Free cytosolic particle
  4. Completed degradation
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. An open cup remains continuous with the exterior. The image establishes neither completed internalization nor degradation. This is a topological interpretation rather than a species-specific conclusion.

M06. Which statement concerning a schematic of a host cell cortex and plasma membrane is correct?

  1. All cortical filaments are secreted into extracellular fluid.
  2. The entire bacterial envelope consists of host cortical actin.
  3. Cortical actin is a structural component on the cytoplasmic side.
  4. Cortical actin forms the hydrophobic core of the lipid bilayer.
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. The cortical network is on the host cytoplasmic side and influences membrane organization. The lipid bilayer has a chemically different core, and bacterial envelopes are distinct structures.

M07. An ideal sphere of radius 1.5 μm has what surface area?

  1. 6π μm²
  2. 9π μm²
  3. 3π μm³
  4. 27π μm²
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. A = 4π(1.5)² = 4π × 2.25 = 9π μm². The alternatives use incorrect multipliers or dimensions.

M08. The ideal-sphere graph shows normalized values at relative radius 2. What are area and volume, respectively?

  1. 4 and 4
  2. 8 and 4
  3. 2 and 4
  4. 4 and 8
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. Area scales as r² and volume as r³. At relative radius 2 their normalized values are 4 and 8. The graph describes ideal geometry, not an infection outcome.

M09. Assertion: A static image of filament localization does not measure polymerization rate. Reason: A rate involves a change per unit time.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  3. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
  4. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and explanation

Correct: D. A single image can describe spatial organization, but a kinetic quantity requires temporal information or another valid rate measurement. The reason directly explains the assertion.

M10. A hypothetical normal epithelial cell loses its organized actin cortex. Which inference is appropriately limited?

  1. Every membrane protein must disappear.
  2. Its capacity for normal shape changes may be disturbed; specific outcomes require further evidence.
  3. Its DNA must become bacterial DNA.
  4. All cellular ATP necessarily becomes zero.
Answer and explanation

Correct: B. The cortex contributes to surface mechanics, so impaired shape regulation is a reasonable general prediction. The other outcomes do not follow from the premise.

M11. Which pair correctly distinguishes the scales of two observations?

  1. Receptor expression = tissue-level disease; membrane enclosure = organism replication
  2. Membrane geometry = amino acid sequence; binding = tissue pathology
  3. Receptor identity = molecular evidence; membrane enclosure = cellular topology
  4. Tissue localization = proof of cytosolic access; actin image = proof of biochemical rate
Answer and explanation

Correct: C. Receptor identity concerns molecular recognition, whereas enclosure concerns membrane topology at the cellular scale. Each evidence type supports a different claim. The alternatives mix scales or overinterpret the measurements.

M12. In a sample of 50 ordinary cells, 10 show a feature at one observation and 15 at another. What are the absolute and relative changes in the proportion?

  1. 10 percentage points; 50%
  2. 50 percentage points; 10%
  3. 10 percentage points; 10%
  4. 5 percentage points; 5%
Answer and explanation

Correct: A. The proportions are 10/50 = 20% and 15/50 = 30%. The absolute increase is 10 percentage points and the relative increase is 10/20 = 50%. This calculation describes the observed feature only.

Examination traps and misconceptions

  1. Adhesion means invasion. Attachment is a surface association; enclosure changes topology. Why it is tempting: The word cell-associated may refer to both attached and internalized objects. Exam cue: A question may describe an external particle held tightly by a receptor.
  2. Intracellular always means cytosolic. A host-derived compartment is intracellular but not cytosolic. Why it is tempting: Two-dimensional drawings often hide the delimiting membrane. Exam cue: Look for a continuous enclosing membrane.
  3. Zipper and trigger differ by presence or absence of actin. Both can use host actin. Why it is tempting: Simplified charts often associate the ruffles of trigger uptake with actin alone. Exam cue: A localized actin signal is compatible with either model.
  4. The shape of a ruffle proves an intracellular bacterial effector is present. Normal cells also produce ruffles; morphology alone is not molecular evidence. Why it is tempting: The classical images are memorable and easily treated as diagnostic. Exam cue: Identify whether the question supplies evidence of the initiating influence.
  5. The trigger model never involves surface contact. Contact and the characteristic initiating intracellular influence are separate stages. Why it is tempting: The term trigger can obscure preliminary association. Exam cue: Separate delivery from attachment in the scenario.
  6. Internalin A is E-cadherin. InlA is bacterial; E-cadherin is a host adhesion protein. Why it is tempting: Both names appear together in diagrams. Exam cue: Match origin, partner and normal function.
  7. Met is another name for E-cadherin. Met is a receptor tyrosine kinase; E-cadherin is a cadherin. Why it is tempting: Both are host receptors in the Listeria literature. Exam cue: Classify each molecular structure separately.
  8. All nonprofessional cells lack endocytic machinery. Epithelial and other cells use endocytosis and dynamic membrane remodeling. Why it is tempting: The term nonphagocytic is often overinterpreted. Exam cue: Distinguish specialization from general cellular capacity.
  9. A phagosome is immediately identical to a lysosome. Initial enclosure and subsequent compartment processing are different. Why it is tempting: The degradative function of phagocytosis is commonly taught as one event. Exam cue: Ask whether maturation has actually been established.
  10. All bacteria entering cells must remain vacuolar. Post-entry fates differ; a species shorthand is not a universal rule. Why it is tempting: Diagrams often show only the first compartment. Exam cue: Read the time point and explicit localization evidence.
  11. An intact bacterium uses a nutrient carrier to enter. Large-particle enclosure involves membrane reorganization. Why it is tempting: Transport and uptake are used broadly in biology. Exam cue: Distinguish molecular transport across a bilayer from endocytosis.
  12. Bright actin fluorescence gives a polymerization rate. Static localization does not measure kinetics. Why it is tempting: Strong color is easily mistaken for greater activity. Exam cue: Check the measured variable and whether there is time-resolved evidence.
  13. The total amount of actin determines local architecture. Actin redistribution and organization can change without altered total protein. Why it is tempting: Students may equate abundance with assembly. Exam cue: Compare total actin versus filamentous actin measurements.
  14. A secretion system is the same as an effector. The former is delivery machinery, the latter acts on a biological process. Why it is tempting: Both terms are often placed in one short phrase. Exam cue: Identify which entity is delivered and which is machinery.
  15. A receptor anywhere in a cell is accessible everywhere. Epithelial polarity influences spatial availability. Why it is tempting: Whole-cell protein expression is often interpreted as surface availability. Exam cue: Distinguish abundance, location and accessibility.
  16. Percent change equals percentage-point change. Relative change divides the difference by the initial proportion. Why it is tempting: Both calculations involve subtraction. Exam cue: From 20% to 30%, distinguish 10 points from a 50% increase.
  17. A larger section profile always means a larger actual object. A section through a different plane may have a different profile. Why it is tempting: A two-dimensional image is easily mistaken for the whole object. Exam cue: Identify the section geometry before inferring volume.
  18. A lack of a stated observation proves absence. Unreported and undetected are different; undetected is also measurement-dependent. Why it is tempting: Short questions may omit details. Exam cue: Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.

Revision and student mastery

Integrated summary: A bacterium outside an animal cell may associate with its surface or become enclosed by a rearrangement of the host membrane. Zipper and trigger are classical models distinguished by receptor-associated versus intracellular effector-associated initiation of a host response. Both involve host membrane dynamics and can involve actin. Professional phagocytes normally capture particulate targets; other cells can display induced uptake. The first enclosing compartment and later localization must be distinguished.

High-yield relationships: InlA–E-cadherin; InlB–Met; actin monomers versus filaments; membrane cup versus sealed lumen; attachment versus entry; vacuolar versus cytosolic location. For ideal spheres, A = 4πr², V = 4πr³/3, A/V = 3/r. For relative percentage change, divide the difference by the original value. These equations apply to the stated geometric and statistical models, not to the prediction of a pathogen's infectivity.

Mastery checklist

  • I can explain the topological distinction between attachment, enclosure and cytosolic access.
  • I can identify the two classical initiating mechanisms and their representative organisms.
  • I can compare the host-cell roles of cadherins, receptor kinases and the actin cortex.
  • I can distinguish professional phagocytosis from induced uptake into a nonprofessional cell.
  • I can interpret images and hypothetical data without overclaiming causality.
  • I can calculate and interpret ideal surface-area and volume relationships.
