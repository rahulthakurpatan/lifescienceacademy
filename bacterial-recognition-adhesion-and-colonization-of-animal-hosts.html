1. Why bacteria need a foothold

A bacterial cell arriving at the surface of an animal encounters a problem quite different from the problem of growing in a nutrient solution. The surface may be wet, moving, chemically heterogeneous and occupied by other microorganisms. A bacterium in the intestinal lumen is carried with the contents of the gut; one in the urinary tract encounters intermittent fluid movement. A cell deposited on a respiratory surface may be transported away with mucus. Even where nutrients are present, a microorganism can leave the local environment before it has divided. Attachment is therefore not merely a geometrical detail of an infection. It can determine whether a bacterial population remains in a suitable habitat long enough for any subsequent interaction to occur. The biological problem is to distinguish a productive local encounter from a transient collision.

In this chapter recognition means that molecules displayed by a bacterium interact preferentially with certain molecules in the animal environment. The bacterium need not consciously detect the identity of a tissue. Selectivity arises because some molecular surfaces fit one another more favorably than others. An adhesin is a bacterial surface component that contributes to attachment, often through a recognizable molecular binding partner. The corresponding host structure is commonly called a receptor or ligand for that adhesin. The terminology is reciprocal: from the perspective of the animal cell the bacterial protein may itself be regarded as a ligand. To avoid confusion, identify the origin of each partner explicitly when describing an interaction. The bacterial adhesin and the host binding partner are separate structures even if both happen to be proteins.

Attachment, or adhesion, describes physical association with a surface. Colonization describes establishment of a population at a site, rather than the presence of a single cell at one instant. The difference matters because even a stable molecular contact does not guarantee that descendants will remain viable or reproduce there. A bacterial cell might attach efficiently but experience nutritional limitation, chemical stress or displacement by its neighbors. Conversely, certain organisms may persist in a protected niche where retention does not require a particularly specialized receptor interaction. A population measurement several hours after exposure integrates several processes, including initial attachment, survival, multiplication and loss. It cannot automatically be interpreted as an isolated measurement of adhesion.

A pathogen is an organism capable of causing disease under appropriate conditions, but its presence does not always produce injury. Colonization by a potentially pathogenic organism may be asymptomatic. Disease requires impairment of normal host function, and that impairment may arise at the site of colonization or at a distance. Some bacteria remain extracellular while their secreted products affect nearby cells. Others must undergo an additional process of entry into host cells to establish their characteristic intracellular lifestyle. Accordingly, detection of a bacterial cell near an epithelial surface is evidence of association, not proof of host-cell invasion. A useful mental picture places three distinct compartments side by side: the lumen, the cell surface and the cytoplasm. Movement between those compartments requires separate explanations.

The epithelial cells lining a mucosal organ form a sheet. They expose an apical face toward the lumen and generally connect through specialized junctions to neighboring cells. Their basal region contacts an underlying matrix. An intact epithelial barrier thus does more than separate compartments physically; it limits which molecular structures are available to an approaching bacterium. A receptor located mainly at the basal surface may be abundant in the tissue yet inaccessible from the lumen. In a tissue section stained for that receptor, a strong overall signal does not establish that an intact apical surface offers the same binding opportunities. This distinction between molecular expression and molecular accessibility will recur throughout the chapter.

The organism and the tissue both contribute to specificity. Bacterial strains within the same species need not possess identical surface proteins or display them in identical amounts. Likewise, two animal tissues may differ in their surface carbohydrates, proteins, mucus composition and physiological movement. A pair of cells that can bind strongly in an artificial system may seldom encounter one another in the living animal. Tissue preference is therefore a conditional outcome of compatible molecular recognition and the biology of the location. To explain a colonization phenotype, first identify the relevant stage and then ask which bacterial property and host property influence that stage. This approach is more informative than assigning the entire process to a single virulence factor.

A useful comparison comes from normal resident microorganisms. Their associations with host surfaces can also involve adhesive molecules and local nutritional relationships. Adhesion is not an exclusive property of pathogens, because beneficial and harmless organisms face the same physical challenge of remaining in a habitat. The distinction between commensal and pathogenic behavior depends on the interaction and its consequences, not simply on whether an organism adheres. An organism that is normally tolerated in one compartment can become harmful if it reaches another. This is why an exam question about a bacterial adhesin is often best answered at the level of the immediate attachment phenotype rather than by an unsupported prediction that the organism necessarily causes disease.

2. From visible attachment to molecular recognition

Early observations of bacteria at animal surfaces raised a mechanistic question: was the association caused simply by the tendency of particles to stick, or did bacterial cells carry particular structures that recognized particular host components? Both explanations can contribute. General surface chemistry influences how close a bacterium approaches a material, while a specific interaction can stabilize selected contacts. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. A weak initial interaction may precede a strong specific bond. The important advance in understanding was to distinguish a surface property that promotes contact generally from a molecular partner that explains why adhesion differs between otherwise comparable surfaces.

The phenomenon of hemagglutination supplied a useful conceptual bridge between visible adhesion and carbohydrate recognition. Red blood cells display numerous membrane-associated molecules. If bacterial surfaces bear multiple binding sites for a compatible red-cell ligand, one bacterial cell can participate in connections involving several erythrocytes. Repeated connections produce an aggregate rather than a uniform suspension. The visible outcome provides evidence that an association can link cellular surfaces. It does not, by itself, reveal the chemical identity of the molecular partner. An aggregation phenotype must also be interpreted carefully because a change in bacterial aggregation can alter the apparent red-cell pattern even when the underlying interaction has not changed in the expected manner.

Carbohydrate competition helped distinguish classes of adhesive interactions. Consider the principle rather than a laboratory procedure. If a freely soluble carbohydrate occupies a binding site that would otherwise recognize a similar carbohydrate displayed by a host surface, the two forms compete for access to the same bacterial protein. The surface association may consequently decrease. An unrelated carbohydrate may fail to produce this effect because it lacks the required arrangement of chemical groups. Selective competition thus connects a visible attachment phenotype to a more specific molecular hypothesis. However, a soluble compound can also affect cell physiology or the properties of the surrounding fluid. Evidence for direct competition is strongest when those alternatives have been considered and the observations agree with independent evidence about the molecules involved.

The distinction between mannose-sensitive and mannose-resistant hemagglutination is a classic example of this reasoning. Mannose-sensitive behavior indicates that a mannose-dependent interaction contributes to the observed association under the conditions examined. Mannose resistance means that mannose does not prevent the observed interaction in that setting; it does not mean that no carbohydrate receptor participates. An adhesin could recognize a galactose-containing structure or another glycan instead. In fact, the distinct receptor preferences of type 1 and P fimbriae illustrate how different appendages of superficially similar appearance can mediate different molecular recognition events. A morphological classification of appendages and a biochemical classification of binding specificity address different questions.

Electron microscopy made it possible to associate bacterial surfaces with thin extracellular appendages. Structures called fimbriae or pili can extend from a cell and place a binding domain away from the bacterial envelope. Observing such structures is valuable because it establishes the physical architecture through which an interaction might occur. Yet an image showing appendages cannot establish that every one has the same biological function. Some pili are associated with motility or other processes, and the vocabulary has historically varied. A convincing assignment of adhesive function requires a connection between the structure and the attachment phenotype. If a bacterium still carries visible fibers after losing a particular binding domain, its ability to attach specifically may nevertheless be diminished.

Modern structural evidence allows the binding interface itself to be interpreted. A binding protein can contain a pocket or surface whose shape and chemical groups complement a host glycan. An image or molecular structure of a bound complex explains why a given arrangement of hydroxyl groups is accommodated while another arrangement may be unfavorable. Such observations are especially helpful when the macroscopic phenotype is selective competition. The two results answer complementary questions: the functional observation establishes that an interaction contributes to association, and the structural observation explains a plausible molecular basis for specificity. Neither result alone captures all the determinants of whole-tissue colonization.

The distinction between affinity and avidity provides another historical lesson in interpreting adhesion. A purified binding domain may form a reversible interaction with one ligand. A whole bacterium, however, can carry many binding sites, and the tissue presents repeated ligands. The association of the two larger objects may be stable even though each individual contact is reversible. A molecular observation and a whole-cell observation can therefore differ without contradiction. When a student compares these levels of evidence, the correct approach is to determine whether the measurement concerns a single bond, the summed behavior of several bonds or the persistence of an entire population.

One particularly instructive published finding concerns the effect of mechanical force on FimH-mediated binding. In 2002 Thomas and colleagues reported a transition from weaker to firmer bacterial association in a flow-based experimental model as shear increased over the examined range. The significance of that result was conceptual: mechanical load does not necessarily shorten the lifetime of every biological bond in the same way. It challenged a simple expectation derived from ordinary slip-bond behavior. Later work interpreted this as force-dependent conformational behavior of the adhesin. This finding should not be generalized to all bacterial adhesion or all fluid conditions. It demonstrates that attachment at moving animal interfaces can depend on the mechanical properties of the specific interacting molecules.

3. The bacterial surface as a molecular interface

To understand where adhesins are located, begin with the bacterial envelope. The cytoplasmic membrane separates the cell interior from the exterior and supports many essential physiological functions. Outside it lies the cell wall in typical bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria generally have a thick peptidoglycan layer, whereas Gram-negative bacteria typically have a thinner peptidoglycan layer within the space between the cytoplasmic and outer membranes. These architectural differences determine which structures are exposed and how a surface protein can be held in place. They do not dictate a single adhesive strategy for either group. Many bacteria also possess extracellular materials such as capsules or released polymers that influence the immediate environment of their envelope.

An adhesive protein has two conceptually different responsibilities. One region must present a molecular surface that interacts with the binding partner. Another structural arrangement must retain that protein in association with the bacterium. If a binding domain were completely released into the surrounding fluid, its ability to recognize a host ligand would not automatically attach the bacterial cell. Attachment involves a mechanically continuous connection between bacterial and host structures. The anchoring and recognition functions may be carried by different parts of one polypeptide or distributed among the subunits of a larger assembly. Distinguishing these functions helps explain why different structural defects can generate a similar reduction in whole-cell attachment.

Fimbrial architecture illustrates the division of labor. In the familiar type 1 fimbrial system of Escherichia coli, the protruding filament includes many structural subunits, while its specialized adhesive component FimH is located toward the tip. The extended arrangement can present a recognition domain beyond the immediate envelope. That spatial separation is useful when the bacterial surface and host membrane are surrounded by other molecules. A bacterium need not bring the full envelope into intimate contact before a projecting structure can encounter a compatible ligand. The shaft is not simply another name for the receptor-binding protein. Alteration of the shaft may change presentation of the binding domain, whereas alteration of the domain can affect its specificity.

The word non-fimbrial describes adhesins that are not presented as part of such a fimbrial filament. It does not imply that the protein is small or that it cannot project away from the surface. A large surface-associated protein may extend through material surrounding the envelope while remaining part of a different architectural class. Gram-positive surface proteins that bind extracellular matrix components provide important examples. For an extracellular matrix binding protein, the relevant binding partner may be a protein outside the animal cell rather than a glycolipid in its plasma membrane. These cases demonstrate that a host receptor need not be a signaling receptor in the classical hormone-receptor sense.

The physical visibility of a binding domain also depends on neighboring surface structures. A thick extracellular layer can change the distance between bacterial and host surfaces. Such material can influence electrostatic interactions, hydration and steric access to proteins. A surface polysaccharide may therefore have different effects in different adhesive contexts. It is unsafe to conclude that every capsule increases or decreases binding. A capsule may obscure an adhesive protein while contributing to other forms of persistence, and certain polysaccharides themselves participate in surface or matrix associations. The net phenotype depends on the identities and arrangement of the exposed components, not simply on their total number.

Surface charge offers a useful connection with the biophysical chemistry of Unit 1. Many biological surfaces carry charged groups and interact with surrounding ions and water. Electrostatic forces may favor approach or create repulsion depending on the local charges and solution. Hydration and steric exclusion can prevent close approach even when attractive interactions exist at longer distances. Once complementary molecular groups come close enough, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic contacts, van der Waals interactions and hydrophobic effects may collectively contribute to recognition. These contributions do not usually imply formation of a new permanent covalent linkage between the bacterium and the host. A reversible molecular association is entirely compatible with prolonged attachment at the scale of a whole cell.

Bacterial adhesion is therefore a property of an organized interface, not simply a list of genes or protein names. A gene may encode a binding protein, but the amount displayed at the surface also depends on synthesis, folding and correct localization. Surface display is logically distinct from receptor-binding capacity. An exam problem might present an organism with unchanged total adhesin protein but reduced surface accessibility. In such a case the expression measurement does not contradict a reduction in attachment. The missing link is presentation. By keeping synthesis, presentation and binding conceptually separate, one can interpret perturbations without assuming that every decrease in adhesion is caused by a change in binding chemistry.

4. Host glycans and the chemistry of specificity

The outside of an animal plasma membrane differs chemically from its cytoplasmic face. Many membrane proteins carry attached carbohydrate chains, and some lipids also bear carbohydrates on their external headgroups. Together with other extracellular molecules, these structures contribute to the glycocalyx, the carbohydrate-rich environment at the cell surface. It is not a uniform coating of free glucose molecules. Rather, individual chains have particular compositions, linkages, branching arrangements and spatial distributions. A bacterial adhesin that recognizes a carbohydrate motif can thus distinguish surfaces that contain the same general classes of membrane lipids and proteins but differ in their detailed glycosylation. This is a direct biological connection between carbohydrate chemistry and host recognition.

A glycoprotein is a protein with covalently associated carbohydrate groups. A glycolipid has a lipid component joined to a carbohydrate moiety. These names identify the carrier molecule, not necessarily the part recognized by the bacterial adhesin. A bacterial protein may recognize an exposed sugar on a glycoprotein without making important contacts with the protein backbone. In a different system it may recognize the folded polypeptide itself. The distinction can be essential when interpreting a loss of glycosylation. A glycoprotein might remain present at the host membrane yet no longer display the glycan required for recognition. An antibody detecting the protein and an adhesin detecting the glycan could therefore produce different results on the same cells.

The stereochemistry of sugars explains why carbohydrate recognition can be highly selective. Glucose and mannose are related monosaccharides, but they differ in the configuration of a particular carbon atom. This changes the orientation of a hydroxyl group in three-dimensional space. A binding pocket that forms favorable contacts with one arrangement may not accommodate the other as well. Glycosidic bonds introduce further distinctions. The carbon atoms joined by a linkage and the alpha or beta configuration of that linkage affect the orientation of neighboring sugar residues. A motif written as two galactose residues with a specified linkage is not interchangeable with every disaccharide containing galactose. Students should resist reducing a glycan receptor to a list of its component monosaccharides.

Lectins are carbohydrate-binding proteins that recognize sugars without necessarily modifying them chemically. A lectin-like bacterial adhesin is a recognition molecule, not a carbohydrate-degrading enzyme simply because its ligand contains sugar. A host glycan that binds reversibly can remain chemically intact when the interaction dissociates. This is a useful contrast with an enzyme that catalyzes hydrolysis of a glycosidic linkage. For adhesion, the relevant consequences of recognition include localization and mechanical association, not obligatory metabolism of the ligand. A sugar may of course also serve as a nutrient in another biological context, but those two functions cannot be inferred from one another.

Animal tissues can display different glycan patterns because the cellular processes that attach and modify carbohydrate chains differ among cell types and physiological states. The same underlying polypeptide expressed in two tissues need not have exactly the same carbohydrate decorations. Additionally, the physical organization of the carriers may vary. A motif that is chemically present may be hidden by neighboring molecules, or it may be exposed at the end of an extended chain. Consequently, the measured abundance of a sugar residue in a tissue extract is not identical to the density of usable adhesive ligands at the intact surface. This distinction parallels the difference between total protein abundance and surface accessibility considered earlier.

Glycans on a mobile membrane are presented in a complex, three-dimensional environment. Some glycolipids and membrane proteins can diffuse laterally, while others are restricted by protein associations or cytoskeletal connections. Nearby carbohydrate chains can influence the orientation and spacing of the binding sites. These physical properties help explain why an isolated glycan in solution and a similar glycan attached to a membrane do not always produce identical apparent binding behavior. A biochemical measurement identifies the chemical preference of the adhesin under a particular set of assumptions; the intact cell adds spatial constraints and multiple potential interactions.

A soluble glycan can also act as a decoy rather than as an anchor to an epithelial cell. Imagine that a bacterial binding site has occupied a compatible carbohydrate on a mobile extracellular molecule. The chemical specificity of the contact is real, but the bacterium has not necessarily become attached to the animal membrane. If the molecule is carried away by fluid movement, the association may contribute to removal rather than retention at that membrane. Thus the biological effect of binding depends partly on the location of the host molecule. The same broad class of molecular recognition may be associated with attachment to a fixed surface or sequestration by a soluble component. This is why an adhesion receptor should be described with its biological context as well as its chemistry.

5. Fimbrial and pilus-mediated attachment

A familiar bacterial cell in a schematic drawing is shown as a rod with a number of thin extensions. Such drawings are useful only when each structure is interpreted according to its actual function. Fimbriae are frequently involved in adhesion, whereas flagella are generally associated with motility. The distinction is functional and structural, not merely a difference in the length of the line used in a diagram. Some types of pili also serve functions other than adhesion, and terminology is not perfectly uniform across bacterial systems. A student should therefore avoid the sweeping statement that all surface filaments attach bacteria to host receptors. The specific system identified in a question determines what conclusion is justified.

Consider a fimbrial assembly as a projecting structure made of protein subunits. Its repeated structural units form a physical connection from the bacterial envelope toward the exterior. A specialized component can present a binding site near the exposed end. In type 1 fimbriae of E. coli, FimA is the main shaft subunit and FimH is the well-known tip-associated adhesive component. FimH recognizes mannose-containing structures. These roles illustrate why the structural abundance of a protein in an appendage need not correspond to its contribution to the chemistry of the attachment interface. A small number of binding domains located where contact occurs can have a disproportionately important influence on specific recognition.

The shaft helps place the recognition domain in a position where a compatible host molecule can be reached. Consider two surfaces covered by hydrated molecules that prevent the bacterial envelope itself from closely approaching the animal membrane. A projecting structure may bridge part of the intervening distance. The adhesive contact then forms between the exposed domain and the compatible host structure. This arrangement does not require the full bacterial surface to become flattened against the epithelial membrane. It also shows why an electron micrograph of an adhering bacterium can exhibit a small gap between the major surfaces without implying absence of molecular contact. A schematic must distinguish the spatial scale of the bacterial cell from the much smaller scale of the actual interaction.

Type 1 and P fimbriae provide a particularly valuable comparison because their broad architectural similarity is accompanied by different chemical recognition. The type 1 tip adhesin is FimH; the tip adhesin associated with P fimbriae is PapG. PapG recognizes particular galactose-containing motifs, commonly represented by the Gal alpha 1 to 4 Gal unit in appropriate glycolipids. The details of the larger glycan and the adhesin variant matter, but this distinction is sufficient to explain the examination-level contrast. Mannose-sensitive attachment points toward a type 1-like recognition mechanism in the appropriate model, whereas P-fimbrial recognition does not become mannose dependent simply because the projecting structure is also called a pilus.

The architecture of adhesive appendages must not be confused with the classification of bacterial lifestyles. A bacterium with a fimbrial adhesin can remain outside the host cell. The appendage does not itself indicate that the organism has crossed a membrane. The ability to attach is often an early prerequisite for a later process, but that does not mean it is sufficient for that process. Some attachment events are followed by prolonged extracellular association; others are followed by uptake or by contact-dependent changes in the host. The next chapter treats the distinct molecular events associated with pathogen-induced internalization. Here the primary question is whether an adhesive molecular contact physically connects a bacterium to a surface.

Fimbrial display is also a cellular phenotype rather than an immutable consequence of bacterial species identity. Bacterial gene expression can vary within a population and over time. In some familiar systems, expression of surface structures can switch between states. The broad principle is that populations may contain cells with different levels of exposure of a particular surface component even though the relevant genetic capacity is present. A measurement averaged over the entire population may conceal this heterogeneity. A change in the proportion of visibly piliated cells and a change in the ligand-binding ability of each displayed pilus are different explanations for a difference in mean attachment. This distinction is analogous to the difference between changing the number of functional receptors and changing the activity of an individual receptor.

The importance of mechanical properties follows from the environment in which the interaction occurs. A protein appendage and its binding domain can experience tension when a bacterium is carried relative to a surface. A useful conceptual distinction is between the strength of an instantaneous contact and the duration of a contact under load. A contact may form frequently but be brief, or it may form rarely but last longer. The total number of retained bacteria at a given time reflects both arrival and departure. The 2002 FimH study introduced earlier is noteworthy because it showed that the departure behavior could change in a manner not predicted by treating every interaction as a simple slip bond. The result supports a model in which force can alter the distribution of molecular conformations, rather than a model in which force universally stabilizes biological adhesion.

This comparison also prevents a common error in diagram-based examination questions. A long fiber drawn between the bacterial body and the host cell is not the receptor itself, and the receptor is not necessarily an entire transmembrane protein. In the type 1 example the bacterial tip domain recognizes a carbohydrate structure displayed by a host molecule. The carrier can be a glycoprotein. The figure therefore needs three correctly labeled elements: the bacterial shaft, the bacterial binding domain and the host ligand-bearing structure. A student who labels all three as FimH has collapsed different structural roles into one name and cannot explain why a loss of any one element may disrupt association by a different route.

6. Non-fimbrial adhesins, proteins and extracellular matrix

Not all bacterial adhesion depends on filamentous structures. A recognition domain may belong to a protein retained at the bacterial surface without being incorporated into a long fimbrial shaft. This is a broad organizational category. Some non-fimbrial adhesins extend considerably from the cell wall, whereas others lie nearer the envelope. Their different shapes reflect differences in how they are displayed and what partners they recognize. An appropriate diagram emphasizes these structural alternatives without suggesting that one class must always bind more strongly than the other. Binding specificity depends on the molecular interface, and persistence depends on the collective behavior of the cells and the tissue.

Extracellular matrix is the network of macromolecules outside animal cells that contributes to tissue architecture and mechanical properties. Collagen provides tensile strength in many tissues, and fibronectin participates in cell–matrix interactions. Laminins are important components of basement membranes. These proteins have biological roles independent of bacterial infection. Their relevance to the present chapter is that particular exposed regions can act as binding partners for bacterial proteins. This is an example of a normal host structure participating in an unintended interaction. The host has not evolved a matrix specifically to accommodate bacteria; the bacterial surface contains a recognition system that interacts with a pre-existing feature of the tissue.

The acronym MSCRAMM refers to microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules. It is commonly encountered in discussions of adhesive proteins of Gram-positive pathogens. Its useful meaning for examination purposes is that surface-associated microbial proteins can interact with host extracellular adhesive molecules. It should not be expanded into a claim that all Gram-positive adhesins recognize matrix or that all matrix-binding bacterial proteins belong to one structural family. In Staphylococcus aureus, for example, fibronectin-binding proteins and a collagen-binding surface protein illustrate distinct ligand preferences. Clumping factor A is another well-known surface protein, particularly associated with fibrinogen recognition. Fibrinogen is principally a soluble blood plasma protein that can also be present in deposits at tissue interfaces; it is not the same molecule as collagen or fibronectin.

A protein recognition surface may bind a linear sequence of amino acids, a region whose conformation depends on folding, or another molecular feature of the protein partner. In each case the interaction depends on accessibility of complementary chemical groups. A protein that is present in a tissue but buried inside an intact supramolecular assembly may be less available than the same protein exposed at a damaged surface. It is therefore possible for tissue injury to change the pattern of available binding partners without causing any change in the bacterial cell itself. This principle also explains why results from isolated matrix proteins cannot simply be treated as a complete map of bacterial localization in a healthy animal.

A further possibility is indirect attachment. The bacterial protein and a host membrane receptor need not contact each other directly if an intervening host molecule binds them both. Fibronectin, for example, has regions involved in its normal association with animal integrins and may also be recognized by certain bacterial surface proteins. In such a conceptual bridge, the bacterial adhesin contacts fibronectin, and fibronectin contacts the host integrin. The bacterial and host membrane proteins may thus become physically connected through an extracellular intermediate. It would be inaccurate to call the bacterial protein an integrin solely because integrins participate in the overall connection. Each binding interface has its own molecular identity.

A bridge can also help explain why blocking a host membrane component changes bacterial association even if that component does not directly recognize the bacterial protein. Conversely, loss of a bridging component can reduce attachment while both endpoint proteins remain present. These predictions follow from connectivity. When a diagram contains an intervening molecule, identify which edges represent direct molecular contacts and which conclusion describes the overall association of the two cells. Such reasoning is useful across cell biology: the same principle applies to an extracellular matrix linking animal cells to their surroundings, even though the individual bacterial and animal systems are not identical.

The contrast between a direct glycan-binding adhesin and a matrix-binding adhesin provides a natural way of organizing receptor classes. The former may recognize a carbohydrate motif carried by a cell-surface glycoprotein or glycolipid; the latter may recognize a structural region of a matrix protein. Some systems combine properties of both categories, because extracellular proteins may themselves be glycosylated. A table that classifies targets should therefore indicate the feature actually recognized rather than relying only on the name of the carrier. This is a more precise way to interpret an attachment phenotype than dividing every interaction into a simplistic protein-versus-sugar dichotomy.

7. Tissue specificity, accessible receptors and the urinary tract example

A tissue is not merely a collection of chemically identical animal cells. Cells differentiate into forms suited to particular physiological tasks. The surface of a urinary epithelial cell, for example, has a different organization from the surface of a skin keratinocyte or intestinal epithelial cell. Surface composition is influenced by membrane proteins, glycosylation, extracellular materials and the polarity of the cells. These differences are relevant to bacterial recognition because attachment requires the coexistence of compatible structures in the same physical compartment. A bacterium that encounters a tissue containing its preferred ligand does not necessarily contact that ligand unless the relevant face or extracellular region is actually accessible.

Tissue tropism describes a tendency to associate with or infect particular tissues. Host range instead concerns the kinds of host organisms in which the relevant biological interaction can occur. The distinction is useful because different species may share a molecular ligand, while different tissues of a single species may display unequal amounts of it. Neither property should be inferred from receptor chemistry alone. A bacterium may bind to an isolated molecule from a host species without establishing a population in that host. Conversely, a broad host range may reflect several different interactions rather than one universal receptor. The relevant biological observation must be stated before a proposed explanation is evaluated.

The urinary tract offers a particularly useful textbook setting because its epithelial surfaces experience fluid movement and show regional differences in organization. The superficial cells of the bladder, often termed umbrella cells, contain specialized membrane proteins called uroplakins. These proteins contribute to the properties of the apical epithelial surface. Some uroplakins display carbohydrate structures recognized by FimH. Recognition of a mannose-bearing host component by a type 1 fimbrial adhesin illustrates a specific molecular association at a tissue interface. Importantly, the normal role of uroplakins is not bacterial attachment. Their participation in bacterial recognition is a consequence of their chemistry and location.

P fimbriae supply a second classic association within the broader urinary tract context. Their tip adhesin PapG recognizes certain galactose-containing glycolipid structures, and these interactions have been associated with attachment to upper urinary tract tissues. The textbook contrast between FimH and PapG illustrates distinct preferences, not an absolute map in which every bacterium expressing one protein is confined to a particular organ. Strains vary in the combinations of structures they display, host tissues differ in ligand presentation, and many factors other than adhesion influence the progression of infection. A correct examination answer should use the stated adhesin and receptor to explain the relevant attachment preference, while avoiding a claim that a single protein determines every disease outcome.

The two examples also clarify the meaning of receptor identity. FimH interacts with a carbohydrate feature of a host molecule, while PapG can recognize a particular linked sugar motif on a lipid-associated glycan. The carrier and the recognized chemical group must be distinguished. A loss of a carrier can remove its displayed ligand, but loss of an individual glycan motif can occur while the carrier remains. A reduction in tissue binding observed after a change in glycosylation therefore does not automatically demonstrate loss of all membrane receptors. When interpreting an exam diagram, identify whether the data show the carrier, the carbohydrate attached to it, or the functional binding interaction.

There are important biological competitors for receptor-mediated attachment. The urinary tract contains soluble molecules, including uromodulin, that can interact with certain type 1-fimbriated bacteria. A molecule of this kind may reduce attachment to an epithelial surface by functioning as a mobile binding partner. This is a useful example of a host molecule whose location changes the consequence of an otherwise compatible molecular interaction. Recognition of a soluble ligand and recognition of a surface-bound ligand can involve related chemistry but have opposing effects on retention at a tissue. Students should not equate all molecular binding with increased colonization.

Consider a comparison between a tissue section and an intact organ. A tissue section can expose structures that would normally be separated from the lumen by an intact epithelial barrier. An adhesin may bind a component visible in the section even though a luminal bacterium would rarely encounter it in a healthy organ. In contrast, a receptor concentrated at an apical surface is directly relevant to association from the lumen. This explains why anatomical context is a necessary part of the interpretation of tissue-binding evidence. A model that assumes every receptor detected in a tissue is equally accessible discards information about cell polarity and barrier integrity.

Another distinction concerns host-cell signaling. Some structures that participate in bacterial adhesion also take part in signaling or mechanical organization of the animal cell. Binding to such a structure may have consequences beyond physical retention, but those consequences must be established independently. A cell-surface glycan carried by a membrane protein does not necessarily initiate the same intracellular signal as a conventional physiological ligand of that protein. Association alone is therefore insufficient evidence for a specified downstream signaling response. This distinction is especially important at the boundary between the present node and the following chapter on bacterial entry.

The significance of tissue tropism extends to interpretation of disease patterns without requiring detailed clinical knowledge. A population associated with one epithelial compartment can alter the function of that compartment through local interactions. Movement to another compartment would expose it to different physiological conditions and different binding partners. Thus attachment at one site and attachment at a second site are separate events in a causal chain. The presence of a particular adhesin may explain an early association but is not, by itself, evidence that the entire chain has occurred. Anatomical localization, viable bacterial population and host response represent separate levels of observation.

8. Epithelial barriers and the moving mucosal environment

An animal mucosal surface is a dynamic interface between internal tissues and the external environment. The lumen of the digestive tract is topologically outside the body's internal tissue compartments even though it passes through the organism. The respiratory and urinary tracts similarly expose epithelial surfaces to material arriving from outside the tissue. These interfaces must allow normal exchange or movement while protecting underlying cells. Their architecture and secretions make a substantial contribution to determining whether a bacterium reaches, contacts and remains associated with a host surface. The same molecular adhesin can therefore have a different apparent effect in two mucosal environments.

Mucus is a hydrated material containing large glycoproteins called mucins and many other constituents. Its physical properties depend on interactions among these molecules and the surrounding water. It can reduce direct contact between microorganisms and epithelial cells by separating them spatially. Mucins also contain carbohydrate structures capable of participating in molecular recognition. An encounter with mucus is consequently not identical to an encounter with the epithelial plasma membrane. A microorganism observed within mucus has not necessarily attached directly to the underlying epithelial cells. The biological significance of this location depends on the behavior and renewal of the mucus layer.

Some mucosal secretions move continuously or are replaced over time. A particle associated with a mobile layer can leave a local region together with that layer. This distinguishes the physical stability of a molecular bond from the stability of the object's position relative to the tissue. Even a contact that remains intact while a mucin is transported may not retain the bacterium near the original epithelial surface. A fixed host membrane, a moving mucus layer and a freely dissolved glycoprotein therefore provide different mechanical contexts. To interpret binding, it is essential to identify which of these structures serves as the binding partner.

In the respiratory tract, the coordinated action of cilia and mucus contributes to the transport of trapped material. Cilia are host-cell structures, not bacterial fimbriae. Their coordinated motion moves the surrounding secretion over an epithelial surface. A bacterial cell carried in that material may be removed without any need to break every molecular contact between the bacterium and the mucus. The respiratory example illustrates how an organ-level process can determine the fate of a molecular interaction. It also explains why evidence that an organism binds mucus cannot be used as automatic evidence that it avoids mucociliary clearance.

Intestinal surfaces present a different combination of physical features. The gut contents move, epithelial cells renew, secretions cover parts of the surface, and dense microbial populations occupy many intestinal habitats. The exposed apical membrane can contain an extensive glycocalyx. The availability of nutrients varies between luminal and tissue-associated locations. These properties mean that an isolated epithelial-cell binding measurement captures only a part of the living intestinal environment. A particular bacterial attachment phenotype may be relevant while a more complete explanation of population establishment also requires the context of fluid transport, nutrient availability and resident microorganisms.

The urinary tract example emphasizes the consequences of intermittent flow. Fluid movement creates a physical mechanism of clearance for organisms that remain suspended in the lumen. Association with a tissue changes the relationship between a bacterium and that flow, but does not ensure unlimited residence. Epithelial turnover, soluble decoys and other host processes remain relevant. The effect of flow on adhesion also cannot be reduced to a single universal direction because both transport toward the surface and detachment from it contribute to the measured outcome. Molecular force response is specific to the interacting system, as the FimH evidence demonstrates.

The distinction between a mucosal surface and keratinized skin is equally useful. Intact skin presents an outer barrier made of differentiated cells and associated extracellular material. The outermost surface is not equivalent to the viable plasma membrane of a living epithelial cell in a laboratory culture. Local moisture and the renewal of the outer layers contribute to the biological environment. A microorganism located on the skin surface may interact with components that differ from those found deeper within the tissue. Damage that exposes an underlying matrix changes the anatomical set of potential binding partners. This is a change in host context, not proof that the bacterium has acquired a new recognition property.

An epithelial barrier also depends on contacts between adjacent host cells. Tight junctions restrict the passage of substances between cells, while other junctional systems contribute to the structural integrity of the sheet. The general molecular basis of those junctions was introduced in the cellular communication subunit. Here their relevance lies in restricting the access of microorganisms to deeper regions. A cell surface that would be easily available to a bacterium in a dissociated-cell preparation can be concealed within the organized tissue. Therefore loss of tissue integrity can alter measured bacterial association independently of a change in the amount of bacterial adhesin.

Physiological surface renewal places a limit on the duration of any individual attachment. Epithelial cells are replaced at rates that differ between tissues, and extracellular secretions are continuously produced or cleared. A bacterium attached to a cell that is shed can be displaced with it. A tissue-associated population may remain detectable even when the individual bacteria or host cells originally involved are no longer present. Colonization at a population level and attachment at the level of one pair of cells must therefore be interpreted over different timescales. Long-term persistence is not evidence that one unchanging molecular bond has lasted for the entire observation period.

9. Resident microorganisms and colonization resistance

The animal environment contains microorganisms that have established stable or recurring associations with it. The microbiota is the collection of organisms present in a particular habitat, whereas the term microbiome is often used more broadly for the community and its genetic or ecological context. Different body locations have different resident communities. The incoming bacterium therefore encounters an ecological environment as well as a physical and molecular environment. Even if a suitable host ligand is present, neighboring microorganisms can influence whether that bacterium becomes part of a persistent population.

Colonization resistance describes the ability of resident microorganisms and their interactions with the host to limit establishment of other organisms. One elementary explanation involves competition for space. A tissue region or surface-associated community has finite physical capacity. An incoming cell may encounter a location already occupied by other cells or extracellular material. The presence of compatible host molecules in a biochemical analysis is not equivalent to the availability of unoccupied binding opportunities in that habitat. Spatial organization can therefore matter even in an environment where the incoming bacterium possesses an appropriate adhesin.

Resource competition is distinct from competition for a receptor. Two bacteria may use different adhesive structures but require some of the same nutrients. If resident organisms consume an available resource, an incoming population may fail to increase even when its initial attachment is unaffected. Conversely, two organisms might compete for the same physical location while depending on different nutrients. These are mechanistically different forms of competition and can produce different time-dependent observations. A measurement of early association and a measurement of later population size are useful conceptual distinctions when identifying the stage at which competition acts.

Resident microorganisms also alter the chemical environment through their metabolic activities. In the gut, for instance, microbial products can influence local acidity and other properties of the surroundings. Such effects can alter the physiological suitability of a habitat without requiring direct contact between the incoming and resident microorganisms. The host may additionally respond to signals associated with its microbiota, so indirect effects through host physiology are possible. These processes should not be confused with a single adhesin competing for a single receptor. A change in colonization resistance can arise from an ecological network rather than the disruption of one isolated molecular binding interaction.

Evidence that a microbial community contributes to colonization resistance does not imply that all resident organisms are protective in all circumstances. Some may have little effect on a particular incoming population, and community interactions can sometimes favor other organisms. The importance of a resident organism depends on its abundance, location, metabolic relationships and the properties of the host environment. For this reason, the observation that a bacterium colonizes a particular host surface does not reveal whether it benefits, harms or has no substantial effect on the animal. Its ecological position and physiological consequences need separate examination.

A useful example of this broader principle is the disruption of normal microbial communities after some antibiotic exposures. A change in the resident community can alter the opportunities available to another organism, even if the latter's adhesive molecules are unchanged. The relevant causal explanation is a change in the environment rather than a necessary change in the incoming bacterium. This example connects the microbial physiology and immune system subunits with host-pathogen recognition without restating their detailed treatment. The key conclusion is that colonization resistance depends on community context, not merely on the intrinsic properties of an individual microbial cell.

The review by Buffie and Pamer illustrates the importance of distinguishing direct microbial competition from effects mediated through host responses. This distinction is important when interpreting a comparison of conventional and microbiota-altered animals. A difference in colonization may reflect more than the presence or absence of competition for surface area. The compared hosts may also differ in their metabolic environment or in the normal physiological functions influenced by resident microorganisms. The evidence must therefore support the level of mechanism claimed. A population-level result is valuable, but it should not be described as direct proof of receptor competition unless the relevant molecular interaction has been independently established.

10. Surface-associated communities and biofilm persistence

Microorganisms are often depicted as separate cells suspended in liquid. Such a cell is described as planktonic when it is free in the surrounding fluid rather than residing in a surface-associated community. The picture is helpful for explaining individual cell physiology, but many microorganisms also exist in groups associated with extracellular material. A biofilm is an organized microbial community embedded in a matrix, often associated with a biological or nonbiological interface. The term refers to more than the presence of several cells close together. The physical association, extracellular environment and population organization are important to its biological meaning. Biofilms may contain one microbial species or members of several species, depending on the habitat.

The initial association of a bacterium with a surface and the subsequent formation of a microbial community are related but distinct processes. The first contact concerns the behavior of an individual cell and its immediate surroundings. If descendants remain in the region or other cells accumulate there, a local group can develop. Extracellular material can connect members of the group with each other and with the surrounding surface. Eventually the biological unit of interest may become the group rather than a single bacterium attached directly to a host cell. This progression is not a rigid universal sequence that every biofilm must follow. It is a useful framework for distinguishing the different physical relationships involved in community development.

The matrix is not identical to the bacterial cell wall. It is extracellular material surrounding or connecting cells. Depending on the community, it can include polysaccharides, proteins, extracellular DNA and host-derived components. The relative importance of these materials varies, so no single polymer should be assumed to be present in every biofilm. A matrix can contain a great deal of water, and the mechanical properties of the resulting material depend on how the constituents are organized. An image showing cells separated by extracellular space therefore does not necessarily indicate that they have no meaningful physical association. The intervening material may connect the cells as part of a larger structure.

A particularly important distinction is between adhesion to a surface and cohesion among members of a community. Surface adhesion connects a cell or group to an underlying substrate. Cohesion concerns the association of the cells and matrix with one another. The two properties need not change together. A community may be mechanically coherent but poorly attached to its substrate, or separate cells may remain attached without forming a substantial matrix-enclosed group. Thus a measurement of total material remaining on a surface after a disturbance does not automatically distinguish altered cell–surface binding from altered cell–cell or matrix association. The measurement concerns the entire retained structure.

Dental plaque provides a familiar biological example of the community concept. The tooth surface is covered by an acquired layer of host-derived material, and oral microorganisms associate with this interface. Different organisms can recognize different components of that environment, including structures on other microorganisms. The resulting community is not adequately described as a uniform layer of cells each binding the same receptor directly on the tooth. Some members are associated indirectly through their neighbors or the extracellular material. This illustrates why community-level persistence cannot always be understood from one adhesin–host receptor pair. The example is useful for examining the difference between direct attachment and biological association mediated by a network of contacts.

A surface-associated population may also experience conditions that differ between its outer and inner regions. Molecules entering from surrounding fluid move through the extracellular environment, while the microorganisms consume or release substances. As a result, cells in different locations may experience different local concentrations of nutrients and waste products. Even cells with the same genetic information can differ in physiological state because of their position within the community. This provides one explanation for heterogeneity within biofilms. It is an extension of elementary principles of transport and metabolism, not a claim that every community has the same spatial pattern of chemical gradients.

This heterogeneity matters when distinguishing antimicrobial tolerance from inherited resistance. Resistance generally concerns a microorganism's ability to grow at antimicrobial concentrations that would inhibit a susceptible organism. Tolerance concerns survival during exposure without necessarily implying a corresponding increase in the concentration required to inhibit growth. Biofilm-associated persistence may reflect several factors, including differences in physiological activity, transport through the matrix and collective organization. It should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that every cell has acquired a resistance mutation. Conversely, inherited resistance can occur in microorganisms that are not organized into a biofilm. These concepts belong to different levels of explanation.

A biofilm is also not a synonym for infection. Microbial communities may occur in ordinary environmental habitats and on host-associated surfaces without producing disease. The effect on the animal depends on the site, the participating organisms, their interactions and the response of the surrounding tissue. A community on an artificial surface associated with the body can have consequences without requiring each member to attach to a particular host-cell receptor. In this case the surface and any host-derived material adsorbed to it form the immediate physical environment. The biological significance lies in the persistence of the population and its association with the host, rather than in proof that the microorganisms are intracellular.

The review by Hall-Stoodley, Costerton and Stoodley published in 2004 provides a broad account of biofilms as a widespread mode of microbial organization. Later discussion of biofilm infections emphasized the challenges of recognizing such organization in living tissues, where bacterial and host-derived extracellular material can both be present. The important evidential lesson is that observing clustered bacteria is a starting point. Evidence of extracellular association and the relevant biological behavior strengthens the interpretation. A single staining signal, especially one that does not distinguish bacterial cells from other material, is not sufficient to establish every property commonly associated with biofilms.

A final distinction concerns dispersal from an established population. A community is not necessarily a permanent static object. Individual cells or groups can leave it, and the community may change in composition or position over time. The disappearance of a group from one surface does not by itself establish that all its cells have died. They may have been removed, redistributed or otherwise become undetectable by the measurement used. A conclusion about viability requires evidence appropriate to viability. This distinction connects the biofilm example with the general principle of separating attachment, retention and survival rather than interpreting any change in surface-associated material as a change in bacterial growth.

11. Physical chemistry and reversible molecular binding

The term affinity is used to describe the tendency of two molecular partners to associate at equilibrium. In a simple model, one protein binding site recognizes one freely available ligand. The association is represented as A + L ⇌ AL, where A is the unoccupied site, L is free ligand, and AL is the complex. The dissociation constant, Kd, is defined as the product of the equilibrium concentrations of free A and free L divided by the equilibrium concentration of AL. When each concentration is expressed in moles per litre, the resulting Kd has units of concentration. A smaller Kd indicates a more favorable association in this simple comparison. The model is useful because it separates the chemistry of a binding interaction from the total number of binding sites.

Suppose the total concentration of the protein binding sites is At. Each site is either free or occupied in the one-site model, so At = [A] + [AL]. The occupied fraction is θ = [AL]/At. Substituting the equilibrium expression Kd = [A][L]/[AL] gives [A] = Kd[AL]/[L]. The denominator becomes [AL](1 + Kd/[L]), which yields θ = [L]/(Kd + [L]). This derivation explains the hyperbolic form of the occupancy curve: at low free ligand concentration, changes in ligand abundance strongly influence the fraction occupied, while at high free concentration the finite number of sites limits the fraction that can become occupied. At [L] = Kd the fraction is one half. No special biological threshold has been assumed; the result follows from the algebra of the simple equilibrium model.

The equation refers to the free ligand concentration, not necessarily the amount initially added to a system. If the amount bound is substantial relative to the total amount of ligand, the free concentration may be lower than the total concentration. Substituting total ligand without considering that distinction can lead to an incorrect estimate of occupancy. Furthermore, the equation assumes a single class of independent binding sites, equilibrium, and no additional competing interactions. These assumptions should be stated before using the result in an examination question. A graph of molecular occupancy from the model does not directly describe the percentage of bacterial cells attached to an epithelial surface.

The distinction between affinity and binding-site number can be understood using two hypothetical soluble proteins unrelated to any particular pathogen. Suppose the two proteins recognize the same freely available ligand with identical Kd values but one sample contains twice as many protein molecules. At the same free ligand concentration their occupied fractions are equal, yet the sample containing more sites has more occupied sites in absolute number. Thus the amount of complex can change without any alteration in affinity. In a cell-associated system the same reasoning cautions against interpreting a larger signal from the whole cell as proof that the molecular binding interface has changed. Surface abundance and affinity are separate parameters.

Kinetics describes how rapidly association and dissociation occur rather than just the final equilibrium ratio. In the simple reversible model, the rate of association is kon[A][L], whereas the rate of dissociation is koff[AL]. At equilibrium these two rates are equal even though individual molecules can continue to bind and release. Rearrangement gives Kd = koff/kon. For a bimolecular association in solution, kon has units of concentration inverse per unit time, and koff has units of time inverse. The ratio therefore has units of concentration, agreeing with the equilibrium definition. This consistency is a useful check on both the interpretation and the algebra.

The relationship between the kinetic constants also shows why the same equilibrium affinity can arise from different kinetic behavior. Two molecular pairs may have equal ratios of dissociation to association rates even if the separate rates differ. One pair could exchange between bound and free states more rapidly than the other. A measurement made before equilibrium could then distinguish them, whereas an equilibrium occupancy measurement might not. This is important in biology because cells frequently encounter each other transiently. The duration of the observation must be considered before a kinetic result is interpreted as an equilibrium property. Neither a single time point nor an endpoint alone necessarily identifies which underlying rate is responsible.

A thermodynamic description links equilibrium to changes in standard free energy. For the association reaction and a specified standard concentration c°, ΔG° = RT ln(Kd/c°), where R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature and the ratio inside the logarithm is dimensionless. When Kd is smaller than the standard concentration, the logarithm is negative. Under these assumptions the standard free energy of association is therefore negative. This statement does not mean that all molecules become bound under every actual condition. The actual free energy change also depends on concentrations, and at equilibrium the net reaction free energy is zero. Distinguishing standard-state thermodynamics from the current state of a system avoids a common conceptual mistake.

Several noncovalent interactions can contribute to the free energy of binding. The complementary chemical groups of two molecular surfaces may form hydrogen bonds or electrostatic contacts, but association also changes the environment of those groups. Water molecules around the partners are displaced or reorganized, and the flexibility of the interacting structures may change. An interaction that appears favorable when considered in isolation does not necessarily contribute the same amount of free energy in the complete system. This explains why it is generally inappropriate to calculate the affinity of a protein simply by counting the number of hydrogen bonds shown in a structural drawing. Binding is a property of the initial and final states as a whole.

In a whole-cell interaction, the term avidity often denotes the collective stability of multiple contacts. A cell may remain associated when an individual contact dissociates because other contacts still connect it to the surface. Reformation of a contact may occur while the cells remain near each other. The overall behavior is not necessarily described by the independent one-site occupancy equation. Site mobility, spacing, orientation and mechanical coupling may matter. It would therefore be inappropriate to identify the apparent dissociation constant obtained from a complex cell-associated measurement automatically with the intrinsic Kd of a single purified binding domain. A model must match the level of organization being measured.

Mechanical force introduces another distinction. A conventional slip bond is one whose dissociation tends to become faster with increased tensile load under the relevant conditions. A catch bond exhibits an increase in lifetime with force over a certain range. These are descriptions of bond behavior, not names for different chemical bond types such as ionic or covalent bonds. The FimH example shows why the sign of the force response should not be assumed to be identical for every molecular system. A qualitative diagram comparing the two behaviors must also indicate that the catch-bond description applies over a limited range rather than implying indefinite strengthening. The experimentally relevant quantity is the lifetime or dissociation behavior under the conditions examined, not a universal law about all adhesive cells.

At a tissue interface the physical chemistry is embedded in an open biological system. Cells arrive and leave, ligands may be renewed, and surrounding material can move. Consequently, a mathematical equilibrium model is best used here to explain elementary concepts such as saturation and affinity, while actual colonization requires a description of changing populations in space and time. Keeping these levels distinct allows mathematical reasoning to clarify rather than obscure the biology. The same distinction appears in enzyme kinetics, where a model describing a purified molecular reaction cannot by itself predict an entire physiological response. The strength of the model is that its assumptions specify exactly what the derived quantities mean.

12. Observations, measurements and what they establish

The language used in a scientific result should match what has actually been observed. The term bacterial association may include organisms located on a host-cell surface and organisms that are already internalized. Unless the observation distinguishes these locations, association is a more defensible term than invasion. Similarly, a measurement of bacterial material associated with a tissue does not necessarily count viable cells. These distinctions are not merely semantic. They determine whether the observation can support a proposed mechanism. A strong examination answer begins by identifying the measured variable, then makes an inference limited to that variable and the relevant controls.

Microscopy can reveal the positions of bacteria relative to epithelial cells and extracellular material. In a two-dimensional image, an object above a cell may appear to overlap its interior. Such overlap does not establish that the object lies inside the cell. Information about depth or the boundaries of the host-cell membrane is needed before the location is assigned confidently. A drawing representing a bacterium within the outline of a cell can therefore be misleading if it does not specify whether that outline represents a surface projection or a cross-section. This is one reason a diagram-based question may ask which additional observation distinguishes attachment from internalization.

Measurements of molecular abundance likewise require careful interpretation. A change in the signal from an antibody recognizing a bacterial surface protein could reflect a change in protein abundance, accessibility of the recognized region or another property affecting detection. A change in binding of a soluble host ligand could reflect a different feature of the same protein. These measurements are complementary rather than interchangeable. If the surface-associated protein is present but ligand recognition is not observed, the result is consistent with altered binding or accessibility, but it is not alone proof of a particular structural explanation. The appropriate conclusion is that the available observations narrow the possibilities without necessarily resolving all of them.

A comparison of bacterial populations must also distinguish absolute numbers from fractions. If two observations begin with different numbers of objects, the larger absolute number associated with a surface need not correspond to a greater proportion of the original population. Normalization can correct for that difference when the denominator is known and appropriate. However, normalization does not fix every possible confounder. If the populations have changed in viability or composition, an association fraction based on the original number may incorporate several processes. Students should identify the denominator and the time to which it refers before interpreting a reported percentage.

Another important distinction is between an absence of evidence and evidence of absence. Failure to detect a binding interaction with a particular measurement may indicate that the interaction is absent, below the sensitivity of the measurement or inaccessible to that method. Similarly, detection of one receptor does not prove that no alternative receptor contributes. A scientific conclusion should reflect the resolution and scope of the observation. The use of independent evidence helps constrain competing interpretations, but a single observation rarely rules out every possible explanation. This habit of reasoning is particularly important when interpreting hypothetical genetic changes or antibody-blocking results in CSIR-NET questions.
