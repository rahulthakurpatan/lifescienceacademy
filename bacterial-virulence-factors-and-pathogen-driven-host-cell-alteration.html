From arrival to alteration

A bacterium attached to the surface of an intestinal epithelial cell faces a different problem from a bacterium enclosed within a macrophage. The first occupies a site where host fluid, mechanical movement and competition may remove it. The second has crossed a boundary but now encounters an intracellular environment that may be hostile to survival. Neither location by itself determines disease. An important part of bacterial pathogenesis is the alteration of the host processes that normally maintain a tissue or remove unwanted microorganisms. Understanding these alterations requires the same cell biology used to explain ordinary membrane traffic, signal transduction and cell survival.

Pathogenicity is the capacity to cause disease in a susceptible host. Virulence describes the degree of disease-producing capacity in a particular biological setting. It is not an absolute property that can be assigned independently of host species, tissue, immune status and the outcome being measured. A virulence factor is a microbial feature that contributes to infection or disease under relevant conditions. It may be a surface structure, a secreted product or another molecular property. A factor essential for the ordinary growth of many bacteria is not automatically a specialized virulence factor, although normal growth and metabolism are necessary for many infections.

The term host-cell alteration refers to a change in the molecular, physiological or structural state of a host cell caused directly or indirectly by the microorganism. A cell may remain alive but transport excessive ions, lose normal epithelial polarity, change its secretory activity or rearrange its actin filaments. Another cell may die, not because the bacterium physically destroys it, but because host stress and signaling pathways activate cell death. These outcomes are different from the initial binding of a microbe to a host receptor. Attachment is a location-changing event, whereas alteration involves a change in host function.

A useful distinction is between the position of the bacterium and the position of its biologically active molecules. A microorganism can remain outside a host cell while one of its protein products enters that cell. Conversely, an internalized bacterium can remain separated from the host cytosol by a membrane. A diagram showing a bacterium within the outline of a cell does not establish that the bacterium occupies cytosol. Many errors in examination reasoning come from overlooking this membrane boundary.

Disease can therefore be separated into a sequence of dependent but distinguishable outcomes: exposure, association, cellular interaction, altered physiology and tissue injury. These stages need not occur with equal efficiency. Extensive bacterial attachment can occur with little detectable injury, and severe physiological disturbance can occur without extensive tissue invasion. A virulence factor should be understood by identifying the host process that it changes and the location at which its action occurs, rather than by memorizing a list of names.

There is a further distinction between changes directly imposed by bacterial molecules and changes caused by the host response. A bacterial product might modify a host enzyme directly. A host cell might instead detect microbial material and change its gene expression or release inflammatory signals. Both processes can occur during the same infection. An observation such as swelling, membrane damage or inflammatory gene expression is therefore a starting point for explanation, not sufficient evidence for identifying the responsible molecular mechanism.

Scientific evidence and the development of a cellular explanation

Early work on bacterial disease had to distinguish two questions that had previously been inseparable: whether an organism is present in the diseased tissue, and whether a substance associated with that organism accounts for a particular symptom. The germ theory of disease established the importance of microorganisms, but it did not by itself explain the biochemical route from infection to injury. A causal link between a microorganism and disease is compatible with several cellular explanations. The organism might occupy a tissue, release an injurious molecule, or evoke a host response that contributes to damage.

The study of diphtheria contributed to the classical recognition of soluble bacterial toxins in the late nineteenth century. Roux and Yersin's work on diphtheria toxin helped establish that biological activity associated with a bacterium could be separable from the intact bacterial cell. The important historical advance was conceptual: the etiological agent and the immediate cause of a cellular disturbance need not be the same physical object. Modern terminology distinguishes the bacterium that establishes the infection from the product that carries out a particular molecular action.

Later studies of cholera revealed a different kind of explanatory problem. Fluid secretion had to be understood through epithelial transport and intracellular signaling rather than through destruction of every intestinal epithelial cell. This change in perspective is important because a tissue can be profoundly dysfunctional even if its cells have retained much of their morphology. The biological action of a toxin may therefore be defined by a change in the regulation of a normal process rather than by a visible lesion.

The discovery of cyclic AMP as a cellular signaling intermediate provided a language in which to explain persistent disturbances of signaling. Instead of treating fluid movement as an isolated symptom, researchers could connect it with a change in an intracellular messenger and with the regulation of epithelial transport. A mechanism of this sort must account for the order of events: an upstream molecular change influences an enzyme, the enzyme influences messenger concentration, and the messenger influences downstream targets. A convincing explanation also has to accommodate the fact that each of those intermediates has a normal physiological role.

The development of electron microscopy and fluorescence microscopy gave scientists a different way to investigate intracellular infection. An object seen within an animal cell could now be examined in relation to membranes and cytoskeletal filaments. Classical observations of actin structures associated with intracellular Listeria in the work of Tilney and Portnoy illustrated how the arrangement of a host structural protein can become part of the explanation of infection. The value of the observation was not simply that it produced an unusual image. It connected host cell architecture with the spatial behavior of an intracellular object.

In all of these historical developments, the advance depended on separating levels of explanation. Identifying a bacterium is not equivalent to identifying its molecular product. Identifying a product is not equivalent to identifying its biochemical substrate. Identifying a substrate is not equivalent to explaining an organism-level symptom. This is why a rigorous textbook account proceeds from the molecule through the affected cellular process to the tissue consequence, while retaining evidence for each link.

The terminology used in current research can make these historical distinctions appear obvious in retrospect. In fact, terms such as receptor, second messenger and intracellular compartment refer to biological models that were developed through many different kinds of observations. A student should not memorize a discovery date as a substitute for understanding why a new model explained an observation that an earlier model could not. The history of pathogenesis is useful because it shows how general principles of cell biology became necessary for the explanation of infectious disease.

Delivery, recognition and molecular action

The physical separation between a bacterium and a eukaryotic cell is fundamental. Bacterial proteins made in the microbial cytoplasm cannot freely cross the bacterial envelope or the host plasma membrane. A bacterial product that acts on the outside of a host cell has a different delivery requirement from one that acts on a cytosolic host protein. Binding to a cell and entering the cell are distinct properties; neither implies that a molecule retains biological activity after entering.

A secreted protein toxin is a bacterial product capable of disturbing host function. Some toxins act on an exposed host membrane, while others bind specific surface structures and subsequently reach an internal compartment. The term exotoxin refers to toxic products, commonly proteins, released by bacteria. It must not be confused with endotoxin, the inflammatory lipid A component of Gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide. Lipid A is not an enzyme toxin that modifies a specific cytosolic substrate by the same mechanism as the classical protein toxins discussed here.

An effector is a molecule that changes a target process in another cell. In bacterial pathogenesis the word often refers to a protein delivered across the host membrane by a bacterial secretion apparatus. The type III secretion system, abbreviated T3SS, is an example found in certain Gram-negative bacteria. The central conceptual distinction is between delivery machinery and the proteins whose activities alter host behavior. The machinery provides a route across biological barriers; the effector accounts for the change in the host target. Neither component should automatically be assigned the catalytic function of the other.

The extracellular toxin versus injected effector distinction describes typical routes, not mutually exclusive chemical categories. Both can be proteins, both can alter host signaling and both may have enzymatic activities. Not every toxin is released as a freely diffusible protein, and not every bacterial effector depends on a T3SS. A mechanistic description is clearer if it specifies the product, the route, the host compartment and the response.

Consider the contrast between a receptor-binding toxin and a normal extracellular hormone. Both may show a selective association with a cell surface. However, the hormone normally triggers a regulated physiological response, and its signaling is limited by cellular mechanisms that reduce the signal after the stimulus disappears. A toxin may alter an internal regulatory component so that the normal relationship between extracellular stimulus and cellular response is lost. Specific binding alone does not make two molecules physiologically equivalent.

Many protein toxins illustrate modular protein organization. A binding component recognizes a host surface molecule, whereas an active component produces the characteristic biochemical effect. In an A-B toxin model the letters identify activities rather than a universal number of polypeptide chains. Some toxins contain multiple binding subunits. Surface recognition establishes where the protein can associate; catalytic activity establishes what happens if the relevant active component reaches its substrate. Confusing these functions obscures the reason why receptor distribution and intracellular susceptibility are both important.

Membrane localization also constrains what an observation means. A fluorescent signal at the cell surface indicates association but does not establish a biochemical change in the cytosol. A whole-cell signal may not distinguish membrane-bound material from material inside the cell. Likewise, an altered host response does not identify the route used to deliver a molecule. Reliable mechanistic conclusions require evidence appropriate to the particular step being inferred.

Historically, the distinction between bacterial cells and the toxic activity of their products emerged from studies in which toxic effects were recognized separately from the presence of intact bacteria. Modern cell biology extends that distinction by asking which molecular event accounts for an altered physiological state. The focus is not on the mere presence of a microbial product, but on the explanatory connection between that product and a measurable change in host function.

Effector delivery as a cell-biological distinction

A secretion system solves a transport problem rather than directly defining the phenotype of an infected cell. A protein produced by a bacterium must be transported to the location at which it can interact with its relevant target. In the case of a type III secretion system, the central feature is the delivery of certain bacterial proteins to a host cell across a cellular boundary. The existence of this route explains how bacterial molecules can alter intracellular processes even when the bacterium itself is located at the host surface. A drawing of this interaction should distinguish the bacterium, host plasma membrane, delivery apparatus and intracellular region.

The bacterial envelope contains several distinct structures in Gram-negative organisms, including an inner membrane and an outer membrane. A secretion apparatus that mediates transfer across this envelope is not equivalent to a eukaryotic receptor embedded in the host membrane. The two molecular systems belong to different cells and have different functions. Host receptors normally participate in recognition or signaling, whereas the bacterial delivery system provides a route for the movement of bacterial protein cargo. The direction of transport and the cellular ownership of each molecular component must therefore be clear in a mechanistic diagram.

The word effector refers to the role of a delivered molecule in changing a target process. An effector need not have the same biochemical activity as the structural components of the delivery system. If an observation establishes that a product has reached a host cell, it does not establish which particular host protein it affects. Likewise, a change in a host protein's activity does not on its own demonstrate the existence of a particular delivery mechanism. Delivery and action are separate causal claims and require evidence appropriate to each claim.

This distinction also explains why the phrase secretion-system activity can be ambiguous. One observation may concern a structural component of an apparatus, another the location of a particular cargo, and a third the activity of a host target. These observations should not be treated as interchangeable. A system may be physically present without every relevant cargo being delivered at the same time, and detection of cargo does not necessarily establish a measurable biological response. In examination problems, the measured feature must be matched to the stage it actually describes.

An instructive textbook contrast is between an extracellular bacterium that affects a distant cell through a soluble product and a bacterium whose interaction with a nearby cell is associated with a contact-dependent delivery apparatus. Both examples can lead to changes in host signaling. The principal difference is the route and spatial context of delivery, not the general ability to change a host cell. A comparison that classifies both examples merely as intracellular infection would obscure the fact that an intact bacterium need not enter the responding cell in either situation.

Bacterial factors can have several biological roles during an infection. A surface association may precede a change in cytoskeletal organization, while later changes in cellular compartments may be associated with a different stage of infection. These stages should be separated when interpreting a general term such as virulence. An observation at one stage is not automatically evidence about every later stage. For example, a measurement of surface association does not by itself establish that host-cell trafficking is altered.

The mechanisms described in this chapter also show how pathogens and ordinary cell physiology can be studied using a common conceptual vocabulary. Delivery routes are constrained by membrane topology; signaling effects are constrained by the identities and states of molecular targets; structural changes are constrained by the properties of cellular networks; and tissue outcomes are constrained by physiological organization. A coherent explanation does not need a separate set of physical principles for each pathogen. It requires careful identification of the normal biological process and the nature of the disturbance.

Contrasting toxin mechanisms and biological consequences

A classification based on what a bacterial product does to the host is more useful for this chapter than a classification based only on the bacterial species from which it originates. Three broad outcomes illustrate this point: disturbance of a regulatory switch, disturbance of an essential cellular process and disturbance of membrane integrity. In the first case the cell may remain metabolically active while executing an inappropriate response. In the second case its ability to maintain essential molecular functions is compromised. In the third case the barrier that keeps the cell's interior distinct from the exterior is damaged. Although these outcomes can eventually overlap, their initial biochemical causes differ.

Diphtheria toxin is a classical contrast to cholera toxin. The two toxins illustrate that a similar broad class of chemical modification can produce different cellular consequences when the substrate differs. Diphtheria toxin affects eukaryotic elongation factor 2, an important protein in translation. This factor normally participates in the movement of the ribosome along its messenger RNA template during elongation. If its function is compromised, efficient protein synthesis becomes impaired. The cell then faces a problem distinct from that of an epithelial cell whose ion secretion is dysregulated: it cannot adequately replenish proteins that are being turned over.

The biological effect of reduced protein synthesis develops in relation to the lifetimes of existing cellular proteins. Some proteins are stable and persist for a considerable period, whereas others are continually degraded and replaced. The decline of a particular protein population can therefore lag behind the biochemical disturbance that initially prevents its replacement. An observation made soon after interference with translation may differ greatly from an observation made later. The timing of cellular dysfunction cannot be read directly from the timing of the first biochemical event.

This comparison reinforces a principle from fundamental processes: the substrate of a molecular alteration determines the specific stage that is disrupted. Interference with a translation elongation factor does not necessarily prevent the initial transcription of RNA from DNA. Messenger RNA can be present while protein production is diminished. Similarly, an observed reduction in a particular protein may result from impaired synthesis or accelerated degradation. A whole-cell protein measurement without evidence about synthesis and turnover is insufficient to localize the primary disturbance.

Membrane-active toxins represent another mechanistic category. The integrity of the lipid bilayer is essential for maintaining chemical differences across the membrane. Proteins that disrupt membrane integrity can increase inappropriate exchange between normally separated environments. This may disturb ion gradients, cellular volume and membrane potential. In contrast to a toxin acting through a specific intracellular signaling switch, a membrane lesion can affect multiple processes because so many cellular functions depend on an intact boundary.

It is important not to equate every membrane-active toxin with a physiological ion channel. A normal ion channel generally has defined selectivity and regulated gating. Membrane disruption can produce inappropriate permeability without the usual controls that preserve homeostasis. The presence of a route for ion movement is not necessarily beneficial to a cell, even when a similar ion moves through normal transport proteins. The biological meaning depends on the location, selectivity and regulation of the pathway.

Cells may respond to membrane injury through mechanisms that restore or remove damaged membrane regions. The effectiveness of such responses depends on the scale and character of the injury and on the state of the cell. Thus the observation that a membrane has become permeable does not establish that recovery is impossible. In an examination question, the distinction between a transient permeability change and irreversible cellular damage matters when interpreting observations made at different times.

The inflammatory activity of bacterial envelope components must also be distinguished from the direct biochemical action of a secreted protein toxin. Lipopolysaccharide is a structural component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Its lipid A portion is recognized by components of the host innate immune system. The resulting host signaling can produce substantial physiological changes. These effects are not explained by the same kind of receptor-associated intracellular enzyme activity used to describe cholera or diphtheria toxin.

An antibody directed against a protein toxin may interfere with its association with a host cell or with another necessary step in its extracellular action. Such interference is an example of neutralization, which is conceptually different from killing the bacterium itself. The corresponding immunological mechanisms have been treated in the preceding subunit. Here the relevant implication is that the organism and its soluble product are separable biological targets. A reduction in a toxin-mediated phenotype does not necessarily establish that the microorganisms are absent.

A toxin that has lost its harmful activity but retains sufficient antigenic properties is known as a toxoid. The classical use of toxoids in immunization follows from the distinction between the structural features recognized by antibodies and those needed for a toxin's biological effect. Antigen recognition and toxicity are not identical properties. A molecular product can therefore be relevant to immunity even when the specific damaging function that originally made it important is no longer present.

The division of toxins into signaling-active, protein-synthesis-disrupting and membrane-active examples is deliberately functional rather than exhaustive. Some products influence more than one cellular process, and secondary effects can spread far beyond the initial molecular target. A useful question about any proposed toxin mechanism is to identify the earliest well-supported host change. Later disturbances may be consequences of that change rather than additional independent actions of the toxin itself.

Specificity, protein domains and response magnitude

A protein that associates with the surface of an animal cell encounters a chemically diverse environment. Surface proteins, glycolipids and carbohydrate chains display three-dimensional structures that may interact with complementary regions of a bacterial product. These interactions often depend on noncovalent forces, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions and the hydrophobic effect. No single category of interaction is sufficient to explain specificity in every case. The shape and chemical properties of the interacting surfaces together determine whether an association is energetically favorable.

Binding affinity refers to the tendency of an individual binding interaction to favor its associated state. Avidity refers to the combined strength of multiple interactions, which can depend on how the interacting molecules are arranged. In a multivalent interaction, a complex with several binding sites may remain associated even when one contact is transiently lost. That does not imply that each individual contact has a high affinity. The distinction is important for reasoning about proteins with more than one binding domain, including the pentameric binding component of cholera toxin.

Molecular recognition is selective but not perfectly binary. A host membrane may contain chemically related structures with different affinities for a product. The effective amount of association may also depend on whether the surface structure is accessible in its membrane environment. Expression of a particular molecule at the RNA level does not guarantee that it is present in an accessible form at the appropriate surface. Protein processing, membrane sorting and the orientation of molecular domains all intervene between gene expression and surface recognition.

The active component of a protein toxin illustrates another aspect of specificity. An enzyme recognizes particular features of a substrate and catalyzes a reaction involving that substrate. The concentration of an enzyme is not equal to the number of substrate molecules that can be modified over time. Catalysis can allow a relatively small number of active molecules to produce a substantial biochemical change, provided that substrates and required reactants remain available. However, amplification has limits because the substrate pool is finite and cells continually turn over molecular components.

It is also necessary to distinguish binding to a receptor from the normal physiological signaling function of that receptor. A host molecule can act as a binding site without transmitting a conventional signal through a cytoplasmic domain. A membrane glycolipid, for example, does not have the same molecular architecture as a receptor tyrosine kinase. Recognition by a bacterial product therefore does not imply activation of the familiar signaling pathway associated with a protein receptor. The molecule that mediates attachment and the intracellular molecule that mediates the response may be completely different entities.

An association between a host molecule and a bacterial product may be necessary for a particular response without being sufficient for that response. If binding occurs but the biologically active region does not encounter its target, the downstream effect may be absent. Conversely, a normal signaling response to a different stimulus does not establish that the same host surface binding site is required. The idea of necessity and sufficiency is central to interpreting molecular perturbations: a missing response can identify a dependency, whereas an observed response may still be consistent with several causal routes.

The distinction between affinity and physiological effectiveness also prevents an incorrect inference from quantitative binding observations. Two cells may exhibit comparable amounts of surface-associated material but show very different cellular outcomes. A difference could occur after binding. Conversely, if a downstream response is saturated, differences in the amount of binding may produce little difference in the response. A graph of binding versus concentration and a graph of physiological response versus concentration need not have identical shapes.

Normal signaling systems contain mechanisms that reduce the effect of continued or repeated stimulation. Protein dephosphorylation, messenger degradation, receptor internalization and protein turnover can all change the duration of a response. These are not equivalent molecular processes. For example, reducing the abundance of a messenger does not necessarily restore the original regulatory state of an upstream protein. Restoration of physiological function may depend on the recovery of multiple components operating at different time scales.

A signaling disturbance: cholera toxin

An intestinal epithelium must regulate the movement of salts and water while preserving a barrier between the intestinal contents and the underlying tissue. Epithelial cells are polarized: their apical surface faces the lumen, whereas their basolateral surface faces the interstitial environment. The unequal distribution of channels, carriers and pumps across these surfaces allows directional transport. A cell can remain structurally intact while the rate and direction of its net transport change substantially.

Cholera provides a textbook illustration of abnormal transport caused by a bacterial product rather than by extensive invasion of epithelial cells. Vibrio cholerae colonizes the small intestine and cholera toxin acts on epithelial cells. The toxin has an A component associated with a pentameric B component. GM1 ganglioside, a membrane glycolipid, is a classical receptor for the binding component. Binding helps associate the toxin with a susceptible host membrane, but the downstream disturbance is produced by the active component within the cell, not simply by occupancy of GM1.

The toxin undergoes intracellular trafficking. The distinction between initial uptake and arrival at an intracellular destination is important because a molecule enclosed in the lumen of a membrane-bound compartment is still separated from a cytosolic target. In the standard textbook model cholera toxin follows a retrograde route through the secretory system and the active A1 portion ultimately acts in the cytosol. The biological meaning of this route is that the catalytic component and its substrate become accessible to each other.

The main signaling target is Gs alpha, the stimulatory alpha subunit of a heterotrimeric G protein. In ordinary signaling its GTP-bound form stimulates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP. GTP hydrolysis returns the alpha subunit toward its inactive GDP-bound state. This molecular switch allows the activity of adenylyl cyclase to depend on both activation and termination of signaling. A receptor normally regulates the switch without needing to remain active forever.

Cholera toxin catalyzes an ADP-ribosylation reaction affecting Gs alpha. The modification impairs normal GTPase-dependent signal termination, favoring prolonged stimulatory signaling. The important distinction is that the toxin is not itself adenylyl cyclase and does not directly manufacture cAMP. It changes the regulatory state of a host protein, which in turn influences a host enzyme. The amplified cellular response therefore depends on multiple host molecules downstream of the toxin.

Cyclic AMP is a second messenger. Increased cAMP alters the activity of cAMP-dependent protein kinase, commonly called protein kinase A or PKA. PKA-dependent phosphorylation influences the activity of CFTR, an apical anion channel expressed in intestinal epithelial cells. Increased chloride secretion and changes in absorptive transport disturb net salt and water movement. Water follows osmotic gradients rather than being manufactured by the toxin. The increase in fluid within the intestinal lumen reflects the behavior of a coupled transport system.

CFTR is a member of the ATP-binding cassette protein family, but it functions as an ion channel rather than as a typical primary active solute pump. ATP-dependent gating and phosphorylation-dependent regulation are not the same as pumping chloride against its electrochemical gradient. Ion movement through an open channel depends on the gradient maintained by the entire epithelial transport network. A change in open probability can increase flux without changing the basic chemical nature of the transported ion.

The cholera example illustrates why physiological output cannot be predicted merely from the name of an affected protein. The direction of secretion depends on membrane polarization, ionic gradients and the activities of other transport proteins. In a cell that does not express the relevant downstream transport machinery, an increase in cAMP would not necessarily reproduce the intestinal response. Similarly, an alteration of CFTR-dependent secretion does not itself establish whether the initial cause was a microbial toxin, a hormone or another stimulus.

A common oversimplification is to say that the affected G protein is permanently active in the sense that the cell can never recover. The conventional examination description of sustained activation refers to disruption of normal GTPase-dependent switching. Signal magnitude and duration still depend on substrate availability, turnover of modified molecules, phosphodiesterases that remove cAMP and the viability of the affected cells. Long-lasting activation is a mechanistic tendency, not a claim of mathematically infinite signaling.

The most informative comparison is between signaling that is normally regulated by an external stimulus and signaling that persists after its regulatory component has been modified. Both pathways may use adenylyl cyclase, cAMP and PKA. The difference is located upstream of cAMP accumulation, and the phenotype can be understood by tracking the causal chain through each distinct molecular step.

Epithelial transport and tissue function

The movement of water across an epithelial tissue is a consequence of the thermodynamic behavior of water and the distribution of dissolved solutes. It is helpful to distinguish the movement of a solute across a membrane from movement of water across the tissue as a whole. Solute transport changes the chemical environment on each side of a barrier. Where water permeability is appreciable, differences in water chemical potential drive water redistribution. An increase in water flow does not require that water be actively pumped by a toxin.

Intestinal epithelial cells ordinarily participate in both secretion and absorption. These opposing processes are not contradictory because they occur through different transport mechanisms, in different physiological contexts, and sometimes in different cell populations. Net movement depends on their combined rates. If secretory transport increases while absorptive transport remains unchanged, net fluid movement may shift toward secretion. If absorptive transport simultaneously decreases, the shift can be larger. A measured net output therefore cannot identify the individual contributions without additional information.

The basolateral sodium–potassium ATPase helps establish ionic gradients across many animal cell membranes. Those gradients can support secondary active transport through carriers. A channel at the apical membrane may provide a route for an anion to move down its electrochemical gradient. These components act as a system. The energetic cost of maintaining the gradients is ultimately borne by cellular metabolism and ion pumps, even though passage through an open channel itself does not directly require the channel to pump each ion using ATP hydrolysis.

An electrochemical gradient contains both a chemical and an electrical contribution. For an ion of charge z moving from compartment 1 to compartment 2, the molar free energy difference can be expressed as RT ln(a2/a1) + zF(V2 − V1), where a represents ion activity, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, F is Faraday's constant, and V is electrical potential. The sign of the expression depends on the specified direction. A negative value favors movement in that direction under the stated conditions; the gradient is not defined solely by the concentration ratio.

For chloride, z is negative. A voltage contribution that favors the movement of a cation in one direction has the opposite sign for chloride. It is therefore incorrect to reason about chloride movement by copying the electrical argument for sodium without reversing the charge sign. In an epithelial cell, the actual gradient across the apical membrane depends on the intracellular ionic composition and the lumenal environment. The presence of a channel establishes a possible route for flux, not its direction in every imaginable circumstance.

As an illustration unrelated to an actual infection, consider a cell in which the chemical contribution to the molar free energy for anion movement out of the cell is negative 2 kilojoules per mole and the electrical contribution is positive 0.5 kilojoules per mole. The net contribution is negative 1.5 kilojoules per mole, so outward movement is energetically favored. If the electrical contribution instead becomes positive 3 kilojoules per mole, the net becomes positive 1 kilojoule per mole and the favored direction reverses. This exercise is a reminder that the electrochemical gradient must be evaluated as a whole.

Channel gating regulates whether a pathway for ion movement is available at a particular moment. The driving force regulates the tendency for ions to move when the pathway is available. These concepts should not be conflated. Even a channel with a high open probability may carry little net current when the electrochemical gradient approaches zero. Conversely, a strong gradient cannot produce appreciable flux through a channel that is almost always closed. The distinction between permeability and driving force is equally useful in the study of nerves, transport epithelia and organelles.

Epithelial tissues are multicellular barriers. Tight junctions help maintain differences between the apical and basolateral environments and limit the unrestricted passage of material between cells. A change in transcellular transport refers to movement across the cellular membranes; a change in paracellular transport refers to movement between neighboring cells through the junctional region. Both can contribute to the observed movement of ions and water. A physiological change that preserves cellular viability need not preserve the normal balance between these pathways.

The effect of cholera toxin belongs to the category of functional disturbance because abnormal secretion does not require direct destruction of all the cells carrying out transport. This is not a claim that every bacterial toxin acts without damaging cells. Rather, it illustrates why the molecular target and tissue architecture must be specified before predicting the physiological outcome. An examination problem that contrasts secretory dysfunction with extensive epithelial destruction is testing this distinction.

Finally, the magnitude of fluid movement depends on more than the activity of a single channel or the concentration of a single messenger. The surface area of functioning epithelium, the composition of fluids on either side, the availability of metabolic energy and compensatory responses all affect the outcome. An isolated cellular signaling measurement can clarify part of the mechanism but cannot by itself predict a quantitative clinical manifestation.

Quantitative reasoning about a second messenger

The concentration of a second messenger at a given moment reflects the balance between its formation and removal. A large concentration does not by itself prove that synthesis is increased: reduced degradation can produce a similar observation. A simplified balance equation is useful because it makes those two mechanisms explicit without pretending that a real cell is spatially uniform.

Let C be the cytosolic concentration of a hypothetical second messenger in micromoles per litre and let v be a constant rate of production in micromoles per litre per minute. Assume first-order removal with rate constant k in inverse minutes. Over a short interval, the net rate of concentration change is dC/dt = v − kC. The two terms on the right have identical dimensions. Multiplying a concentration by an inverse time gives concentration change per unit time. At steady state the rate of change is zero, so the concentration satisfies v = kC and C = v/k.

The steady state is not an equilibrium at which synthesis and removal stop. Production continues, and removal continues at the same rate. This is an important distinction for signaling molecules that are continually formed from precursors and destroyed or converted into other products. The model also assumes that the production rate is independent of C and that removal is not saturated, assumptions that may fail in a real signaling network.

Suppose the production rate of a hypothetical messenger is 0.6 micromoles per litre per minute and k is 0.3 per minute. The model gives a steady concentration of 2 micromoles per litre. If the production rate becomes 1.2 while removal remains unchanged, the new steady concentration is 4 micromoles per litre. The same concentration of 4 could instead arise if production remains at 0.6 and k falls to 0.15 per minute. This numerical example is an invented teaching illustration, not a measured value for cholera or any infection.

The concentration alone cannot distinguish the two explanations. Time-dependent behavior may provide additional information. If formation is absent and first-order removal remains valid, concentration obeys C(t) = C(0) exp(−kt). Its half-life is ln(2)/k. With k equal to 0.3 per minute the half-life is about 2.31 minutes; with k equal to 0.15 per minute it is about 4.62 minutes. Thus the two hypothetical states with equal steady concentrations differ in the time required for an existing signal to decay under the assumptions of the model.

There is no need to infer a linear relation between messenger concentration and epithelial fluid secretion. Downstream kinases, channels and membrane transport systems may show thresholds or saturation. Cells also contain local signaling compartments in which the effective concentration near a target differs from a whole-cell average. The model helps distinguish formation from removal but cannot replace the evidence that connects a messenger to a physiological response.

A second distinction concerns amount and concentration. If the volume of the compartment changes, the same number of messenger molecules can give a different concentration. The simple rate equation assumes that the relevant volume remains approximately constant. In a real epithelial cell, changes in transport can themselves influence cell volume and regulatory responses. The mathematical simplification is appropriate for isolating one principle, not for predicting the severity of intestinal disease.

Host shape and the actin cytoskeleton

The actin cytoskeleton is a changing network of protein filaments that supports cell shape, motility and membrane remodeling. Actin is not merely a rigid structural scaffold. Monomeric actin can join filaments, filaments can disassemble, and their arrangement can be reorganized by regulatory proteins. A host cell uses these properties to extend its edge, internalize material and maintain junctions with neighboring cells.

The actin network is linked to the plasma membrane through many proteins. When a host cell changes the distribution of actin assembly near its surface, membrane geometry can change as a consequence. The shape change is a visible readout of the underlying remodeling but is not a direct measurement of the activity of any individual regulator. Different molecular perturbations can produce superficially similar changes in cell shape.

Bacterial effectors can disturb host cytoskeletal regulation. The previous chapter treats bacterial uptake and the distinction between localized receptor-driven remodeling and induced ruffling. The present question is broader: how can the altered host machinery continue to influence cell architecture and physiology? Cytoskeletal changes may occur at a surface contact, at an intracellular compartment or near a junction. Their significance depends on location and on the normal role of actin in that region.

An epithelial cell uses cortical actin in maintaining its apical architecture, and actin-dependent structures contribute to the organization of junctions. Disrupting this organization may alter barrier properties even before a cell dies. Barrier dysfunction and plasma membrane rupture must therefore be distinguished. The former can involve inappropriate passage between neighboring cells; the latter involves loss of integrity of an individual cell membrane.

The redistribution of actin can also be understood in terms of competing cellular functions. A network assembled near one region may require monomers and regulatory proteins drawn from a shared cellular pool. Nevertheless, a change in local filament abundance does not necessarily mean that the total amount of actin protein has changed. Redistribution, altered polymerization, changed gene expression and degradation are separate explanations. A student interpreting a micrograph must identify which claim the observation supports.

In the classical literature, certain intracellular bacteria, including Listeria monocytogenes, are associated with actin-rich structures in infected cells. These observations contributed to the recognition that intracellular particles can interact with the host cytoskeleton. The scientific lesson is that filament organization provides a connection between a molecular interaction and the spatial behavior of a cell or intracellular object. It should not be confused with bacterial flagellar motility, which depends on a different molecular apparatus.

The interpretation of a static image requires particular care. Accumulated actin may reflect local assembly, impaired disassembly or redistribution. A series of images may reveal movement or changes over time, but evidence of temporal association alone does not establish which molecular component is necessary. The distinction between the observed structure and its proposed cause is a recurring principle throughout cell biology.

The molecular logic of actin organization

Actin filaments have structural polarity because their subunits are assembled in a consistent orientation. The two ends have different assembly and disassembly behavior. This polarity is relevant to the movement of cells and to the formation of cellular protrusions. An apparently stationary cell can nevertheless contain highly dynamic actin filaments whose individual subunits are continually exchanged.

The amount of filamentous actin is regulated in part by the availability of soluble actin and by proteins that influence filament assembly and turnover. The spatial arrangement of the filaments is equally important. A cell may organize bundles of filaments in one region and a branched network in another. A branched network can support a broad protruding edge, whereas bundles can contribute to other forms of mechanical organization. Consequently the observation that actin is polymerized does not by itself specify the geometry or function of the resulting network.

Small GTPases of the Rho family are important host regulators of the cytoskeleton. Their activities are commonly described in terms of transitions between nucleotide-bound states, with regulatory proteins influencing activation and inactivation. Rac and Cdc42 are standard examples associated with particular patterns of actin remodeling, although their functions depend on the cellular setting. These molecules do not assemble the entire cytoskeleton themselves. Instead they influence downstream regulators that modify the organization of actin filaments.

Host cells use networks of these regulatory proteins to integrate signals at particular locations. The same amount of a signaling molecule distributed uniformly throughout a cell may not reproduce the effect of activity concentrated in a small region near the membrane. A local regulatory event can produce a spatially organized response because the relevant structural proteins and membrane-associated components are available there. Spatial information is therefore a fundamental part of cell signaling.

In the classical trigger model of bacterial entry, Salmonella is associated with effector-induced changes in host actin organization. The preceding chapter explains the entry process. The present chapter uses that model to identify the two levels of the causal explanation: delivery of a microbial product and a response mediated by host cytoskeletal machinery. The shape change is not caused by the bacterium becoming a new source of eukaryotic actin. It depends on the host proteins whose normal roles include membrane remodeling.

The distinction between activation and localization is particularly important when interpreting cytoskeletal data. A measurement of the total amount of a small GTPase does not indicate how much of it is in its active nucleotide-bound state. A measurement of its active fraction does not necessarily indicate where in the cell the activity occurs. A microscopy observation showing accumulation near a membrane does not alone establish the activation state. Each measurement addresses a different aspect of the underlying regulatory process.

Mechanical changes at the cell cortex can also affect membrane tension. When a cell changes shape, the plasma membrane and its connections to the cytoskeleton must accommodate altered geometry. A region of newly extended membrane requires appropriate membrane organization as well as structural forces. A cytoskeletal phenotype can therefore reflect interactions between actin regulation and membrane trafficking. These processes should not be treated as unrelated topics simply because they are taught in different chapters.

Actin is also involved in cell adhesion. Junctional structures between neighboring epithelial cells are linked directly or indirectly to the cortical cytoskeleton. Alterations in actin organization may influence the stability of these structures, the distribution of membrane proteins or the transmission of mechanical forces between cells. The consequence can extend from one cell to the organization of the tissue. This connection explains why a molecular change in a cytoskeletal regulator can be relevant to barrier physiology.

When comparing two micrographs, a student should distinguish the total intensity of an actin label from the shape and position of the structures it reveals. A brighter local region may represent a greater concentration of labeled actin, but it does not necessarily establish an increase in total cellular actin. Differences in the thickness of the imaged region, optical settings or accessibility of the label may also affect apparent intensity. A conclusion about organization is more secure when the relevant spatial relationships are visible and the measurements are comparable.

The historical study of intracellular bacterial association with actin also established that static morphology and movement are different kinds of evidence. An elongated filament-rich structure is consistent with a directional process, but a sequence of observations is required to establish whether an associated object actually moves. The arrangement of a structure relative to the direction of motion may provide additional mechanistic evidence. The textbook lesson is that a structural explanation becomes stronger when it accounts for a dynamic observation.

The host cell may have several actin-dependent processes occurring simultaneously. Endocytosis, junctional remodeling and changes in cell shape can all involve the same broad class of structural protein. It follows that a broad disturbance of actin dynamics can affect many cellular functions at once. Such a disturbance cannot automatically be interpreted as evidence that only one infection-specific pathway has been altered. A scientifically appropriate explanation must identify the affected process rather than merely associate a general actin phenotype with a particular mechanism.

A classical molecular example is the Salmonella effector SopE, which acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for host Rho-family GTPases. Guanine nucleotide exchange is a regulatory event: release of GDP allows binding of GTP, shifting the switch toward an active state. The GTPase remains a host protein; the bacterial effector changes its regulatory state. Activated host regulators influence factors controlling the architecture of actin networks. This example connects the bacterial trigger model with the general molecular-switch concept developed in the signaling chapters. It also distinguishes nucleotide exchange from hydrolysis, which have different effects on the switch.

The Arp2/3 complex is a host actin regulator associated with formation of branched filament arrays. Such arrays contribute to the structural support of membrane protrusions. An activated upstream regulator and the resulting actin arrangement are therefore connected through intermediates; it would be incorrect to describe the small GTPase as the actin filament itself. A pathway representation should distinguish the signal, regulatory switch, downstream machinery and structural output. The conventional shorthand that a bacterial factor changes actin refers to the overall outcome of these interactions.

Listeria provides a distinct classical example of the association of bacteria with host actin. Its surface protein ActA is associated with recruitment of host actin assembly machinery, including Arp2/3. The resulting filament-rich structures are commonly called actin comet tails. The directionally organized architecture helps explain why the distribution of actin may be asymmetric around an intracellular object. This is a separate phenomenon from surface ruffling during bacterial uptake, although both involve host actin. The contrast helps identify two different stages of a host–pathogen interaction rather than assuming that all actin-rich structures have the same biological meaning.

The importance of this comparison is that the force associated with filament growth is a host-cell physical process, not flagellar propulsion. Growing actin arrays can exert forces when polymerization occurs against an appropriate boundary. The organization of the filaments and their connections to surrounding structures determine how the force affects a membrane or an associated object. In an examination, a model of flagellar rotation should not be substituted for a model of actin-based movement merely because both result in displacement.

Membrane compartments and intracellular niches

When a phagocytic cell engulfs a particle, a portion of its plasma membrane encloses the particle to form a phagosome. The interior of the phagosome is topologically related to the extracellular environment, even though the entire compartment is physically inside the cell. A molecule within its lumen cannot automatically interact with a soluble cytosolic protein. This is the same topology principle encountered in endocytosis and secretory pathway organization.

Phagosome maturation involves changes in membrane composition, interactions with other compartments and development of conditions suited to processing the enclosed material. Acidification is an important feature of many degradative compartments. It is supported by proton transport and affected by the movement of other ions, membrane properties and buffering. Maturation also involves changes in the collection of proteins present on or within the compartment, so the pH alone does not specify its complete identity.

A lysosome is an organelle specialized for intracellular degradation. Its enzymes and acidic environment allow many macromolecules to be broken down. Fusion or exchange between appropriate compartments can deliver degradative material to an enclosed particle. The result is a changing chemical environment rather than a static container. A membrane-bound microbial compartment should therefore not be classified solely by its shape or size.

Classical textbook comparisons distinguish several broad intracellular bacterial lifestyles. Listeria is often presented as a cytosolic example, Salmonella as a bacterium commonly associated with a modified vacuole, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis as an example associated with disturbance of normal phagosome maturation. These labels describe representative biological patterns rather than rigid species-wide rules. Variation occurs across cell types and stages of infection. Not every internalized bacterium is in the same compartment, and a single pathogen name cannot substitute for evidence of localization.

The distinction between cytosol and vacuolar lumen is useful because each compartment has different molecular contents, constraints and surveillance mechanisms. In cytosol, a bacterial cell is surrounded directly by host cytoplasmic material. In a vacuole, a surrounding membrane maintains a separate lumen. In either setting, location does not establish viability. A dead bacterial particle can persist inside a compartment, while a viable cell may be present without detectable multiplication.

A host-cell compartment may contain several markers, and their meanings must be interpreted in context. A marker associated with a late endosomal or lysosomal membrane does not, on its own, prove that the entire complement of degradative enzymes is present or active. Likewise, reduced accumulation of one marker does not establish that all trafficking into a compartment has stopped. Membrane identity is an ensemble property, and organelles exchange material dynamically.

Comparative cell biology helps prevent a misleading binary model in which every intracellular microorganism either escapes all membrane compartments or remains in a permanently unchanging vesicle. Intracellular environments are heterogeneous. The appropriate question is which cellular compartment is being observed, which host functions have changed, and what evidence supports those claims. The existence of a modified compartment is not evidence that a bacterium has evolved immunity to every host defense.

Interpreting the three classical intracellular lifestyles

The textbook comparison of Listeria, Salmonella and Mycobacterium becomes useful when it is understood as a comparison of cellular environments rather than simply a list of pathogen names. Each example raises a different question about the relationship between an internalized object and the eukaryotic membrane system. In a cytosolic setting the key structural distinction is absence of an enclosing host membrane. In a vacuolar setting the key distinction is presence of such a membrane. In a maturation-altered setting the key distinction concerns changes in the normal development of the compartment.

In the classical account of Listeria infection, the bacterium is initially enclosed within a host-derived compartment. Subsequent observations may identify bacteria located in the cytosol. The important concept is that cellular entry and cytosolic localization are different events. A membrane that initially encloses an internalized object can represent a further barrier even after the object's crossing of the cell surface. Cytosolic localization is therefore not an automatic consequence of uptake.

This distinction also changes how host molecules can interact with the internalized object. A soluble cytosolic protein does not ordinarily cross the membrane of an intact vacuole freely. Conversely, a soluble enzyme in the vacuolar lumen is separated from the host cytosol. The composition of the surrounding environment depends on the state of the enclosing membrane and the transport systems associated with it. Consequently a description of an intracellular microorganism should specify whether the relevant molecular interactions take place in the cytosol or within a membrane-bound compartment.

Salmonella is commonly used to illustrate a modified intracellular vacuole. A membrane-bound compartment associated with infection need not have the same properties as a typical uninfected endosome or phagosome. The distinction is not that such a vacuole is an entirely new kind of cellular material. It is formed in the context of the host's existing membrane system and must be interpreted in relation to that system. Its properties can change over time, and its membrane-associated and lumenal features need not change in identical ways.

A Salmonella-containing vacuole is often abbreviated SCV in textbooks. The abbreviation identifies a compartment by its associated bacterium rather than defining all of its properties. Calling a compartment an SCV does not establish that it lacks every lysosomal component, is at neutral pH or has the same properties in all host cells. These are separate empirical questions. Some Salmonella populations in epithelial cells can also be cytosolic, illustrating why the representative textbook pattern should not be treated as an absolute species-level rule.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis illustrates the concept that phagosome maturation may be altered. Macrophages ordinarily participate in processing internalized material through interactions of compartments and development of antimicrobial conditions. A maturation disturbance concerns one or more of these changes. It should be distinguished from failure of initial engulfment. A macrophage may contain an internalized organism while its subsequent compartmental development differs from the expected pattern.

The concept of delayed or altered maturation should not be expressed as the claim that the entire host lysosomal system disappears. A macrophage contains numerous compartments, and the properties of one compartment can differ from those of others. A measurement averaged across the whole cell might therefore obscure a local difference. Conversely, a local measurement cannot establish that the same change has occurred throughout the cell. Spatial resolution matters for deciding how broadly a conclusion applies.

The biological significance of an intracellular niche follows from the fact that a host cell is not chemically homogeneous. Cytosol, endosomal lumen, lysosomal lumen and extracellular fluid differ in composition, pH, proteins and transport constraints. The word niche describes the environment experienced by an organism within a larger biological system. In an infected cell this includes the physical boundaries and chemical properties of the relevant location. The environmental description does not, on its own, determine whether an organism is viable or dividing.

A useful conceptual exercise is to compare an enclosed particle with an otherwise identical particle in the cytosol. Both are physically inside the host cell, but the accessible sets of host molecules differ. A membrane-bound enzyme facing the lumen may interact with the first particle and not the second. A cytosolic protein may have the opposite pattern of accessibility. The difference follows from membrane topology rather than from the biological identity of the particles. This is why a compartment-specific explanation is more precise than the statement that all intracellular objects are exposed to the same cellular conditions.

A second comparison involves two particles within apparently similar vacuoles. One surrounding membrane has a characteristic early-compartment marker, while the other has several late-compartment markers. The observation supports a difference in membrane identity, but it does not establish how acidic either lumen is or how much degradative activity is present. If both vacuoles have similar pH readings, that does not invalidate the marker observations. It indicates that the measured properties are distinguishable.

A third comparison concerns the relationship between persistence and multiplication. The presence of a microbial marker at successive times may be consistent with persistence, but the marker could remain after viability has been lost. Similarly, a larger total marker signal might reflect an increase in the number of objects, increased signal per object or a change in the number of host cells being examined. Evidence of multiplication requires evidence that distinguishes these possibilities. These are general quantitative principles for interpreting biological observations.

The three representative pathogen categories can now be stated without overgeneralizing. Cytosolic localization emphasizes loss or absence of a surrounding host membrane at the stage under discussion. Vacuolar residence emphasizes retention of a surrounding host membrane with a particular set of properties. Maturation disturbance emphasizes deviation from a normal sequence of compartmental changes. None of these categories by itself specifies disease severity, and they are not a complete classification of all bacterial pathogens.

The classical Listeria model also identifies a membrane-active protein, listeriolysin O, in the transition between the enclosing vacuole and cytosolic localization. It is a pore-forming toxin rather than a protein whose principal action is the assembly of actin. Its role is thus conceptually distinct from ActA. The two named factors connect different host-cell structures to different cellular observations. One concerns membrane integrity and the other concerns actin organization. This distinction illustrates why a single word such as invasion cannot adequately describe every stage of an intracellular infection.

In Salmonella cell biology, two type III secretion systems are conventionally associated with the pathogenicity islands designated SPI-1 and SPI-2. A pathogenicity island is a region of the bacterial genome associated with functions relevant to pathogenesis. In the familiar teaching model, SPI-1 is associated with host-cell entry and actin rearrangement, while SPI-2 is associated with modification of the intracellular vacuolar environment. These roles emphasize different cellular stages; the classification should not be treated as an assertion that every individual effector acts at precisely one time or that all Salmonella-containing compartments behave identically.

A bacterium enclosed in a vacuole is physically separated from soluble cytosolic components by the vacuolar membrane. Nevertheless, the vacuole remains part of a host membrane system whose cytosolic surfaces interact with trafficking machinery. This makes it possible to discuss alterations of membrane organization without equating vacuolar localization with free cytosolic localization. A schematic of the Salmonella model should therefore retain the host-derived membrane around the bacterial cell, even when its associated host membrane properties differ from those of a typical degradative compartment.

For the classical Mycobacterium example, a useful host-cell comparison concerns the progression from an early phagosomal identity toward a more degradative compartment. In a standard endocytic model, Rab5-associated early compartments and Rab7-associated later compartments illustrate a change of membrane identity. Mycobacterium tuberculosis-containing phagosomes can display altered progression toward normal degradative conditions. The important inference is not that every early marker remains indefinitely, but that a normal maturation sequence can be disturbed. Acidity, protein composition and interactions with lysosomes should each be interpreted as distinct features of that sequence.

These examples can be connected to the general distinction between membrane fusion and membrane disruption. Fusion ordinarily joins two membrane-bound compartments and changes the continuity of their lumens. Disruption compromises the integrity of an existing boundary. A cytosolic localization resulting from loss of a surrounding boundary is therefore not equivalent to fusion with a lysosome. Conversely, delivery of lumenal material by vesicle fusion need not expose that material to the cytosol. Keeping these topological outcomes separate is central to understanding the representative bacterial lifestyles.

Vesicle identity, pH and membrane topology

A membrane-bound compartment is not simply a bag of material. Its membrane contains lipids and proteins that determine its interactions with other compartments. The lumen contains solutes, macromolecules and any enclosed particles. These two regions are physically and chemically distinct. A protein on the cytosolic surface of the membrane may participate in trafficking without being exposed to lumenal material, while a lumenal hydrolase may act on enclosed molecules without directly contacting cytosolic proteins.

Rab GTPases are commonly introduced in cell biology as molecular switches associated with membrane trafficking. Different Rab proteins are associated with different stages or regions of the endomembrane system. Their distribution is useful as evidence for compartment identity, but the presence of a particular Rab does not mean that every process normally associated with that compartment has occurred. A mature organelle is characterized by many interacting properties, not by a single name attached to a marker.

The acquisition of a membrane marker can occur by a different process from the acquisition of a soluble lumenal enzyme. The former may reflect the delivery or recruitment of membrane-associated components; the latter depends on transport of lumenal material and its retention or processing. These distinctions explain why different readouts of endosomal maturation can yield apparently discordant results. The results may reveal different stages or different aspects of the compartment's behavior rather than an experimental contradiction.

Acidification of an intracellular compartment provides another example of the separation between physical location and biological function. The pH is defined as the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity. A change from pH 7 to pH 6 represents a tenfold increase in hydrogen ion activity, not a one-unit linear increase in the amount of acid. A change from pH 7 to pH 5 represents a hundredfold increase. These ratios are appropriate for comparing pH values, but they do not specify the total number of protons transported because buffers can bind protons.

The energy required to move protons across a membrane depends on both the existing pH difference and the electrical potential. Proton pumps can use metabolic energy to establish a gradient. The resulting gradient is affected by proton leakage, counterion movement and buffering within the compartment. An acidic lumen is consequently a dynamic steady state under many physiological conditions, not a fixed chemical property that remains unchanged after transport ceases.

Acid hydrolases generally display greater catalytic activity in an appropriate acidic environment than in the neutral cytosol. Enzyme activity nevertheless depends on more than pH. The enzyme must be present in its functional form, reach the substrate and have a suitable chemical environment. A probe that reports low pH does not directly demonstrate hydrolysis of a particular substrate. Conversely, evidence of a particular hydrolytic reaction does not specify the exact pH without an independent measurement.

Compartmental changes must also be understood over time. Immediately after uptake, the membrane of an enclosing vesicle may resemble the membrane from which it originated. Subsequent trafficking changes its composition. An image taken at one stage should not be interpreted as a permanent property of all compartments containing the same object. A time course may reveal an ordered sequence, but there may be variation among individual vesicles and among individual cells.

The morphology of an intracellular compartment is another imperfect indicator of function. A large vacuole may contain more lumenal volume than a small vacuole, but the size alone does not specify its pH, membrane composition or degradative activity. A small punctate fluorescent signal may represent many molecules concentrated into a restricted volume. Without an understanding of the relationship between signal intensity and concentration, apparent brightness should not be equated with biological effectiveness.

The representative intracellular bacterial lifestyles introduced in this chapter provide a reason to understand these principles. When a textbook describes a modified vacuole or an altered maturation process, the description concerns a change in one or more properties of a compartment. It should not be read as a claim that membrane traffic has stopped throughout the entire host cell. Other organelles in the same cell may continue to perform essential functions, and altered compartments can retain some normal features while differing in others.

A compartment should therefore be described along separate axes: whether a membrane is present, its relationship to the cytosol, the distribution of membrane markers, the composition of the lumen, its acidity and its functional activity. This multi-dimensional description is more informative than the simple assertion that a bacterium is inside a cell. It also gives students a framework for interpreting the organelle diagrams used in questions about intracellular infection.

Cell damage, death and resource limitation

A living host cell maintains ATP production, membrane potential, ion balance, protein turnover and regulated gene expression. Damage to any of these processes can change cellular performance before a gross morphological defect becomes apparent. For example, an epithelial cell may lose an important absorptive function even when its nucleus and plasma membrane remain visibly intact. Functional injury should not be defined solely by cell death.

Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death associated with characteristic biochemical and structural changes. In the context of bacterial infection, a change in apoptotic signaling may reflect direct action of bacterial products, host detection of stress, or interactions among neighboring cells. The detailed apoptotic machinery belongs to the cellular organization unit. Here the important issue is that death of a host cell is a regulated biological outcome rather than an automatic consequence of bacterial entry.

The effect of cell death on infection is context dependent. Removing a dysfunctional cell can protect tissue homeostasis, but widespread cell death can compromise the integrity of a barrier. Conversely, preservation of cell viability is not identical to preservation of its normal function. A surviving cell can display abnormal secretion, abnormal gene expression or altered transport. The terms survival and recovery should therefore be used separately.

Necrosis describes a form or appearance of cell death involving loss of membrane integrity, although modern classifications recognize multiple regulated and unregulated routes to lytic death. An increase in extracellular material that is normally intracellular may indicate membrane injury, but it cannot identify the precise death pathway by itself. A single molecular marker or microscopy image is seldom adequate to classify a complex death response. Several independent features may be required for a scientifically defensible conclusion.

Resource availability is another component of the host environment. Living cells contain carbon compounds, nitrogen compounds, ions and other substances required for metabolism, but their total abundance does not determine how accessible they are in a particular compartment. Much cellular iron, for instance, is associated with proteins or other binding molecules. An element can therefore be plentiful in a tissue and nevertheless be present at a low freely available concentration. This distinction is relevant to both cellular metabolism and host restriction of microbial growth.

A bacterium and its host cell have distinct metabolic requirements. A host cell uses its own transport and storage systems to maintain homeostasis; the bacterial cell maintains a separate boundary and metabolism. Detecting a metabolite in the host tissue does not demonstrate its utilization by the microorganism. Nutrient access, growth, survival and the alteration of a host transport pathway are different variables. Keeping them separate is more valuable for examination reasoning than memorizing detailed pathogen-specific metabolic reactions.

The degree of tissue dysfunction also depends on the tissue's architecture. Loss of a small fraction of cells may be tolerated if their neighbors can compensate. In another tissue, interruption of a junctional barrier or loss of a specialized cell population may have disproportionate effects. The relation between altered cellular activity and organism-level disease is therefore mediated by tissue physiology. The same molecular alteration need not produce the same visible injury in every tissue.

Resource access and interpretation of nutrient measurements

The chemical environment within a host is partitioned into many compartments. Proteins, ions and metabolites can be present in different chemical forms, and their movement between locations is constrained by membranes and binding interactions. These distinctions apply to nutrient access as well as to signaling. A measurement of total nutrient content in an entire cell does not specify the quantity present in a freely accessible form at a particular location.

Iron illustrates this distinction because it can participate in essential biochemical processes while also being involved in potentially damaging redox reactions. Organisms regulate its distribution and chemical availability. In animal cells and extracellular fluids, iron is frequently associated with proteins that participate in its storage or transport. The total amount of iron detected in a sample includes such associated forms. It should not be interpreted as the concentration of freely available iron ions in every cellular compartment.

Nutrient availability is related to metabolic activity, but the relation is not always direct. A cell may have abundant material that it cannot currently metabolize because an essential enzyme or transport process is impaired. Conversely, a change in the rate of a metabolic reaction can occur without a detectable change in the total amount of its substrate if the substrate is continually replenished. A steady concentration can conceal substantial metabolic flux. This principle parallels the dynamic steady state already discussed for second messengers.

When interpreting changes in host metabolism during infection, one must distinguish changes in the host cell's own metabolic requirements from changes associated with other organisms present in the sample. A whole-tissue measurement integrates the contributions of many cells. An increase in a metabolite could reflect changes in production, removal, transport, cell composition or compartment volumes. It is not sufficient evidence of transfer of that metabolite between host and microorganism. The distinction between abundance and use is fundamental to the interpretation of metabolic data.

The biological significance of resource limitation is not confined to infection. Cells maintain internal chemical conditions through transport, binding and storage because uncontrolled concentrations can be harmful or incompatible with normal function. Microorganisms within a host encounter these regulated conditions. The presence of nutrient-dependent processes in both organisms does not make their metabolism a single shared system; each has its own cellular organization and biochemical requirements. Understanding nutrient availability therefore requires the same attention to boundaries and chemical form as understanding the location of a toxin or an intracellular compartment.

Cellular stress, regulated death and interpretation of injury

Cellular stress occurs when normal homeostatic mechanisms must respond to a disturbance. It is not synonymous with irreversible injury. Changes in ATP availability, protein folding, redox balance or membrane properties may evoke compensatory responses that restore function. Whether the disturbance results in recovery, altered physiology or death depends on its nature and duration and on the capacity of the cell to respond.

In infection biology it is particularly important to distinguish a direct microbial action from a secondary stress response. A toxin that interferes with a defined protein may have one immediate molecular substrate but numerous downstream consequences. For example, disturbance of protein synthesis may affect the supply of short-lived regulatory proteins, which can in turn alter gene expression or cellular survival. It would be incorrect to attribute every downstream change to direct chemical interaction of the toxin with every affected molecule.

Apoptosis involves a coordinated program that can result in controlled dismantling of a cell. Initiator and executioner caspases are important components of the classical apoptotic pathways. Changes in mitochondrial integrity can participate in some forms of apoptosis, while signals transmitted through cell-surface death receptors can initiate other routes. The details of these pathways have already been assigned to Unit 2. The present application is to recognize that the same core host pathways can be relevant during a microbial disturbance.

A change in a cell-death marker is often interpreted too broadly. Detection of a cleaved protein associated with apoptosis may provide evidence for an event in the pathway, but it may not specify the original trigger. A membrane permeability measurement may indicate loss of integrity, but it does not necessarily distinguish all forms of death that ultimately involve lysis. A reduction in metabolic activity can reflect severe stress without establishing the morphological or biochemical identity of the death process.

The proportion of cells affected also matters. Imagine a population in which the mean value of a metabolic readout decreases to half its original value. One possible explanation is that every cell reduces its activity by half. Another is that half of the cells lose the measured activity entirely while the other half remain near their original activity. These two populations have identical average values under equal initial contributions, yet their biological states differ. An average conceals information about the distribution among cells.

If the affected tissue has an essential barrier function, the spatial distribution of dysfunctional cells can be important. A small continuous region of disruption may have different consequences from the same number of affected cells dispersed throughout the tissue. Loss of junctional integrity can create a route for material to pass between cells. Reduced secretory or absorptive activity can change the composition of fluid adjacent to the tissue. These outcomes are determined by organization at the tissue level as well as by the condition of individual cells.

Host signaling may also propagate consequences beyond the cells directly exposed to a microbial product. Cells can release signaling molecules that affect nearby cells, and tissue-level responses can involve the movement and activation of other cell populations. A neighboring cell displaying altered gene expression is not necessarily itself associated with a bacterium. The presence of altered cells outside the area of microbial localization is therefore compatible with indirect signaling.

Inflammatory injury is an important example of this principle. Host defense reactions are normally protective, but their intensity and duration can influence tissue damage. A pathological response may involve both microbial molecules and host-derived mediators. Establishing that an inflammatory response is present does not identify the precise relative contributions of those processes. This distinction links the host-cell alteration chapter to the immune response chapters without repeating their detailed pathways.

The terms cytostatic and cytotoxic also describe different effects. A cytostatic disturbance reduces or stops proliferation without necessarily killing cells. A cytotoxic disturbance causes cellular injury or death. A decline in the number of cells in a growing population can reflect reduced proliferation, increased cell loss or both. It is not scientifically sufficient to classify every reduction in cell number as direct cytotoxicity.

This reasoning is important for interpreting time-course data. A population that initially shows a stable cell count may contain dividing cells whose numbers are balanced by cell loss. A population with a rising count may still contain a fraction of injured or dying cells. The net change in number is the difference between additions and losses. An isolated final count provides no direct measurement of either of those individual processes.

The same principle applies to interpreting a biochemical response after recovery from stress. Restoration of a messenger concentration does not guarantee that protein turnover, membrane organization or tissue function have fully recovered. Different components can return toward normal at different rates. A scientifically useful definition of recovery must specify the function being evaluated. This prevents a single favorable observation from being misinterpreted as evidence that every consequence of the disturbance has been reversed.

The term programmed cell death includes more than classical apoptosis. Pyroptosis is a lytic inflammatory form of regulated cell death often discussed in the context of bacterial infection. In a standard textbook distinction, inflammatory caspases and gasdermin pore formation are associated with pyroptotic responses, whereas executioner caspase activity and organized cellular dismantling are familiar features of classical apoptosis. These mechanisms can have overlapping secondary consequences, so their names should not be inferred from a single endpoint such as loss of membrane integrity.

Host cells exposed to Salmonella can display different death responses according to cell type and biological context. Older literature frequently described bacterial-associated macrophage death as apoptosis; later work established the importance of inflammatory cell-death pathways in relevant settings. The development of terminology illustrates how additional molecular evidence can revise the interpretation of an earlier morphological observation. It does not mean that all bacterial-associated cell death is pyroptotic, nor that classical apoptosis never occurs.

A bacterial-associated change in a host survival pathway may also delay rather than initiate a death response. The final state of the cell depends on the balance of pro-survival and pro-death signaling, the extent of damage and the timing of observation. A snapshot showing a surviving cell does not establish whether its death program has been actively suppressed or whether it has simply not encountered sufficient stress to initiate the program. A comparison across appropriate molecular readouts is required to distinguish these explanations.

Comparative cell biology and limits of inference

Animal cells vary greatly in the structure of their exposed surfaces. A polarized epithelial cell has different apical and basolateral regions, whereas an immune cell may continually change its shape and the distribution of membrane structures as it moves. These differences matter because an identical host molecule can be accessible in one location and relatively inaccessible in another. Host specificity is not simply a list of receptors; it also depends on their anatomical and cellular context.

Plant cells provide a useful contrast because their plasma membranes are surrounded by a cell wall. A bacterium associated with a plant tissue may occupy the extracellular apoplast while molecular interactions alter plant-cell function. The fact that host cells respond to a bacterial product does not establish that intact bacteria have crossed the plant plasma membrane. The later chapters on plant infection develop this distinction more fully. At this stage it illustrates a general rule: the location of a microorganism is not necessarily the location of the host process that changes.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells share certain broad biochemical principles, including the use of nucleotides, proteins and lipid membranes. They differ in the organization of their organelles, cellular signaling systems and cytoskeletal machinery. The eukaryotic endomembrane system creates many topologically distinct environments that have no simple equivalent in the ordinary bacterial cell. A term such as intracellular trafficking must therefore be interpreted in the context of the relevant cell type.

Evolutionary similarity does not imply molecular identity. A bacterial product may influence a conserved process in animal cells, but different host species can vary in the molecular details or abundance of a target. A change in one step may alter the phenotype without abolishing all association between the organisms. This is another reason to interpret the term host range at more than one level: cellular binding, intracellular compatibility and organism-level susceptibility are distinguishable.

From an evolutionary perspective, virulence is not necessarily synonymous with severe injury. A microbial trait can contribute to association with a host or survival in a particular ecological context without being associated with the most dramatic tissue damage. Disease severity reflects interactions between microbial traits and host biology. A trait may have consequences that differ across environments, and a measurable effect in one type of cell cannot automatically be generalized to every host.

The term adaptation should be used carefully in biological explanation. A molecular activity can be described in terms of its immediate effect on the host cell. A claim about why that activity evolved requires different evidence concerning inheritance, variation and selection across generations. The immediate biochemical effect is not proof of a particular evolutionary history. Students should distinguish a functional explanation from a historical evolutionary explanation.

The medical and biotechnological relevance of the material follows from understanding host processes rather than from memorizing infectious disease details. For example, recognizing that a secreted protein can cause a specific change in signaling helped establish the value of studying biochemical targets independently of whole-organism symptoms. Knowledge of epithelial physiology provides a framework for interpreting abnormal transport. Knowledge of the endomembrane system provides a framework for interpreting intracellular localization. These applications depend on the basic cell biology taught in earlier units.

A final limitation is that an explanatory diagram necessarily omits some complexity. A pathway drawn as a series of arrows may leave out parallel routes, feedback loops and differences among cells. Such a diagram remains valuable if its purpose is stated clearly and if the arrows represent defensible relationships. It becomes misleading when the diagram is treated as proof that the omitted processes never occur. The best use of a diagram in examination preparation is to identify its assumptions and determine which prediction actually follows from those assumptions.

Reading evidence without overinterpreting it

The most useful examination questions distinguish observations at different levels of biological organization. The observation of a receptor at the cell surface concerns structure and distribution. A change in a second messenger concerns signaling. Altered channel activity concerns a functional target, and altered fluid movement concerns physiology. Even if all four occur in the same system, a causal ordering must be supported rather than assumed.

Consider a hypothetical diagram of two epithelial cells exposed to a receptor-binding molecule. Both cells show a surface-associated fluorescent signal, but only one shows an increase in a second messenger. The evidence establishes that the first measured step can occur in both cells, whereas the downstream response differs. It does not establish the identity of the missing step. Possibilities include differences in trafficking, availability of the intracellular target, or regulation of the messenger. The correct interpretation is narrower than simply claiming that one cell lacks the receptor.

A second hypothetical comparison shows two cell populations with equal amounts of a messenger but differing anion transport. The messenger measurement alone is insufficient to predict channel function. Channel abundance, activation state, driving force and cell polarization could differ. A proposed explanation must be compatible with both the messenger data and the physiological output. More information about downstream components would be needed to distinguish these possibilities.

The same reasoning applies to intracellular localization. A micrograph in which a bacterial object appears surrounded by a membrane identifies a compartmental relationship at the time of imaging. It does not necessarily reveal what happened earlier or what will happen later. The absence of a clearly visible membrane in another image may reflect section orientation or inadequate resolution rather than true absence. Observations at different resolutions answer different questions.

Suppose a simplified textbook dataset reports that a compartment contains a late endosomal marker but has weak signal from a probe responsive to low pH. The two observations need not contradict one another. Membrane composition and lumenal acidity are related but distinguishable features. One must also consider probe calibration and accessibility before interpreting signal intensity as an exact pH. This is an example of how the same cellular system can have more than one experimentally measurable characteristic.

Controls distinguish a biological interpretation from an artifact. A measurement that depends on fluorescence may be affected by probe concentration, instrument settings or the number of cells represented in the sample. A comparison of molecular abundance should account for the quantity of material measured; a comparison of cell survival should not confuse cell loss with changes in the intensity of a marker per remaining cell. These principles are general to experimental biology and do not require a specialized infection protocol.

The overall framework for reasoning is to identify the boundary crossed, the host process affected, the level at which the measurement is made and the alternative explanations that remain. A specific biochemical mechanism must not be inferred simply from a common endpoint such as cell rounding or reduced viability. This framework prepares the student to examine viral receptor recognition in the next chapter, where attachment, penetration and permissiveness must again be treated as distinct biological properties.

Consider a hypothetical teaching comparison of cells in which the recognition site for a receptor-binding protein is absent. The predicted initial difference concerns binding to that site, rather than the intrinsic capacity of adenylyl cyclase to synthesize cAMP. A control observation of ordinary hormone-dependent cAMP signaling can help separate loss of surface recognition from a general failure of the downstream signaling system. Such a comparison would not establish that all other surface interactions are absent, because the hypothesis concerns one defined recognition route.

In a second simplified comparison, a bacterial delivery component is absent while ordinary surface association remains detectable. The observations distinguish attachment from the delivery-dependent host response. They would not establish that every microbial product depends on the missing component. A conceptual control is a host response mediated through an unrelated physiological pathway, which helps distinguish a broadly unresponsive cell from a change restricted to the process under discussion. These are hypothetical reasoning scenarios, not laboratory procedures or measured findings.

A third example concerns a host trafficking alteration that affects the distribution of membrane markers. The predicted interpretation depends on which feature changes: a difference in marker acquisition is evidence about membrane identity, while a difference in a calibrated lumenal measurement concerns the internal compartment. Neither observation alone establishes a change in bacterial viability. The most informative control comparison therefore distinguishes the general host trafficking phenotype from any inference about microbial persistence. A student should identify the missing evidence rather than conclude that one marker proves the entire infection mechanism.

Quantitative and graphical interpretation across levels

Quantitative reasoning is useful in host-cell alteration because different mechanisms can produce similar numerical outcomes. The task is to select a model that corresponds to the measured variable and to state the assumptions that connect the calculation to biology. A numerical answer without an interpretation can conceal rather than clarify the distinction between association, signaling, cell injury and tissue function.

The simplest reversible binding model involves a ligand L and a binding site R. If the bound complex is RL, then at equilibrium the dissociation constant is defined as Kd = [R][L]/[RL]. The expression relates concentrations under the assumptions of a simple one-to-one equilibrium model. A smaller Kd means that the bound state is favored at a lower free ligand concentration. This is a general biochemical model and does not describe all interactions between bacterial products and host surfaces.

If free ligand concentration is effectively fixed and binding sites behave independently, the fraction of sites occupied can be written as [L]/(Kd + [L]). The expression follows by replacing the concentration of unbound sites in the equilibrium relation with the total site concentration minus the bound-site concentration. When free ligand concentration equals Kd, half of the sites are occupied. The result does not require knowledge of the total number of sites, provided the stated assumptions are valid.

For a hypothetical harmless receptor ligand with Kd equal to 4 arbitrary concentration units, free ligand concentration of 4 units yields fractional occupancy of 4/(4 + 4), or 0.5. At a concentration of 12 units, the occupancy is 12/(4 + 12), or 0.75. The increase in occupancy is not proportional to the increase in concentration because the number of available binding sites is limited. At progressively higher free ligand concentrations the fraction approaches, but does not exceed, one.

The model does not equate receptor occupancy with downstream biological response. A response may be amplified, saturated or subject to additional regulation. Furthermore, a multivalent interaction may not satisfy the independent one-site assumptions, and surface association may be influenced by cellular trafficking. A fitted binding curve may provide a convenient descriptive parameter without proving that all underlying interactions conform to the simplified model.

Another quantitative issue concerns net transport. Suppose a hypothetical epithelium secretes 8 units of fluid per hour and absorbs 10 units per hour. Define net secretion as secretion minus absorption. The initial net secretion is negative 2 units per hour, meaning that net absorption occurs. If secretion becomes 11 units per hour while absorption falls to 7, the net secretion becomes positive 4 units per hour. The sign of the result indicates direction under the defined convention. The total difference between these states is 6 units per hour, not merely the change of 3 in secretion alone.

This arithmetic model is deliberately independent of a particular pathogen or clinical situation. It illustrates the fact that a measured net physiological flux combines opposing processes. In a real epithelium the rates may themselves depend on fluid composition, transport regulation and the properties of the tissue. The example cannot be used to infer how much fluid a person will lose during an infection. Its purpose is to show why an increase in net secretion need not be explained exclusively by increased secretory flux.

Compartment measurements provide a different form of quantitative reasoning. Imagine a teaching dataset in which 100 vesicles are examined, and 60 display a marker associated with membrane maturation. Of those 60, 30 also have a positive readout for an independent lumenal property. The proportion of all vesicles displaying both features is 30/100, or 30 percent. Among the marker-positive vesicles, the corresponding conditional proportion is 30/60, or 50 percent. These percentages answer different questions and must not be interchanged.

This example also illustrates a limitation of reporting percentages without denominators. A high fraction of double-positive compartments in one sample may refer to a small absolute number of compartments. A lower fraction in another sample may refer to a larger number. If the aim is to compare the composition of the compartment population, proportions may be appropriate; if the aim is to estimate total abundance, absolute counts are relevant. Scientific interpretation depends on the question, not on the mere availability of a percentage.

Ratios are similarly useful for distinguishing activity per cell from total activity in a population. Suppose a hypothetical sample contains 200 cells with a total fluorescence signal of 1,000 units. The mean signal is 5 units per cell. A second sample has 100 cells and a total signal of 500 units. The mean signal remains 5 units per cell despite the reduction in total signal. On these measurements alone, a decline in per-cell activity is not supported. Equal average signal also does not establish equal distributions among the cells.

When a graph displays a change with time, the slope and the ordinate are different variables. The ordinate may represent a concentration or an amount; the slope represents its rate of change. A horizontal line at a nonzero concentration indicates no net change in concentration over that interval, not absence of the measured substance. For a dynamic steady state it may represent balanced formation and removal. This distinction is particularly important for interpreting second-messenger responses.

The biological context determines whether an approximately linear segment of a graph has a simple explanation. A short interval may look linear even when the long-term behavior is nonlinear. A response may initially change quickly and then approach a plateau because a limiting component becomes saturated or because compensatory processes reduce the net rate. The graph alone may not distinguish among these explanations. A model should be judged by its predictions and assumptions rather than by its ability to match one visually appealing curve.

The most common mathematical mistakes in examination questions of this kind involve using the wrong denominator, overlooking the sign of a flux, confusing an amount with a concentration and treating a fold change as an absolute difference. A change from 2 units to 6 units is a threefold final value, an absolute increase of 4 units and a 200 percent increase relative to the initial value. These three descriptions are all valid, but they express different relationships. The appropriate expression depends on the intended comparison.

All of these examples are hypothetical teaching datasets or general physical models. They are not measured infection parameters. Their value is that they transfer familiar quantitative concepts into the interpretation of cellular evidence without conflating an idealized mathematical relationship with the complex behavior of a living tissue.

1. Which definition best describes a virulence factor?

  1. Any molecule essential for the growth of every bacterium
  2. A host protein that exclusively destroys pathogens
  3. A microbial feature contributing to infection or disease in a specified host context
  4. A substance that invariably kills every exposed host
Answer and explanation

C. A microbial feature contributing to infection or disease in a specified host context

Virulence is context dependent. A contributing trait need not kill cells directly, and an ordinary essential metabolic molecule is not automatically a specialized virulence factor.

2. Which molecule is the principal inflammatory component of Gram-negative LPS?

  1. Peptidoglycan cross-links
  2. Lipid A
  3. The O-antigen repeat alone
  4. A secreted ribosomal protein
Answer and explanation

B. Lipid A

Lipid A is the endotoxic portion of LPS. The O antigen is a polysaccharide region, and the other alternatives are not lipid A.

3. The B pentamer of cholera toxin primarily illustrates which concept?

  1. Host surface recognition
  2. Direct synthesis of cyclic AMP
  3. Hydrolysis of host actin
  4. ATP-dependent pumping of chloride
Answer and explanation

A. Host surface recognition

The classical binding component recognizes GM1. The intracellular catalytic action is distinct from binding; host adenylyl cyclase and CFTR account for downstream effects.

4. Which host molecule is the classical signaling target of cholera toxin?

  1. CFTR itself as the direct covalent target
  2. Gs alpha
  3. Eukaryotic elongation factor 2
  4. The actin monomer
Answer and explanation

B. Gs alpha

The toxin modifies Gs alpha and disturbs signal termination. CFTR is downstream; elongation factor 2 is the classic diphtheria toxin target.

5. What does adenylyl cyclase normally produce from ATP?

  1. Cyclic AMP
  2. Cyclic GMP
  3. ADP-ribosylated actin
  4. Chloride ions
Answer and explanation

A. Cyclic AMP

Adenylyl cyclase forms cAMP, a second messenger. Guanylyl cyclases instead form cGMP; the other products are unrelated.

6. CFTR is best classified functionally as a:

  1. Primary active chloride pump
  2. Regulated anion channel
  3. Cytosolic protein kinase
  4. Bacterial secretion apparatus
Answer and explanation

B. Regulated anion channel

Although CFTR belongs to the ABC protein family, its physiological role is an anion channel. ATP-dependent regulation does not establish primary active pumping.

7. What is the most direct function of a bacterial type III secretion apparatus in the host interaction model?

  1. Translation of host messenger RNA
  2. Synthesis of all bacterial lipids
  3. Delivery of certain bacterial proteins across cellular boundaries
  4. Formation of host actin monomers
Answer and explanation

C. Delivery of certain bacterial proteins across cellular boundaries

The delivery system provides a route for cargo. The cargo and host proteins account for subsequent molecular responses.

8. Which feature distinguishes an internalized vacuolar object from a cytosolic object?

  1. Its distance from the host nucleus alone
  2. An enclosing host-derived membrane
  3. Whether the object is detectable by microscopy
  4. The presence of any protein within the object
Answer and explanation

B. An enclosing host-derived membrane

Membrane topology, not position within the cell outline, defines the distinction. Either type of object can be microscopically detectable.

9. Which representative pathogen is conventionally associated with a cytosolic lifestyle after entry?

  1. Vibrio cholerae as its defining epithelial disease mechanism
  2. A typical extracellular intestinal commensal
  3. Listeria monocytogenes
  4. A generic soluble toxin
Answer and explanation

C. Listeria monocytogenes

Listeria is a classical example of cytosolic localization. This is a representative pattern rather than a universal description of all bacteria in all cells.

10. The abbreviation SCV most commonly denotes:

  1. Secreted cyclic voltage
  2. Sodium-channel vesicle
  3. Salmonella-containing vacuole
  4. A specialized bacterial nucleus
Answer and explanation

C. Salmonella-containing vacuole

SCV names a host compartment associated with Salmonella. The term alone does not define all of its membrane or lumenal properties.

11. Which pairing best represents the classical Mycobacterium tuberculosis model?

  1. A free cytosolic object defined by ActA-dependent tails
  2. An intracellular phagosome with altered maturation
  3. An exclusively extracellular mechanism defined by CFTR-mediated fluid secretion
  4. A vacuole assumed to be identical to every mature lysosome
Answer and explanation

B. An intracellular phagosome with altered maturation

The Mycobacterium example concerns phagosomal maturation. The first alternative describes the classical Listeria comparison; the second describes cholera toxin physiology; the last mistakes a compartment's location for proof of its function.

12. Which pairing correctly identifies a microbial factor and its host-cell process?

  1. SopE — regulation of host Rho-family GTPases
  2. ActA — cAMP synthesis by adenylyl cyclase
  3. Cholera toxin B component — direct inhibition of EF-2
  4. Listeriolysin O — formation of actin monomers
Answer and explanation

A. SopE — regulation of host Rho-family GTPases

SopE is a bacterial exchange-factor example associated with Rho-family switch regulation. ActA is associated with host actin organization; the cholera B component mediates recognition; listeriolysin O concerns membrane integrity.

13. Diphtheria toxin is classically associated with disruption of:

  1. DNA replication through bacterial DNA polymerase III in the host nucleus
  2. Apical chloride conductance through direct CFTR pumping
  3. Translation elongation through eukaryotic EF-2
  4. Glycolysis through destruction of every hexokinase molecule
Answer and explanation

C. Translation elongation through eukaryotic EF-2

The recognized target is eukaryotic elongation factor 2. The other proposed targets do not describe this classical mechanism.

14. What distinguishes a toxoid from the corresponding active toxin?

  1. Reduced or abolished toxic activity with sufficient retained antigenicity
  2. Obligatory loss of all recognizable structure
  3. Conversion into a living bacterium
  4. Acquisition of a new bacterial chromosome
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced or abolished toxic activity with sufficient retained antigenicity

The useful conceptual distinction is antigen recognition versus toxic function. Toxoids are not living bacteria.

15. The apical membrane of a polarized intestinal epithelial cell faces the:

  1. Intestinal lumen
  2. Nucleoplasm
  3. Basolateral interstitial compartment
  4. Mitochondrial matrix
Answer and explanation

A. Intestinal lumen

The apical surface faces the lumen, while the basolateral surface faces the tissue environment. The distinction is essential for directional transport.

16. Which statement about actin is correct?

  1. Filaments undergo regulated assembly and disassembly
  2. All actin filaments are permanent structures
  3. Actin is identical to bacterial flagellin
  4. Total actin abundance uniquely specifies cellular filament architecture
Answer and explanation

A. Filaments undergo regulated assembly and disassembly

Actin is dynamic, and spatial organization can change without a change in total protein abundance. Flagellin is a different protein.

17. What does a change from pH 6 to pH 5 represent?

  1. A tenfold increase in hydrogen ion activity
  2. A twofold increase in hydrogen ion activity
  3. A tenfold decrease in hydrogen ion activity
  4. A one-unit increase in total proton number
Answer and explanation

A. A tenfold increase in hydrogen ion activity

pH is logarithmic. Total proton amount also depends on buffering and volume, so it cannot be inferred from pH alone.

18. A steady-state messenger concentration means that:

  1. Formation and removal must both have stopped
  2. Its formation and removal rates are balanced
  3. Its concentration is necessarily zero
  4. The cell is in thermodynamic equilibrium
Answer and explanation

B. Its formation and removal rates are balanced

Dynamic steady states maintain constant concentrations despite ongoing fluxes. They are not equivalent to equilibrium.

19. Which term best describes a disturbance that arrests proliferation without necessarily killing cells?

  1. Cytostatic
  2. Cytolytic in every case
  3. Osmotically neutral by definition
  4. Exclusively apoptotic
Answer and explanation

A. Cytostatic

Cytostasis refers to reduced growth or proliferation. It does not establish death or a particular death pathway.

20. Which distinction is essential when measuring iron in a host tissue?

  1. Iron and sodium are chemically identical
  2. All measured iron must be free in cytosol
  3. Total iron content versus chemically accessible iron
  4. Iron concentration directly identifies the number of living bacteria
Answer and explanation

C. Total iron content versus chemically accessible iron

Binding and compartmentalization affect availability. Total abundance alone cannot identify uptake, utilization or microbial viability.

CSIR-NET Part C analytical practice

Original examination-style questions; not previous-year questions.

21. A hypothetical host cell lacks the usual recognition site for a receptor-binding protein. Ordinary hormone-dependent cAMP signaling remains intact. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. The recognition step and downstream messenger competence are experimentally separable.
  2. The cell necessarily lacks adenylyl cyclase.
  3. The recognition site must itself synthesize cAMP.
  4. The hormone response proves that the bacterial product binds normally.
Answer and explanation

A. The recognition step and downstream messenger competence are experimentally separable.

A normal physiological messenger response supports competence of the tested downstream system. It does not establish binding of the unrelated protein or prove that every component of the signaling system is unchanged.

22. A hypothetical messenger is produced at 0.9 µM/min and removed with k = 0.3 min⁻¹. What is its steady-state concentration?

  1. 0.27 µM
  2. 3 µM
  3. 0.6 µM
  4. 1.2 µM
Answer and explanation

B. 3 µM

Set dC/dt to zero in the model dC/dt = v − kC. C = 0.9/0.3 = 3 µM. Multiplication would give the wrong units.

23. Two cells have equal steady concentrations of a messenger but different formation rates. In the simple first-order model, what follows?

  1. Their removal rate constants must differ.
  2. Their removal constants must be identical.
  3. Neither cell removes the messenger.
  4. Their volumes must necessarily be equal.
Answer and explanation

A. Their removal rate constants must differ.

At steady state C = v/k. If C is identical and positive while v differs, k must differ under the model. No conclusion about actual volumes follows.

24. A hypothetical messenger has a removal constant of 0.2 min⁻¹ after formation stops. What is its approximate half-life?

  1. 0.14 min
  2. 3.47 min
  3. 5.00 min
  4. 2.00 min
Answer and explanation

B. 3.47 min

First-order decay gives t½ = ln(2)/k = 0.693/0.2 = 3.465 min. The reciprocal 1/k is the time constant, not the half-life.

25. In the graph of messenger trajectories, both curves start at zero but approach different plateaus with identical removal constants. Which model parameter differs?

  1. The initial concentration
  2. The constant production rate
  3. The first-order removal constant
  4. The units of time
Answer and explanation

B. The constant production rate

The caption specifies equal k and zero initial concentration. Different v produces different steady concentrations v/k.

26. For outward anion movement, the chemical contribution is −3 kJ/mol and the electrical contribution is +1 kJ/mol. What is the predicted net free energy?

  1. +4 kJ/mol; outward movement is favored.
  2. −2 kJ/mol; outward movement is favored.
  3. −4 kJ/mol; inward movement is favored.
  4. +2 kJ/mol; outward movement is favored.
Answer and explanation

B. −2 kJ/mol; outward movement is favored.

Add the two signed terms: −3 + 1 = −2 kJ/mol. A negative value favors the specified direction.

27. An open ion channel is present but the electrochemical gradient for its permeant ion is zero. What is expected?

  1. No net passive ionic flux through that channel at equilibrium
  2. Maximum outward pumping
  3. An obligatory increase in cellular ATP synthesis
  4. A fixed positive current independent of the ion
Answer and explanation

A. No net passive ionic flux through that channel at equilibrium

Channel availability and driving force are separate. Random exchange may occur in both directions, but the net passive flux is zero at electrochemical equilibrium.

28. A hypothetical epithelium initially secretes 6 and absorbs 9 units/h. Later it secretes 8 and absorbs 5. What is the change in net secretion?

  1. An increase of 6 units/h
  2. An increase of 2 units/h
  3. A decrease of 6 units/h
  4. An increase of 3 units/h
Answer and explanation

A. An increase of 6 units/h

Initial net secretion = −3; final = +3. The difference is +6. Both secretion and absorption changed.

29. A benign one-site ligand has Kd = 5 µM and free ligand concentration 15 µM. Under independent equilibrium binding, what fraction of sites is occupied?

  1. 0.25
  2. 3.00
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.50
Answer and explanation

C. 0.75

The fractional occupancy is L/(Kd+L) = 15/20 = 0.75. Three is a concentration ratio, not a fraction of occupied sites.

30. A whole-cell protein assay shows unchanged total actin while microscopy shows redistribution to the cell cortex. Which explanation is supported?

  1. Actin transcription necessarily doubled.
  2. Every filament was permanently destroyed.
  3. The cell acquired bacterial flagellin as its cytoskeleton.
  4. Spatial reorganization without a demonstrated change in total abundance
Answer and explanation

D. Spatial reorganization without a demonstrated change in total abundance

A localization change can occur without an abundance change. Neither transcription nor specific filament turnover has been directly measured.

31. Two images show similar actin-rich structures; one time series shows movement and the other does not. What additional distinction is necessary?

  1. Static architecture and dynamic behavior must be evaluated separately.
  2. The two objects must move at identical speeds.
  3. Movement is proved by any filament stain.
  4. Both images prove identical molecular causation.
Answer and explanation

A. Static architecture and dynamic behavior must be evaluated separately.

A time series adds evidence about dynamics. Similar architecture cannot establish identical motion or identical underlying regulation.

32. A hypothetical localization image shows an intracellular membrane-bound Salmonella-containing compartment with a late endosomal marker but no detectable activity of a particular lumenal enzyme. What follows?

  1. The bacterium is necessarily free in cytosol.
  2. All lysosomal membrane traffic has stopped throughout the cell.
  3. The compartment is necessarily a fully functional degradative lysosome.
  4. Marker acquisition does not establish the functional state of every lumenal component.
Answer and explanation

D. Marker acquisition does not establish the functional state of every lumenal component.

An enclosing membrane supports vacuolar localization. Membrane composition and lumenal enzyme activity are different properties; neither local observation establishes cell-wide trafficking or full degradative function.

33. A teaching dataset includes 120 compartments, 80 marker-positive and 20 positive for both that marker and another property. What is the conditional proportion of double-positive among marker-positive compartments?

  1. 16.7%
  2. 66.7%
  3. 25%
  4. 80%

C. 25%

Use the specified denominator: 20/80 = 0.25. The fraction among all compartments would instead be 20/120.

34. A vesicle has pH 5.5 and another pH 6.5. What is the ratio of hydrogen ion activity in the first to the second?

  1. 1
  2. 0.1
  3. 100
  4. 10

D. 10

The pH difference is one, giving 10^(6.5−5.5) = 10. This does not determine total proton content.

35. In a textbook comparison, a loss of a bacterial delivery component is associated with unchanged surface attachment but absence of the usual host-cell response. What does this observation distinguish?

  1. Direct synthesis of actin by the bacterium from host synthesis of flagellin
  2. Normal host-cell response from increased total bacterial numbers, which have been proved equal
  3. Surface association from the delivery-dependent response
  4. Presence of a vacuolar membrane from complete absence of host proteins
Answer and explanation

C. Surface association from the delivery-dependent response

Attachment can remain detectable while a later response changes. The observation does not establish every intermediate step, the number of viable bacteria, or a general failure of all secretion.

36. A signal derived from intracellular objects remains detectable at later times. What claim remains unproven?

  1. That the assay detected a signal
  2. That an observation was made at a later time
  3. That the readout is a measured variable
  4. That the objects are viable and multiplying
Answer and explanation

D. That the objects are viable and multiplying

A marker may persist independently of viability. Replication is an additional biological property requiring evidence distinct from mere persistence.

37. Population A contains 100 cells with total signal 400; B contains 50 cells with total signal 200. What is supported?

  1. B has half the mean activity of A.
  2. A has twice the mean activity of B.
  3. Both have identical cell-to-cell distributions.
  4. Both have mean signal 4 units per cell.
Answer and explanation

D. Both have mean signal 4 units per cell.

400/100 and 200/50 each equal 4. The averages reveal nothing about the variability or exact cellular distribution.

38. A host population has half its original average metabolic signal, but individual-cell information is missing. Which conclusion is valid?

  1. Exactly half the cells are dead.
  2. Several distributions of cellular activity could explain the mean.
  3. Every cell is necessarily exactly half as active.
  4. All cells must have intact membranes.
Answer and explanation

B. Several distributions of cellular activity could explain the mean.

A mean can arise from a uniform decline or a mixed population. Additional evidence is necessary for claims about individual cells or viability.

39. Tissue cells remain viable but net fluid movement is abnormal. What does this most directly illustrate?

  1. No physiological injury is possible.
  2. All toxins cause obligatory lysis.
  3. Functional injury can occur without extensive cell death.
  4. Viability establishes normal epithelial polarity.
Answer and explanation

C. Functional injury can occur without extensive cell death.

Viability and physiological function are distinct. Abnormal transport can substantially impair tissue function without wholesale epithelial destruction.

40. A host-cell analysis detects inflammatory caspase activity and gasdermin-dependent membrane permeabilization. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

  1. The findings specifically establish classical apoptosis and exclude other death pathways.
  2. They prove the cell is merely cytostatic and remains undamaged.
  3. They uniquely identify the microbial species present.
  4. The findings support a pyroptotic component of the response.
Answer and explanation

D. The findings support a pyroptotic component of the response.

Inflammatory caspase and gasdermin-associated lytic responses support pyroptosis. Additional evidence can clarify overlapping death pathways; the findings do not identify a species or prove classical apoptosis.

41. A cell contains increased messenger RNA for a protein, but the protein level decreases. Which interpretation is sound?

  1. Transcription must have ceased completely.
  2. Protein synthesis must necessarily have increased.
  3. The protein assay proves the RNA result is incorrect.
  4. RNA abundance alone cannot determine protein abundance.
Answer and explanation

D. RNA abundance alone cannot determine protein abundance.

Protein abundance depends on translation and degradation as well as RNA abundance. The two measurements need not change in the same direction.

42. Two tissue samples contain equal total iron but different proportions bound to storage proteins. What conclusion is warranted?

  1. Both must contain equal free iron activity.
  2. Total iron alone cannot establish equal accessibility.
  3. The sample with more bound iron must have more living bacteria.
  4. All protein-bound iron is equivalent to freely dissolved ion.
Answer and explanation

B. Total iron alone cannot establish equal accessibility.

Chemical form and compartmental distribution determine availability. No microbial viability or nutrient utilization follows from these values alone.

43. A neighboring cell shows altered gene expression although no bacterium is detected adjacent to it. What is a plausible general explanation?

  1. A bacterium must be physically inside its nucleus.
  2. The observation proves direct covalent toxin modification.
  3. Indirect host-derived intercellular signaling
  4. No alteration can occur in an uninfected cell.
Answer and explanation

C. Indirect host-derived intercellular signaling

Host-derived signals can affect neighboring cells. The observation does not specify the mediator or prove direct microbial action.

44. A protein concentration changes from 5 to 15 arbitrary units. Which description is mathematically correct?

  1. A threefold final value and a 300% increase
  2. A twofold final value and a 300% increase
  3. A 10% increase
  4. A threefold final value and a 200% increase
Answer and explanation

D. A threefold final value and a 200% increase

15/5 = 3; (15−5)/5 × 100 = 200%. Fold value and percentage increase are not identical.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Virulence is a fixed number for a microbial species.

    It depends on host context and endpoint. Students confuse pathogenicity and virulence; questions may change host susceptibility while preserving microbial identity.

  2. Any host-cell alteration requires an intracellular bacterium.

    A bacterial product can influence a cell independently of bacterial entry. Drawings may show a product inside a cell and the bacterium outside; distinguish their locations.

  3. Exotoxins and endotoxin are the same chemical class.

    Exotoxins are commonly proteins; lipid A is the endotoxic LPS component. The word toxin is shared, but the chemical structures and actions differ.

  4. The toxin binding component and catalytic component are interchangeable.

    Recognition and biochemical action are distinct molecular roles. An examination may present normal binding with absent downstream response.

  5. Cholera toxin makes cAMP directly.

    Host adenylyl cyclase synthesizes cAMP downstream of altered Gs regulation. Remember the difference between the direct substrate and the downstream enzyme.

  6. CFTR pumps chloride against its gradient because it binds ATP.

    CFTR is an ATP-regulated anion channel. Transport questions often contrast gating with active pumping.

  7. Water is actively generated by a toxin.

    Water movement follows changes in chemical potential and solute transport. A physiological symptom should be connected to net transport rather than treated as a separate chemical product.

  8. High cAMP alone proves increased synthesis.

    Reduced removal can also elevate concentration. Use formation–removal balance and identify the needed additional observation.

  9. An object inside a cell must be in cytosol.

    An enclosing membrane can separate it from cytosol. Microscopy problems often test topology rather than position within a cell outline.

  10. One late-compartment marker proves complete degradative function.

    Membrane identity, pH and lumenal enzyme activity are separate characteristics. Do not treat a marker as a complete functional assay.

  11. Every Salmonella-containing vacuole has identical properties.

    Compartment properties depend on cell type and stage. A representative textbook example is not an absolute rule.

  12. Every change in actin morphology means total actin increased.

    Spatial organization and total abundance are distinct. Compare microscopy and whole-cell assays rather than treating them as interchangeable.

  13. A decrease of one pH unit means a one-unit increase in proton amount.

    Hydrogen ion activity increases tenfold; total proton amount depends on buffering and volume. The logarithmic definition and denominator are frequent examination targets.

  14. A cell that survives is physiologically normal.

    Cells can remain viable with altered transport or signaling. Questions may describe preserved viability but defective tissue function.

  15. A reduced population signal proves reduced activity in every cell.

    Population size and distribution of individual activity also matter. Normalize by the relevant denominator and distinguish averages from distributions.

  16. Nutrient abundance equals accessible nutrient concentration.

    Chemical form and localization influence accessibility. Total tissue measurements cannot establish local metabolic use.

  17. A stable cell number proves absence of proliferation.

    Division and cell loss can balance. Net population change is not a direct measure of individual rates.

  18. A host response in a neighboring cell proves bacterial entry into that cell.

    Indirect intercellular signals can explain an alteration. Distinguish direct microbial action from host-derived secondary signaling.

Integrated revision and mastery

Distinguish the location of a bacterium, the location of its active products and the location of the host target. A changed host phenotype is the result of molecular action, cellular regulation and tissue organization. Reconstruct the causal chain rather than memorizing a list of diseases.

  • Recognition is not equivalent to delivery or downstream response.
  • Gs regulation, messenger balance and epithelial flux are distinct levels of the cholera example.
  • Cytosolic and vacuolar lifestyles differ in membrane topology.
  • Compartment identity, acidity, enzyme activity and viability require separate evidence.
  • Cell survival is not equivalent to physiological recovery.
