1. The biological problem: one genome, two developmental outcomes

A bacteriophage is a virus whose host is a bacterium. A temperate bacteriophage differs from a strictly lytic phage because it can establish a long-lasting association with a bacterial cell rather than immediately completing a cycle of virus multiplication. Bacteriophage lambda, commonly written with the Greek letter λ, is the classical example of this capacity in Escherichia coli. Its genome contains genes whose products promote viral reproduction, and it also contains genes whose products can prevent that reproductive program from being expressed. The question is not whether the viral DNA possesses the instructions for lysis. It possesses those instructions in either developmental state. The question is which sets of instructions become functional at the appropriate time and how the consequences of their expression reinforce a particular developmental outcome.

Consider a bacterial cell into which a lambda genome has entered. In one outcome the viral genome directs production of factors that support DNA replication, structural components of virus particles and proteins that ultimately allow release of progeny. This is the lytic developmental pathway. In the other outcome the viral genome becomes established as a prophage, normally integrated into the bacterial chromosome. The bacterial cell can continue growing, and replication of its chromosome transmits the prophage to its descendants. A bacterium carrying such a prophage is a lysogen. The lytic genes remain present in the prophage but are kept transcriptionally repressed. The lysogenic condition is therefore not a condition in which the viral genome has been destroyed.

There is an important distinction between choosing lysogeny, establishing lysogeny, and maintaining lysogeny. Immediately after entry, the viral genome has no established regulatory history in its new host. It must produce regulatory proteins that influence its future transcription. Establishment includes the production of enough active repressor to prevent progression of the lytic program and normally includes chromosomal integration. Maintenance is a continuing problem faced by a lysogen after the initial regulatory decision has been completed. The protein molecules that first established repression do not remain forever: growth dilutes them, and proteins may be removed. Thus a continuing source of repressor is needed even when the cell experiences no new infection.

A fourth process, induction, is the conversion of a previously established prophage into a lytic developmental program. It does not follow exactly the same molecular chronology as the initial infection, because a lysogen already contains abundant active repressor and its prophage is already integrated. A signal that reduces the activity of that repressor can release repression of early phage genes. The induced genome can then progress through the other necessary steps of productive development. The term induction must therefore be used with care. In the lac operon, an inducer changes the behavior of a regulatory protein in response to a nutrient. In lambda, prophage induction denotes a transition in an entire developmental program.

The ability to use either program has an evolutionary interpretation. Immediate reproduction can be advantageous when susceptible bacteria and cellular resources are readily available. Retention of a viral genome inside a surviving bacterial lineage can offer another means of propagation when immediate reproductive success is uncertain. These advantages are conditional, not universal rules about what an individual phage will do. The molecular circuitry responds to properties of its host and the infecting genomes, rather than measuring future fitness directly. Different outcomes can occur among genetically similar infected cells, which shows why regulation should be understood as a probabilistic biological process with a molecular basis rather than as a conscious decision.

Throughout the chapter, gene names such as cI, cro and cII denote DNA sequences, whereas CI, Cro and CII denote their protein products. A promoter is a DNA region at which RNA polymerase initiates transcription; an operator is a regulatory DNA region recognized by a regulatory protein. A promoter and an operator can overlap, but they are conceptually distinct because the two terms describe different functional relationships. The lambda switch is a particularly valuable example of this distinction: one protein can repress initiation at one promoter while helping initiate transcription at another nearby promoter. The effect of a regulator is not a permanent property of its name but depends on the molecular configuration of the regulatory region.

2. From genetic observations to a molecular switch

The existence of bacteria that release bacteriophages without having been recently infected posed a problem for a simple model of viral reproduction. If every virus multiplied immediately upon infection, a bacterial lineage could not remain viable while retaining a heritable capacity to produce virus. The phenomenon of lysogeny suggested that viral genetic information could persist in a form transmitted through host divisions. Work by André Lwoff and other investigators on lysogenic bacteria established the conceptual foundation for understanding the dormant prophage and its inducibility. The apparent inactivity of the phage did not mean that it had ceased to exist as genetic material.

Esther Lederberg discovered bacteriophage lambda in the context of studies of E. coli. Its association with a bacterial strain made it possible to investigate both its transmission with the bacterial chromosome and its production as infectious particles. The availability of genetic variants was crucial because a difference in viral behavior could be correlated with a particular genetic alteration. Genetic analysis established that lysogen formation was not simply an inevitable physical consequence of carrying viral DNA. Particular phage genes were required for a successful lysogenic outcome, and alterations affecting those genes could favor clear plaques rather than the characteristic turbid appearance of plaques produced under conditions allowing lysogen formation.

A plaque forms where viral multiplication and bacterial killing have reduced the density of a susceptible bacterial lawn. A turbid plaque contains surviving bacteria, and in an appropriate temperate phage system some survivors may be lysogens. A clear plaque suggests more complete local killing but does not, by itself, establish which molecular step has changed. A mutation could change the production of a repressor, the ability of a regulatory protein to recognize DNA, or a requirement for successful establishment of the prophage. A comparison of plaque appearance is a useful starting point for a hypothesis, not an unambiguous identification of a particular gene function. Confirming lysogeny requires evidence of stable phage carriage and an appropriate immunity or genetic test.

A powerful observation was that a lambda lysogen resisted productive superinfection by a phage with the same immunity specificity. The resident prophage appeared to provide a diffusible activity that could act on an incoming genome. This property differs from a receptor mutation that prevents viral entry: in the immunity mechanism relevant here, incoming DNA can encounter a repressor already present in the bacterial cytoplasm. If that repressor recognizes its regulatory sequences, early transcription of the incoming genome is inhibited. The distinction provides a genetic clue to the difference between a cis-acting operator sequence and a trans-acting regulator.

Subsequent molecular work, notably by Mark Ptashne and colleagues, connected repressor action to sequence-specific binding of protein to operator DNA. This transition from genetic phenotype to physical interaction is central to the history of molecular biology. A mutation altering operator DNA could change recognition by the repressor even when the protein remained normal. Conversely, a mutation in a repressor gene could change the behavior of multiple suitable DNA copies inside the same cell. Demonstrating specific binding with isolated components helped establish a molecular explanation for the earlier genetic results.

The lambda system also broadened the meaning of a repressor. At the right operator, bound CI could inhibit a promoter for lytic genes and promote transcription from a promoter that supplies additional CI. A simple model in which a protein is always either an activator or a repressor would not explain this behavior. The molecular arrangement of the operator, promoter and protein determines which interactions are possible. Cooperative binding of CI dimers to adjacent DNA sites and contacts between CI and RNA polymerase explain why a small regulatory region can have different effects on two directions of transcription. The regulatory network also contains feedback, which allows a transient initial decision to be maintained over later cell divisions.

3. Reading the regulatory DNA map

Lambda genes are arranged in a double-stranded DNA genome that can be transcribed by bacterial RNA polymerase. Its regulatory map is easiest to understand if the central immunity region is viewed as a segment of double-stranded DNA rather than as a list of protein names. The two main early lytic promoters are P_L and P_R. The letters L and R refer to the directions traditionally assigned to transcription on the phage genetic map. The promoter P_R drives rightward transcription beginning with cro, while P_L drives leftward transcription beginning with N. Each promoter lies near an operator region where repressor binding can influence transcription.

The right operator O_R contains three neighboring sites, O_R1, O_R2 and O_R3. In the conventional diagram in which the cI gene lies to the left and cro to the right, their physical order from left to right is O_R3, O_R2, O_R1. P_R is directed toward cro, and the opposing promoter P_RM is directed toward cI. The subscript RM refers to repressor maintenance. These sites and promoters overlap in a way that makes occupancy of a particular operator informative about promoter activity. It is not enough to say that CI is bound somewhere within O_R; one must identify which sites are occupied and how that occupancy affects each promoter.

When neither CI nor Cro is present at an effective concentration, P_R and P_L can be recognized by the host RNA polymerase. Transcription initiated at those promoters first produces immediate early RNA molecules that support synthesis of Cro and N, respectively. P_RM is not a substitute for strong initial production of CI: its activity is normally enhanced by CI already bound to O_R2. The existence of this dependence creates an establishment problem. A newly infecting genome that starts with little or no CI cannot simply rely on a mature maintenance circuit to bring itself into the lysogenic state.

The establishment promoter P_RE solves that problem under suitable conditions. Its name refers to repressor establishment, and it is activated by the regulatory protein CII. P_RE supplies a transcript directed toward cI independently of the positive autoregulatory interaction at P_RM. Although both promoters ultimately contribute to production of the same CI protein, they operate in different regulatory circumstances. A defect that affects one of these promoters can therefore have a different phenotype from a defect in the other. An intact P_RE does not automatically guarantee indefinite maintenance, and a working P_RM does not guarantee efficient establishment when the infection lacks a sufficient initial source of CI.

The left operator O_L likewise contains three sites, O_L1, O_L2 and O_L3, associated with control of leftward early transcription. CI binding within O_L is necessary for full control of P_L and can contribute to the overall stability of the lysogenic circuit. The right operator alone is an excellent system for understanding local promoter regulation, but the natural phage contains both regulatory regions. Interactions between CI bound at the two operator regions can bring distant DNA segments together through looping. A map that shows only O_R is thus a reduction of the complete system, useful for particular questions but not a claim that the left region is biologically unimportant.

Other promoters become important in the subsequent temporal program. P_I supports production of integrase under CII-dependent regulation, linking transcriptional choice to establishment of the prophage. P_aQ is a CII-activated promoter associated with an antisense transcript that reduces Q function. The late promoter P_R′ is distinct from P_R; its productive transcription requires the action of Q. The prime mark matters: the presence of rightward early RNA does not establish that the late genes have been expressed. The genome is organized so that early regulatory events influence the capacity of RNA polymerase to reach later genes, not merely its ability to bind the earliest promoters.

When reading an examination diagram, first identify the DNA segments, then the direction of each promoter, then the genes transcribed from it, and finally the regulatory proteins shown on the DNA. The arrow indicating transcription direction is not the same thing as an arrow indicating positive regulation. A line terminating in a bar represents repression in a regulatory network, but the actual molecular cause could be exclusion of RNA polymerase, a change in an initiation step, or premature transcription termination. Separating the physical map from the functional network prevents many common errors about gene order and the sequence of expression.

4. CI structure, sequence recognition and cooperative binding

CI is a DNA-binding regulatory protein commonly called the lambda repressor. Its organization into functional regions explains how it can recognize a specific operator and also participate in interactions with other protein molecules. The amino-terminal region includes a DNA recognition domain with a helix-turn-helix motif. In this kind of motif, an alpha helix contributes to recognition of exposed chemical features in a DNA groove. The carboxy-terminal region contributes to dimerization and higher-order interactions. A connecting region permits communication between these domains and is important in the response to prophage-inducing conditions. These structural facts link directly to the phenotypes of mutations that alter recognition, cooperation or susceptibility to cleavage.

Operator recognition is a noncovalent process. Proteins encounter DNA through molecular motion and bind when the contacts between their surfaces and DNA are energetically favorable. Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic contacts with the phosphate backbone and interactions involving the shape and chemistry of DNA all contribute. The operator sequence matters because changing the pattern of bases can change the energetic favorability of the protein-DNA complex. Nevertheless, recognition is not an all-or-none chemical transformation: bound molecules can dissociate, and the fraction of time a site is occupied depends on affinity and free protein concentration.

The active DNA-binding arrangement of CI is a dimer. Two protein subunits cooperate in recognizing the operator sequence, which has approximate symmetry suited to recognition by a protein dimer. One should distinguish the association of two monomers into a dimer from cooperativity between different dimers on neighboring operator sites. Dimerization produces a functional binding species. Cooperative occupancy occurs when one bound dimer makes binding of a second dimer more favorable. A mutation in a protein interaction surface can therefore preserve the ability of individual dimers to bind DNA but change the probability that neighboring sites will be occupied together.

At O_R, CI has a strong intrinsic preference for O_R1 relative to the other right-operator sites. CI at O_R1 can stabilize the occupancy of O_R2 through favorable contacts with a second dimer. In consequence, O_R2 becomes effectively occupied at concentrations at which binding of an isolated O_R2 site would be less favorable. Occupation of these two sites is central to lysogenic regulation. It suppresses expression from the rightward lytic promoter while placing a CI molecule in a suitable position to assist transcription from the maintenance promoter. The two consequences are produced by the geometry of one DNA region.

The value of cooperative binding is especially evident when the concentration of repressor varies. If the regulatory sites acted independently, intermediate concentrations would produce combinations of occupied and unoccupied sites according to each separate affinity. When favorable interactions exist between adjacent dimers, the joint occupancy of the two sites can increase more sharply. This couples regulation of two nearby promoters, although the response is still governed by binding probabilities rather than an absolutely rigid threshold. Cooperativity helps maintain a useful configuration despite fluctuations in protein concentration and allows a substantial change in promoter behavior when repressor abundance falls.

At higher CI concentrations, occupation of O_R3 becomes important. This site is positioned so that CI can repress P_RM. The fact that CI activates maintenance transcription through O_R2 at one concentration and inhibits it through O_R3 at another is a key principle of the switch. Positive feedback is balanced by negative feedback, preventing an uncontrolled increase in the concentration of the regulator. The natural system also includes contributions from O_L and long-range interactions, which enhance regulation at concentrations relevant to the lysogen. A simple three-site diagram can introduce the mechanism, but the physiological response is influenced by the complete operator organization.

Mutations provide a way to separate these molecular tasks. A change in the DNA-recognition helix could weaken binding to both operator regions and consequently disrupt repression of early lytic genes. A mutation affecting cooperative contacts might leave binding to a strong isolated site relatively normal but decrease occupancy of a neighboring site. A mutation affecting an interaction with RNA polymerase could preserve repression of P_R yet impair activation of P_RM. Therefore, a statement that a mutant CI protein binds DNA does not establish that it maintains lysogeny normally. At examination level, the appropriate prediction depends on the specific activity that is removed, rather than on treating all cI mutations as functionally identical.

5. Maintenance of lysogeny and concentration control

An established lysogen must solve two simultaneous problems. It must prevent transcription of genes that would initiate the lytic developmental pathway, and it must continue making the regulator responsible for repression. A circuit that shut down every phage promoter would solve the first problem only temporarily. As the bacterial cell grows, the existing repressor molecules become distributed through a larger volume and between daughter cells. Without additional production, the active concentration would fall. This explains why lambda cannot maintain lysogeny by a single transient pulse of CI.

Binding of CI to the right operator represses P_R, limiting the synthesis of Cro and the other rightward early products. At the left operator, CI represses P_L and reduces N production and the subsequent leftward program. At O_R2, CI can also assist the initiation of transcription from P_RM through an interaction with RNA polymerase. This is an example of positive autoregulation, because the product of the cI gene facilitates production of additional copies of itself. The regulator is a repressor in relation to the lytic promoters and an activator in relation to the maintenance promoter. Both descriptions are simultaneously correct.

Positive autoregulation provides a molecular explanation for a limited form of cellular memory. After initial CII activity has subsided, the cI gene can continue to be transcribed from P_RM because of CI that is already present. Newly synthesized CI helps preserve the same transcriptional environment. The genetic information is unchanged, but the current concentrations and DNA occupancy of regulatory proteins influence future expression. This provides a useful analogy with other biological systems in which a transcriptional program perpetuates itself, without requiring the distinct chromatin mechanisms used by many eukaryotic genes.

If positive feedback operated without constraints, high production of the regulatory protein could become metabolically costly or prevent an appropriate response to unfavorable host conditions. CI occupation of O_R3 inhibits P_RM when the active repressor concentration becomes sufficiently high. This negative autoregulatory contribution limits synthesis of new CI. As concentration declines from an excessive level, the inhibitory occupancy can diminish. Over a useful range, the lysogen therefore has a mechanism for stabilizing the amount of repressor rather than maximally expressing cI at all times.

The importance of long-range DNA organization can be understood by thinking about binding at O_L and O_R as parts of a single molecular system. Dimers bound at neighboring sites can assemble into higher-order complexes, and complexes at the two operator regions can interact while the intervening DNA forms a loop. Loop formation changes the energetics of different configurations. It can stabilize repression of the lytic promoters and help make regulation of the maintenance promoter effective over its physiological concentration range. A loop is not a new DNA sequence, and its formation does not mean that the two operator regions have become adjacent in the primary sequence. It reflects the ability of a flexible polymer to fold in three dimensions.

The molecular switch also explains immunity to infection by a related phage with the same operator specificity. CI molecules synthesized from the resident prophage can diffuse through the bacterial cell and encounter the operator regions of a newly introduced lambda genome. They need not be made from the same DNA molecule on which they act. The DNA operators are cis elements, whereas CI is a trans-acting protein. A sufficiently different operator specificity can escape recognition by that particular repressor. Consequently, immunity is a specific regulatory phenomenon, not a universal inability of the cell to be infected by any bacteriophage.

When considering a lysogen with a weakened P_RM promoter, it is important to distinguish a reduction of new synthesis from immediate removal of existing protein. The cell may retain enough CI for a time after the promoter defect takes effect. During subsequent growth and division, however, insufficient replenishment can make repression less stable. An endpoint observation made soon after a perturbation could consequently differ from one made many generations later. The feedback circuit is a dynamic system with production, removal and dilution, not a static diagram in which a gene name is instantly converted into a permanent state.

6. Cro and the regulation of the lytic program

Cro is another sequence-specific DNA-binding protein encoded in the lambda immunity region. Like CI, it can bind operator DNA as a dimer, but its binding preferences and regulatory consequences differ. Cro is among the first proteins produced from the rightward early transcription unit when an incoming genome is not already repressed. Its position in the genetic map makes it available early enough to influence the expression of other regulatory genes and the progress of the phage developmental program.

In the classical model, Cro has a preference for O_R3, which is associated with the repressor maintenance promoter P_RM. Binding of Cro to this site reduces transcription from P_RM. This property is often contrasted with the effect of CI at O_R2, which promotes transcription from the same promoter. The different site preferences explain how related operator-binding activities can have contrasting effects. Cro does not need to destroy CI molecules chemically to limit production of new repressor from P_RM. It changes the availability of the promoter by occupying regulatory DNA.

At higher concentrations Cro can occupy additional operator sites and inhibit early transcription. This feature is important because a lytic developmental program is not a state in which every lytic promoter remains maximally active throughout the infection. Early gene products must accumulate before they execute their functions, and excessive persistence of early expression may not be useful after the developmental program has progressed. Negative feedback by Cro contributes to regulation of its own early transcription unit and affects production of other proteins required for the alternative developmental outcome. The phrase that Cro turns on lytic genes is therefore mechanistically misleading: its direct activities include repression, and its presence favors a regulatory context in which lytic development can proceed.

The classical CI–Cro opposition is a useful model for describing maintenance and switching, but it is not a complete explanation of the initial decision after infection. During establishment, the amount and activity of CII and the timing of Q function are also central. Cro can favor lytic development indirectly by reducing early transcription and hence the amount of CII available to activate establishment promoters. An exam question that explicitly gives a simplified CI–Cro toggle can be solved according to the stated model. A question that asks how wild-type lambda establishes lysogeny should additionally account for CII-dependent activation of P_RE and the competition between establishment and late-gene expression.

A loss-of-function mutation in cro does not automatically make every infected cell a stable lysogen. The result depends on the remaining gene functions, host physiology and whether sufficient CI is produced from an establishment promoter. Similarly, detecting Cro protein in an early infection does not prove that a cell will eventually lyse. Both regulatory pathways begin with some early gene expression. The relevant issue is the concentrations and timing of their products, together with the interactions at the regulatory sites. This distinction between an early molecular event and a final phenotype is particularly important when interpreting time-course experiments.

7. The temporal program immediately after infection

The lambda developmental program can be understood as a succession of changes in which newly made products change the expression of genes that were not efficiently expressed before. The genome that first enters the bacterial cell is not supplied with a fully assembled transcriptional program for either outcome. It depends on the bacterial transcription and translation machinery. The early promoters P_L and P_R can therefore begin to function before the phage has manufactured its own regulatory proteins. This arrangement solves a general problem encountered by viruses: if an essential viral transcription factor were required for expression of every viral gene, including its own gene, the new infection could not begin unless that factor were supplied by some other route.

Cro and N are described as immediate early products because their genes lie in positions that allow expression from the primary early transcription units without the full set of downstream phage factors. The presence of these products does not mean that lysis has already been selected. In fact, the same early program produces the regulatory conditions from which lysogeny may be established. The initial output is better regarded as a set of regulatory possibilities rather than as a completed pathway. At a later time, CI-mediated repression or progression of late gene expression makes the pathways diverge more decisively.

The primary transcripts encounter termination signals. A transcription terminator is a sequence whose properties can lead to release of the growing RNA and separation of the RNA polymerase from the DNA template. In bacterial transcription, the probability of termination can depend on features of the RNA, associated proteins and the state of the transcription elongation complex. If polymerase starting at an early lambda promoter consistently stopped at its first effective terminator, many of the downstream genes required for either developmental outcome would not be efficiently expressed. Lambda overcomes this temporal limitation by producing N, an antitermination protein.

N does not simply act as a second promoter activator. It functions on the elongation process by participating in the modification of transcription complexes. Specific recognition elements called nut sites are transcribed into the nascent RNA. N and bacterial Nus proteins assemble with components of the elongation complex so that polymerase becomes more resistant to termination. The RNA elements contribute to the specificity of recruitment, which helps explain why N does not cause every cellular RNA polymerase to ignore its termination signals. The regulatory consequence is that polymerases entering the appropriate phage transcription units are more likely to continue into downstream regions.

This distinction between initiation and elongation can be tested with a conceptual pair of reporter genes positioned on the same transcription unit, one before and one after a terminator. Loss of N function may leave substantial expression of the upstream reporter while reducing expression of the downstream reporter. A measurement made only at the promoter or only with the upstream reporter could therefore suggest that early transcription is normal despite a major defect in phage gene expression. In contrast, a mutation that abolished promoter recognition would reduce transcription of both reporters, because polymerases would not enter the unit efficiently in the first place.

N-dependent readthrough makes additional early gene products available, including regulatory factors involved in the developmental decision and factors required for the lytic program. Rightward transcription supplies CII and the replication and late-regulation functions represented by O, P and Q, while the leftward program includes CIII and other functions. The precise amounts of these products depend on several layers of regulation; the educational point is that access to delayed early genes is not identical to the activation of P_L or P_R. This places the control of transcript length alongside promoter control as a fundamental component of lambda development.

Consider an N-defective phage in the absence of functional N supplied by another genome. It may produce Cro from the immediate rightward region but fail to produce sufficient delayed early gene products. A simplistic rule that Cro causes lysis would predict an efficient lytic cycle. The mechanistic model predicts otherwise because successful lytic reproduction also requires other proteins whose expression depends on effective antitermination. The mutant may also establish lysogeny poorly because the production of CII and CIII is affected. Preventing the lytic program is not equivalent to completing the lysogenic one. Both require particular molecular functions.

The temporal order thus emerges from a causal dependency rather than from an external clock. Initial transcription yields N; N changes the behavior of elongating polymerase; downstream regulators become available; their activities alter which programs can continue. Such an architecture is a common principle in biological regulation. When the output of one stage enables another stage, a mutation at the first stage can affect many later products without directly interacting with any of their coding sequences. Distinguishing direct and indirect effects is essential for interpreting expression patterns in genetic experiments.

8. CII, CIII and the establishment of lysogeny

The production of functional CI during initial infection poses a problem different from that faced by a mature lysogen. At the beginning, little active CI is present, so the maintenance promoter cannot receive its usual positive autoregulatory stimulation. The phage solves this problem through an additional regulatory route involving CII. CII is a transcriptional activator that recognizes particular promoter regions and helps initiate expression from them. Its biological role is to create a favorable window for the establishment of lysogeny rather than to serve indefinitely as the repressor of the lytic promoters.

Activation of P_RE by CII provides new cI transcripts. Once sufficient CI accumulates, the products of those transcripts bind operator DNA and begin to suppress P_L and P_R. This produces a significant change in the whole early transcriptional system. As early transcription decreases, the production of factors that originally helped establish CI can also decline. That decline does not necessarily reverse the developmental outcome because CI can now stimulate its own production through P_RM. The system has handed control from a transient establishment circuit to a continuing maintenance circuit.

CII activates more than the establishment promoter for cI. It also stimulates expression from P_I, which helps provide integrase, the protein that carries out the phage-specific recombination necessary for normal integration. The detailed chemistry of the recombination reaction belongs to the study of site-specific recombination. The gene-regulatory significance here is that two separate requirements for stable lysogeny are linked to the same regulatory input: repression of the reproductive program and provision of a factor needed to establish the prophage. Without this coordination a cell could acquire the repressor but lack efficient integration, or acquire an integration function while continuing to express incompatible lytic genes.

CII also activates P_aQ, whose transcription produces an RNA antisense to part of the Q expression region. This supplies an additional way to oppose the progress of the late lytic program. Q is needed for efficient productive transcription of late genes, and the antisense route contributes to reduced Q function under lysogeny-favoring conditions. It is not necessary to assume that a single CII molecule switches off every lytic gene directly. CII can change the regulatory balance by increasing CI production, enabling integration and restraining progression toward late gene expression.

The amount of CII present is affected not only by the rate at which it is synthesized but also by the rate at which it is degraded. Host proteolysis plays an important part in this regulation. FtsH, also historically called HflB in this context, is a bacterial ATP-dependent protease that contributes to CII turnover. ATP-dependent proteases use chemical energy to facilitate the handling and degradation of protein substrates. In the present context, their key effect is to change the lifetime of a phage regulatory protein. A high synthesis rate need not produce high CII abundance if the protein is removed very rapidly.

CIII favors CII persistence by protecting it against FtsH-dependent degradation. CIII is not simply an alternative name for CI, and it does not replace the role of CI at the immunity operators. Its important contribution is upstream of the establishment program: it increases the opportunity for CII to accumulate and activate its target promoters. This is an example of gene regulation operating through protein stability. The final readout may be altered transcription from P_RE, yet the immediate regulatory event involves changes in protein degradation rather than a direct interaction between CIII and the cI promoter.

This distinction becomes clearer in a hypothetical experiment where CII messenger RNA abundance is identical in two infected cell populations but CII protein disappears more rapidly in one population. If the rate of translation is also comparable, a difference in protein removal could account for different CII concentrations. The population with shorter CII lifetime may produce less CI from P_RE and fewer stable lysogens even though transcription of cII itself is unchanged. An investigator who measured only cII RNA would miss the important regulatory difference. Protein abundance, protein lifetime and promoter output are different readouts.

A second hypothetical experiment compares a normal CII protein with a variant that is produced normally but cannot activate P_RE. If the variant retains other biochemical properties, the amount of CII detected may be high even though repressor establishment is defective. This illustrates why concentration and activity are not interchangeable terms. A protein can be present without carrying out its required molecular interaction. To distinguish this case from rapid protein degradation, one would compare measurements of CII abundance and the activity of a CII-responsive promoter, using appropriate controls for DNA sequence and basal promoter function.

Host physiological conditions can influence CII accumulation and the balance between developmental pathways. Classical discussions of lambda describe greater lysogeny under some conditions associated with poor host growth and multiple infecting genomes, although the relationship depends on the infection context. These tendencies should not be turned into universal deterministic rules. Host physiology affects several quantities at once, including protein production, proteolysis, genome replication and resource availability. The fact that lysogeny is favored under a particular condition does not prove that one specific protease is the only mediator of that effect.

Multiplicity of infection is commonly defined as the ratio of infecting particles to susceptible cells in a specified population. This average does not establish that each cell receives exactly that many genomes. Under ideal assumptions of independently distributed infection events, individual cells can receive zero, one or multiple genomes even when the mean is one. Multiple genomes can alter regulatory protein dosage and the balance of the establishment circuit. However, neither the average multiplicity nor the number of infecting genomes is by itself sufficient to predict the outcome of every cell. Actual genome entry, physiological differences and regulatory fluctuations also matter.

A key consequence of the CII pathway is that an intact CI protein alone cannot guarantee successful establishment. A defect in a CII-responsive establishment promoter can reduce early CI synthesis even when cI itself encodes a functional repressor. Similarly, removing CIII may reduce CII stability without making the cI coding sequence defective. Conversely, supplying CI in trans may restore repression of a susceptible operator but will not necessarily restore all the separate functions of CII, such as activation of the integrase promoter. The correct genetic prediction is conditional on which missing functions have been supplied.

The handover from P_RE to P_RM is therefore best understood as a change in the regulator that controls cI transcription. CII is useful while the system is being initialized; CI becomes its own maintenance input once the system has established the appropriate operator configuration. The change resembles a biological circuit that uses one pathway to enter a state and another to preserve it. Questions about the initial infection and questions about a pre-existing lysogen consequently require different reasoning even if they refer to the same cI gene.

9. Q and the timing of late lytic gene expression

A phage that follows productive lytic development must eventually make structural and lysis-related proteins. Producing those proteins as soon as DNA enters a cell would not in itself ensure production of infectious progeny. The earlier program must provide regulatory functions and support production of phage DNA before assembly and release become effective. Lambda coordinates much of the late transcriptional program through the product of the Q gene. Like N, Q is described as an antitermination factor, but it acts in a different context and has a different position in the developmental sequence.

Q acts on RNA polymerase associated with the late promoter P_R′. It is recruited through specific interactions at the late regulatory region and a promoter-proximal paused transcription complex. Once the appropriate complex is formed, polymerase becomes able to transcribe through a downstream termination barrier and into the late gene region. The promoter can have some initiation activity even in the absence of Q, but short transcripts are not equivalent to productive expression of the full late transcription unit. This provides another example of regulation after transcription has already begun.

The division between N-dependent and Q-dependent transcription is useful because it creates a cascade of gene-expression capabilities. N permits efficient expression of delayed early genes, one of which is Q. Q then enables expression of genes whose products carry out late functions. A defect in N can consequently reduce Q production and indirectly reduce late gene expression. A defect confined to Q can permit a substantial portion of the early program while preventing efficient productive late transcription. These different positions in the regulatory hierarchy allow genetic experiments to distinguish early and late requirements.

It is possible for a cell to initiate transcription from P_R′ but fail to express its late genes because elongation is terminated. An examination question might report similar amounts of promoter-proximal RNA in two mutants but sharply different quantities of downstream late RNA. Such a result favors a difference in transcript readthrough over a simple change in promoter initiation, provided that RNA processing and stability have been considered. The interpretation becomes stronger if direct measurements of newly synthesized RNA confirm the same pattern. Steady-state abundance alone cannot always identify a specific kinetic step.

The function of Q can also be understood through the distinction between repression of an alternative fate and activation of productive reproduction. Low CI permits early lytic transcription, but this does not automatically create infectious progeny. If Q is absent or inactive, the late program can still fail. Similarly, the presence of Q protein does not prove successful completion of all later stages: replication, assembly and release require additional functional components. Thus gene expression and the final production of a biological phenotype occupy different levels of causation. A question about late promoter activity should not automatically be answered using plaque counts as though the two measurements were identical.

During the establishment of lysogeny, the activities associated with CII oppose advancement of the Q-dependent program. A favorable early balance allows CI to accumulate and repress the early lytic promoters before the phage becomes committed to productive late expression. This temporal relationship is the reason why a textbook drawing containing only two opposing arrows between CI and Cro is inadequate for explaining the entire infection. That drawing describes a central regulatory opposition, while the CII and Q pathways explain how an initially uncommitted genome approaches a particular developmental outcome.

The lytic program itself should not be imagined as a permanent equilibrium in an indefinitely growing cell. It proceeds through sequential events and normally terminates in release of progeny and destruction of the host cell. When a mathematical or schematic model refers to a stable low-CI, high-Cro state, it is describing the stability of a regulatory configuration within the modeled time interval. It is not claiming that a productively infected cell persists forever. Keeping this distinction in mind prevents a useful abstract genetic switch from being mistaken for the entire biological life cycle.

10. Prophage induction and the bacterial DNA damage response

A lysogen can remain stable over many bacterial generations, yet it must retain the capacity to enter productive development when the circumstances become appropriate. The central problem for induction is that the lytic promoters P_L and P_R are already repressed by CI. Merely providing a reason for the phage to enter lytic development does not remove the molecular obstacle. A cellular signal must change the activity or abundance of the repressor so that the operator regions become accessible to RNA polymerase.

DNA damage can trigger the bacterial SOS response. In E. coli, DNA damage and the associated disturbances to DNA replication can lead to accumulation of single-stranded DNA. RecA can assemble on such DNA in an activated nucleoprotein state. This activated form of RecA participates in the regulation of the bacterial DNA damage response, including promotion of self-cleavage of the LexA repressor. LexA normally represses expression of a set of bacterial SOS genes; its loss of repressor activity allows increased expression of those genes.

Lambda CI is also susceptible to RecA-stimulated autocleavage. This feature connects the physiological state of the host to the phage regulatory circuit. It is important to describe the mechanism accurately: activated RecA is not simply a protease that cuts CI in the manner of a nonspecific protein-degrading enzyme. Rather, RecA in its activated state facilitates a cleavage reaction carried out by the repressor itself. The cleavage separates functional portions of CI and compromises its effective repressor function. It therefore changes the fraction of CI molecules capable of maintaining the normal operator configuration.

The initial consequence of CI inactivation is a change in occupancy of the operator regions, not instant destruction of the phage genome or immediate production of virus particles. As active CI becomes less available, P_L and P_R can resume transcription. Their products then initiate subsequent stages of the lytic program. Cro production contributes to preventing recovery of CI maintenance expression, and other early factors support the progression toward productive development. Prophage induction consequently uses a molecular signal from the host to alter the feedback conditions that had preserved lysogeny.

Induction also involves a change in the physical relationship between the phage genome and the bacterial chromosome. The integrated prophage must normally be excised before productive multiplication of the phage DNA. Integrase and the directionality factor Xis participate in this recombination process, with other required factors. This is not the same molecular event as release of repression from P_R and P_L. Loss of CI function is a transcriptional control event, while excision is a DNA rearrangement event enabled by expression of the appropriate functions. A genetic defect can affect one stage without necessarily abolishing the other.

The distinction between CI cleavage and ordinary protein dilution is important for understanding the rapidity of induction. In an untreated lysogen, CI molecules are continually produced to balance loss and dilution. A sudden increase in the rate of repressor inactivation can drive the concentration of active molecules below the range needed for effective operator repression much faster than ordinary cell growth would do. If the perturbation is sufficiently brief or weak, however, remaining repressor and continued synthesis may allow the circuit to recover. Whether a transient perturbation produces a lasting developmental change depends on the dynamics of the system, not simply on the existence of some DNA damage.

A classical genetic prediction concerns an induction-defective CI variant that retains DNA binding and the capacity to repress but is resistant to RecA-promoted cleavage. Such a repressor can preserve repression when the ordinary DNA damage response would inactivate the wild-type protein. The mutant is not equivalent to a complete loss of cI, because its basal repressor function can remain intact. Conversely, a repressor variant that loses DNA binding after an unrelated physical perturbation might release repression even when the normal RecA-dependent signaling route is unavailable. This illustrates the difference between an upstream signaling requirement and the direct molecular target that controls the promoters.

A recA-defective host can also show defective induction through the ordinary SOS-linked mechanism, assuming the relevant phage and host conditions. However, RecA has additional cellular roles in recombination and DNA repair. Therefore, a difference in progeny yield between recA-positive and recA-defective cells cannot be attributed exclusively to CI cleavage without additional evidence. A reporter for early promoter output, measurement of intact CI and an independent measurement of downstream phage production would help distinguish an induction defect from other consequences of host damage or altered DNA metabolism.

The bacterial LexA regulator and lambda CI provide a comparative illustration of a similar regulatory principle operating on different sets of genes. In each case, activated RecA promotes repressor self-cleavage and thereby relieves inhibition of target transcription. LexA controls host DNA damage response genes; CI controls the phage immunity region and related regulatory functions. A normal SOS response is therefore not evidence that phage induction has necessarily occurred, particularly when the prophage is absent or its repressor is resistant to cleavage. The host response and the prophage response share a signaling input but have different direct targets and consequences.
