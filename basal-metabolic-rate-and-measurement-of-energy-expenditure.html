1. Energy expenditure as a physiological problem

A resting animal may appear inactive, yet its cells continually use chemical energy. The heart contracts, respiratory muscles move air, membrane pumps restore ion gradients, kidneys transport solutes and proteins undergo turnover. Each of these processes requires a supply of free energy. Even when no food is entering the gut and no voluntary movement occurs, chemical energy from previously absorbed nutrients is transformed into cellular work and heat. The persistence of these processes is the reason a physiological measurement of energy expenditure is meaningful even in an apparently motionless animal.

Energy and energy expenditure describe different quantities. Energy is an amount, expressed in joules or kilocalories, whereas expenditure is a rate, expressed in joules per second, kilojoules per hour or kilocalories per day. A watt represents one joule per second. A dietary kilocalorie equals 4.184 kilojoules. Thus a rate of 1 kcal per minute is equivalent to approximately 69.7 W. A statement that an organism uses 1440 kcal per day does not necessarily mean that it used exactly one kilocalorie every minute; it is a daily average. Metabolic measurements therefore need a time interval and conditions of observation, as well as a numerical value.

An organism is an open thermodynamic system. It exchanges matter and energy with the environment. During a sufficiently long observation interval the energy supplied by metabolizable nutrients can be partitioned into the change in chemical energy stored in tissues, external work and heat transferred to the surroundings, with additional outputs such as energy in secreted products when relevant. This conservation principle does not imply that every calorie released from food is instantly dissipated as heat. The oxidation of stored fat may supply energy for maintenance during a fast, whereas a growing animal may retain some of its absorbed energy in new tissue. Distinguishing these possibilities requires measurements of expenditure and, when relevant, changes in body energy stores.

At the cellular level oxidation of organic substrates transfers electrons to carriers that ultimately supply the mitochondrial respiratory chain. The resulting proton electrochemical gradient supports ATP synthesis. ATP hydrolysis then drives processes that are not thermodynamically favourable on their own, such as ion pumping, biosynthesis and contraction. Some energy is dissipated as heat during oxidation, and much of the energy used in internal work also ends as heat. Not all oxygen consumption produces an identical quantity of ATP, because coupling efficiency and the substrates used can differ. Nevertheless, the chemical relation between oxygen consumption, carbon dioxide production and energy release is sufficiently predictable under defined conditions to form the basis of indirect calorimetry.

Metabolic rate is not a direct measurement of the rate of one particular enzyme or pathway. It is the combined outcome of many cellular and tissue processes. A higher whole-animal rate can arise from greater activity in existing tissues, a larger quantity of metabolically active tissue, a different physiological state or a combination of these. A calorimeter alone cannot tell which organ caused a change. An experimental design must separate these explanations by controlling the circumstances and measuring appropriate additional variables. This distinction will recur when interpreting body mass, hormones and temperature effects.

2. Basal, resting and standard metabolism

The practical difficulty in comparing animals is that energy use varies even within one individual. A recent meal stimulates intestinal movement, secretion, absorption and nutrient processing. Standing and walking require muscle work. Cold exposure can stimulate heat production. Anxiety changes neural and endocrine activity. Comparing measurements taken under different conditions may reveal these temporary costs rather than a difference in maintenance metabolism. Basal metabolic rate, abbreviated BMR, is an operationally standardized measurement intended to reduce such influences in endotherms.

In a conventional human basal protocol, the individual is awake, physically relaxed, usually lying supine, postabsorptive after an overnight fast, and thermally comfortable in a thermoneutral environment. Prior physical exertion is restricted and adequate rest is allowed before measurement. A classic FAO protocol describes approximately ten to twelve hours after the last meal and a period of overnight physical rest. The fasting duration is a human convention rather than a universal interval for every animal; digestive transit time and metabolic responses differ among species. The term postabsorptive means that the immediate processing of the most recent meal is no longer a major contributor to measured expenditure, not that the organism has stopped absorbing every molecule.

Thermoneutrality is an especially important condition. An endotherm can maintain body temperature across a broad range of ambient temperatures, but it does not necessarily do so at the same metabolic cost. Within its thermoneutral zone, changes in heat transfer can ordinarily maintain thermal balance without a substantial additional requirement for regulatory heat production. Below the lower critical temperature, increased metabolic heat production may become necessary; above the upper critical temperature active cooling responses may raise expenditure. The thermoneutral zone depends on body size, insulation, posture, wind, humidity and other conditions. The ambient temperature suitable for an unclothed small mammal cannot simply be borrowed from a protocol for a clothed adult human.

Resting metabolic rate, RMR, also represents energy expenditure during quiet rest, but its measurement conditions are generally less restrictive. The exact fasting period, duration of prior rest, time of day and posture must be reported, because RMR is not defined by a single universally interchangeable protocol. It often exceeds rigorously measured BMR, although the difference in any comparison depends on method and physiological circumstances. A sample taken after lunch while a person sits quietly is a resting measurement in an ordinary descriptive sense, but it is not automatically a valid BMR measurement. Neither BMR nor RMR includes the full expenditure of an active day.

Standard metabolic rate, SMR, in ectothermic physiology refers to a minimal maintenance rate measured in an inactive, postabsorptive animal at a specified temperature and under suitable stable conditions. The temperature must accompany the value because an ectotherm's body temperature and reaction rates frequently track the thermal environment. A fish may require an acclimation and observation period in a respirometer before spontaneous activity declines. An apparently low rate measured during severe hypoxia or physiological collapse does not represent a suitable standard rate. The animal must be capable of normal maintenance under the specified conditions. For comparisons among fish, both oxygen availability and water temperature matter.

Sleep is also a distinct condition. An awake basal measurement minimizes voluntary activity but does not require sleep; sleeping expenditure can vary across stages and need not equal BMR. Torpor, hibernation and other regulated hypometabolic states likewise differ from ordinary basal measurements, even if they yield lower rates. Basal should not be interpreted as the lowest metabolic value an animal could ever exhibit. It is a reproducible reference state. This definition enables comparison only when physiological context is respected.

3. The cellular and organ costs of maintenance

The resting body allocates energy unevenly across tissues. An active transport epithelium can expend substantial ATP while its cells remain stationary. The sodium-potassium ATPase consumes ATP to maintain gradients that support electrical excitability, nutrient uptake, volume regulation and secondary active transport. Ion gradients are not permanent batteries: channels, carriers and passive leaks continually dissipate them. Pumps restore the gradients, creating a recurring metabolic cost even at rest. The kidney illustrates this principle particularly well because much of its transport activity depends directly or indirectly on ATP-supported sodium transport.

Protein synthesis and degradation also continue in a weight-stable animal. Old or damaged proteins are replaced; enzymes and membrane components are renewed; molecular quality-control systems maintain cellular function. Biosynthesis requires activated intermediates, ATP and other nucleotide triphosphates. A constant tissue mass therefore does not mean that synthesis has stopped. Equal rates of synthesis and breakdown give no net accumulation, but both fluxes can consume energy. Similar reasoning applies to some substrate cycles, in which opposing reaction pathways permit rapid regulation yet can dissipate chemical energy.

The brain requires energy to maintain membrane gradients and support synaptic activity. The heart must pump continuously and the respiratory system must sustain ventilation. Liver metabolism supports numerous transformations that maintain circulating substrates and remove or process metabolites. The kidneys reabsorb filtered materials and regulate extracellular composition. These organs contribute disproportionately to resting expenditure relative to their mass. Skeletal muscle generally consumes less energy per kilogram at quiet rest than several visceral organs, but its large total mass and capacity for contraction make it highly important for whole-body expenditure. Adipose tissue is living tissue too, not an inert storage bag, although its resting metabolic rate per unit mass is generally lower than that of several major organs.

This heterogeneity explains why fat-free mass is a useful but imperfect predictor of BMR. Fat-free mass includes skeletal muscle, bone, body water and organs, which differ in their specific metabolic rates. Two individuals with the same fat-free mass may have different organ proportions and consequently different resting expenditure. Fat mass also has metabolic activity and affects body size and physiological regulation. An investigator should therefore avoid interpreting an association between BMR and fat-free mass as proof that all lean tissue has the same metabolic rate.

A simple tissue model writes whole-animal rate as the sum of products of tissue masses and tissue-specific rates. If tissue i has mass m_i in kilograms and specific expenditure r_i in kcal per kilogram per day, then its contribution is m_i times r_i in kcal per day. The sum is an accounting model, not a claim that organs are independent. Altering cardiac work, endocrine status or perfusion may change several tissue rates at once. For an illustrative animal, a tissue of 0.5 kg using 200 kcal/kg/day contributes 100 kcal/day, while a tissue of 5 kg using 20 kcal/kg/day also contributes 100 kcal/day. A tenfold difference in mass need not produce a difference in total contribution when specific rates differ inversely. This arithmetic explains why a small organ can matter greatly to basal expenditure.

4. Heat, work and direct calorimetry

One of the earliest routes to quantitative animal metabolism was to measure heat. Classical experiments associated with Lavoisier and Laplace used an ice calorimeter to relate animal respiration to heat production. Later animal and human calorimeters refined the measurement of heat transfer and gas exchange. The conceptual achievement was to treat metabolism as a measurable chemical and physical process rather than a special force unique to living matter. Historical calorimeters were limited by instrumentation and control of heat transfer, so modern experimental results should not be inferred from a schematic reconstruction of the old apparatus.

Direct calorimetry measures the heat given off by a subject in a defined system. In a whole-body calorimeter a chamber surrounds the subject; heat exchange through the walls, moving air or a circulating fluid can be quantified. If a fluid of known mass flow rate enters at one temperature and leaves at a higher temperature, the heat transferred to the fluid per unit time is mass flow multiplied by specific heat capacity and the temperature rise. For water near ordinary laboratory temperatures, a specific heat capacity of about 4.18 kJ per kilogram per degree Celsius is a useful approximation. A water flow of 0.10 kg/min warming by 0.8 °C carries approximately 0.334 kJ/min, or 5.57 W. That result is the contribution of the measured water stream, not automatically the entire expenditure of the animal.

Heat can leave the subject as sensible heat through conduction, convection and radiation or as latent heat through evaporation. A calorimeter that measures only sensible heat will underestimate total heat loss when evaporation is substantial. Humidity changes and the energy associated with water vapour must therefore be considered. During exercise or changes in ambient temperature, some metabolic heat can be temporarily stored within the body, changing body temperature. In that interval the heat escaping from the chamber is not necessarily equal to heat being generated by metabolism. A large thermal mass also causes delays in the response of the apparatus.

Mechanical work matters when comparing heat measurements with metabolic energy release. A person cycling on an ergometer may transfer useful mechanical energy to a device. If the energy leaves the calorimeter as external work it must be included in the accounting. If it is dissipated as heat inside the measured system, counting it separately would double-count that contribution. At quiet basal rest, external work is small and stable body temperature reduces the heat storage term, making measured heat loss a useful approximation to expenditure after appropriate corrections.

An experimental calibration can test recovery of a known heat input, for example with a resistor powered at a measured electrical rate. A 20 W heater supplies 72 kJ in one hour. If the instrument reports substantially less without a corresponding storage correction, the result suggests incomplete heat recovery or calibration error. Calibration with a heater cannot fully reproduce evaporation or movements of an animal, so additional checks may be needed. The strength of direct calorimetry is that the heat measurement does not require assigning substrate oxidation proportions. Its limitations include expense, thermal inertia, the need for complete heat accounting and difficulties when measuring rapid metabolic transitions.

5. Indirect calorimetry and respiratory gas balance

Indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from chemical consequences of metabolism rather than measuring heat itself. In aerobic animals oxygen is consumed during substrate oxidation and carbon dioxide is released. The rates of these exchanges can be measured with a mask, ventilated hood, whole-room chamber or animal respirometer. Oxygen consumption is conventionally written VO2 with a dot above V to indicate volume per unit time. Carbon dioxide production is similarly written VCO2. The two volumes must refer to comparable temperature, pressure and moisture conventions.

An open-flow respirometer admits air of known gas composition and measures the outgoing flow and gas fractions. Oxygen disappears from the gas stream and carbon dioxide is added. Oxygen uptake is the difference between the incoming oxygen volume per unit time and the outgoing oxygen volume per unit time. If the inlet and outlet dry gas flows are VI and VE, with corresponding oxygen fractions FIO2 and FEO2, oxygen uptake is VI times FIO2 minus VE times FEO2. Carbon dioxide output is VE times FECO2 minus VI times FICO2. Fraction means a dimensionless number, not the percentage written as a whole number: 20.95 percent is 0.2095.

Inlet and outlet flow rates need not be identical because oxygen is removed and carbon dioxide replaces it in amounts that may differ. In some open-circuit calculations the inlet flow is estimated using conservation of inert nitrogen, an approximation underlying the Haldane transformation. Nitrogen conservation is useful when the subject is not exchanging a significant amount of nitrogen gas and the measurement system is correctly characterized. It does not remove the need to measure gas fractions accurately, correct for water vapour or ensure that the gas flow represents the animal rather than a leak.

Suppose an illustrative measurement has a corrected inlet flow of 10.00 L/min with oxygen fraction 0.2095 and an outlet flow of 9.95 L/min with oxygen fraction 0.1850. Oxygen uptake is 2.095 minus 1.84075, or 0.25425 L/min. If inlet carbon dioxide fraction is 0.0004 and outlet fraction 0.0210, net carbon dioxide output is 0.20895 minus 0.004, or 0.20495 L/min. The resulting ratio is approximately 0.806. These numbers are constructed for teaching; they are not measurements from a published experiment. Their purpose is to show why both flow and fraction must enter a conservation calculation.

Gas measurements are sensitive to instrument delay. Air from a chamber does not instantly reach the analyser, and gas within the chamber takes time to mix. At steady state a constant metabolic flux produces stable gas differences; during a transition the outflow signal can lag behind the actual rate. A sudden decrease in chamber oxygen fraction can reflect increased uptake, delayed mixing or a change in flow. A researcher measuring transient activity needs a suitable response model or a different apparatus. Stable measurements at known flow are easier to interpret.

For an aquatic ectotherm, oxygen concentration can be measured in water instead of air. In intermittent-flow respirometry, a sealed measurement period allows oxygen to decline, followed by a flush period restoring the water. If a chamber contains an effective water volume of 4 L after accounting for displacement and oxygen concentration declines by 0.30 mg/L over ten minutes, the apparent uptake is 1.2 mg in ten minutes, or 0.12 mg/min. Microbial oxygen consumption and other background demand must be measured and subtracted. If background is 0.02 mg/min, net fish uptake is 0.10 mg/min. Temperature affects both oxygen solubility and metabolism; maintaining appropriate oxygen availability avoids confusing hypoxic depression with a low standard metabolic rate.

6. Respiratory quotient from balanced reactions

Respiratory quotient, RQ, is the molar ratio of carbon dioxide generated to oxygen consumed in cellular substrate oxidation. When gases are compared under the same temperature and pressure, the ratio of their volumes equals the molar ratio. The quotient has no units. Its significance is that different substrates contain different proportions of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen and therefore demand different amounts of external oxygen to achieve complete oxidation.

For glucose the balanced overall reaction is C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. Six moles of carbon dioxide appear for six moles of oxygen consumed, so the RQ is 1.0. This is a stoichiometric statement about complete oxidation, not a description of every intermediate reaction. Glycolysis alone does not consume molecular oxygen directly, and glucose can be converted into other products without complete oxidation. Only the specified overall aerobic transformation justifies this calculation.

For palmitic acid the balanced overall reaction is C16H32O2 + 23 O2 → 16 CO2 + 16 H2O. Here RQ is 16/23, approximately 0.696. The molecule is relatively reduced and contains less oxygen per carbon than glucose. More oxygen must be supplied from outside the molecule to form carbon dioxide and water. A typical fat oxidation RQ is therefore represented approximately by 0.70. The value is an approximation for mixtures of fatty acids; different individual fatty acids have slightly different stoichiometric ratios.

Proteins are more complicated because amino acids have different elemental compositions and their nitrogen is excreted in products such as urea in mammals. Not all of their chemical energy or atoms end up in carbon dioxide and water. A representative protein oxidation RQ near 0.8 is often used in basic physiology, but the exact value depends on amino acid composition and nitrogen handling. The urinary nitrogen correction in energy expenditure calculations reflects the importance of protein metabolism. It is inappropriate to assume a single exact RQ for every protein across all animals.

A useful general derivation considers a nitrogen-free substrate C_aH_bO_c that undergoes complete oxidation. Carbon balance requires a moles of CO2; hydrogen balance requires b/2 moles of H2O. The products contain 2a+b/2 oxygen atoms per substrate molecule, of which c are already present in the substrate. The remaining oxygen atoms require a+b/4−c/2 moles of O2. The resulting RQ is a divided by that oxygen requirement. For glucose, substituting a=6, b=12, c=6 gives 6/(6+3−3)=1. For palmitate, a=16, b=32, c=2 gives 16/(16+8−1)=16/23. The derivation assumes complete oxidation to only carbon dioxide and water and does not apply unchanged to nitrogen-containing or incompletely oxidized substrates.

For a mixture of glucose and fat, the observed cellular quotient is weighted by the oxygen used to oxidize each substrate, not by the number of grams eaten. If carbohydrate oxidation uses 0.12 L/min oxygen at RQ 1 and fat oxidation uses 0.18 L/min at RQ 0.70, total carbon dioxide is 0.12+0.126=0.246 L/min, total oxygen is 0.30 L/min and the mixed quotient is 0.82. It would be incorrect to take a simple arithmetic average of 1 and 0.70 unless the two oxygen contributions were equal. Likewise the fraction of oxygen consumed by each substrate is not numerically identical to the fraction of energy derived from each substrate.

7. Respiratory exchange ratio and non-steady-state limitations

Respiratory exchange ratio, RER, is measured at the respiratory boundary of an animal, commonly from expired gas. It is calculated as VCO2 divided by VO2. The formula resembles cellular RQ, but the quantities refer to different locations. RQ describes cellular production and consumption; RER describes net exchange across the respiratory system. Under stable aerobic conditions, body gas stores are approximately constant and RER can approximate the whole-body cellular RQ. This equivalence is an assumption that must be assessed, rather than a consequence of writing the same mathematical ratio.

Carbon dioxide is carried in blood and tissues in dissolved and chemically combined forms, notably bicarbonate. The body therefore has a reservoir of carbon dioxide equivalents. When ventilation increases suddenly, CO2 can be washed out of this reservoir faster than it is newly produced. A measured RER can exceed 1 even though oxidative substrate reactions alone would not produce that ratio. During intense activity, acid buffering by bicarbonate contributes additional CO2 release; changing ventilation and gas storage further complicate the measurement. The appearance of a high RER is not proof that cells are oxidizing a hypothetical fuel with a fixed RQ above 1.

The opposite situation can occur when CO2 accumulates in body stores or ventilation is transiently reduced. Measured VCO2 can fall relative to ongoing cellular production. The measured ratio can then be lower than expected for the actual fuel mix. Metabolic processes such as net lipogenesis, ketone production and altered substrate handling may also alter the relationship between gas exchange and simple complete-oxidation expectations. The common interval near 0.7 to 1.0 is useful for interpreting stable, predominantly oxidative metabolism; it is not an absolute physical bound on respiratory exchange data.

Imagine a subject whose stable VO2 is 0.25 L/min and VCO2 is 0.20 L/min. Baseline RER is 0.80. After a brief period of voluntary hyperventilation, a sample shows VO2 0.25 L/min and VCO2 0.30 L/min, so RER is 1.20. The oxygen data do not indicate increased aerobic oxygen demand, while the CO2 data are consistent with release from storage. A single sample cannot quantify the true cellular RQ because the storage term is unknown. Repeated measurements after breathing and gas exchange stabilize would provide better evidence. The example is illustrative, and actual ventilation changes can also affect oxygen exchange temporarily.

To reason experimentally, write a carbon dioxide balance: rate of change of body CO2 stores equals cellular CO2 production minus net respiratory CO2 elimination, with additional routes accounted for when significant. If the stores are falling, elimination exceeds production. In a steady state the storage term approaches zero and the two rates approach equality. Oxygen also has storage and transport dynamics, although the specific behaviour differs. A ratio of two fluctuating measured variables is not necessarily a robust estimator of metabolic substrate use; inspect their individual traces before interpreting the ratio.

This distinction has another consequence for dietary interpretation. A RER near 0.70 during a stable fast suggests a relatively large contribution from fat oxidation, not necessarily a large loss of body fat over an entire day. Body fat change depends on both storage and mobilization over time. A subject can oxidize fat during a measurement and store fat during other periods. Fuel selection and net energy balance are connected but not identical observations.

8. Converting oxygen and carbon dioxide into energy expenditure

A litre of oxygen consumed does not release precisely the same amount of energy for every substrate. Complete glucose oxidation produces approximately 5.05 kcal per litre of oxygen under standard gas conventions, whereas oxidation of representative fats yields about 4.7 kcal/L. Differences arise because the ratio between chemical energy liberated and oxygen required depends on substrate composition and combustion enthalpy. A rough value of 5 kcal/L is convenient for rapid estimates when the fuel mix is unknown, but a precise answer should use appropriate gas data and assumptions.

The abbreviated Weir equation estimates expenditure in kilocalories per minute as 3.941 times VO2 plus 1.106 times VCO2, when both gas rates are in litres per minute. The coefficients have units of kcal/L and express an empirical thermochemical relationship. If gas rates are entered in millilitres per minute, first divide them by one thousand. The abbreviated equation omits an explicit urinary nitrogen correction. This may be acceptable for many short measurements or when protein metabolism is estimated implicitly, but it is not exact under all conditions.

The full form includes a correction of approximately −2.17 times urinary nitrogen excretion in grams per minute, provided compatible gas and nitrogen rates are used. For a 24-hour calculation the gases must be expressed as litres per day and nitrogen as grams per day, or all rates must first be converted to the same time base. Omitting the nitrogen term should be described as an approximation. Nitrogen excretion does not instantaneously mirror amino acid oxidation, so interpretation of a short urinary collection needs care.

Since VCO2 = RER times VO2 by definition, the abbreviated equation can also be written as VO2 times (3.941+1.106 RER). The bracket has the units kcal per litre of oxygen and acts as a gas-derived caloric equivalent. At RER 0.70 the bracket is approximately 4.715; at RER 1.00 it is approximately 5.047. These values agree with the usual teaching approximations for fat and carbohydrate oxidation. The relation is particularly useful for seeing why a fixed oxygen equivalent introduces some error when substrate oxidation changes.

Consider stable gas exchange at VO2 = 0.250 L/min and VCO2 = 0.200 L/min. The quotient is 0.80. The two energy contributions are 3.941 × 0.250 = 0.98525 kcal/min and 1.106 × 0.200 = 0.22120 kcal/min. Their sum is 1.20645 kcal/min. Multiplying by 1440 minutes gives about 1737.3 kcal/day, or about 7270 kJ/day. Multiplying by 4184 J/kcal and dividing by 60 s/min gives about 84.1 W. The daily equivalent describes the extrapolation of the measured rate for a full day, not a measurement of the individual's actual activity and meal costs during that day.

Compare two hypothetical subjects with identical oxygen consumption of 0.25 L/min but RER values of 0.70 and 1.00. The first has estimated expenditure 0.25 × 4.7152 = 1.1788 kcal/min, while the second has 0.25 × 5.047 = 1.26175 kcal/min. The difference is about 0.083 kcal/min. Equal oxygen consumption therefore does not imply exactly equal energy expenditure. Conversely, identical calculated expenditure can be achieved with different gas rates and fuel mixtures. For an examination problem, identify whether the question asks about oxygen flux, energy flux or substrate preference; each requires a different inference.

The method is limited when rapid anaerobic energy production, changing body gas stores, leakage or other disturbances invalidate the link between measured gas exchange and net oxidative energy release. It may also be inaccurate if the organism's chemistry departs substantially from the assumptions behind the coefficients. A precise numerical display should not be mistaken for proof of accuracy. The measurement conditions, calibration, observation length and biological assumptions determine the reliability of the calculated result.

9. Components of total daily energy expenditure

Total daily energy expenditure, TDEE, integrates energy use over a day in the conditions actually experienced. A useful conceptual partition separates basal or resting maintenance, the thermic effect of food, and expenditure associated with activity. Regulatory changes in thermogenesis can alter one or more components. The partition is a bookkeeping convention, so a physiological response should not be added as a separate component if it has already been measured within another component.

The thermic effect of food describes the increment in expenditure associated with processing nutrients after feeding. It includes the work of digestion, secretion, transport, absorption and the metabolic transformations needed to use or store substrates. Protein-rich meals often produce a greater thermic response relative to their energy content than fat-rich meals, partly because amino acid metabolism and nitrogen disposal require energy. The precise response depends on meal composition, size, prior physiological state and observation period. The thermic effect cannot be assigned an exact fixed percentage for every person or species.

A meal experiment illustrates the need for a baseline. Suppose expenditure during standardized rest before feeding is 1.10 kcal/min. During the first two hours after a meal it averages 1.30 kcal/min, and during the following two hours it averages 1.18 kcal/min. The incremental expenditure is (1.30−1.10) × 120 + (1.18−1.10) × 120 = 24+9.6=33.6 kcal. This calculation assumes that the baseline would have remained stable over the observation and that activity and temperature were matched. If the meal increases spontaneous movement, the additional expenditure may include activity as well as the thermic response. A time-matched fasting control helps separate these effects.

Activity expenditure includes voluntary exercise and numerous nonexercise movements such as standing, walking during ordinary tasks and maintaining posture. Activity can vary substantially among individuals with similar BMR. A long period of small increases in metabolic rate may contribute more daily energy than a very short vigorous bout. When the energy cost of activity is expressed as expenditure above rest, the resting portion should not be added twice. If an activity lasts 60 minutes and total expenditure during it is 4 kcal/min, but baseline is 1 kcal/min, the incremental activity cost is 180 kcal rather than 240 kcal. The latter includes the energy that would have been expended during the hour even without activity.

Adaptive thermogenesis refers to changes in expenditure beyond those expected from some specified comparison, often after changes in energy availability, body composition or thermal environment. The reference model is essential: after body mass decreases, expenditure normally decreases in part because there is less tissue and moving a smaller body is less costly. A remaining reduction relative to a model that accounts for these changes may be described as an adaptive component. It is not synonymous with all changes in BMR and is not an independent additive term in every accounting scheme.

As an illustrative daily budget, suppose maintenance expenditure is estimated at 1500 kcal/day, net activity expenditure at 500 kcal/day and incremental food-associated expenditure at 180 kcal/day. Under that partition the total is 2180 kcal/day. The physical activity level or PAL, when defined as total daily expenditure divided by BMR, is approximately 1.45. PAL has no units and its value depends on the measurement basis; using RMR in place of BMR changes the denominator. TDEE also need not equal energy intake on an individual day. The difference can be supplied from or stored in body tissues.

Free-living daily expenditure can be estimated with doubly labelled water, using water enriched with stable isotopes of hydrogen and oxygen. Both labels leave the body through water turnover, while the oxygen label also leaves through carbon dioxide. The difference in disappearance rates allows estimation of carbon dioxide production using an appropriate model and corrections; conversion to expenditure requires additional assumptions about respiratory quotient or diet. This method integrates expenditure over days under usual living conditions, but it cannot by itself tell when a particular activity occurred or provide a standardized BMR. A room calorimeter provides much more temporally resolved gas data but may alter normal behaviour because the subject is confined. Different methods answer different biological questions.

10. Physiological determinants and experimental separation

Body size and composition are prominent determinants of basal expenditure, but they are not the only ones. Age influences the proportions of organs and skeletal muscle, growth-related biosynthesis and hormonal state. Growing animals can retain energy in new tissue while also spending energy on synthesis. During aging the amount and composition of active tissue may change, and differences among individuals cannot be inferred from age alone. Comparing an adult and a growing juvenile simply on total mass neglects these changes.

Thyroid hormones are important regulators of metabolism. They affect gene expression and cellular processes influencing substrate utilization, mitochondrial function and ATP demand. Increased thyroid hormone action can raise resting energy expenditure, while reduced action can lower it. The exact whole-body response depends on tissue sensitivity, hormone concentrations and other physiological variables. This chapter uses thyroid status to explain metabolic rate rather than treating the detailed endocrine axis, which belongs in the endocrinology subunit.

Temperature has different immediate effects in endotherms and ectotherms. In an endotherm below its lower critical temperature, greater heat production may be required to balance heat loss. Shivering increases muscle ATP turnover. Nonshivering thermogenesis in mammalian brown adipose tissue involves uncoupling protein 1, which permits proton return across the inner mitochondrial membrane without the corresponding ATP synthesis through ATP synthase. Substrate oxidation and heat production may increase, while ATP produced per oxygen consumed decreases. This does not mean that mitochondrial energy disappears; it is transferred into heat rather than conserved in the usual ATP yield.

For an ectotherm, warming within an appropriate physiological range commonly increases biochemical reaction rates and thus standard metabolism. The temperature coefficient Q10 can express the ratio between rates separated by ten degrees Celsius. For rates measured at temperatures T1 and T2, Q10 = (R2/R1) raised to the power 10/(T2−T1). If oxygen consumption is 0.10 mg/min at 15 °C and 0.18 mg/min at 25 °C, Q10 is 1.8 over that interval. A Q10 of 1.8 does not justify extrapolating indefinitely to colder or hotter temperatures; thermal performance can become nonlinear and extreme temperatures can impair physiological function. Acclimation can change the relationship itself.

Pregnancy, lactation, fever, stress and other physiological states can alter energy use through different mechanisms. A lactating mammal exports chemical energy in milk in addition to energy it dissipates as heat or work, so energy requirements may exceed its measured expenditure by the energy exported. During fever, changes in regulated body temperature and immune activity can alter heat production. Acute stress may increase autonomic activity or movement. These influences are important reasons for documenting physiological status when interpreting basal measurements.

A controlled comparison might examine the effect of thyroid status on metabolic rate in animals of similar age and body composition at thermoneutrality. The investigator should control feeding state, recent activity, time of day, stress associated with handling and gas measurement method. Measuring hormone concentrations provides evidence of the endocrine difference, but a simple observational correlation cannot by itself prove that hormone action caused all of the difference. A suitable intervention and controls can strengthen causal inference, while consideration of changes in body composition prevents confusing direct regulatory effects with a change in the amount of tissue. In all such experiments, measurement of oxygen uptake is evidence about oxidative metabolism, not direct proof of a particular molecular pathway.

11. Allometric scaling and mass-specific metabolism

Metabolic rate tends to increase with body size because a larger animal usually contains more metabolically active tissue. However, the increase is not always proportional to mass. A widely used empirical model writes whole-animal metabolic rate R = a M^b, where M is body mass, b is a dimensionless scaling exponent and a is a normalization coefficient whose units depend on the chosen units of R and M. If R is measured in watts and M in kilograms, a has units W kg raised to the power −b. The equation must be fitted or otherwise specified for a particular group and metabolic condition.

An exponent of one describes isometry: doubling mass doubles whole-animal rate. When b lies between zero and one, total rate increases less rapidly than mass. Dividing the model by M gives mass-specific rate R/M = a M^(b−1). If b = 0.75, mass-specific rate is proportional to M^(−0.25), so larger animals have lower expenditure per unit mass under the stated model. An exponent above one would imply the opposite mass-specific trend. Total and mass-specific rates therefore answer different questions and must not be interchanged.

For two hypothetical mammals that follow the same model with b = 0.75, if the second is sixteen times heavier, its total metabolic rate is 16^0.75 = 8 times the first. Its mass-specific rate is 8/16 = 0.5 times the first. If the smaller animal consumes 2 W at 0.1 kg, the model predicts 16 W for the larger 1.6 kg animal, with mass-specific rates of 20 and 10 W/kg respectively. These are constructed values, not a universal comparison between particular species. The conclusion follows from the assumed exponent and common coefficient.

A geometric argument helps explain why sublinear scaling was historically plausible. For geometrically similar bodies, surface area is proportional to characteristic length squared and volume to length cubed. At constant density, mass is proportional to volume, so surface area is proportional to M^(2/3). If heat loss per unit surface were fixed and heat production matched it, a two-thirds exponent would follow. But this depends on geometric similarity, insulation, environmental conditions and the assumed relationship between heat flux and area. Animals differ in body shape and insulation, and metabolic scaling also occurs in ectotherms that do not maintain body temperature through the same mechanism. Surface area reasoning is therefore not a proof of a universal metabolic exponent.

Kleiber's classical work helped establish the approximate three-quarter-power relationship as an influential model for mammalian metabolic comparisons. Later analyses have shown that estimates depend on taxon, metabolic state, body-size range and statistical treatment. Several physiological explanations have been proposed, involving transport networks, tissue composition, surface relations and other mechanisms. No single fixed exponent should be asserted for all living organisms. For NET questions, use the exponent given in the problem and distinguish an empirical fitted model from a universal law.

Taking logarithms yields log R = log a + b log M. A straight line on a log-log plot has slope b. For two exact observations governed by the model, b = log(R2/R1)/log(M2/M1), with the same logarithm base in numerator and denominator. If mass increases fourfold while rate increases twofold, the exponent for those observations is log 2/log 4 = 0.5. Two points determine a line but cannot establish whether the relationship is reliable across a population. A real comparative study needs multiple observations, uncertainty estimates and consideration of common ancestry, temperature and other covariates.

Dividing by body mass is not a universal solution for controlling size differences. If the exponent is not one, R/M remains systematically related to M, so a comparison of mass-specific rates can create a predictable difference even if two groups obey exactly the same physiological relationship. An analysis might instead compare rates at a common body mass using an appropriate fitted model. Such interpretation is more meaningful than assuming that every kilogram of tissue has an identical cost.

12. Integrating measurement and evidence

A successful metabolic experiment begins with a question rather than an instrument. If the question concerns basal maintenance, restrict meal-related, activity-related and thermal influences. If it concerns daily expenditure, an observation in a quiet laboratory may be inadequate. If it concerns substrate oxidation, obtain gas exchange measurements in a suitable steady state and account for protein metabolism where important. If it concerns thermal regulation, measure the environmental temperature and the animal's state rather than relying on an isolated rate.

Consider an illustrative comparison of three gas measurements in one animal: trial A at quiet thermoneutral rest after fasting yields VO2 0.25 L/min and VCO2 0.20 L/min; trial B shortly after a meal yields 0.28 and 0.25; trial C during a brief breathing perturbation yields 0.25 and 0.30. The corresponding RER values are 0.80, about 0.89 and 1.20. Trial B is consistent with higher oxidative expenditure and a change in fuel use under steady-state conditions, but it does not prove that the meal changed cellular substrate oxidation by a particular amount. Trial C cannot be interpreted by applying ordinary steady-state fuel assumptions. It may reflect CO2 storage changes. Only trial A approximates basal conditions as described, and even A requires confirmation of the remaining protocol requirements.

Applying the abbreviated Weir equation to trial A gives 1.20645 kcal/min. Trial B gives 3.941 × 0.28 + 1.106 × 0.25 = 1.37998 kcal/min. The calculated difference is about 0.174 kcal/min. If the higher rate persisted for two hours, the additional energy would be approximately 20.8 kcal, assuming the baseline remained stable. Applying the equation mechanically to trial C gives 1.31705 kcal/min, but the physiological interpretation is unreliable during the stated transient because gas stores may be changing. A calculation can be arithmetically valid yet biologically misleading.

Instrument errors can sometimes be diagnosed by their patterns. A drifting oxygen analyser can create an apparent change in VO2 while the animal remains stable. An air leak can alter measured gas fractions or flow and may produce implausible quotients. A chamber with slow mixing may smooth or delay an actual change. A calibration gas, known flow verification, background measurement and time-aligned signals address different errors. No single calibration eliminates every possible source of bias. Replicate observations and documentation of the animal's behaviour help distinguish an instrument problem from physiological variability.

An experiment comparing two ambient temperatures should allow appropriate stabilization, randomize or otherwise control order effects, maintain comparable feeding state and assess activity. If the first trial always occurs after handling and the second after several hours of rest, a decline in rate could arise from reduced stress rather than temperature. Repeated measures within the same animal reduce some between-individual variation but create possible carryover effects. Comparing separate groups avoids certain carryover problems while introducing differences in individual composition and behaviour. These are standard experimental trade-offs, and an examination question may require choosing the design that most directly isolates the proposed cause.

The broader relationship between digestion and energy expenditure is now apparent. Digestion and absorption supply metabolizable substrates. Cellular oxidation transforms them to support maintenance and activity; storage buffers the mismatch between intake and use. Calorimetric methods measure important consequences of these transformations, but each method observes a particular boundary and operates under particular assumptions. Hormonal control of the rates and distribution of energy use will be developed in the next subunit, while later discussions integrate host-associated microbes and organ-to-organ energy regulation. The central skill is to match the measurement to the physiological question and limit the conclusion to what the data actually demonstrate.

Common misconceptions

BMR means zero activity in every tissue

Internal maintenance and organ work continue.

BMR is measured during sleep

The conventional basal state is awake and relaxed.

RMR and BMR are always identical

Protocol stringency differs.

Thermoneutral means one universal temperature

The range varies with species and insulation.

SMR can be compared without temperature

Ectotherm rates depend on thermal conditions.

Oxygen fraction alone measures uptake

Both gas fraction and flow are required.

All oxygen consumption yields the same ATP

Substrates and mitochondrial coupling vary.

RQ and RER are always identical

Changing gas stores break the steady-state equivalence.

RER above one always indicates measurement error

It can arise physiologically during transient gas exchange or altered metabolism.

RQ 0.7 proves loss of body fat over a day

Oxidation during one interval is not net daily storage.

Direct heat loss equals expenditure in every state

Work, evaporation and storage need accounting.

A calculated daily basal equivalent is actual TDEE

Extrapolation of a resting rate omits daily activity and meals.

Fat-free tissue has one uniform specific metabolic rate

Organ composition matters.

The three-quarter exponent is universal

Scaling exponents vary by taxon and state.

Mass normalization removes every size effect

R/M still scales with mass when b differs from one.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

Which measurement most closely satisfies the operational definition of human BMR?

Answer and reasoning

Awake, supine, postabsorptive and thermoneutral. A meal, recent exercise, sleep or cold exposure changes the physiological state relative to a standard basal protocol.

2. Part B

What is the unit of RER?

Answer and reasoning

Dimensionless. Both rates are gas volumes per unit time measured in comparable conditions, so units cancel.

3. Part B

Why is palmitate RQ less than glucose RQ?

Answer and reasoning

It requires more external oxygen per carbon oxidized. Balanced palmitate oxidation gives 16 CO₂ per 23 O₂, while glucose gives six of each.

4. Part B

Which instrument measures heat transfer rather than estimating expenditure from respiratory gases?

Answer and reasoning

Direct calorimeter. Indirect calorimetry instead relies on oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output.

5. Part B

What physiological condition must accompany an ectotherm SMR measurement?

Answer and reasoning

Specified temperature. Temperature influences reaction rates and standard metabolic demand; oxygen availability and activity must also be controlled.

6. Part B

Why does a stable body mass not imply zero ATP use for protein turnover?

Answer and reasoning

Synthesis and degradation can balance at nonzero rates. A net change of zero does not imply that underlying fluxes are zero.

7. Part B

What is the principal mechanistic role of UCP1 in brown adipose tissue?

Answer and reasoning

Facilitates proton return without the corresponding ATP synthesis. This decreases coupling of substrate oxidation to ATP synthesis and increases heat dissipation.

8. Part B

Why is fat-free mass not an exact predictor of BMR?

Answer and reasoning

Different lean tissues have different specific metabolic rates. Fat-free mass includes tissues with markedly different metabolic costs, as well as body water.

9. Part B

What distinguishes RQ from RER?

Answer and reasoning

RQ refers to cellular substrate oxidation; RER to respiratory gas exchange. Body gas stores can change, so the measurements coincide approximately only in suitable steady states.

10. Part B

What does a scaling exponent below one imply for mass-specific rate?

Answer and reasoning

It declines with increasing mass for a common coefficient and positive mass. Dividing R=aM^b by M gives aM^(b−1).

11. Part C

A fasting subject uses 0.30 L O₂/min and releases 0.24 L CO₂/min. Calculate RER and abbreviated Weir expenditure.

Answer and reasoning

0.80 and 1.44774 kcal/min. 0.24/0.30=0.8; 3.941×0.30+1.106×0.24=1.44774. This is an instantaneous rate under suitable conditions.

12. Part C

Two groups have the same oxygen uptake of 0.25 L/min, but RER is 0.7 in A and 1.0 in B. Which has higher estimated oxidative energy expenditure?

Answer and reasoning

B. The caloric equivalents from the abbreviated equation are approximately 4.72 and 5.05 kcal/L respectively, making B higher for equal oxygen consumption.

13. Part C

During hyperventilation, VO₂ remains 0.25 L/min but VCO₂ increases from 0.20 to 0.30. Can the new ratio establish pure carbohydrate oxidation?

Answer and reasoning

No. RER is 1.20; washout of CO₂ stores can elevate the ratio independently of cellular oxidative substrate selection.

14. Part C

A 4 L aquatic respirometer declines by 0.25 mg O₂/L in five minutes. Blank oxygen demand is 0.03 mg/min. Calculate animal uptake.

Answer and reasoning

0.17 mg/min. Apparent uptake is 4×0.25/5=0.20 mg/min; subtract blank demand.

15. Part C

An animal's body mass increases by a factor of 81. Under b=0.75, determine total and mass-specific expenditure ratios.

Answer and reasoning

27 and 1/3. 81^(3/4)=27; the mass-specific ratio is 27/81=1/3.

16. Part C

A basal rate is 1 kcal/min. A 90-minute activity costs 3 kcal/min including maintenance. What is the incremental cost?

Answer and reasoning

180 kcal. Subtract the maintenance rate before multiplying: (3−1)×90=180. Total expenditure during the activity is 270 kcal.

17. Part C

A direct calorimeter captures 60 W of heat, while the subject delivers 20 W external mechanical work outside the chamber and body heat stores are stable. Ignoring other outputs, what is expenditure?

Answer and reasoning

80 W. Heat transfer plus exported mechanical work is 80 W. If work dissipated within the measured chamber, separate addition would double-count.

18. Part C

An ectotherm has rates 0.08 and 0.16 mg/min at 20 and 30 °C. What is Q10 and what is its limitation?

Answer and reasoning

2 over this interval. The temperature interval is 10 °C and rate doubles. The coefficient need not stay constant at other temperatures or acclimation states.

19. Part C

A meal increases the rate by 0.15 kcal/min for 90 min, then 0.05 for 60 min. Find incremental expenditure.

Answer and reasoning

16.5 kcal. 0.15×90+0.05×60=13.5+3=16.5, assuming a stable matched baseline.

20. Part C

Two animals have masses in a ratio of 8 and total rates in a ratio of 4. Find the implied two-point exponent.

Answer and reasoning

2/3. b=log 4/log 8=2/3. Two observations cannot establish a universal scaling law.

Revision and active recall

Distinguish operational measurement conditions, the location of gas exchange, and the time interval of energy accounting. Before interpreting a numerical result, establish whether gas exchange is stable and whether the relevant rates share a consistent unit system.
