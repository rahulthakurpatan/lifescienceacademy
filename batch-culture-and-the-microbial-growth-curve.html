1. Why microbial populations cannot grow indefinitely

A bacterial cell placed in a suitable environment can acquire nutrients, enlarge, duplicate its hereditary material and divide. If the two daughters behave similarly, the process repeats and a population develops. The arithmetic of this process suggests an extraordinary possibility: repeated doubling produces very large populations in surprisingly few generations. Yet a suspension of bacteria in a vessel supplied with a finite amount of food does not ordinarily become denser at an unchanged rate forever. After some time the increase slows, eventually the population may stop increasing, and the number of cells capable of reproduction may decline. The discrepancy between the potential for multiplication and the observed behavior of a culture is the central problem of microbial growth physiology.

A culture is a population maintained under defined or partly defined environmental conditions. The word culture can refer to the organisms and the surrounding medium together. The medium supplies water and substances that cells use to build their constituents, obtain energy or maintain internal conditions. An inoculum is the material introduced at the beginning of a culture: it may consist of a suspension containing many cells, rather than a single individual. The amount of inoculum affects the initial population, whereas its physiological condition influences how those cells behave after transfer. Two samples containing the same number of bacteria can therefore produce quite different early growth curves.

The defining feature of a batch culture is that the initial medium is provided as a finite batch, rather than continually replenished while spent medium is continually withdrawn. A simple teaching model is a well-mixed vessel containing a finite quantity of nutrients and a microbial population. The term closed in this context refers primarily to the absence of routine replacement of the liquid medium, not necessarily to the absence of all exchange of gases or heat. A vessel may exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide with its surroundings; temperature may be maintained externally. Even when some gas exchange occurs, the carbon source, nitrogen source or another essential nutrient in the original medium may be consumed. Conversely, a strictly sealed vessel may also run short of an initially available gaseous resource. It is important to specify the boundary of the model before predicting its behavior.

A batch culture should not be imagined as a permanent environment of fixed chemical composition. Its contents change because cells remove substances and release others. The cells themselves alter their surroundings. A nutrient molecule may become incorporated into biomass, oxidized to release energy, or converted to a secreted end product. If the medium has weak buffering capacity, some metabolic products can change its pH. Dissolved oxygen can become scarce when consumption outruns supply. The ratio of available resources to the number of consumers changes dramatically as the population increases. As a result, an environment that originally supported rapid reproduction may later support little new growth, even though the same vessel remains at the same temperature.

There are at least three different meanings of growth that matter in this discussion. A single cell grows when it increases its biomass or dimensions. A population grows numerically when the number of cells increases. A culture grows in biomass when the combined mass of its organisms increases. These usually correlate during sustained balanced reproduction, but they need not do so during transitions. A newly transferred bacterium may synthesize new proteins and expand before it divides. A damaged population may retain considerable biomass even while losing the capacity to reproduce. A curve of cell concentration, therefore, is not automatically a curve of total biological activity.

The earliest prerequisite is an understanding of cell membranes and energy conversion. Nutrients must reach transport proteins or otherwise enter cells; their conversion into useful precursors and ATP must be coordinated with the synthesis of proteins, nucleic acids and envelope materials. A cell can perform some of these functions without completing a new division. The next prerequisite is the difference between counting structures and detecting reproductive competence. A direct cell count may include intact dead cells, while a colony count measures units capable of forming colonies under the conditions used. An optical measurement responds to the physical properties of suspended material. These are different windows on one culture, not mutually interchangeable definitions of life.

The study of a batch curve connects all these concepts. A student who understands it should be able to infer which constraints are likely to be operating from the shape of a curve, recognize when a particular measurement cannot resolve the mechanism, and decide what comparison would distinguish two explanations. Merely memorizing the order of four named phases is not sufficient. The phases describe broad population behavior; the underlying causes concern energy, nutrient use, cell structure, regulation and survival. The goal is to reconstruct that causal connection whenever a graph or a set of measurements is presented.

The simplest reference population will be a unicellular bacterium capable of binary fission in a homogeneous liquid environment. This model is useful because population increase can be related directly to division. It is not a claim that every microorganism is a bacterium or reproduces by binary fission. Budding yeast, filamentous fungi and other organisms may require different operational measures of growth. Even among bacteria the shape of a batch curve depends on the organism, the inoculum, the medium and the environmental conditions. The model should be used as a framework for thinking rather than as an invariant schedule that all cells obey.

2. From a culture to a meaningful growth curve

A growth curve is a plot of a specified population measurement against elapsed time. Its horizontal axis is normally time, often measured in hours. Its vertical axis might represent viable colony-forming units per milliliter, total cells per milliliter, dry biomass per liter, or optical density. The choice must be stated before phases are assigned. It is especially useful to plot the logarithm of a cell concentration because populations may change across several orders of magnitude. An increase from one million to two million cells and an increase from one hundred million to two hundred million are equal fold increases, despite their very different arithmetic increments.

The classical four-phase picture contains an initial interval of little detectable increase, an interval of rapid approximately exponential multiplication, a plateau of little net increase, and a period of declining viable counts. These are called lag, exponential or log, stationary, and decline or death phases. Real transitions may be gradual, and some authors distinguish acceleration and deceleration intervals separately. The phases are recognized by the behavior of the measured population, not by a universal number of hours from inoculation. A sampling interval that is too large can miss an entire short transition. For example, two points separated by several hours might conceal a lag and a subsequent rise even if their average slope seems modest.

Jacques Monod's 1949 review of bacterial cultures helped formalize the distinction between cell concentration, bacterial mass and the quantitative behavior of growing populations. The importance of this approach is not that the four-phase graph originated in that one publication; earlier microbiologists had already described changes during culture development. Its importance is that growth curves became a way to study the properties of cells rather than simply record whether a tube appeared turbid. One can infer properties of a population by asking how quickly its concentration changes and whether biomass changes in proportion to the number of individuals. The review also reminds us that a mathematical description of positive growth does not, on its own, explain the mechanisms that cause loss of viability.

Imagine observing equal volumes from a culture at several times. At an early time the suspension appears nearly clear. Several hours later it looks cloudy, and a cell count confirms multiplication. Much later, the suspension may remain cloudy even though substantially fewer colony-forming units are recovered. The discrepancy is not necessarily a contradiction between instruments. Cells and fragments scatter light whether or not they can reproduce; a colony requires repeated division on the recovery medium. During the later culture phases, the physical persistence of cells may become uncoupled from their reproductive potential.

The distinction between a population level and a population rate is fundamental. A population can be large and grow very slowly. It can be small and grow rapidly relative to its own size. The height of the graph represents the former measurement, while the slope concerns the latter, with the interpretation depending on the axis scale. A high plateau does not represent a high growth rate just because it lies high on the page. On a graph of viable concentration, a horizontal region means that gains and losses of detectable viable units approximately cancel or are both negligible over the interval studied.

A logarithm converts multiplicative changes into additive changes. For positive counts expressed as cells per milliliter, a base-ten logarithmic plot gives equal vertical distances for equal fold changes. Counts of ten thousand, one hundred thousand and one million lie at successive equal intervals on this scale. A curve cannot plot a true zero on a logarithmic axis because the logarithm of zero is undefined. An observation below the detection limit is not evidence for a precisely zero population. It is best represented as a censored observation or limit rather than by taking the logarithm of zero or silently substituting one.

The shape of the graph depends on whether one uses a linear or a logarithmic vertical scale. Repeated proportional multiplication produces an increasingly steep curve on a linear axis, but a straight rising segment on a semilogarithmic axis. A plateau appears horizontal on both scales when the measured value is constant. A constant proportional decline gives a straight falling segment on a semilog plot. A downward line is not proof that every bacterium has the same risk of death, since a heterogeneous population may show an approximately linear interval by coincidence. The slope is an observed population pattern; a mechanism must be inferred using additional information.

Worked example 1. A simple example illustrates how to read a graph without using the full kinetic machinery developed in the next chapter. Suppose a culture has two million viable units per milliliter at an early point and eight million two hours later. The increase is fourfold. In a different period a culture with eight hundred million units increases to nine hundred million in two hours. Its absolute increase is much greater, but its fold increase is only 1.125. The former interval has the greater proportional change. If the purpose is to compare the physiological speed of reproduction, proportional change is usually more informative than raw additions of cells.

A graph constructed from optical density should not be labelled viable count unless an appropriate conversion has actually been established for the conditions. Optical density is normally dimensionless and is not itself a concentration in cells per milliliter. A direct count is expressed in counted cells per volume, while a colony count is expressed in CFU per volume. An aggregate that forms one colony may contain several cells. The resulting distinction is often particularly important in late cultures, where aggregation, shrinkage or damage can change the relation between measurements.

Comparisons require controls. If the purpose is to compare two environmental conditions, it is necessary to consider whether the inocula, sampling times, measured quantity and other conditions are comparable. A medium-only control can show whether an optical signal arises from the medium rather than the cells. Repeated measurements from independent cultures help distinguish consistent features from sampling variation. A culture that differs in its starting viable concentration may reach the detection threshold earlier even when its intrinsic physiology is identical. Therefore a shorter time to detectable cloudiness does not establish a faster intrinsic rate unless starting abundance and the lag interval have been accounted for.

All numerical datasets in the teaching examples that follow are hypothetical unless an actual study is named. They are intended to separate the reasoning from the peculiarities of a particular organism and should not be treated as empirical growth schedules. The classical model provides an expected sequence, but the scientifically useful question is what each observation does and does not establish.

3. Lag phase: adaptation before sustained multiplication

The apparent paradox of lag is that favorable medium does not always produce immediate numerical multiplication. When a population is moved to fresh surroundings, it carries the biochemical history of its previous environment. Some enzymes may be present in abundance, others scarce. The cells may have accumulated damage, altered their membranes, reduced protein synthesis or exhausted internal stores. Fresh medium offers resources, but the cells must possess the machinery and physiological state needed to use them before sustained cell division can occur.

The first step is the interaction between the cells and the new chemical environment. Solutes encounter the cell surface and membrane transport systems. Some molecules enter readily using existing transporters, whereas others require proteins that were not expressed at high abundance in the previous environment. Uptake supplies intracellular substrates that can enter metabolism. Cells must coordinate energy production and the regeneration of molecules that carry electrons or activated chemical groups. If energy production and transport are initially limited, the synthesis of new macromolecules may also be limited. The appearance of extracellular nutrients is therefore not identical to the immediate appearance of a high rate of biosynthesis.

Consider a bacterium previously growing on a carbon source that enters one metabolic route. After transfer, a different carbon source may be supplied. Existing enzymes that process the old substrate do not necessarily catalyze the reactions required for the new substrate. Regulatory proteins respond to available metabolites and change the expression of relevant genes. Messenger RNA is transcribed, translated and the resulting proteins become available to catalyze new reactions or transport substrates. As the new substrate becomes accessible to metabolism, ATP and precursor production can increase. The cell can then invest more effectively in growth. This sequence explains why a change of medium can introduce a lag even when the new medium is capable of supporting rapid eventual growth.

Cells transferred from an exhausted culture face additional demands. Proteins damaged by previous environmental stress may require repair or replacement. Membrane transport and ion gradients must remain functional or be restored. Cellular structures may need to be reorganized after prolonged nongrowth. Ribosomes are the structures that synthesize proteins from messenger RNA; their functional abundance and activity can limit the rate at which a cell produces growth-related proteins. A cell with insufficient active biosynthetic machinery can require a period of rebuilding before it divides at the rate characteristic of a fresh, favorable environment.

The completion of adaptation is not necessarily simultaneous with the first metabolic reaction, the first molecule of new DNA or the first appearance of new cell material. Different cell functions can resume at different times. A bacterium may increase its volume before completing chromosome duplication and septation. Septation is the process of constructing the division partition between future daughter cells. An increase in cell material therefore can be detected before an increase in the number of separately counted cells. If the vertical axis is dry biomass, an early rise may occur while a graph of cell number still appears nearly horizontal.

Lag should not be described as a compulsory period of metabolic inactivity. The appropriate claim is that sustained net numerical increase is not yet evident. Metabolism and gene expression may be active and may change rapidly. Conversely, the observation of a flat viable count alone does not prove that every cell in the sample is busy adapting. Some cells may remain inactive, some may divide, and others may die or fail to recover. The population response summarizes the combined outcomes of these individual cells.

Evidence for this interpretation comes from direct examination of early culture physiology. Rolfe and colleagues investigated the transition of stationary-phase bacteria into fresh medium and measured changes in gene expression and other physiological features during lag. Their 2012 study established that the early interval contains organized physiological events rather than simply an empty waiting period. The use of measurements before the onset of rapid multiplication allows gene expression changes to be placed temporally relative to population growth. The result does not establish that every organism activates the same genes or exhibits the same sequence; it supports the broader conclusion that numerical lag can involve extensive preparation.

A comparison that illustrates this distinction would follow two quantities in the same culture: viable count and an indicator of new protein synthesis or biomass. If biomass increases while viable count is initially stable, a reasonable inference is that some cells accumulate material before producing additional separately counted viable units. A control without viable inoculum would help establish the background signal. If the culture contains aggregates, changes in aggregation must also be considered. These measurements together provide more information than either alone, but they do not directly establish the exact order of all molecular events inside individual cells.

The duration of lag depends on the starting population. Actively growing cells transferred into the same kind of favorable medium may resume proliferation with a very short or experimentally undetectable lag, because their transport, metabolic and biosynthetic systems are already functioning in an appropriate state. Cells from a prolonged stationary phase may need more time before they recover. Transfer between very different media may require adjustment even if the old culture was growing rapidly. An inoculum injured by a previous stress may display delayed recovery or incomplete recovery. The new environment can thus influence both the speed at which growth resumes and the fraction of cells that successfully resume it.

Lag is not simply the generation time. Generation time describes how long a population takes to double during a suitable interval of exponential growth. A cell transferred from an unfavorable condition can have a long time to first division and subsequently produce descendants with short division intervals. Another population may begin dividing promptly but grow slowly afterward because its new medium supports only a modest rate of biosynthesis. Separating these properties is necessary when interpreting two growth curves whose rising segments have similar slopes but different starting times.

A further complication concerns the initial density and the detection method. An inoculum of one thousand viable units and an inoculum of one hundred thousand units may have exactly the same physiological lag. The larger inoculum will nevertheless reach a chosen measurable concentration earlier. If a measurement system detects only concentrations greater than one million per milliliter, the first sample must increase one thousandfold to cross that threshold, whereas the second must increase tenfold. Time to detection therefore contains a contribution from starting abundance as well as lag and subsequent growth. An apparent difference in lag estimated from detection times alone can be misleading.

Even genetically identical cells can differ in their readiness for growth. The distribution of damaged molecules, levels of particular proteins and previous histories can differ among individuals. If some cells divide early while others remain inactive, their descendants can rapidly dominate the measurable culture. An aggregate population curve may then mostly reflect the behavior of cells that recovered early. A long lag does not necessarily mean that every viable cell delayed for exactly the duration shown. Single-cell observations can distinguish variation in first division times from a shared delay; such observations are useful conceptually without requiring specialist methods.

Lag can therefore be understood as a transition from one physiological state toward another, superimposed on a population measurement. The new environment does not erase the previous history of the cells. Once enough of the population can coordinate nutrient uptake, energy generation, macromolecular synthesis and division, the characteristic rising segment begins. The next question is why the rate of numerical increase then becomes approximately proportional to the population itself.

The actual time course in Rolfe and colleagues’ study concerned Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium. Comparing early post-transfer samples with the stationary inoculum, the researchers detected changes in transcripts associated with phosphate acquisition within minutes, followed by increased expression of genes involved in biosynthetic functions before exponential population increase. They also measured changes in cell-associated metals, providing an independent physiological observation rather than relying exclusively on transcriptional data. Transcript abundance cannot alone establish protein abundance, catalytic activity or the necessity of a gene for recovery. Indeed, their comparison with a Fis-deficient strain distinguished a reduced subsequent growth rate from a demonstrably prolonged lag under the tested conditions. The evidence is particularly valuable because it separates preparation, measurable population increase and rate of multiplication instead of treating them as one event.

4. Exponential phase: coordinated synthesis and repeated division

During the exponential phase, a substantial proportion of the population can complete successive rounds of growth and division under conditions that remain sufficiently favorable. Each reproducing cell gives rise to descendants that can themselves reproduce. If the average multiplication rate remains approximately constant and losses are small, the number of descendants produced during an interval is proportional to the number of cells present at its beginning. This is the biological source of exponential behavior; it is not a mysterious property imposed by a graph.

To see the difference between additive and multiplicative growth, imagine four cells that each divide once in a common interval. There are then eight cells. If each of the eight divides in the next interval, there are sixteen, and a further such interval produces thirty-two. The additions are four, eight and sixteen rather than a constant four. This example treats divisions as synchronous for ease of calculation. A real culture is generally asynchronous: different cells divide at different times, and the total curve may be smooth rather than step-shaped. A smooth population curve does not imply that an individual cell splits gradually or that every cell divides simultaneously.

The ability to reproduce at a sustained rate requires more than duplication of DNA. Cells need sources of carbon skeletons, nitrogen for amino acids and nucleotides, phosphorus for nucleic acids and other compounds, sulfur for sulfur-containing biomolecules, and appropriate inorganic ions and cofactors. They also need an energy supply to power synthesis, transport and maintenance. Carbon and energy metabolism produce small molecules that are converted into cellular building blocks. These building blocks become proteins, nucleic acids, membrane components, cell wall materials and other constituents. New cellular material must be assembled into functional structures rather than simply accumulated.

Protein synthesis illustrates the coordination required. Many enzymes responsible for metabolism are proteins. Ribosomes are themselves complexes containing RNA and proteins, and their production consumes resources. More protein-synthesizing capacity can support faster production of transporters, metabolic enzymes and division-related components, provided the required precursors and energy are available. But producing ribosomes also carries a resource cost. Growth therefore reflects coordinated allocation of cellular resources rather than the unconstrained increase of one enzyme. A cell cannot indefinitely increase division rate by increasing a single component if another required function becomes limiting.

DNA replication and segregation must also be coordinated with cell enlargement. The chromosome provides hereditary information that daughter cells require. Envelope expansion must keep pace with increasing internal material, and the division machinery must create two viable compartments. A defect in the completion of division can cause biomass or cellular length to increase without the expected numerical multiplication. This is one reason why optical density cannot always be substituted for a viable count in a perturbation experiment. The details of bacterial division machinery belong to the later chapters on microbial cell division.

A population in balanced growth exhibits approximately constant average composition over the period studied: major cellular constituents increase in similar proportions to overall biomass. Balanced does not mean that every cell has identical size, identical DNA content or identical momentary activity. It means that the distributions and average composition are relatively stable when viewed at the population level. For example, in a reproducibly growing asynchronous culture, some cells may be small and newly divided, while others are larger and approaching division. The proportion in each state can remain broadly stable even while the number of cells increases.

Balanced growth provides conditions in which different measurements may track each other. If the average biomass per cell is approximately constant and cells remain viable, both biomass and cell number increase proportionally. A doubling of cell concentration should then approximately correspond to a doubling of biomass concentration. Outside this condition, the relation can fail. When cells enlarge without dividing, the average biomass per cell increases; if cells lose viability while retaining their material, biomass may remain high while viable count falls. Therefore the usefulness of a conversion from biomass to cell number depends on the physiological state.

The term log phase refers to the linearity of the growth curve when the logarithm of the relevant population measurement is plotted against time. It does not mean that growth follows a logarithmic function of time on an arithmetic scale. In this phase a roughly constant doubling interval generates exponential numerical increase. The proportional rate is more useful than the number of cells added per hour. Equal successive intervals of doubling produce equal vertical changes on a base-two logarithmic plot. On a base-ten plot, a doubling increases the ordinate by approximately 0.301, because log base ten of two is approximately 0.301. The full derivations and use of the specific growth rate are developed in Node 2.E.03.

Worked example 2. Suppose two cultures in their rising regions are measured at equally spaced times. Culture A has counts of two, four and eight million viable units per milliliter. Culture B has counts of two, three and four million. Culture A doubles at each interval, while B adds the same absolute number. B might be undergoing a transition or its apparent increments might arise from measurement variation. Three data points cannot conclusively establish the law over a long period, but they are more consistent with constant proportional multiplication for A than for B. Exam questions often ask which graph represents exponential growth; testing ratios is a reliable way to decide when the scale is not obvious.

It is tempting to assume that exponential growth always occurs at the organism's maximum possible rate. That inference is not justified. A population may sustain an approximately constant proportional rate at a temperature, pH or nutrient availability below its optimum, so long as the constraint is effectively stable over the measurement interval. Under more favorable conditions the same organism could have a steeper semilog slope. Maximum growth rate is a conditional property of an organism in a specified environment, not a universal constant independent of the medium.

The concept of exponential growth is also conditional on losses. Suppose cells divide, but some viable units disappear at the same time. A graph of viable concentration then records the net effect. If death is negligible relative to division, the slope closely reflects reproductive increase. If death is substantial and both processes occur, the slope represents their difference rather than the gross birth rate. A population can show exponential net growth even when individual losses occur. In elementary treatments it is usually appropriate to assume low losses during favorable early exponential growth, but an examination problem may explicitly supply evidence that invalidates this assumption.

Exponential cultures are often selected for physiological comparisons because their properties are more reproducible over time than those of rapidly transitioning cultures. Nevertheless, reproducibility depends on defining the state, not just on choosing a fixed number of hours after inoculation. A sample collected at six hours may be exponential in one medium but stationary in another. If protein expression or sensitivity to a perturbation is compared without verifying phase, physiological state can confound the result. A comparison at similar position within the growth curve may answer a different question from a comparison at the same elapsed time. Both designs can be useful, but they are not equivalent.

As the population expands, its success changes the conditions that made such expansion possible. The nutrient demand per unit volume increases, available resources decline, and metabolic products may accumulate. At some point the processes that had supported a stable proportional rate no longer keep pace with the demand for new biomass. This is the transition out of exponential growth.

5. Deceleration: why a growing culture changes its own environment

The exponential phase cannot continue indefinitely in a finite batch because production of new organisms consumes matter and free energy. A newly produced cell contains atoms that must come from available resources, and synthesis requires energy released or captured through metabolism. Multiplication therefore imposes physical demands on the culture environment. Even a medium that was initially rich may eventually fail to supply one or more required resources at a rate sufficient to maintain the previous rate of division.

A limiting resource is one whose availability constrains further growth under the existing conditions. It need not be the nutrient present in the smallest numerical concentration, since different substances are required in different amounts. For example, nitrogen may remain available but a usable carbon source may become scarce; alternatively the medium may contain considerable carbon yet have insufficient nitrogen for additional protein and nucleic acid synthesis. Which resource limits growth depends on the organism and the relative supplies and demands. The presence of some residual nutrient does not demonstrate that the culture is unrestricted.

The distinction between nutrient concentration and uptake flux is helpful. Concentration describes the amount of a substance per unit volume, while flux describes the rate of movement or conversion. A cell can experience restricted uptake before a nutrient reaches zero concentration. Transport systems may acquire substrate more slowly as extracellular availability falls, and metabolism may receive insufficient precursor or energy input to sustain the earlier synthesis rate. In a dense population many cells compete for the available supply. Therefore the end of rapid exponential growth may develop progressively before the limiting resource is completely exhausted.

Oxygen provides an important example of a constraint that depends on both supply and consumption. In an aerobic culture, cells may use oxygen in respiration as an electron acceptor. When dissolved oxygen is consumed more rapidly than it enters the liquid, its concentration can decline. The resulting limitation may change the rate and pathways of energy metabolism. For a facultative organism, alternative metabolism may sustain slower growth under some conditions, whereas an obligately aerobic organism may be more strongly restricted. An organism that does not require oxygen for growth cannot automatically be assigned the same response. The direction of an oxygen effect depends on its physiology.

The chemical consequences of metabolism may also restrict growth. A substance produced by cells is not necessarily harmful, but some products can accumulate to levels that affect transport, enzyme activity or membrane function. When acidic products accumulate, for instance, extracellular pH may fall if buffering is insufficient. The cells must expend resources to maintain an internal pH compatible with their enzymes. A growing culture might have plenty of remaining carbon and nitrogen but still decelerate because the altered external pH increases the cost of homeostasis or inhibits essential functions. Waste accumulation and nutrient depletion are therefore different hypotheses, even though they often coexist.

Temperature can affect the activities of enzymes, the properties of membranes and the stability of cellular structures. In the ideal batch model, temperature is controlled so that changes in growth are primarily associated with the changing medium and physiology. In a real experiment, a change of temperature or another external condition may produce a slope change unrelated to ordinary nutrient exhaustion. The same observed flattening of a curve can therefore arise through different causal routes. The four-phase model cannot identify the limiting factor from shape alone.

The departure from rapid growth is not an instantaneous switch required to occur at one precise cell density. Cells sense changes in nutrient and energy status, and the ability to maintain biosynthesis decreases as constraints become stronger. Different processes may respond at different rates. Some cells may cease division before others. The resulting deceleration interval is often curved on a semilog plot, linking the straight rising segment with the stationary region. The point where a researcher labels the transition is partly a practical convention based on the available measurements.

Consider two hypothetical cultures with comparable initial states and identical starting nutrient supplies, except one contains an additional source of a nutrient that is limiting in the other. If both cultures initially have sufficient amounts of that nutrient to sustain the same growth rate, their exponential slopes may be similar. The culture with more of the limiting resource can continue increasing for longer and reach a higher population level, assuming no different constraint intervenes. Thus a greater final population need not indicate a faster earlier multiplication rate. This distinction between growth rate and amount of growth will become central in the separate chapter on growth yield.

A well-designed interpretation distinguishes between observations and interventions. A drop in substrate concentration concurrent with a fall in slope is consistent with nutrient limitation, but does not by itself establish causation. The culture could simultaneously acidify or lose oxygen. A comparison that changes one candidate constraint while holding others as comparable as possible can strengthen the inference. Recovery of growth after relieving a constraint suggests that it contributed to the slowdown, whereas failure to recover may indicate that another limitation remains or that irreversible damage has already occurred. The interpretation requires attention to the time of the intervention and the physiological condition of the cells.

The deceleration interval prepares the population for stationary behavior. A stationary region is not simply an arbitrary horizontal line drawn after a rising one; it is the observable consequence of a new balance between the processes that produce additional viable units and the processes that prevent or remove them. The cells have not ceased to be biological systems simply because the graph becomes nearly horizontal.

6. Stationary phase: the difference between a population plateau and cellular inactivity

Stationary phase is the interval during which a specified population measure, commonly viable concentration, undergoes little net change. In the classical growth curve this appears as a plateau. Its name refers to a population-level observation rather than to the cessation of every process in every cell. A culture can retain considerable metabolic activity during this interval because survival itself requires cellular work. A stationary-phase cell does not escape the chemical demands of maintaining its membrane, repairing damaged molecules or preserving the information needed to reproduce when conditions improve.

It is useful to separate gross biological events from the net change measured by the experimenter. If a population contains a hundred viable cells at the beginning of an interval, ten additional viable cells may be produced by division while ten previously viable cells are lost. The final count is still one hundred. Alternatively, the same final count could arise if none of the cells divided and none lost viability. The two cases have the same net result and potentially very different internal processes. The plateau alone cannot decide which is occurring. A third possibility is a mixture of slowly dividing subpopulations, nondividing survivors and cells that are gradually becoming unable to form colonies.

The elementary statement that the rate of cell division equals the rate of death in stationary phase is therefore best understood as a possible balance of gross rates. Where both rates are negligible, they are also approximately equal. Where appreciable turnover occurs, equality means that production compensates for loss. It does not require a one-to-one exchange of an individual dying cell for a newly dividing one at every instant. Moreover, if a colony count rather than individual viable cell number is measured, changes in aggregate formation or recovery conditions can influence the apparent balance. An operational plateau must be interpreted with these qualifications.

To explain why a culture adopts this behavior, return to the distinction between building cells and maintaining them. Growth requires the coordinated production of many kinds of cellular material. If one essential building block is unavailable, new complete cells cannot be assembled indefinitely, even if substrates for energy production remain. For example, consider a population that still has access to carbon compounds but lacks an adequate supply of nitrogen. Nitrogen is necessary to synthesize amino acids and nucleotides. Energy obtained by oxidizing carbon cannot replace the nitrogen atoms required to make proteins and nucleic acids. Thus cellular metabolism may continue in the absence of appreciable net biomass production.

The reverse situation is also instructive. A culture with abundant mineral nutrients but inadequate usable energy cannot invest those nutrients in new structures. Chemical synthesis, active transport and maintenance of ion gradients require energy. The ability to synthesize a particular constituent is not sufficient if the energy supply cannot sustain coordinated growth. In either situation, a population may reach a state in which the resource supply is enough for some maintenance or repair but insufficient for sustained reproduction. The resources required for survival can differ from those required for rapid growth, although survival itself will eventually fail if essential resources become too scarce.

Bacteria can respond to nutritional restriction by changing which cellular functions receive resources. When the environment no longer supports rapid multiplication, continued production of large quantities of growth-related machinery can become wasteful. Reduced synthesis of growth-associated proteins and ribosomal components, together with increased emphasis on repair and protective functions, can help cells remain viable under unfavorable circumstances. The extent and timing of these changes depend on the organism and the particular limitation. The general principle is that the stationary state emerges from physiological reorganization as well as from direct chemical restriction.

One important concept in many bacteria is the stringent response, a regulatory response to nutritional deprivation and related stresses. The nucleotide alarmones collectively called (p)ppGpp participate in signaling changes in cellular resource status. In the familiar model of amino acid starvation in Escherichia coli, a shortage of charged transfer RNA can be detected in relation to translation, leading to increased alarmone production and changes in gene expression. The resulting shift can reduce investment in the apparatus needed for very rapid growth and favor functions useful under the changed conditions. The detailed regulatory networks differ among organisms and are developed in the later chapter on starvation and stress. For the present discussion, the important connection is between nutrient availability, regulation of biosynthesis and a population-level slowing of growth.

A second useful example is the general stress response of E. coli involving the alternative sigma factor RpoS. A sigma factor helps bacterial RNA polymerase recognize groups of promoters. As cells enter unfavorable conditions, changes in the abundance and activity of RpoS can promote expression of genes useful for stress protection. This is not a universal definition of stationary phase, and an organism lacking the same regulatory protein can still enter a nongrowing state. It illustrates how a change in the growth environment can alter the cellular program rather than merely reduce the rate of all reactions by the same factor.

Protection becomes important because unfavorable medium can damage biological molecules. Proteins may unfold or become chemically altered, membranes may lose functional integrity, and nucleic acids may acquire damage. Maintenance and repair mechanisms consume energy and materials. Cells with effective protective responses may survive an interval of deprivation even though they cannot build new daughters. Cells unable to maintain essential functions may progressively lose reproductive competence. The plateau can therefore be a transient period in which the culture contains a substantial surviving population, not a permanent equilibrium guaranteeing indefinite viability.

Changes in cellular size can alter the relationship between biomass and number. A cell entering stationary phase may complete certain ongoing division events even as synthesis slows, and stationary populations may contain smaller cells than those in a rapidly growing culture. In other organisms or under other restrictions, storage material, swelling or altered cell shape may complicate this simple pattern. The mean mass of an individual cell is therefore not fixed throughout the growth curve. A stable direct count does not establish a stable dry mass, and a stable dry mass does not prove that every cell remains viable. Any claim about size or composition should be supported by the appropriate measurements.

Microorganisms also differ in the strategies by which they survive nutrient-poor conditions. Some bacteria can produce highly resistant endospores as a form of cellular differentiation. An endospore can survive conditions that would be damaging to the vegetative cell, but its formation is not equivalent to multiplication: one vegetative cell typically gives rise to one endospore, which can subsequently germinate into a vegetative cell. Many bacteria do not form endospores at all. The existence of stationary-phase survival therefore does not imply sporulation, and a flat curve cannot establish that a particular survival structure has been produced.

Storage compounds accumulated during earlier growth may contribute to survival when external nutrients become limiting. A cell can mobilize internal material for metabolism if the relevant pathways are available. This may sustain energy production or particular cellular functions without enough resources for population expansion. Storage is not an unlimited source of material, and different microorganisms store different compounds. The balance between the use of internal reserves, uptake of residual nutrients and maintenance expenditure influences how long cells can remain viable. Consequently, cultures with identical counts at the beginning of stationary phase may exhibit different later survival patterns.

Some extracellular products accumulate mainly during particular growth states. A molecule required directly for construction of new cell material may be formed in close association with rapid growth, whereas other products may be produced or accumulate during slower growth or the transition to stationary behavior. The distinction is useful for biological reasoning, but not an absolute division of all compounds into products of only one phase. Product accumulation reflects synthesis, degradation, secretion and other processes. It may continue even if cell numbers remain nearly constant. Conversely, a stable product concentration does not necessarily mean that its synthesis has stopped if it is simultaneously being removed.

A central biological consequence of stationary-phase reorganization is that cells collected at different phases need not respond identically to the same challenge. A rapidly growing cell may be sensitive to a perturbation of an active biosynthetic process, whereas a nongrowing but viable cell may be less affected over the same interval because it uses that process less intensively. Protective systems induced by deprivation can also affect survival under another stress. These statements do not establish universal greater resistance of stationary cells. A challenge directed against a function crucial for maintenance may still be highly damaging, and the response depends on the organism and the particular stress. The detailed distinction between tolerance and heritable resistance belongs to subsequent nodes.

The stationary phase is therefore a useful reminder that cell number is an incomplete description of physiological state. Two cultures with similar viable counts can differ in metabolic activity, average cell composition, capacity for repair and time required to resume growth after transfer. The curve tells us that the population is no longer increasing appreciably under the original conditions. The reasons can be explored by examining the medium, the cells and their ability to recover when constraints are changed.

7. Decline phase: why reproductive viability can fall while material remains

When a culture remains under unfavorable conditions, the number of cells capable of successful reproduction may eventually decrease. In the classical curve this is the decline or death phase. The use of the word death must be tied to the measurement. In many growth curves the decline is detected by counting colonies recovered from the culture. A falling colony count demonstrates a decrease in the number of colony-forming units under the specified recovery conditions. It is consistent with loss of viable cells, but the exact physiological fate of each missing unit cannot be determined from this observation alone.

The underlying biological problem is an inability to sustain essential cellular functions. A microbial cell is not a structure that can be preserved indefinitely without expenditure. Membranes must retain appropriate permeability and electrochemical gradients. Proteins and nucleic acids must remain sufficiently functional to support metabolism and recovery. Damaged components may have to be repaired or replaced. If nutrient depletion reduces energy availability while damaging conditions persist, maintenance and repair can become insufficient. Loss of function in one system can affect others: a membrane that cannot preserve an ion gradient may impair transport and energy conversion, reducing the capacity for further repair.

Metabolic products and external conditions can contribute to the same process. An increasingly unfavorable external pH can make internal pH regulation more difficult. A chemically reactive product may damage important cellular constituents. Oxygen limitation can restrict energy metabolism in organisms dependent on aerobic respiration. These are potential contributors rather than universal requirements for the decline phase. A culture can lose viability because several restrictions act together. The growth curve alone does not distinguish one chemical cause from another.

There is also a difference between the failure to reproduce and the physical disintegration of a cell. Lysis refers to disruption of a cell such that its contents are released and the structure breaks down. Lysis can reduce the number of intact cells, but a bacterium can lose reproductive competence before undergoing visible lysis. Its envelope may still scatter light, and its shape may still be recognizable in a direct count. Conversely, cellular debris may continue to scatter light even after lysis. This is why the optical density of a declining culture may decrease slowly, remain approximately constant for a time or show a more complicated pattern than the viable count.

Consider a hypothetical sample with one hundred million directly counted intact cells per milliliter and eighty million colony-forming units per milliliter. Later, the total intact count is ninety million but only eight million CFU per milliliter are recovered. If there is no important aggregation and the methods are comparable, a large loss of colony-forming ability has occurred with much less loss of intact structures. One cannot infer from the direct count alone that the population remains healthy. At the same time, the numerical difference between the counts is not necessarily a direct census of dead cells because inability to form colonies can have more than one cause.

Worked example 3. The classical exponential death model describes a case in which a roughly constant fraction of the viable population is lost during equal time intervals. If the count falls from one million to one hundred thousand and then to ten thousand in successive intervals of the same length, each interval has removed ninety percent of the previously detected population. The absolute losses differ: the first interval loses nine hundred thousand units, and the second loses ninety thousand. An arithmetic graph therefore becomes less steep as the count becomes smaller, whereas a semilog graph has a constant negative slope under this model.

Worked example 4. A constant proportional loss is different from the loss of a constant number of cells per hour. Suppose a population of one million loses two hundred thousand units in each of two successive hours. The counts become eight hundred thousand and six hundred thousand. The first loss is twenty percent and the second is twenty-five percent of the population present at the beginning of that hour. The logarithmic slope is not constant. A student can distinguish these patterns by checking whether differences or ratios remain constant. The biological interpretation of the exponential loss model is conditional on the observed population and interval; it should not be generalized to every declining culture.

Worked example 5. A useful quantitative descriptor is the fraction of the earlier recoverable population that remains at a later time. With an initial viable concentration V0 and a later concentration V, this fraction is V/V0, provided the concentrations are comparable and the reference time is stated. Multiplying by one hundred expresses the fraction as a percentage. If a sample falls from two million CFU per milliliter to fifty thousand CFU per milliliter, the ratio is 50,000 divided by 2,000,000, or 0.025. The recoverable fraction is 2.5 percent, and the reduction in measured CFU is 97.5 percent. This calculation describes a population outcome, not a direct measurement of the probability that a particular individual died.

If the volume changes appreciably, concentration and total number must be distinguished. A reduction in culture volume by evaporation, for example, could increase the concentration of a constant number of cells. Conversely, addition of liquid without adding cells could decrease concentration without causing death. The simple batch model normally assumes an approximately constant volume or appropriate correction for changes. Without this assumption, a calculation based solely on concentrations may incorrectly attribute a physical dilution or concentration effect to biological gain or loss.

Decline curves often depart from a single straight falling line. A period of relatively slow initial decline can be followed by a steeper fall. A steep fall may eventually give way to a slowly decreasing residual population. Such patterns can reflect different physiological states, delayed accumulation of damage, changing environmental conditions or variation in recovery behavior. The curve does not automatically prove genetic variation. Genetically similar cells can differ in their stress protection, previous metabolic history and ability to resume growth. A late tail in the viable curve is therefore evidence for a change in net recovery dynamics, not by itself proof of a particular survival mechanism.

One reason the interpretation becomes difficult is that some stressed microorganisms retain indicators of biological function while failing to form colonies under a particular set of conditions. The term viable but nonculturable is used for certain such states when the evidence supports continued viability despite loss of ordinary culturability. An intact membrane or a detectable enzyme activity alone does not settle the question of whether a cell can recover. Multiple observations and suitable recovery evidence may be necessary. It is equally mistaken to classify all non-colony-forming cells as certainly dead and to classify every intact structure as certainly alive.

Dormancy describes a reversible state of greatly reduced growth or activity in some organisms. A dormant cell may not immediately produce new descendants under conditions that support growth of an actively reproducing cell. Persistence, in its more specific later use in antimicrobial physiology, concerns a surviving subpopulation under particular challenges without requiring a heritable change in susceptibility. These concepts are related to the physiological heterogeneity of declining cultures but are not synonymous with the decline phase. A population-level curve may contain contributions from viable nondividing cells, dead cells, injured cells and cells that continue to divide slowly. Distinguishing these states requires evidence beyond the curve.

In some old cultures, materials released from damaged or lysed cells can provide resources for surviving organisms. This can allow limited new growth even when the original supply of usable nutrients has largely disappeared. The original medium has not been externally renewed, yet the chemical form and accessibility of material have changed within the vessel. Internal recycling therefore complicates the idea of a batch as a simple one-way progression from all growth to all death. The remaining population may undergo turnover for a long period, and different cell lineages can contribute unequally to it.

A late population need not decrease smoothly to zero. In long-term stationary cultures, a small surviving population can persist, sometimes with turnover and changes in its composition. This phenomenon has been studied in bacterial physiology, including E. coli. It is not necessary to invoke a new named phase in every elementary examination graph, but it is important to recognize that a decline segment is an approximate representation of one observed interval. Extrapolating a short interval of constant proportional loss indefinitely may misrepresent the later biology. Even an exponential equation predicts a positive mathematical value at every finite time; in a finite actual population, stochastic disappearance of the last viable cells is a different question.

The biological meaning of decline is thus best described by identifying what is being lost. If reproductive competence falls, the viable count declines. If intact structures are destroyed, a direct count can also decline. If biomass is degraded or dispersed, the mass measurement may change. These events can occur together or at different times. The next section connects these observables in a framework that can be used to interpret apparently conflicting graphs.

The long-term stationary phase also has an experimental basis. Zambrano and colleagues reported in 1993 that variants isolated from aged Escherichia coli cultures could outcompete other cells in stationary-phase populations. The key comparison concerned relative success in the shared nutrient-depleted environment, rather than the fastest growth in an abundant fresh medium. Such a result supports the possibility of selection during prolonged nongrowth at the population level. It does not prove that every surviving cell has acquired a mutation, or that every late tail arises through selection. Physiological survival and competitive changes in the population can coexist.

8. One culture, several curves: counts, biomass, optical density and metabolic activity

A useful way to study the four phases is to imagine measuring several different properties of the same population over time. In favorable exponential growth the properties may increase together. During lag, stationary and decline phases they may separate. These differences should not immediately be dismissed as measurement error. They may be the most informative features of the experiment because each measurement responds to a different biological property.

Total cell concentration is the number of recognizable individual cells in a given volume, within the limits of the chosen counting method. It includes intact cells irrespective of whether they can reproduce. Viable concentration in a conventional growth curve is often estimated through colony formation. A colony-forming unit is a cell or association of cells that produces a detectable colony under the test conditions. The word unit is essential: a chain of several viable cells might produce one colony if the chain remains intact. A change in the number of colonies may therefore reflect a change in the number of reproductive units rather than a proportional change in the number of living individual cells.

Biomass concentration expresses the mass of cellular material in a specified volume. A dry mass measurement aims to reduce the contribution of water, which can vary greatly among cells and environmental conditions. Biomass includes material from cells that may no longer be reproductively competent. The average dry mass per counted cell can be estimated by dividing the cellular mass concentration by the cell concentration, assuming the measurements correspond to the same material and volume. The result is a mass per cell, not a direct measure of metabolic rate.

Optical density is an indirect signal related to how much light is transmitted through a suspension. In microbial suspensions scattering commonly contributes substantially to the signal. In a suitable range, optical density may be approximately proportional to biomass or cell concentration for a particular organism and physiological condition. At high densities, multiple scattering and instrument characteristics can make the relationship nonlinear. Cell size, shape, aggregation and refractive properties can also affect the signal. A calibration obtained from one physiological phase should not be assumed to remain exact in all other phases.

Metabolic activity can be estimated in different ways depending on the process being studied. Uptake of a substrate, production of a metabolic end product, consumption of oxygen or incorporation of a precursor into new material can provide information about particular aspects of physiology. Such an indicator is not necessarily a comprehensive measure of all metabolism. A decrease in oxygen consumption could indicate reduced aerobic respiration, but a facultative organism may retain other metabolic activities. A constant rate of one measured process does not establish that every individual cell remains active or that no changes occur elsewhere in the metabolic network.

During early lag, the total count and colony count may remain approximately stable while biomass increases modestly. If metabolic measurements indicate substantial activity, the most straightforward interpretation is that some cells are preparing for reproduction. However, another explanation could involve changes in average cell size or the composition of the suspension. A measurement of newly synthesized material can distinguish accumulation of new biomass from an optical change caused by alterations in cell shape. The choice of additional measurement should follow from the competing hypotheses.

During balanced exponential growth, consider a simplified example with negligible death, stable aggregation behavior and constant average mass per cell. Let a culture double its cell number from one million to two million per milliliter. If each cell has the same average dry mass in both measurements, the total dry biomass per milliliter will also double. Optical density measured in a calibrated linear range should increase approximately in proportion to the biomass. Viable counts may also double if each viable individual remains a separate colony-forming unit. These parallel trends reflect the assumptions of balanced growth, not an identity that holds universally.

Now consider a perturbation that prevents completion of cell division while allowing continued synthesis of cellular constituents for some time. Cells may elongate or enlarge without separating into additional individuals. The direct count can remain almost unchanged while dry mass and optical density rise. Colony-forming ability depends on whether the cells can subsequently resume division under the recovery conditions. If the perturbation is reversible, the CFU measurement may behave differently from the count observed in the original medium. This pattern separates biomass growth from completed population reproduction and explains why a high optical signal need not imply a high number of viable units.

In stationary phase, average mass and measured activity may change even though the viable count is roughly constant. Some cells may continue using residual substrates for repair and maintenance, and others may cease detectable activity. A stable cell count does not establish a stable amount of RNA, protein or ATP per cell. If one compares biochemical measurements from an exponential culture and a stationary culture, it is important to decide whether the comparison should be normalized per unit culture volume, per unit dry mass or per counted cell. These normalizations answer different questions.

Imagine two cultures containing the same number of cells per milliliter but different average cell masses. A measurement of oxygen consumption per milliliter could be higher in the culture containing more biomass simply because it contains more metabolically active material. Expressing consumption per unit dry mass may change the comparison. Similarly, a stationary culture might have low activity per cell but a large population, resulting in a substantial total activity per milliliter. The statement that one culture is more active is incomplete unless the measurement and normalization are specified.

In decline phase, total counts may remain high while CFU fall steeply. The divergence suggests that reproductive competence is being lost faster than intact structures disappear, provided aggregation and recovery artifacts are considered. If the optical signal also falls, physical loss or modification of scattering material becomes a plausible additional process. If the signal remains unchanged, this does not disprove loss of viability. If all three measurements fall in parallel, lysis or loss of cell material may contribute, but parallel trends do not identify the precise cause without further evidence.

Worked example 6. A worked example combines the measurements. Suppose a culture at the beginning of a late interval has a total count of 2 × 10^8 cells per milliliter and 1.6 × 10^8 CFU per milliliter. At the end of the interval its total count is 1.8 × 10^8 cells per milliliter and its CFU count is 4 × 10^7 per milliliter. The total count has decreased by (2.0 − 1.8)/2.0 = 0.10, or ten percent. The CFU count has decreased by (1.6 − 0.4)/1.6 = 0.75, or seventy-five percent. The ratios CFU/total count are initially 0.8 and finally approximately 0.222. These ratios are useful indicators of the divergence between methods; they should not be described as exact proportions of living cells unless each recoverable individual reliably produces one colony and the total count accurately counts individuals.

Worked example 7. A second example concerns biomass. A sample initially contains 0.20 milligrams of dry cellular material per milliliter and 2 × 10^8 counted cells per milliliter. Dividing the first measurement by the second gives 1 × 10^-9 milligrams per cell. Because one milligram equals 10^-3 grams, the value is 1 × 10^-12 grams, or one picogram per cell. Later, the biomass is 0.30 milligrams per milliliter with the same direct count. The average dry mass is then 1.5 picograms per cell, a fifty percent increase. If the cellular material and count are correctly measured, this supports accumulation of mass without a net increase in counted cells. It does not establish that every cell enlarged by exactly fifty percent.

Measurement limitations are especially relevant near the beginning and end of a curve, where counts can be low. A negative observation at the detection limit cannot support claims about the absolute absence of microorganisms. At the high-density end, optical measurements may saturate or require correction. Comparison of samples requires consistent definitions and appropriate units, as well as reasonable attention to measurement error. In an examination, a problem may deliberately provide reliable idealized values; the student can perform the requested calculation and then state what assumptions are required for a biological inference.

The value of measuring multiple quantities is that they constrain explanations. A plateau in cell number and a rise in biomass support a different hypothesis from a plateau in both measures and continued oxygen consumption. A steep decline in CFU alongside a stable direct count supports a different interpretation from a proportional fall in all indicators. The appropriate response to a seemingly contradictory dataset is to ask which property each axis represents, whether the methods share the same assumptions and which biological processes would cause the observed divergence.

9. Reading net changes and proportional slopes without confusing phases with mechanisms

The observed behavior of a culture can be expressed with a small amount of mathematics that clarifies the biology. Let V denote the concentration of recoverable viable units, measured in CFU per milliliter, at a specified time. Over an interval, let G represent the number of additional recoverable units gained per milliliter and L the number lost per milliliter. If the volume is fixed and changes in aggregation and measurement are negligible, the later value is the earlier value plus gains minus losses. In symbols, V2 = V1 + G − L. This identity is simply a statement of accounting. It does not impose a law on the time course of either gains or losses.

Worked example 8. For example, suppose V1 is 10 million CFU per milliliter, G is 3 million and L is 1 million during the interval. V2 is 12 million. The net gain is 2 million even though 3 million additional recoverable units appeared. If gains and losses are both 3 million, V2 equals V1. A plateau therefore provides one constraint on two unknown quantities, not separate estimates of them. If additional data show that few cells are completing divisions, a low-turnover explanation becomes more plausible. If data instead show active division and increasing loss of viability, a high-turnover balance is supported.

The expression can also be stated for short time intervals in terms of rates. The net rate of change in viable concentration equals the rate of gain minus the rate of loss, with both rates expressed in viable units per milliliter per unit time. A positive net rate produces a rising arithmetic graph, a rate near zero produces a plateau, and a negative rate produces a decline. This classification does not explain the molecular mechanisms controlling the gross rates. The same numerical net rate can arise from different combinations of production and loss.

During a sufficiently stable exponential interval, the net gain per unit time is approximately proportional to the population present. This means that the net proportional change is approximately independent of the population level during that interval. For phase identification it is often enough to calculate the ratio of successive values recorded at equal intervals. Ratios close to the same number greater than one suggest exponential net increase. Ratios close to one indicate near-stationarity. A constant ratio less than one is consistent with exponential decline. A systematic decrease in the positive ratios can indicate deceleration.

Worked example 9. A semilogarithmic slope is useful because it describes proportional rather than absolute change. Suppose the base-ten logarithm of V increases from 6.0 to 6.6 in two hours. The slope is (6.6 − 6.0)/2, or 0.3 log-base-ten units per hour. The total fold increase is ten raised to 0.6, approximately 3.98, close to a fourfold increase. If this interval truly represents constant exponential growth, that corresponds to approximately two doublings in two hours. It does not mean that the culture adds 0.3 cells per hour. Logarithmic coordinates represent changes in orders of magnitude, not changes in the original count units.

Worked example 10. A falling semilog segment is interpreted similarly. If the logarithm of V changes from 8.0 to 6.0 over four hours, the slope is −0.5 log-base-ten units per hour. The viable concentration has declined from 10^8 to 10^6 CFU per milliliter, a hundredfold loss in measured concentration. The remaining fraction is 10^6/10^8 = 0.01, or one percent. The reduction is ninety-nine percent. Under a constant proportional loss model, a two-hour interval would correspond to a one-log reduction, leaving one tenth of the concentration present at its beginning. The observed four-hour reduction is consistent with two such intervals. The negative sign of the slope describes the direction of change, not a negative population.

There is an important distinction between a log reduction and a percentage reduction. A one-log reduction leaves ten percent of the earlier population, equivalent to a ninety percent reduction. A two-log reduction leaves one percent, a ninety-nine percent reduction. Repeating a ninety percent reduction does not produce a reduction of one hundred eighty percent; the second reduction acts on the smaller remaining population. If a dataset specifies successive reductions, their remaining fractions must be multiplied. This is a general consequence of proportional change and is applicable to the interpretation of late culture curves.

Worked example 11. Suppose a population falls from 8 × 10^7 to 2 × 10^7 CFU per milliliter over a given interval. The remaining fraction is one quarter and the reduction is seventy-five percent. The base-ten log reduction is log10(8 × 10^7 / 2 × 10^7) = log10(4), approximately 0.602. A seventy-five percent decrease is therefore less than one log of decline. If another population falls from 10^7 to 10^6 over the same interval, the latter shows a larger proportional decline despite a smaller absolute loss. The appropriate metric depends on whether the question concerns total losses or relative survival.

When a graph spans all four phases, calculating a single average slope across the whole experiment can hide the physiologically important intervals. A culture that remains in lag for three hours and then multiplies rapidly may have the same overall endpoint as one that starts increasing immediately but grows more slowly. The same endpoint ratio is compatible with different histories. It is necessary to inspect the local behavior of the curve or use observations that isolate the appropriate phase. Likewise, a culture that first grows and then declines can end at its starting viable concentration even though substantial multiplication and loss occurred in between.

One common examination question supplies a graph with an approximately straight semilog rising segment followed by a curved transition and a plateau. The straight region is the appropriate place to characterize a constant net growth rate. The deceleration region is not appropriate for assuming a single constant rate without qualification. A whole-culture average may still be calculated if explicitly requested, but it should not be interpreted as the characteristic exponential-phase rate. Detailed relationships between generation number, generation time and specific growth rate are developed in the following node; the present aim is to identify the biological conditions under which those equations describe the observations.

The connection between mathematical form and cellular mechanism is therefore limited but valuable. A straight line in logarithmic coordinates identifies approximately constant proportional change. It does not prove that all cells divide synchronously, that every cell has equal reproductive ability or that a single molecular pathway controls the process. A plateau identifies near-zero net change in the measured property, not an absence of metabolism. A downward line identifies loss of the measured property, not necessarily lysis. Recognizing what the mathematics actually establishes is central to scientific interpretation.

10. Diauxic growth and other departures from the four-phase curve

A characteristic growth curve is useful because it provides a reference pattern against which unusual observations can be interpreted. It becomes misleading if it is treated as a mandatory sequence of four sharply defined stages. Cultures may omit a detectable lag, display multiple intervals of rapid growth, reach a plateau without an observable decline during the experiment, or show a late increase after a long period of slow change. Such departures may contain information about the environment and the physiology of the organisms.

A well-known departure is diauxic growth, in which two periods of population increase are separated by a temporary interval of slow or negligible net growth. The term describes a biphasic pattern rather than a particular organism. A common explanation involves a mixture of two substrates that can support growth but are not used at the same time or at the same effective rate. If cells preferentially use one substrate and must change their metabolic machinery before using the other effectively, a pause can appear between the two increasing regions.

To understand this pattern, consider a bacterium supplied with glucose and lactose as potential carbon sources. Lactose cannot be used efficiently merely because it exists outside the cell. The organism must possess transport and metabolic functions appropriate to lactose. In the classical E. coli model, the lac operon encodes functions involved in lactose utilization. Lac permease contributes to lactose uptake, and beta-galactosidase hydrolyzes lactose and also participates in the formation of the natural inducer allolactose. If expression of these functions is low, the available lactose may not sustain the rapid growth possible when the corresponding machinery is expressed.

The regulation of carbon source use connects with the earlier chapter on operons. In the familiar E. coli model, allolactose reduces repression of the lac operon by LacI. Efficient transcription also depends on the regulatory state associated with the availability of preferred carbon sources, including activation by the cyclic AMP receptor protein when conditions favor it. Glucose availability can also affect lactose uptake through inducer exclusion associated with the phosphotransferase system. The combined regulatory effects can keep effective lactose utilization relatively low while glucose is being used. It would be an oversimplification to explain every instance of preferential carbon use by a single universal regulatory mechanism.

As the first carbon source becomes unavailable, the earlier route can no longer support the same rate of biomass synthesis. If the second substrate remains accessible and the relevant regulatory conditions change, the cells can increase production of transport and metabolic machinery for the second substrate. The interval in which these activities are reorganized may appear as a pause in numerical growth. Once a sufficient fraction of cells can use the second substrate effectively, growth resumes. The second rising segment may have a different slope because the second substrate supports a different rate of metabolism and biosynthesis.

The intermediate pause is often called a diauxic lag. It should not be confused with the final stationary phase simply because both appear relatively flat. In the diauxic pause, another usable resource remains and renewed growth follows under the same batch conditions after physiological adjustment. In a terminal stationary region, the available combination of conditions no longer supports substantial net increase for the period observed. A graph that stops at the first plateau cannot reveal whether growth would resume later. The time range of observation matters.

Monod's classical studies of growth on mixed carbon sources made these relationships experimentally tractable. His 1949 review presents examples in which the extent of growth in successive cycles depended on the supplies of different substrates. Rather than regarding all available carbon as a homogeneous resource, the experiments helped establish that the identity and sequence of substrate use influence the pattern of growth. The molecular account of the lac operon developed through subsequent work; the growth curve itself did not directly reveal all the molecular components of regulation. This historical distinction is important because a physiological observation can motivate a mechanistic explanation without proving every step of that explanation.

A conceptual way to distinguish the sequential-substrate hypothesis from an unrelated transient restriction is to compare cultures supplied with each substrate individually and with their mixture, while following the concentrations of the two substrates. If the first rising region of the mixed culture coincides with consumption of one substrate, the pause occurs near its depletion, and the second rise coincides with consumption of the other, the observations support sequential use. Measurements of the relevant cellular enzyme activities or gene expression would strengthen the interpretation. Controls with comparable initial states are needed because prior exposure to either substrate could influence the duration of the pause.

Worked example 12. Suppose a hypothetical culture starts at 1 × 10^6 CFU per milliliter and rises to 8 × 10^6 while substrate A decreases substantially and substrate B remains nearly unchanged. The viable count remains around 8 × 10^6 for an interval. Later the count rises to 3.2 × 10^7 as B decreases. The first multiplication is eightfold, equivalent to three doublings of the recoverable population; the second is fourfold, equivalent to two doublings. The total increase is thirty-two-fold, not twelvefold: the fold increases multiply. The number of effective doublings is five, provided the counts are comparable and the term refers to population multiplication rather than the actual division history of every cell.

The observation of two growth segments alone is not sufficient proof of catabolite repression. A culture could temporarily encounter oxygen restriction, undergo a change in pH or contain different organisms whose growth dominates at different times. Even a pure culture can consist of cells in different physiological states. Conversely, microorganisms can sometimes consume multiple substrates concurrently and show no clearly separated growth segments. Diauxic behavior is a conditional outcome of a particular combination of regulation, substrates and physiological state; it is not a law that every mixture of sugars produces two phases.

Another departure arises when cells transferred during active growth to a comparable environment resume multiplication promptly. A conventional lag might be too short for the chosen sampling schedule to resolve. This is not a violation of the growth model: the adaptation demands have simply changed. Conversely, a strongly stressed inoculum might recover only after a very long interval or fail to grow detectably at all. If the culture never enters an exponential region, one should not force a label of log phase onto it because a typical textbook figure contains one.

A late curve may also show oscillations or nonmonotonic behavior. Some changes can reflect genuine cycles of resource availability, loss and recovery of viable units, or changing population composition. Other changes may reflect measurement noise, settling, aggregation or changes in sampling. A single anomalous point should be distinguished from a reproducible pattern across independent observations. The correct response to an unusual graph is to construct explanations that predict additional observations, not to assume that the graph must represent either a particular known mechanism or an experimental error.

Filamentous organisms offer another important boundary of the classical picture. In a fungus growing as branching hyphae, a single continuous structure can extend and generate more biomass without producing a correspondingly interpretable number of independent cells. Fragmentation can alter the number of units recovered by a colony assay even when total biomass changes little. Budding yeast may also be counted differently depending on whether attached buds are treated as separate units. The qualitative logic of resource limitation still applies, but the observable must fit the organism's growth strategy. The classic curve of individually reproducing bacteria is not a universal description of microbial morphology.

Viruses provide a particularly useful contrast. They do not independently increase biomass by metabolism or multiply by binary fission in a nutrient medium. Their reproduction requires appropriate host cells, and the time course of virus production is described using different concepts, including the eclipse and latent periods in appropriate viral growth models. The names of these viral intervals should not be equated mechanically with bacterial lag or stationary phase. The present chapter concerns cellular microorganisms that can grow under appropriate culture conditions.

The four-phase model is thus best regarded as a causal framework whose assumptions can be tested. Its value lies not in the ability to fit every curve into four labels, but in the ability to identify how growth, limitation, maintenance and loss interact. When the curve departs from the expected shape, the investigator should ask what resource, measurement, physiological transition or population structure might account for the departure.

11. Testing causes rather than merely labeling curves

A microbial growth curve is an observational record. To turn that record into a causal explanation, a student must learn to formulate alternative hypotheses and identify the observations that would favor each one. This approach is central to experimental questions in CSIR-NET. The relevant skill is not memorizing a specialized laboratory protocol but understanding why a comparison can discriminate between mechanisms.

Imagine a culture that initially grows exponentially and then reaches a plateau. Measurements reveal that the available carbon source is low and that the medium is more acidic than at the beginning. Both findings are consistent with restricted growth. One hypothesis is that a shortage of usable carbon prevents additional biosynthesis. A second is that the changed pH restricts essential reactions even if carbon remains available. A third is that both restrictions act together. The initial observations do not settle which hypothesis is correct, because carbon consumption and acid production can occur in the same culture at the same time.

A conceptual comparison would examine the response of equivalent samples when a candidate constraint is relieved. If restoring carbon availability leads to a reproducible increase in viable counts while the relevant pH is unchanged, carbon availability probably contributed to the restriction under those conditions. If adjustment of pH alone restores growth while carbon remains at the same level, the pH hypothesis receives support. If neither individual change restores growth but both together do, the combined restriction becomes plausible. A comparison in which neither condition is altered gives a baseline against which spontaneous changes can be evaluated.

These interventions must be interpreted carefully. The response may be delayed because cells need to adapt after a period of limitation. A failure of growth to resume does not necessarily exclude the importance of the candidate resource at an earlier time. Cells might have suffered irreversible injury, or another uncorrected constraint might now prevent recovery. Even a positive response does not demonstrate that the altered variable was the sole original cause. Restoring one resource can change other aspects of physiology, and an intervention may have effects beyond its intended target. Proper causal language is conditional: the data support a contributing limitation in the observed conditions.

The distinction between cells and their medium can also be investigated conceptually using reciprocal comparisons. Suppose fresh cells that were actively growing are placed into cell-free spent medium obtained from a culture that has reached a plateau, while cells from the plateau are transferred into comparable fresh medium. If fresh cells fail to grow well in the spent medium and old cells recover in the fresh medium, the results support the importance of extracellular conditions. However, transfer into fresh medium changes several variables at once. The comparison does not establish whether nutrient depletion, waste accumulation, pH or another factor was the specific cause.

If old cells remain slow to grow even in fresh medium, their physiological history may have become important. This could reflect repair requirements, a need to rebuild biosynthetic capacity or a fraction of injured cells that do not recover promptly. A short period of low population growth followed by the same eventual exponential slope as fresh cells would be consistent with an altered lag rather than a permanent reduction of the subsequent growth rate. If the later slope remains lower, other differences may be present. The observation time and the starting number of recoverable units must both be considered.

A rigorous comparison also recognizes that dilution changes population concentration. If one group receives a larger volume of additional medium than another, the immediate drop in CFU per milliliter may reflect dilution rather than loss of reproductive competence. Interpreting biological change requires accounting for the physical change in volume or using equivalent treatment volumes. Likewise, a sudden increase in recovered CFU after mixing or dispersion might partly reflect separation of clusters. The measured quantity is meaningful only when the experimental comparison respects its operational definition.

The same reasoning helps distinguish high turnover from low turnover in a stationary population. If the viable count remains constant, the equation of population balance cannot independently determine the rates of division and loss. Evidence of continued DNA synthesis could indicate that some cells are active, but it does not necessarily demonstrate completion of cell division; DNA may be synthesized without producing additional viable units. Observations of actual divisions and subsequent losses, or appropriately interpreted lineage measurements, would provide stronger evidence for turnover. A measurement of ATP concentration alone cannot separate the hypotheses either, because maintenance metabolism can support ATP turnover in a nondividing population.

Consider a hypothetical dataset in which the viable count remains around 10^8 per milliliter for several hours, while a measurement of incorporation into new protein remains detectable. This establishes that protein synthesis continues in at least part of the population, assuming the measurement is specific and valid. It is compatible with new cell construction, protein replacement or production of protective functions. It does not prove that gross division exactly equals gross death at a high rate. If a second observation shows that cells rarely complete divisions and few lose recoverability, low turnover is favored. If many completed divisions and losses are directly observed, high turnover is favored. The curve alone could not resolve the difference.

A further comparison concerns death and physical lysis. If the viable count falls but the number of intact structures remains stable, it is reasonable to conclude that disappearance of intact cells cannot fully explain the decline in recoverable units, assuming the counting methods are comparable. If intact cell numbers and recoverable units both fall, destruction of structures may contribute. Evidence of released intracellular material would be consistent with lysis but requires controls to exclude other sources of extracellular molecules. These distinctions prevent an overly strong inference from a single observable.

A metabolic measurement may also be confounded by the number of organisms. Suppose two cultures consume the same total amount of oxygen per milliliter during an interval, but one contains twice as many viable units. If the counts represent individual cells and all cells contribute similarly, the average oxygen consumption per viable cell is lower in the denser culture. If instead much of its oxygen consumption comes from a small active subpopulation, the average per viable cell obscures the heterogeneity. Both population normalization and the assumption about which cells contribute to the signal matter.

An evidence-based approach can be condensed into a sequence of reasoning. First identify the measured quantity and its units. Then determine the time interval and relevant graph behavior. Next distinguish the observed pattern from candidate mechanisms. Ask which alternative explanation would produce a similar pattern, and select an additional observation or comparison that could distinguish the alternatives. Finally state the limitations of the conclusion, including the conditions under which a measurement is a valid proxy. This procedure turns an unfamiliar growth-curve problem into a series of manageable biological questions.

12. Integrating phase physiology, biological applications and the limits of the model

The population growth curve describes a changing relationship between an organism and its environment. The same organism can display different apparent behaviors because its cellular resources and external conditions are changing. During adaptation the dominant limitation may be readiness to exploit the environment. During exponential growth the central feature is sustained proportional increase. During deceleration and stationary behavior the available resources and conditions become insufficient to support the earlier rate of reproduction. During decline, the loss of recoverable units exceeds their production. These labels summarize outcomes; they do not replace the causal explanation.

The phases have practical significance when physiological samples are compared. Suppose a researcher wishes to examine the abundance of a protein involved in growth. Comparing an exponentially growing culture with a stationary culture may reveal a difference because of the underlying changes in resource allocation. But if the objective is to compare two genotypes, one must distinguish a direct effect of genotype on the protein from an indirect effect mediated through different positions on their growth curves. A slower-growing genotype sampled at the same clock time as a faster-growing genotype might be in a different phase. Such a difference is biologically meaningful, yet it cannot automatically be assigned to direct regulation of the protein.

A similar distinction applies to the use of a batch culture as a model of environments outside a vessel. A small isolated nutrient patch can initially support multiplication, followed by slowing as available resources change. In more complex natural environments, resources may arrive intermittently, organisms may move into or out of the local region, and other species may consume or release compounds. The assumption of a single well-mixed closed population may therefore be inadequate. A four-phase pattern can provide a useful analogy without establishing that a natural community passes through synchronized phases.

Microbial populations in food or other nutrient-rich environments may also experience a period before rapid multiplication, followed by constraints associated with changing conditions. The length of that period cannot be inferred from the name of the lag phase alone; it depends on the organisms, their physiological history and the environment. A growth curve obtained under one set of conditions should not be assumed to predict an exact timeline under another. The model explains the principle that adaptation and subsequent multiplication are distinct, while actual predictions require appropriate data.

In the context of industrial microbiology, the physiological state of a culture can influence the kinds and amounts of products accumulated. Some products are closely related to rapid biomass formation, whereas others can be associated with slower growth or differentiation. The present concept is simply that the time course of a product need not be identical to the time course of viable counts. Detailed fermentation operation, feeding strategies, continuous systems and reactor calculations are distinct topics. The study of a batch curve prepares the student to understand why controlling the culture environment can change both population behavior and biological output.

It is also useful to contrast a batch system with the idea of continuous culture at a purely conceptual level. If an environment receives a continuing supply of fresh nutrients and loses spent medium, the conditions need not follow the same sequence of progressive exhaustion as an unreplenished batch. A population could remain in a reproducible physiological state for a longer interval under appropriate conditions. However, a constant population concentration in such a system may result partly from the removal of cells rather than from the balance of division and biological death in a closed culture. The boundary conditions are different, so the explanation of a plateau is different. Quantitative treatment of continuous culture belongs to the bioprocess unit.

One of the most important limitations of the four-phase model is its reliance on a population average. A count or a biomass measurement combines many individuals, whose internal conditions can differ. The average can conceal rare survivors, nondividing cells and cells that undergo different rates of reproduction. The existence of heterogeneity does not invalidate population measurements; it limits the conclusions that can be drawn about individuals. If a question explicitly describes an unusually resistant or slow-recovering fraction, the student should distinguish the behavior of that fraction from the net behavior of the entire culture.

Another limitation is the assumption that measured concentration represents biological changes rather than physical changes. Settling, aggregation, volume differences and methodological detection limits can alter observations. An apparent plateau at the upper limit of an optical instrument may not indicate cessation of growth. A sudden decline in recoverable units may reflect changed culturability or aggregation rather than immediate loss of all cellular function. These limitations are not reasons to abandon growth curves. They are reasons to combine them with appropriate complementary measurements.

A third limitation is the conventional sharpness of phase boundaries. Biological transitions are often distributed across time, and different measures may define their beginnings and ends differently. A period in which biomass still increases while viable counts cease to increase may represent continued synthesis without corresponding numerical growth. A period in which the proportional rate steadily decreases may be classified as deceleration rather than as fully exponential or fully stationary. Recognizing such transitions is more informative than assigning each time point to a rigid category.

Finally, the time course is not a single description of nutritional efficiency. A steep exponential slope concerns a rate of increase, whereas a high stationary biomass level concerns the amount of material accumulated. A culture with a longer lag can eventually attain the same maximum concentration as another, and a culture that grows faster can reach a lower final concentration if the environment limits it sooner. The next two chapters separate these concepts formally: growth kinetics describes proportional increase and generation time, while growth yield relates the amount of new biomass to consumed resources. The distinctions established here make those mathematical concepts biologically meaningful.

A complete explanation of a growth curve begins with the boundary of the batch system and ends with an explicit interpretation of the measured variable. It should identify the physiological processes that can change the slope, acknowledge the possible difference between gross and net change, and distinguish empirical observations from proposed mechanisms. If the student can reconstruct these connections, an unfamiliar curve becomes a problem in cell physiology rather than a test of memorized phase names.

13. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

The following original questions are designed for conceptual practice; they are not previous-year examination questions.

B1. Which statement defines an ordinary batch culture most accurately?

  1. No gas or heat exchange is permitted.
  2. The liquid medium is supplied initially rather than continually replaced.
  3. The population must be at stationary phase.
  4. The vessel always contains only one nutrient.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B specifies the relevant liquid-system boundary. A confuses batch operation with hermetic sealing; C is a phase, not a culture type; D is not required.

B2. A stable direct cell count with rising dry mass immediately after transfer most directly suggests which possibility?

  1. All cells are dead.
  2. Cell material increases before net numerical multiplication.
  3. Every cell divides at a constant rate.
  4. The medium has no nutrients.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B is consistent with biomass accumulation before completed divisions. A and D are not supported by rising biomass; C predicts a numerical increase if losses are negligible.

B3. Which graph shows a straight rising segment during ideal exponential growth?

  1. Arithmetic count versus time.
  2. Log of positive cell concentration versus time.
  3. Cell concentration versus reciprocal time in every culture.
  4. Dry mass per individual versus time in balanced growth.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B transforms proportional increase into a linear relation. A bends upward, C has no general linear relationship, and D is approximately stable in balanced growth.

B4. A stationary viable-count plateau, measured reliably at adequate time resolution, establishes that:

  1. Every cell is metabolically inactive.
  2. No cell can divide.
  3. Net recoverable population change is approximately zero.
  4. The nutrient concentration is exactly zero.
Answer and scientific reasoning

C. C follows from the plateau. A and B concern unmeasured cellular events; D is a possible but not necessary cause, and residual resources may remain.

B5. A steep decline in CFU with stable intact counts is most consistent with:

  1. Loss of recoverability without equivalent disappearance of intact structures.
  2. Immediate lysis of every missing CFU.
  3. An obligatory increase in cell division.
  4. Proof of a heritable resistance mutation.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. A is the warranted population interpretation, subject to counting assumptions. B predicts a much greater loss of intact structures; C and D are not established.

B6. Which condition most strongly supports approximately proportional biomass and numerical growth?

  1. Changing mean cell mass and large death rate.
  2. Balanced growth with stable mean mass and negligible losses.
  3. A long stationary interval with extensive lysis.
  4. Arbitrary optical signal outside calibration.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B is the condition under which cell number and mass can track each other. A and C uncouple them; D cannot supply a reliable conversion.

B7. Assertion: A rapidly growing inoculum transferred to similar fresh medium may show little detectable lag. Reason: Existing transport and biosynthetic systems may already suit the new environment.

  1. Both true, and reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true, but reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. Both are true, and readiness of existing cellular machinery reduces adaptation demands. B ignores the causal relationship; C and D deny valid statements.

B8. Which observation best supports sequential substrate use in a diauxic curve?

  1. Two rising segments alone prove catabolite repression.
  2. A falls in the first rise, B falls in the second, with a pause between.
  3. Both nutrients remain constant and the instrument saturates.
  4. The final count equals the initial count.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B supplies substrate-specific evidence. A overstates a curve shape, C suggests a measurement limitation, D does not discriminate resource use.

B9. The stringent response is most directly connected with:

  1. Nutrient-associated regulation of bacterial resource allocation.
  2. The compulsory lysis of all stationary cells.
  3. The optical scattering of dead cells.
  4. Mitosis in every prokaryote.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. A describes the regulatory role of alarmones in many bacteria. B, C and D are unrelated or false generalizations.

B10. A culture is sampled at the same clock time in two media. Which issue matters most for a comparison of growth-associated protein abundance?

  1. Clock time guarantees identical physiological phase.
  2. Cultures may occupy different growth phases at that time.
  3. Stationary cultures cannot contain protein.
  4. Identical time guarantees identical biomass.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B identifies a physiological confound. A and D do not follow from time alone; C is false.

B11. Which statement about endospore formation is correct?

  1. It is always a doubling event.
  2. It is universal in stationary bacteria.
  3. It can preserve survival without increasing individual number.
  4. It is required for exponential growth.
Answer and scientific reasoning

C. C distinguishes differentiation from reproduction. A, B and D incorrectly generalize a survival strategy found in certain bacteria.

B12. Assertion: A falling optical signal is not required for a falling CFU count. Reason: Non-colony-forming cells or debris can still scatter light.

  1. Both true, and reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true, but reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. Both statements are true and causally related. The other options deny one statement or its explanatory connection.

14. Part C — Graph, experimental and quantitative reasoning

All numerical scenarios in this section are hypothetical. Apply the stated assumptions before drawing a biological conclusion.

C1. A hypothetical culture gives CFU/mL at hourly intervals: 10⁵, 10⁵, 2×10⁵, 4×10⁵, 8×10⁵, 9×10⁵, 9×10⁵. Which interval most clearly exhibits exponential behavior?

  1. Hours 0–1
  2. Hours 1–4
  3. Hours 4–6
  4. The entire interval
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. The ratios for 1–2, 2–3 and 3–4 are all 2, so B is supported. A is near-flat, C decelerates then plateaus, and D combines different states.

C2. During a late interval CFU falls from 4×10⁸ to 4×10⁶ per mL; total count falls from 5×10⁸ to 4×10⁸. Which pair gives the reductions?

  1. CFU 99%; total 20%
  2. CFU 90%; total 80%
  3. CFU 1%; total 20%
  4. Both 99%
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. CFU remaining fraction = 4×10⁶/(4×10⁸) = 0.01, so reduction 99%. Total remaining fraction = 4/5 = 0.8, so reduction 20%. B confuses fractions, C gives the remaining CFU fraction, D ignores the total-count data.

C3. Two stationary samples start at 10⁷ CFU/mL. In A, 2×10⁶ new units are gained and 2×10⁶ lost. In B, each rate is negligible. What follows?

  1. Only A is stationary.
  2. Only B is stationary.
  3. Both can be stationary, but turnover differs.
  4. A must have doubled.
Answer and scientific reasoning

C. Both net changes are approximately zero: A gains and loses equal amounts, B has little turnover. A and B options incorrectly equate stationary with one mechanism; D is inconsistent with the net count.

C4. Log₁₀ CFU/mL falls from 7.5 to 5.5 in four hours. Which statement is correct?

  1. Slope −0.5 log units/h; 1% remains.
  2. Slope −2 log units/h; 50% remains.
  3. Slope +0.5 log units/h; 99% remains.
  4. Slope −0.5 log units/h; 10% remains.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. Change = −2 over 4 h = −0.5 log units/h. Remaining fraction = 10⁻² = 0.01. B uses total change as slope, C reverses the sign and survival, D confuses two logs with one.

C5. A culture's dry mass rises from 0.15 to 0.24 mg/mL while direct count stays 3×10⁸ cells/mL. What is the change in mean dry mass?

  1. 0.50 to 0.80 pg; 60% increase
  2. 0.50 to 0.80 pg; 30% increase
  3. 5 to 8 pg; 60% increase
  4. No change because count is stable
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. 0.15 mg = 1.5×10⁻⁴ g; divided by 3×10⁸ gives 5×10⁻¹³ g = 0.50 pg. Later 0.80 pg, increase (0.80−0.50)/0.50 = 60%. B confuses an absolute increment with the percentage increase, C has a factor-of-ten error, D confuses biomass with count.

C6. Four matched stationary samples are compared: neither correction gives no growth; carbon restoration alone gives no growth; pH correction alone gives no growth; both together give growth. Best interpretation?

  1. Carbon was certainly the only constraint.
  2. pH was certainly the only constraint.
  3. Both constraints may jointly restrict growth.
  4. All cells were irreversibly dead.
Answer and scientific reasoning

C. C is supported by the conditional combined rescue. A and B ignore the failure of single interventions; D contradicts renewed growth. The result does not establish that no other factor contributes.

C7. Two cultures have identical exponential slopes but one begins its rise three hours later, from the same starting CFU. Which inference is best?

  1. The delayed culture necessarily has a longer exponential doubling time.
  2. The curves support a longer apparent lag with comparable subsequent net growth rate.
  3. The later curve has greater yield by definition.
  4. The earlier culture must have a different genome.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. Identical semilog slopes imply comparable net proportional growth during that interval, while onset differs. A confuses lag with doubling time, C requires final biomass or count data, D is not established.

C8. A two-substrate batch grows from 2×10⁶ to 1.6×10⁷ CFU/mL, pauses, then reaches 6.4×10⁷. How many effective population doublings occurred across the two rises?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 5
  4. 12
Answer and scientific reasoning

C. First rise = 8-fold = 3 doublings; second = 4-fold = 2 doublings; total = 32-fold = 5. A and B count only one interval, D adds fold changes rather than multiplying them.

C9. A culture plateaus in optical density but direct counts keep rising. Which follow-up is most informative?

  1. Assume every cell has stopped growing.
  2. Check the optical calibration and mean cell size or aggregation.
  3. Conclude the batch has no remaining nutrients.
  4. Reject all direct counts without evidence.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B tests whether optical response is nonlinear or average scattering properties have changed. A conflicts with the direct counts under stable counting conditions, C is not measured, D is unjustified.

C10. Fresh growing cells fail to proliferate in spent medium, while old stationary cells resume growth after transfer into fresh medium. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. Extracellular conditions contributed; the specific factor remains unresolved.
  2. The old cells were all dead.
  3. Carbon depletion alone has been proven.
  4. There was no physiological history effect.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. A follows from the reciprocal comparison. B contradicts recovery; C overlooks multiple simultaneous changes; D cannot be concluded because recovery lag and other cell-history differences may remain.

C11. In equal culture volumes, two populations consume oxygen at the same rate. A has 2×10⁸ CFU/mL, B has 4×10⁸ CFU/mL. Assuming CFU represent individual contributing cells, how do mean rates per cell compare?

  1. A is twice B.
  2. B is twice A.
  3. Both necessarily equal.
  4. A is four times B.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. Mean rate equals volume-normalized rate divided by cell concentration. Equal numerator and half the denominator give twice the rate for A. B reverses the relationship, C confuses volume and cell normalization, D introduces an unsupported factor.

C12. A late culture goes from 10⁶ to 10⁵ to 10⁴ CFU/mL in equal intervals, then stays near 10⁴ for several intervals. What is supported?

  1. A constant proportional decline across the entire observation.
  2. Two successive intervals of 90% proportional loss followed by a tail; cause needs further evidence.
  3. A proven resistance mutation in every survivor.
  4. All structures must have lysed.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. The early ratios are 0.1, indicating 90% loss per interval. Later ratios are near 1, so a single rate no longer describes the whole record. A ignores the tail; C and D overinterpret population data.

C13. Two tubes have the same initial viable population and proportional growth rate. X contains more of an essential nutrient that is not initially rate limiting, and other conditions remain comparable. Which pattern is most plausible if that nutrient later limits both?

  1. X necessarily has a steeper early slope.
  2. X may have a similar early slope but a longer rise and higher final count.
  3. X must have a longer lag.
  4. X must have a lower final count.
Answer and scientific reasoning

B. B follows from initially sufficient supply in both and later exhaustion at different times. A wrongly assumes more nutrient always increases rate, C lacks evidence about adaptation, D reverses the plausible resource effect.

C14. A vessel evaporates from 100 to 80 mL. The total recoverable population remains 8×10⁸. Which apparent concentration change occurs?

  1. 8×10⁶ to 10⁷ CFU/mL without population multiplication.
  2. 8×10⁶ to 6.4×10⁶ CFU/mL because cells die.
  3. 10⁷ to 8×10⁶ CFU/mL.
  4. Concentration remains constant.
Answer and scientific reasoning

A. Initially 8×10⁸/100 = 8×10⁶; finally 8×10⁸/80 = 10⁷. A is physical concentration, not cell growth. B and C reverse the volume effect; D ignores the changing denominator.
