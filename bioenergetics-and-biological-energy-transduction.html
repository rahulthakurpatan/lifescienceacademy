1. Why cells need energy conversion

A seed begins to germinate, a bacterium swims towards nutrients and a muscle fibre contracts. These events seem unrelated until we ask what keeps each process moving in one direction. A cell builds ordered molecules from smaller units, concentrates ions against their natural tendency to disperse and generates movement against resistance. None of these activities can continue merely because enzymes are present. They require a source of free energy and machinery that connects its release to useful work.

Consider glucose and oxygen. Their conversion to carbon dioxide and water is strongly favourable, yet a cell does not simply burn glucose in a single violent reaction. Instead, a sequence of enzyme-catalysed transformations separates energy release into manageable steps. Some steps produce activated chemical intermediates, some transfer electrons to coenzymes, and others ultimately establish an electrochemical difference across a membrane. ATP can be regenerated using these intermediates or gradients and then spent elsewhere. Bioenergetics asks how much work such changes can support, how the transfer occurs and why the process has a direction.

Metabolism can be viewed as a network rather than two independent lists of reactions. Catabolism breaks down fuels and often produces ATP and reduced electron carriers; anabolism consumes activated precursors and usually requires ATP and reducing power. The same cell may be simultaneously oxidising one molecule and reducing another. The intermediates joining the processes are shared, but their pools are maintained by different reactions. This distinction explains why a supply of ATP cannot always substitute for a lack of NADPH, and why providing fuel cannot restore growth if the cell has lost the machinery that converts its energy.

A living cell is an open system. Matter and energy enter and leave. Its local molecular order does not contradict the second law: the total entropy change of the system and surroundings is positive for a spontaneous process. At steady state, concentrations may remain approximately constant even while large amounts of material flow through the system. At equilibrium there is no net reaction flux. Living cells normally maintain many processes far from equilibrium by continuously supplying substrates and removing products. If ATP production stops while consumption continues, the chemical driving forces gradually disappear.

The thermodynamic rules studied in 1.A.05 and enzyme mechanisms developed in 1.D.05 now come together. An enzyme provides a reaction pathway; a free-energy difference provides direction and potential work. In this chapter we first learn how to calculate that difference, then discover why ATP and other activated carriers can transmit it, and finally examine how chemical, electrical and mechanical forms of energy are interconverted.

2. Free energy under standard and cellular conditions

At constant temperature and pressure, Gibbs free energy combines the enthalpy and entropy changes of a process: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Here ΔH and ΔG are commonly expressed in kJ mol⁻¹, T in kelvin and ΔS in kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A negative ΔG indicates that the forward direction is thermodynamically favourable; a positive value favours the reverse direction, and zero indicates equilibrium. It does not give a reaction rate. A favourable reaction may remain extremely slow without a suitable catalytic pathway, as the relative stability of an ATP solution illustrates.

For a reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the free energy of each species depends on its activity, an effective concentration relative to its standard state. Its chemical potential is μ = μ° + RT ln a. Multiply the chemical potential of each product by its stoichiometric coefficient, subtract the corresponding reactant terms, and collect the logarithms. The result is ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, where Q = a(C)^c a(D)^d / [a(A)^a a(B)^b]. Activities and Q are dimensionless. In dilute ideal solutions we may replace a solute activity by its molar concentration divided by 1 M. Pure liquid water is usually assigned activity one. This approximation becomes less reliable in concentrated or highly ionic solutions.

Biochemists often work with transformed standard free energies, written ΔG°′, conventionally at pH 7. Hydrogen ions exchanged with the buffer are not explicitly included in the corresponding transformed quotient Q′. Temperature, ionic strength and magnesium conditions still matter, and reference tables do not necessarily use identical conventions. For a problem, use the conditions and constants given. Do not multiply by a proton concentration again when using a properly transformed expression; that would count the pH effect twice. The ordinary chemical standard state and a living cell are not equivalent.

At equilibrium, Q′ = K′ and ΔG′ = 0. Thus ΔG°′ = −RT ln K′. Substitution gives ΔG′ = RT ln(Q′/K′). The forward direction is favourable whenever Q′ is below K′, regardless of whether the standard free energy is positive or negative. For example, a biochemical isomerisation with ΔG°′ = +7.5 kJ mol⁻¹ has K′ ≈ 0.0486 at 298 K, using R = 0.008314 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. If product-to-substrate ratio is 0.010, then ΔG′ = 7.5 + 2.478 ln(0.010) = −3.91 kJ mol⁻¹. Continuous product removal can therefore sustain the forward conversion without any direct ATP consumption.

A positive standard value is not a declaration that a reaction cannot occur inside a cell. Conversely, a highly negative standard value cannot guarantee a negative cellular value at every concentration ratio. A useful exam habit is to identify which quantities belong to reference conditions and which describe the actual mixture. Compute Q from the balanced equation before deciding direction. If a reaction is reversed, reverse the sign of its ΔG; if it is doubled, double its ΔG and square its equilibrium constant. These rules follow from the fact that free energy depends on the initial and final states and on the amount transformed.

Cells can keep a near-equilibrium reaction moving by supplying its substrate and withdrawing its product. A strongly displaced reaction is often a regulatory point, but thermodynamic displacement is not the same as control of pathway flux. The rate also depends on enzyme activity and substrate access. This is why a change in a metabolite concentration can affect both the equilibrium driving force and the kinetics, and the two effects must be considered separately.

3. ATP: a recyclable chemical carrier

ATP is adenosine 5′-triphosphate, consisting of adenine, ribose and three phosphoryl groups. The phosphate closest to ribose is α, followed by β and γ. The ribose–α linkage is a phosphoester; the α–β and β–γ linkages are phosphoanhydrides. In aqueous cells ATP exists as several protonation and metal-bound species; magnesium-bound ATP is the substrate for many kinases and ATPases. The negative charges of ATP help explain both its water solubility and its interactions with positively charged amino acid side chains and Mg²⁺ in an active site.

The common ATP hydrolysis reaction, written in a biochemical shorthand, is ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ. Pᵢ denotes inorganic orthophosphate, not a free phosphoryl radical. The frequently used standard transformed free energy is approximately −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹. Hydrolysis is favourable because the complete products plus solvent have lower Gibbs free energy than the complete reactants plus solvent. Breaking a covalent bond by itself requires energy; energy is not released simply because a particular bond snaps. The favourable overall change reflects the bonds and interactions formed after hydrolysis, changes in charge repulsion, hydration, resonance stabilisation and entropy.

In ATP the adjacent phosphoryl groups carry negative charge, and their proximity creates electrostatic interactions. Separating the terminal phosphoryl group reduces some of this repulsion. Inorganic phosphate has several resonance contributors, and hydrolysis changes the distribution of electronic charge. ADP and phosphate interact with the surrounding water in ways different from ATP. The number and distribution of solute species also change. These contributions are not fixed independent energy packets; they depend on solvent, protonation and magnesium binding. Consequently it is safer to interpret a measured free energy as a property of the whole reaction rather than to assign exact portions to each structural feature.

Why, then, is ATP not instantly hydrolysed by water? Although the products are favoured, the uncatalysed pathway has an activation barrier. Enzymes lower that barrier by positioning the attacking nucleophile, stabilising developing charge and arranging proton transfers. A kinase positions an acceptor group instead of water, while an ATPase promotes hydrolysis. The selectivity lets the cell preserve a useful ATP pool without indiscriminately phosphorylating every hydroxyl group in its environment.

ATP can also be cleaved to AMP and pyrophosphate (PPᵢ) by reactions involving the α phosphorus. This is not the same chemistry as removing γ phosphate. PPᵢ can subsequently be hydrolysed to two Pᵢ by inorganic pyrophosphatase. ATP → AMP + PPᵢ and PPᵢ → 2Pᵢ together create a larger free-energy decrease under many cellular conditions than the ordinary ADP route. ATP is regenerated from AMP via ADP or related phosphoryl transfers, so ATP-to-AMP reactions with PPᵢ hydrolysis are usually counted as consuming two ATP equivalents, although only one ATP molecule initially enters the reaction.

ATP is a short-term working carrier, not a long-term caloric store. A cell does not have to accumulate enormous amounts of ATP before it can function; it recycles ADP continually. Glycogen and lipids are better suited to storing large amounts of fuel, whereas ATP links energy production and consumption at compatible enzyme sites. GTP, UTP and CTP are chemically related carriers with specialised biological roles: GTP in translation and signalling, UTP in activated sugar formation and CTP in particular lipid biosynthetic reactions. Their uses depend largely on enzyme recognition rather than a fundamental difference in the chemistry of terminal phosphoryl transfer.

4. Calculating the cellular phosphorylation potential

The direction of ATP hydrolysis depends strongly on the ATP, ADP and inorganic phosphate activities. For the transformed hydrolysis reaction, Q′ = a(ADP)a(Pᵢ)/a(ATP). Therefore ΔG′hyd = ΔG°′hyd + RT ln Q′. The free energy required to synthesise ATP from ADP and phosphate under precisely those conditions is −ΔG′hyd. The magnitude of this positive synthesis cost is often called the phosphorylation potential (some texts instead use the term for the ratio ATP/(ADP·Pᵢ); check the stated convention). In a living cell it can be considerably larger than 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹.

Suppose ATP = 5 mM, ADP = 0.5 mM and Pᵢ = 5 mM at 310 K. Using ideal activities, the quotient is (0.0005 × 0.005)/0.005 = 0.0005. R T = 2.577 kJ mol⁻¹. Thus ΔG′hyd ≈ −30.5 + 2.577 ln(0.0005) = −50.1 kJ mol⁻¹. In this calculation we have approximated the reference value as temperature independent across the range and neglected non-ideal effects. Plugging millimolar numbers directly into the logarithm would instead give 0.5 and the wrong answer, because the reaction has a net increase in the number of solute species. Convert to molar concentrations or explicitly use the 1 M reference.

Now reduce ATP to 2 mM, increase ADP to 2 mM and leave phosphate unchanged. Q′ becomes 0.005. The calculated ΔG′hyd is approximately −44.2 kJ mol⁻¹. The reaction is still favourable, but each mole hydrolysed can drive less work. ATP concentration and ATP free energy are different measurements. Two cells with equal ATP concentrations may have very different phosphorylation potentials if their ADP or phosphate levels differ. Nor can the ATP/ADP ratio alone determine ΔG if phosphate differs.

If one mole of ATP hydrolysis supplies 50 kJ of available free energy, a reversible ideal device could perform at most 50 kJ of non-expansion work per mole. Real biological processes operate at finite rates and dissipate some free energy. A process requiring 60 kJ mol⁻¹ cannot be driven by one ATP at that particular phosphorylation potential unless other favourable steps contribute. Two ATP molecules might provide enough thermodynamic driving force, but only if the molecular machinery actually couples both hydrolyses to the desired transformation. Thermodynamic sufficiency does not establish a mechanism.

A cell is constantly adjusting its ATP pool. Adenylate kinase catalyses 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP. When ATP consumption raises ADP, this reaction can regenerate some ATP while increasing AMP. It redistributes existing phosphoryl groups rather than creating energy. A useful index of the adenine nucleotide pool is the adenylate energy charge, EC = ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP]+[ADP]+[AMP]). The limiting values are zero for all AMP, one-half for all ADP and one for all ATP. With 5, 1 and 0.5 mM respectively, EC = 5.5/6.5 = 0.846. After redistribution to 3, 2, 1.5 mM, EC is 4/6.5 = 0.615. The index is convenient for describing nucleotide distribution, but does not include phosphate and is not a free energy in kJ mol⁻¹. It cannot replace the reaction quotient in thermodynamic calculations.

5. Coupled reactions: why adding equations is not enough

A cell cannot change the free energy of a specific chemical reaction just by adding an enzyme. It can, however, replace an unfavourable net transformation by a different overall reaction that includes a favourable process. Suppose A → B has ΔG°′ = +18 kJ mol⁻¹ and ATP hydrolysis has ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹. When the two processes are coupled in a 1:1 stoichiometry, their combined standard transformed free energy is −12.5 kJ mol⁻¹. This follows from state-function additivity, not from a special energy substance flowing between separate molecules. At actual cellular concentrations the two actual ΔG values must be added instead.

Imagine mixing A and ATP with two separate enzymes, one catalysing A ⇌ B and one hydrolysing ATP to ADP and phosphate. ATP hydrolysis may proceed rapidly, but unless it changes the concentrations relevant to A ⇌ B or is mechanistically linked to it, B will still approach its own equilibrium. The heat generated by uncoupled hydrolysis is not equivalent to a molecular pathway forcing A into B. Effective coupling typically uses a shared intermediate, a conformational transition, a mechanically linked motor or a common electrochemical gradient.

The hexokinase reaction is a clear example. Glucose + Pᵢ → glucose 6-phosphate + H₂O has ΔG°′ ≈ +13.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Write ATP hydrolysis as ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ, with ΔG°′ ≈ −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹. On adding the equations, water and inorganic phosphate cancel, leaving glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP and ΔG°′ ≈ −16.7 kJ mol⁻¹. The equation is a thermodynamic cycle, not a literal claim that hexokinase first releases free phosphate. Its active site binds glucose and MgATP, rearranges around them and facilitates transfer of the terminal phosphoryl group directly to the hydroxyl on carbon 6. This makes the coupled pathway kinetically accessible while limiting wasteful ATP hydrolysis.

We can express the same result through equilibrium constants. At 298 K, K′ for glucose phosphorylation by inorganic phosphate is exp(−13.8/2.478) ≈ 0.0038; for ATP hydrolysis it is exp(30.5/2.478) ≈ 2.2 × 10⁵. Their product is approximately 850. Alternatively, exp(16.7/2.478) gives the same result apart from rounding. The overall reaction strongly favours products under standard transformed conditions. This is why adding free energies corresponds to multiplying equilibrium constants, a useful shortcut in numerical questions.

A different example shows the importance of concentration. Suppose the actual free energy of an endergonic biosynthetic step is +42 kJ mol⁻¹ and ATP hydrolysis is −50 kJ mol⁻¹. The overall value is −8 kJ mol⁻¹, but if ATP depletion changes hydrolysis to −38 kJ mol⁻¹, the combined value becomes +4 kJ mol⁻¹. The same molecular mechanism can lose forward driving force without a change in enzyme abundance. A process can be approximately reversible near the point at which the combined free energy reaches zero.

Coupling has a stoichiometric cost. If a transformation requires +76 kJ mol⁻¹, hydrolysis at −50 kJ mol⁻¹ cannot support it in a 1:1 scheme. A 2:1 scheme gives −24 kJ mol⁻¹ before other contributions. An exam question may ask the minimum integer number of ATP molecules: divide the positive free energy by the magnitude supplied per ATP, round upward and check whether the net value is strictly negative if spontaneous forward progress is required. At an exact zero there is no net driving force. This calculation yields a thermodynamic lower bound, not necessarily the number used by the actual enzyme.

6. Three modes of group transfer and chemical activation

The phosphoryl groups of ATP can be transferred in different ways depending on which phosphorus is attacked. In terminal phosphoryl transfer, a substrate hydroxyl or another nucleophile attacks γ phosphorus and ADP leaves. Hexokinase and protein kinases follow this general pattern. In a nucleotide polymerisation reaction the growing chain hydroxyl attacks α phosphorus of an incoming nucleoside triphosphate, incorporating its nucleoside monophosphate residue while PPᵢ leaves. In adenylyl transfer an acceptor such as a carboxylate attacks α phosphorus of ATP, forming an AMP-linked intermediate. Transfer of a pyrophosphoryl group is another possibility, for example in formation of phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) from ribose 5-phosphate and ATP, which yields AMP.

Why make an intermediate rather than simply combine two molecules? Consider formation of glutamine from glutamate and ammonia. Direct formation of an amide from an aqueous carboxylate is energetically and kinetically difficult. Glutamine synthetase uses ATP to phosphorylate the side-chain carboxylate of glutamate, generating an enzyme-bound γ-glutamyl phosphate and ADP. Ammonia attacks the activated carbonyl and phosphate leaves, yielding glutamine. The transient acyl phosphate changes the chemical leaving group and makes the nucleophilic substitution practicable. The overall reaction consumes ATP, but no indiscriminate burst of heat is needed to make the nitrogen–carbon bond.

The formation of an aminoacyl-tRNA provides a complementary example. An amino acid carboxylate attacks the α phosphorus of ATP, producing aminoacyl-AMP and PPᵢ within the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. A hydroxyl on the tRNA terminal adenosine then attacks the activated carbonyl, producing aminoacyl-tRNA and AMP. The ester linking the amino acid to tRNA subsequently helps drive peptide-bond formation. Energy invested in activation can therefore be used at a later reaction site. This example is about chemical coupling; the fidelity and full mechanics of translation belong to Unit 3.

Why does hydrolysis of PPᵢ strengthen such biosynthesis? If PPᵢ is removed, the quotient for the activation reaction decreases. This favours further adenylate formation and reduces the tendency toward reversal. For illustration, standard transformed values of −45.6 kJ mol⁻¹ for ATP → AMP + PPᵢ and −19.2 kJ mol⁻¹ for PPᵢ hydrolysis yield approximately −64.8 kJ mol⁻¹ for ATP → AMP + 2Pᵢ. These are conventional approximate values, not universal cellular constants. One should add values tabulated under consistent conditions and include any other reactants and products in the actual coupling.

Not every group transfer requires ATP in the same step. Enzymes can use activated sugar nucleotides, acyl-CoA molecules or previously formed phosphorylated substrates. Such reactions often appear to proceed without ATP because the energetic investment was made upstream. Follow the atoms and groups through the pathway: if a new bond is formed while a suitable leaving group is generated, the energy accounting must include the preceding activation. This reasoning is more reliable than classifying every ATP-dependent reaction as a simple ATPase.

7. Phosphoryl-group transfer potential and the hierarchy of donors

The ability of one compound to transfer a phosphoryl group to another can be compared through their standard free energies of hydrolysis. Let a donor D–P hydrolyse to D + Pᵢ with ΔG°′D. Let ATP hydrolysis have ΔG°′ATP. Reverse the second reaction and add it to the first. The net transfer D–P + ADP → D + ATP has ΔG°′ = ΔG°′D − ΔG°′ATP. If the donor hydrolyses with a more negative standard free energy than ATP, the transfer to ADP is favourable under standard conditions. In the cell, the activities of donor, acceptor and products still determine the actual direction.

Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) is an enol phosphate with a particularly high phosphoryl transfer potential, approximately −61.9 kJ mol⁻¹ for hydrolysis. Removing the phosphoryl group produces enolpyruvate, which converts to the more stable keto form of pyruvate. This additional product stabilisation accounts for much of the strong overall driving force. In pyruvate kinase, the transferred group goes to ADP rather than directly to water. The standard transformed free energy for PEP + ADP → pyruvate + ATP is therefore −61.9 + 30.5 = −31.4 kJ mol⁻¹. The word kinase does not mean an enzyme must always consume ATP; here the kinase forms ATP.

1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG) contains an acyl phosphate at carbon 1 and a phosphoester at carbon 3. Hydrolysis of the acyl phosphate yields 3-phosphoglycerate, whose carboxylate is strongly stabilised by resonance. The hydrolysis value is around −49.3 kJ mol⁻¹. Transfer to ADP yields a standard change of −18.8 kJ mol⁻¹. The carbon-3 phosphate remains attached to the product; the prefix bisphospho indicates separate phosphate groups, not a pyrophosphate linkage. Knowing this structure prevents incorrect phosphate counts in glycolysis.

Phosphocreatine has a phosphagen role in cells with rapidly changing ATP demand, especially vertebrate muscle. Its phosphoryl group can be transferred to ADP by creatine kinase. With a standard hydrolysis value of about −43.1 kJ mol⁻¹, the forward transfer has ΔG°′ ≈ −12.6 kJ mol⁻¹. In resting muscle, when ATP is plentiful and creatine accumulates, the same enzyme can transfer phosphate from ATP back to creatine. During contraction the increased ADP favours ATP regeneration. The phosphocreatine pool buffers short bursts of demand; it is replenished by energy metabolism and cannot sustain contraction indefinitely without fuel oxidation.

ATP occupies an intermediate position in this hierarchy. It accepts groups from donors such as PEP and phosphocreatine and transfers them to acceptors such as glucose. A simple sugar phosphate such as glucose 6-phosphate has a much less negative hydrolysis free energy (approximately −13.8 kJ mol⁻¹); it cannot normally make ATP by direct standard-state transfer to ADP. But it can enter reactions that rearrange or oxidise its carbon skeleton to produce higher-potential intermediates. This is the chemical strategy of glycolysis: a low-transfer-potential phosphate ester is converted through a series of reactions into intermediates capable of supporting ATP formation.

Do not assume that any compound with two or three phosphate groups has a higher transfer potential than ATP. The structural context and stability of the hydrolysis products matter. For instance, a phosphoester in a nucleic acid backbone and an acyl phosphate in a metabolic intermediate differ in reaction energetics and in accessible pathways. Nor is a high standard transfer potential enough to predict whether a transfer occurs quickly: without an enzyme that accepts both partners, the chemically favourable route may be kinetically negligible.

8. Thioesters and activated acyl groups

Phosphoryl transfer is only one way to link fuel metabolism to biosynthesis. Carbon-containing groups can also be activated by attaching them to a carrier. Coenzyme A contains a terminal thiol group, written CoA–SH. When an acyl group is attached, the linkage is R–C(=O)–S–CoA, a thioester. Acetyl-CoA carries a two-carbon acetyl group; succinyl-CoA carries a four-carbon succinyl group. The identity of the acyl group determines which pathways can use it, while the common CoA portion allows enzymes to recognise the carrier.

An oxygen ester has resonance interaction between the oxygen lone pair and the adjacent carbonyl system. A thioester generally has weaker effective resonance donation from sulfur, in part because of less effective orbital overlap. The acyl group therefore has useful transfer potential; hydrolysis yields a resonance-stabilised carboxylate and free CoA. For acetyl-CoA the standard transformed hydrolysis free energy is approximately −31.5 kJ mol⁻¹, similar in magnitude to ATP hydrolysis. This numerical resemblance does not make acetyl-CoA a direct phosphoryl donor. It transfers an acyl group rather than a phosphate, and its chemistry supports distinct classes of reactions.

In citrate synthase, the carbon atoms of acetyl-CoA combine with oxaloacetate to form a six-carbon intermediate that is initially linked to CoA. Hydrolysis of the citryl-CoA thioester contributes strongly to the favourable net citrate formation. The enzyme controls the order of binding, condensation and thioester hydrolysis, so acetyl-CoA is not simply wasted as free acetate. This introduces a recurring principle: invest free energy in a chemically activated carrier, then make its release coincide with formation of a useful bond.

Succinyl-CoA synthetase illustrates how a thioester can indirectly support nucleoside triphosphate formation. In the citric acid cycle, inorganic phosphate participates in formation of an activated succinyl-phosphate intermediate, followed by transfer through a phosphorylated enzyme histidine to GDP or ADP. The result is succinate, free CoA and GTP or ATP, depending on the enzyme form. The incoming phosphoryl group originates from inorganic phosphate, not from a phosphate group on the succinyl acyl linkage. Energy from thioester conversion is linked to phosphate activation and subsequent transfer. This distinguishes it from pyruvate kinase, which already has a phosphorylated donor.

Acetyl-CoA itself can be formed by oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate, by fatty-acid oxidation, or by ATP-dependent activation of acetate. In each case the molecule entering subsequent pathways may look identical, but the energetic accounting of how it was produced is different. For an acyl-CoA synthetase reaction that makes AMP and PPᵢ, count the upstream activation cost when comparing the net energetic yield of a fuel. CoA is regenerated after acyl transfer; it is not normally consumed as a permanent product of every reaction. This recycling connects the availability of free CoA to pathway flux.

9. Electron transfer: converting chemical oxidation into usable free energy

Oxidation means loss of electrons and reduction means gain of electrons. These processes are coupled because electrons lost by a donor must be accepted by another species. In organic metabolism, oxidation is often recognised as loss of hydrogen, gain of oxygen or an increase in the number of bonds from carbon to electronegative atoms, but oxidation need not directly involve oxygen. In a dehydrogenase reaction, a fuel can lose hydrogen equivalents while a coenzyme gains electrons. This is how oxidation can be separated from the eventual reduction of oxygen.

A reduction half-reaction is commonly written Ox + n e⁻ ⇌ Red, with appropriate protons or other species. A more positive reduction potential corresponds, under the stated conditions, to a stronger tendency to accept electrons. A reduced carrier with a more negative potential can donate electrons to an oxidised carrier at a more positive potential. To compute the cell potential from tabulated reduction potentials, subtract the potential of the donor couple from that of the acceptor couple: ΔE°′ = E°′acceptor − E°′donor. Do not reverse the sign of the donor and then subtract it again.

The maximum electrical work available when n moles of electrons move across a potential difference ΔE is nFΔE per mole of reaction, with F ≈ 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹ of electrons. The free energy change for the coupled redox process is therefore ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′. If ΔE is positive, ΔG is negative. Potentials are intensive quantities: balancing electrons by multiplying a half-reaction does not multiply its voltage. The electron count n in the combined reaction determines the energy. When several redox reactions are added, add the free-energy changes or electron-balanced reactions, not unweighted potentials indiscriminately.

Consider reduction of oxygen by NADH. For the NAD⁺/NADH couple E°′ ≈ −0.320 V, and for O₂/H₂O E°′ ≈ +0.815 V. Per NADH, two electrons are transferred to one-half O₂. ΔE°′ ≈ 1.135 V, giving ΔG°′ ≈ −2 × 96.485 × 1.135 = −219 kJ mol⁻¹. The negative value is much larger in magnitude than ATP hydrolysis. Rather than converting all of it into heat, the respiratory system couples selected electron transfers to proton movement. The resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis. Calculating 219/30.5 ≈ 7.2 does not predict the biological ATP yield: actual ATP synthesis is more costly than the standard value, and respiratory stoichiometry and energy losses impose additional limits.

Actual reduction potentials depend on the oxidised/reduced ratio. For a simplified transformed half-reaction with fixed pH, E′ = E°′ + (RT/nF) ln(a(Ox)/a(Red)), assuming unit stoichiometric coefficients and other fixed activities. Increasing the oxidised-to-reduced ratio raises the potential and improves the tendency of the couple to accept electrons. For NAD⁺/NADH, a ratio of 100 rather than 1 at 298 K raises the reduction potential by (0.02569/2) ln 100 = 0.0592 V. The same pool can therefore serve as a stronger or weaker oxidant depending on its redox state. For full reactions with other varying species, construct the complete quotient and use the appropriate Nernst equation.

A reaction can move electrons unfavourably if another process supplies enough free energy. Photosynthesis uses light to generate excited states capable of electron transfer that would not occur with the corresponding ground-state molecules. Some microorganisms and mitochondria can also use membrane gradients to drive electron transfers in otherwise unfavourable directions. The general lesson is identical to ATP coupling: identify the complete linked process and calculate its net driving force rather than declaring the uphill component impossible.

10. NAD and NADP: similar molecules, distinct metabolic jobs

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) contains two nucleotides linked through their phosphate groups. One contains adenine, the other nicotinamide, a derivative of niacin (vitamin B₃). The nicotinamide ring is the redox-active part. NAD⁺ represents the oxidised form. A typical dehydrogenase transfers a hydride, H⁻, containing two electrons and one proton, to carbon 4 of the nicotinamide ring, yielding NADH. A second proton from the substrate may be released into solution. The conventional shorthand for oxidation of a substrate SH₂ is SH₂ + NAD⁺ ⇌ S + NADH + H⁺. It describes the net transformation; the actual proton balance varies with substrate ionisation.

Because hydride transfer moves two electrons together, NAD is normally a two-electron carrier in standard metabolic dehydrogenase reactions. It is not generally a stable one-electron shuttle. Enzymes bind the nicotinamide ring in a defined orientation so that hydride removal or delivery occurs at a particular face. This stereochemical specificity is one reason why not every enzyme that binds a nucleotide will catalyse a nicotinamide redox reaction.

NADP differs from NAD by an extra phosphate ester on the 2′ hydroxyl of the adenosine ribose. The phosphate is remote from the reactive nicotinamide carbon and is not the group transferred in the redox reaction. The structural change gives enzymes a recognition handle, allowing separate coenzyme pools. NADP⁺ accepts hydride to become NADPH by the same broad chemistry. Their standard reduction potentials are similar, but cellular oxidised/reduced ratios and enzyme specificities are often very different.

In many cells the NAD pool is kept relatively oxidised to favour fuel oxidation, whereas the NADP pool is relatively reduced to support biosynthetic reactions and antioxidant defence. NADH generated in glycolysis or the TCA cycle can supply electrons to pathways that regenerate ATP; NADPH supplies reducing equivalents for fatty-acid synthesis and maintenance of reduced glutathione, among other reactions. These are dominant roles, not absolute rules. Specific enzymes can use NADPH for catabolic redox chemistry or NADH for reductive transformations, and transhydrogenases can connect the pools. Never infer coenzyme specificity solely from whether a substrate is oxidised or reduced.

NADH also provides a practical window into enzyme function. The reduced nicotinamide ring absorbs strongly at 340 nm, while NAD⁺ has very little absorption there. For a suitably corrected solution, absorbance A = εlc, where ε for NADH at 340 nm is about 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l is optical path length in cm and c is concentration in mM. If absorbance increases from 0.10 to 0.35 in a 1 cm cuvette during a reaction, NADH has increased by 0.25/6.22 ≈ 0.0402 mM. In a 2 mL reaction, that corresponds to 0.0804 μmol. If the change occurred over two minutes in the linear interval, the rate is 0.0402 μmol min⁻¹. This requires appropriate blank subtraction, correct extinction coefficient and no other significant absorbance changes. NADPH absorbs similarly, so the wavelength alone cannot distinguish NADH from NADPH.

An organism cannot keep oxidising a substrate indefinitely if its NAD⁺ supply is not regenerated. The total pool of NAD species is finite over short times. When reduced carrier accumulates, the oxidised form becomes limiting and the thermodynamic and kinetic conditions for further oxidation deteriorate. In aerobic respiration, electrons are ultimately passed to oxygen; during fermentation, internal organic acceptors can restore NAD⁺. The reduced carrier is a temporary link between the two reactions, not the final destination of electrons. This regeneration problem will become central in the next chapter.

11. Flavins and other electron carriers

Flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) are derived from riboflavin (vitamin B₂). The redox-active part is the isoalloxazine ring. Flavins can accept two hydrogen equivalents to reach their fully reduced forms, FMNH₂ or FADH₂, and can also form one-electron semiquinone intermediates. Protein environment influences the relative stability of these states. This flexibility allows flavoproteins to link two-electron substrate oxidation to a sequence of one-electron transfers, a task for which nicotinamide coenzymes are generally less suited.

A flavin is often tightly associated with a particular enzyme, sometimes even covalently attached, unlike the more freely exchanging NAD coenzyme of many dehydrogenases. Succinate dehydrogenase, for example, oxidises succinate to fumarate while reducing its bound FAD. Electrons are subsequently transferred through iron–sulfur centres to ubiquinone. It is misleading to imagine the FADH₂ as a freely diffusing molecule travelling from the TCA cycle into an unrelated respiratory complex. In this case the same enzyme connects both metabolic and respiratory functions.

Why use FAD instead of NAD⁺ in such a reaction? The answer is not simply that FAD has more energy. Oxidation of succinate to fumarate is difficult to couple to reduction of NAD⁺ under standard conditions, given the relatively positive fumarate/succinate reduction potential. A flavin in a suitable protein environment has redox and chemical properties adapted to the oxidation, including formation of a carbon–carbon double bond. The protein tunes the flavin's effective potential and links it to subsequent electron acceptors. Unlike free NAD couples, enzyme-bound flavins do not have one universal biological reduction potential.

Other carriers specialise further. Ubiquinone (coenzyme Q) is lipid soluble, diffuses within membranes and can carry two electrons and two protons in its fully reduced ubiquinol form. Its semiquinone state allows exchange of single electrons. Iron–sulfur proteins transfer electrons by changes in the oxidation states of their iron centres; cytochromes commonly use reversible Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ transitions in haem to carry one electron at a time. Their electron transfer need not be accompanied by proton binding to the metal centre. Because proteins tune their redox potentials, an ordered chain of these components can conduct electrons through a series of energetically useful stages.

A comparison of ATP and reduced coenzymes makes their different roles clear. ATP usually transfers phosphoryl or adenylyl groups or powers changes in protein conformation. NADH and NADPH transfer reducing equivalents, FAD can mediate one- or two-electron chemistry, and CoA transfers acyl groups. None of these carriers is a generic packet of identical energy. Their chemical functionality, localisation, enzyme partners and free-energy differences determine what they can do. For an unfamiliar reaction, identify whether it changes oxidation state, transfers a group or moves a molecule across a barrier before selecting its likely carrier.

A useful experimental prediction follows from these distinctions. Blocking a downstream electron acceptor can cause a reduced carrier to accumulate, even when fuel oxidation is thermodynamically favourable as a complete reaction. The initial electron donation may continue briefly, but once oxidised carrier is scarce, upstream oxidation slows. Adding ATP alone will not regenerate the oxidised carrier. A suitable electron sink, however, may restore oxidation even if ATP synthesis remains defective. This difference helps interpret respiratory inhibition and fermentation experiments without memorising every individual pathway.

12. Substrate-level phosphorylation: how metabolites generate ATP

We have seen that high-potential phosphorylated compounds can drive ATP formation. Substrate-level phosphorylation is the enzyme-catalysed synthesis of ATP or GTP coupled directly to a metabolic substrate conversion, without requiring a transmembrane ion gradient. In glycolysis, phosphoglycerate kinase transfers the carbon-1 phosphoryl group of 1,3-BPG to ADP, yielding 3-phosphoglycerate and ATP. Pyruvate kinase transfers the phosphoryl group of PEP to ADP, yielding pyruvate and ATP. In the citric acid cycle, succinyl-CoA synthetase links thioester conversion to nucleotide phosphorylation through activated intermediates.

How does glycolysis obtain a donor like 1,3-BPG from an ordinary triose phosphate? Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase oxidises the aldehyde group while reducing NAD⁺. A catalytic cysteine participates in formation of an enzyme-linked intermediate. Hydride transfer produces NADH and a thioester at the enzyme; inorganic phosphate then attacks the activated acyl group, releasing 1,3-BPG. The oxidation thus contributes to formation of a high-transfer-potential acyl phosphate without consuming ATP in that reaction. The subsequent kinase recovers some of this free energy as ATP. Notice how a single substrate oxidation can conserve energy both in NADH and in the acyl phosphate; these are distinct products of the complete balanced reaction, not two independent extra energy supplies.

The two-step sequence explains the meaning of energy conservation in a pathway. If the aldehyde were directly oxidised to a free carboxylate without the activated intermediate, some of the free energy would become unavailable for ATP production. By capturing it in a transferable group, the pathway makes the favourable oxidation serve the next reaction. In erythrocytes, diversion of 1,3-BPG through the 2,3-BPG shunt bypasses the ATP-producing phosphoglycerate kinase step. This illustrates a genuine metabolic trade-off: a useful regulatory metabolite can be produced at the expense of ATP yield.

In a glucose molecule, two three-carbon intermediates are formed during glycolysis. Each passes through the phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase reactions, giving four ATP molecules gross by substrate-level phosphorylation. The preparatory phase consumes two ATP, so the usual net glycolytic yield is two ATP per glucose. This simple accounting is a preview, not a complete pathway description. When calculating net ATP, separate molecules produced from those invested; do not add hypothetical oxidative phosphorylation yields for NADH unless the electron-acceptor pathway and assumptions are specified.

Substrate-level phosphorylation can operate in the absence of oxygen because its immediate mechanism is not oxygen reduction. It still requires the relevant substrates, enzymes, nucleotide acceptors and oxidised cofactors. If NAD⁺ cannot be regenerated, the preceding oxidative step of glycolysis can stop, preventing continued formation of 1,3-BPG. Thus an oxygen-independent mechanism does not imply unlimited activity under all anaerobic conditions. Fermentation regenerates the necessary NAD⁺ by coupling reduction of an organic metabolite to NADH oxidation. The detailed carbon and redox balances belong to 1.E.02.

13. Biological energy transducers: from electrons to gradients to work

Energy transduction is the conversion of one form of available free energy into another. Enzymes that couple group transfer to bond formation are chemical transducers; membrane proteins that use ATP to move ions convert chemical driving force into an electrochemical gradient; molecular motors convert chemical reactions or gradients into motion. These devices share a principle: their conformational or chemical transitions are linked so that progress through a favourable reaction biases progress through a useful but unfavourable one.

An ion gradient is an energy reservoir because ions tend to move down both their concentration gradient and their electrical potential gradient. For transfer of one mole of ion of charge z from side 1 to side 2, ΔG = RT ln(a₂/a₁) + zF(ψ₂ − ψ₁), where ψ is electric potential in volts. The first term is the chemical contribution; the second is electrical. This expression follows from the electrochemical potential μ̃ = μ° + RT ln a + zFψ. Subtract the potential of the starting side from that of the destination, so the sign convention is explicit. For an uncharged solute z is zero; for a monovalent cation moving into a negative compartment the electrical contribution is favourable.

For example, a proton moves from an outside solution at pH 7.0 into a compartment at pH 8.0 with a potential 120 mV more negative than outside. At 298 K the ratio of proton activities is 0.1, so the chemical contribution is 2.478 ln(0.1) = −5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical contribution is (+1)(96.485)(−0.120) = −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. Total ΔG is −17.29 kJ per mole of protons. Three moles of protons moving in this direction could, in a reversible idealised coupling, provide approximately 51.9 kJ. If the ATP synthesis cost is 50 kJ mol⁻¹, this establishes energetic plausibility but does not specify the real proton-to-ATP ratio. The rotor architecture and additional transport costs will be discussed in 1.E.04.

A respiratory membrane captures some of the free energy of favourable electron transfer by pumping protons across a poorly permeable membrane. The proton concentration and electrical differences then support ATP formation through ATP synthase as protons return. In photosynthetic systems, light-induced charge separation initiates electron movement and gradient formation. ATP synthase is itself a reversible molecular transducer: in suitable circumstances ATP hydrolysis can drive rotation in the opposite direction and pump ions. Direction is governed by the relative free energies, not by a permanent one-way arrow on a textbook diagram.

A primary active transporter such as the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase converts ATP free energy into ion gradients. Its cyclic changes of conformation and ion affinities coordinate binding, phosphorylation and dephosphorylation; typically three Na⁺ are transported out and two K⁺ in per ATP hydrolysed. A secondary transporter may then use the downhill movement of Na⁺ to support uphill movement of another solute. The secondary transporter need not hydrolyse ATP itself. The energetic link extends across molecules and time through the shared gradient. Full transport mechanisms belong to Unit 2, but this example makes the general principle clear.

Motor proteins such as myosin use nucleotide binding, hydrolysis and product release to change conformation and perform directed mechanical work. A motor does not obtain motion from heat released by a freely hydrolysing ATP molecule; its sequence of chemical states changes the probability of particular mechanical states. A loose coupling or slippage allows some chemical energy to be dissipated without useful work. This is why a measured ATPase rate need not directly report mechanical efficiency or ion pumping.

The evidence for coupling via a gradient came from more than observing ATP synthesis and respiration together. Peter Mitchell proposed chemiosmotic coupling in 1961. In 1966, Jagendorf and Uribe demonstrated ATP formation in isolated chloroplasts after imposing an acid–base transition in the dark in the presence of ADP and phosphate. The imposed proton difference could support phosphorylation even without concurrent light-driven electron transport. This is powerful evidence that the gradient is an intermediate in energy transduction, not a mere by-product. The experiment does not mean a pH difference alone supplies all respiratory ATP under normal conditions; membrane potential and the intact transducing machinery also matter.

If a membrane becomes freely permeable to protons, electron transport may continue or even accelerate because the opposing gradient is relieved, while ATP synthesis falls. In contrast, preventing the formation of a gradient by stopping electron transport can reduce both gradient and ATP formation. These contrasting outcomes motivate the experimental reasoning developed in the oxidative phosphorylation chapter. For now the essential distinction is between a direct chemical intermediate in substrate-level phosphorylation and a spatial electrochemical intermediate in gradient-driven phosphorylation.

14. Evidence, measurements and energetic bookkeeping

A useful biochemical experiment separates a thermodynamic possibility from a proposed mechanism. Imagine a purified hexokinase preparation with glucose, MgATP and a suitable buffer. If glucose 6-phosphate and ADP appear in equal amounts, that supports a one-to-one transfer. If ATP disappears without glucose 6-phosphate formation, ATP consumption alone is not proof of productive coupling: hydrolysis or another contaminating ATP-consuming process could explain it. A no-glucose control, a no-enzyme control and measurement of the products are therefore essential. A terminal-phosphorus label on ATP can provide additional information about the transferred group, as long as exchange and side reactions are considered.

Now consider a simplified preparation containing a dehydrogenase, its substrate and NAD⁺. The absorbance at 340 nm increases initially, then reaches a plateau while much substrate remains. One explanation is that the NAD pool has become predominantly reduced; another is enzyme inactivation or product inhibition. Adding fresh NAD⁺ to one aliquot and fresh enzyme to another helps distinguish these possibilities. If NAD⁺ addition restores the original rate but enzyme addition does not, carrier limitation becomes more plausible. The data do not by themselves establish the exact enzyme mechanism. Measuring products and checking coenzyme concentrations would strengthen the inference.

The following illustrative data compare ATP use with product formation. In condition A the preparation consumes 80 nmol ATP and forms 78 nmol of a phosphorylated product. In condition B it consumes 80 nmol ATP but forms only 20 nmol product. The apparent product per ATP ratios are 0.975 and 0.25. Under an assumed 1:1 ideal stoichiometry, condition B exhibits much greater unproductive consumption. The lost ATP might reflect an ATPase contaminant, futile cycling, side reactions or uncoupling within the enzyme. Do not claim a particular molecular defect from these two numbers alone. Also distinguish stoichiometric coupling efficiency (product/ATP) from energetic efficiency, which requires free energies.

Suppose a membrane preparation uses a reaction releasing 100 kJ per mole of fuel to produce ATP at a cellular synthesis cost of 48 kJ per mole. If it makes 1.5 moles ATP per mole fuel, the free energy conserved in ATP is 72 kJ mol⁻¹ fuel, or 72% of the supplied free-energy decrease. The remaining 28 kJ is not necessarily all immediately released as heat; it may include other products or gradients if they are not included in the measured output. A ratio of ATP free energy to input reaction free energy is meaningful only when the reaction stoichiometry, actual conditions and system boundary are stated. If a question gives standard free energies, use them consistently rather than mixing standard and cellular values.

The ATP concentration measured at one moment also differs from its turnover rate. A constant pool can coexist with rapid hydrolysis and regeneration. For a pool of 2 μmol ATP regenerated at 4 μmol min⁻¹, the pool-equivalent turnover time is 0.5 min, if production balances consumption and the pool is homogeneous. This is not the lifetime of every individual ATP molecule, but it gives a useful scale for flux. Similarly, a large NADH concentration might signify a bottleneck in reoxidation rather than high ongoing fuel oxidation. These are examples of why interpreting metabolites requires both mass balance and kinetic information.

In exam questions, begin by separating group transfer, redox transfer and gradient-driven processes. Balance the relevant reaction; identify what is being transferred and where the carrier is regenerated. Determine whether the question asks for direction, rate, stoichiometry, coupling efficiency or energetic efficiency. Only then choose the free-energy equation, equilibrium relation, redox expression or transport equation. The power of bioenergetics lies in combining a small number of quantitative rules with an understanding of the actual molecular pathway.

15. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason practice

1. Which statement correctly distinguishes ΔG from activation energy?

A. Negative ΔG guarantees speed; B. Enzymes change ΔG; C. ΔG gives thermodynamic direction while activation energy affects rate; D. ΔG is always zero in cells.

Answer and reasoning: C. A favourable reaction can have a large barrier. A confuses rate and direction, B confuses catalysis and equilibrium, and D mistakes a nonequilibrium steady state for equilibrium.

2. Which bond connects the β and γ groups of ATP?

A. Phosphodiester; B. Phosphoanhydride; C. Glycosidic; D. Thioester.

Answer and reasoning: B. The link joins two phosphoryl groups. A describes a phosphate joining two alcohol groups; C links sugar and base in a nucleoside; D involves an acyl group and sulfur.

3. What best explains why ATP hydrolysis is favourable?

A. Breaking bonds alone releases energy; B. ATP is unstable because it is an enzyme; C. Overall products and solvent are at lower Gibbs free energy; D. ADP cannot bind water.

Answer and reasoning: C. Product hydration, resonance and electrostatics contribute to the overall change. Bond cleavage alone costs energy (A); ATP is a substrate, not an enzyme (B); ADP is hydrated (D).

4. Assertion: ATP can drive glucose phosphorylation. Reason: Hexokinase transfers the terminal phosphoryl group directly from ATP to glucose.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason does not explain; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and reasoning: A. Direct transfer provides the mechanistic link to the energetically favourable net reaction; uncoupled hydrolysis would not provide this route.

5. Which compound has the highest standard phosphoryl transfer potential among these?

A. Glucose 6-phosphate; B. ATP; C. Phosphocreatine; D. PEP.

Answer and reasoning: D. PEP hydrolysis is about −61.9 kJ/mol; its enol product converts to stable keto pyruvate. A, B and C have smaller magnitudes.

6. NADP differs from NAD principally by which group?

A. A phosphate on nicotinamide ring; B. A phosphate on adenosine ribose; C. One additional hydride permanently; D. A CoA thiol.

Answer and reasoning: B. This phosphate provides recognition specificity rather than a different redox centre. A locates it incorrectly; C confuses oxidation state with structure; D is unrelated.

7. Which carrier can readily bridge two-electron and one-electron steps?

A. FAD; B. ATP; C. CoA; D. Glucose 6-phosphate.

Answer and reasoning: A. Flavins have semiquinone states. The other three transfer chemical groups and are not normally reversible electron carriers in this sense.

8. Assertion: Substrate-level phosphorylation can occur without oxygen. Reason: It requires a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

A. Both true with explanation; B. Both true without explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and reasoning: C. ATP/GTP formation is chemically coupled to substrate conversion, not necessarily to a gradient. Cofactor regeneration may still be necessary.

9. What is the direct source of the phosphate incorporated into GTP by succinyl-CoA synthetase?

A. CoA's thiol; B. Pᵢ; C. NADH; D. FADH₂.

Answer and reasoning: B. The thioester drives activation of inorganic phosphate through enzyme intermediates. CoA, NADH and FADH₂ are not the transferred phosphate.

10. Why is ATP → AMP plus PPᵢ hydrolysis commonly charged as two ATP equivalents?

A. Two ATP molecules always bind; B. AMP must be rephosphorylated through two nucleotide phosphate additions; C. PPᵢ is ATP; D. AMP contains no phosphorus.

Answer and reasoning: B. One ATP is consumed initially, but both terminal phosphate equivalents are lost from the adenylate pool when PPᵢ is hydrolysed. A, C and D are incorrect.

11. Which comparison correctly describes a steady-state ATP pool?

A. No ATP is consumed; B. Concentration may be constant despite rapid production and consumption; C. ATP hydrolysis is at equilibrium; D. ATP synthesis is impossible.

Answer and reasoning: B. Balanced fluxes maintain a pool; equilibrium is not required. A, C and D incorrectly equate a constant concentration with no chemical turnover.

16. Part C: quantitative and experimental reasoning

1. A cell has 4 mM ATP, 0.4 mM ADP and 2 mM Pᵢ at 298 K. Estimate ΔG′hyd given ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ/mol.

A. −30.5; B. −51.6; C. −25.2; D. +51.6 kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: B. Q′=(0.0004×0.002)/0.004=0.0002. RT ln Q′=2.478×(−8.517)=−21.1; total −51.6. A ignores concentration, C uses the wrong quotient or units, D reverses the sign.

2. A coupled reaction requires +56 kJ/mol. ATP supplies −48 kJ/mol at the given conditions. Minimum ATP count for negative net ΔG?

A. 0; B. 1; C. 2; D. 3.

Answer and reasoning: C. One gives +8; two give −40. Three also works thermodynamically but is not minimal. Actual enzyme stoichiometry is not established by this bound.

3. A donor hydrolyses at −40 kJ/mol. Estimate standard ΔG for transfer to ADP when ATP hydrolysis is −30.5.

A. −70.5; B. +9.5; C. −9.5; D. +70.5 kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: C. Reverse ATP hydrolysis to get +30.5, then add −40. A adds two hydrolyses; B reverses the requested transfer; D reverses the incorrect sum.

4. A reaction has ΔG°′ = +5.0 kJ/mol and Q′=0.10 at 298 K. What is actual ΔG′?

A. +10.7; B. −0.71; C. +5.0; D. −5.0 kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: B. ΔG′=5.0+2.478 ln(0.1)=−0.71. A uses a reversed quotient; C ignores concentrations; D incorrectly assumes a sign reversal of the standard value.

5. Two electron couples have reduction potentials −0.25 and +0.15 V. A two-electron transfer proceeds from the first reduced form to the second oxidised form. ΔG°′?

A. +77.2; B. −38.6; C. −77.2; D. −19.3 kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: C. ΔE°′=0.40 V and −2×96.485×0.40=−77.2. A reverses the sign; B omits the second electron; D uses the difference of magnitudes.

6. A NADH assay absorbance falls by 0.1244 in one minute in a 1 cm cuvette with 3 mL volume. ε=6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Rate of NADH oxidation?

A. 0.020; B. 0.060; C. 0.373; D. 6.22 μmol/min.

Answer and reasoning: B. Concentration falls by 0.020 mM, equivalent to 0.060 μmol in 3 mL. A is the concentration rate, C multiplies absorbance by volume without dividing by ε, and D is the extinction coefficient.

7. An adenylate pool contains 4 mM ATP, 2 mM ADP and 2 mM AMP. Adenylate kinase converts 1 mM ADP into 0.5 mM each ATP and AMP. Energy charge before and after?

A. 0.625, 0.625; B. 0.625, 0.75; C. 0.5, 0.625; D. 1, 1.

Answer and reasoning: A. Before (4+1)/8=0.625. After (4.5+0.5)/8=0.625. Adenylate kinase conserves the weighted numerator and total nucleotide count; it redistributes the species without increasing energy charge by itself.

8. A proton moves from pH 6.5 to pH 7.5, destination potential −0.100 V relative to origin. At 298 K, energy change per mole?

A. −15.36; B. +15.36; C. −3.94; D. −5.71 kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: A. Chemical term −5.71, electrical term −9.65, sum −15.36. B reverses direction; C subtracts the favourable terms; D ignores voltage.

9. An ATP-driven enzyme hydrolyses 200 nmol ATP and forms 120 nmol product (ideal 1:1). Which conclusion follows?

A. Exactly 40% of energy appears as heat; B. Apparent product/ATP coupling ratio is 0.60; C. Enzyme is necessarily denatured; D. ATP synthesis occurred.

Answer and reasoning: B. 120/200=0.60. This is stoichiometric, not an energy efficiency, so A is unsupported; C requires further evidence and D contradicts the observed hydrolysis.

10. An assay contains 100 μM NAD⁺ and excess substrate. After a time, NADH reaches 100 μM and product formation stops. Fresh NAD⁺ restores product formation. Best interpretation?

A. Fuel equilibrium is independent of NAD; B. NAD⁺ availability limits continued oxidation; C. ATP directly oxidises NADH; D. The enzyme must be absent.

Answer and reasoning: B. The finite cofactor pool is reduced; adding oxidised carrier permits continued reaction. A neglects redox quotient, C invents a mechanism, D conflicts with restored activity.

11. A glycolytic preparation forms two 1,3-BPG and two PEP per glucose. Both kinase steps work; ATP investment is two. ATP gross and net?

A. 2 and 2; B. 4 and 2; C. 4 and 4; D. 2 and 0.

Answer and reasoning: B. Each donor makes one ATP, so 2+2=4 gross and 4−2=2 net. A and D omit a producing step, C omits the investment.

12. An artificial membrane is made proton-permeable while electron transfer machinery remains active. Which result most directly supports gradient-mediated ATP synthesis?

A. ATP production falls despite ongoing electron transfer; B. ATP rises with no ADP; C. ATP unchanged proves no gradient; D. Electron flow automatically ceases.

Answer and reasoning: A. Gradient dissipation can separate electron transfer from phosphorylation. B lacks substrate for synthesis; C is not the predicted signature; D is not necessary because relief of backpressure may accelerate flow.

13. NAD⁺/NADH ratio increases 100-fold at 298 K at fixed pH. How does the potential of this couple change?

A. decreases 59 mV; B. increases 59 mV; C. increases 118 mV; D. does not change.

Answer and reasoning: B. (RT/2F)ln100=0.0592 V. A reverses ratio, C omits n=2, D ignores the Nernst relation.

14. A fuel oxidation supplies 180 kJ/mol of free energy and produces 2 ATP/mol, each costing 52 kJ/mol. What fraction is conserved in ATP?

A. 29%; B. 58%; C. 104%; D. 87%.

Answer and reasoning: B. 2×52/180=0.578, or 58%. A counts one ATP; C reports a kJ quantity as percent; D is unsupported. Other energy-conserving outputs may exist.

15. At fixed ATP and ADP, inorganic phosphate rises tenfold at 310 K. By how much does ΔG′ of ATP hydrolysis change?

A. −5.93; B. +5.93; C. +30.5; D. zero kJ/mol.

Answer and reasoning: B. Q′ rises tenfold, so ΔΔG′=RT ln10=2.577×2.303=+5.93. Hydrolysis becomes less favourable. A reverses the ratio; C confuses standard value; D ignores phosphate.

17. Consolidation and the bridge to glycolysis

The key to this chapter is to recognise that neither ATP nor an electron carrier is a universal store of energy divorced from its reaction. A negative free energy indicates a favourable net direction; coupling requires a linked molecular mechanism. ATP transfers groups or powers conformational changes, high-potential intermediates regenerate ATP, NAD and flavins carry electrons, and gradients can transmit free energy across a membrane. In every case the actual driving force depends on concentrations and conditions.

As you enter glycolysis, ask at each step whether the carbon skeleton is being rearranged, a phosphoryl group is being transferred, electrons are being moved or an activated intermediate is being created. Trace ATP investment and recovery separately, and track the regeneration of NAD⁺. That approach turns the ten reactions of glycolysis into a connected biochemical story rather than ten isolated names.
