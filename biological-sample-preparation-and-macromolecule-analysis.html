From biological material to reliable evidence

A leaf, a seed and a drop of milk contain molecules that cannot be understood simply by looking at the intact material. A leaf contains structural polysaccharides, nucleic acids in several compartments, soluble enzymes, membrane proteins, pigments and a variety of lipids. Some of these constituents are present in large amounts; others occur in very small amounts but perform essential functions. The investigator who wishes to compare two leaves must therefore solve two distinct problems. The first is to obtain material that represents the biological state of interest. The second is to measure a defined constituent without mistaking the response of other substances for the response of that constituent. This chapter develops the reasoning that connects these problems.

The analytical objective

A biological sample is a specified portion of biological material collected for an investigation. The analyte is the particular substance or class of substances being measured. The matrix consists of the other constituents and the physical and chemical environment in which the analyte occurs. A leaf extract may be the sample, starch may be the analyte, and pigments, soluble sugars, proteins and buffer may collectively form the matrix. The distinction is relative to the scientific question. If the question changes to protein concentration, starch becomes part of the matrix instead. A matrix can change how a molecule is released, separated, detected or quantified.

Isolation means obtaining the target from its original environment. Fractionation separates a complex mixture into portions enriched in different constituents. Purification specifically reduces unwanted constituents relative to a target. Analysis produces information about a sample, for example its concentration, composition, size distribution or catalytic activity. These operations need not occur in a rigid sequence: an early measurement may guide the next separation, and a later analytical result may show that an earlier operation must be reconsidered. A perfectly valid investigation may not require complete purification if the measurement is sufficiently selective in the original matrix.

Choosing the biological denominator

Consider two preparations of an enzyme from germinating seeds. One contains abundant protein but has little catalytic activity. The other contains less protein, yet catalyses more substrate conversion per minute. Without knowing the basis of each measurement, we cannot tell which preparation is more useful. Total protein may include dozens of unrelated molecules, while activity depends on the target's functional state and assay conditions. For a study of enzyme catalysis the second preparation may be preferable; for a study of total protein reserves the first measurement may be more informative. No single quality number captures both purposes.

The design of a preparation begins with the desired information, not with the available instrument. A DNA fragment intended for sequence analysis requires appropriate integrity and absence of inhibitory substances; a protein intended for activity measurements needs a preserved functional conformation; a carbohydrate analysis may deliberately convert polymers into measurable products. Sample treatment changes the object being measured. Such a change is appropriate when the transformation is understood and connected to the biological question. It is an artefact when the transformation is unintended but interpreted as an original property of the sample.

At the examination level, the first step in solving a methods question is to identify the numerator, denominator and biological entity that each experimental result represents. A concentration is not a total amount. A detector signal is not automatically a chemical identity. A fraction enriched in a target is not necessarily a pure fraction. A quality assessment is strongest when independent measurements examine different properties, rather than when the same measurement is repeated many times and given several names.

The historical development of biochemical methods can be understood as a movement from bulk composition to functional reconstruction and then to selective molecular measurements. Classical chemical analyses established that a tissue contains distinct families of compounds. Fractionation made it possible to associate biochemical reactions with particular cellular components. Later optical and electrophoretic methods allowed investigators to examine quantities and molecular properties with smaller samples and greater selectivity. Modern instrumentation does not remove the need for preparation; it makes previously overlooked properties of preparation more important, because a sensitive measurement can detect a small contaminant as readily as a small amount of the desired molecule.

The classic logic of cell fractionation is described in Molecular Biology of the Cell by Alberts and colleagues. When a disrupted cell is separated into fractions and a biochemical reaction is detected in one fraction more strongly than in others, the observation provides a starting point for locating the molecular machinery responsible. It does not establish localisation by itself. The enriched fraction may contain material from more than one compartment; components may have redistributed during disruption; and an activity may depend on a combination of molecules from several compartments. An independent marker for each compartment, together with the original cellular context, gives the enrichment pattern more evidential weight.

There is a useful distinction between a descriptive experiment and a mechanistic experiment. A descriptive measurement could establish that the soluble sugar concentration of a seed extract changes during germination. A mechanistic experiment would ask whether mobilisation of starch accounts for the increase, and would therefore need evidence relating starch content, sugar production and the relevant catalytic activities. A high-quality sample preparation supports both kinds of experiment, but the required controls differ. The mechanism cannot be inferred solely from a concentration change if extraction efficiency or water content also changes during germination.

A further distinction is between a sample taken directly from the system and a sample produced by an analytical transformation. When a polysaccharide is hydrolysed before an assay, the signal originates from products of the transformation rather than necessarily from the original intact polymer. The reported abundance can still be scientifically valid if the conversion is known and the assay responds appropriately. By contrast, accidental breakdown of a nucleic acid before length analysis changes a property that the investigator intended to preserve. Recognising intentional and unintentional transformations prevents confusion between preparation and biological mechanism.

The analytical objective should specify identity, amount and relevant condition of the target. For example, a protein sample may need to answer whether a specific enzyme exists in the leaf, how much protein belonging to that enzyme is present per gram of tissue, and whether the enzyme remains catalytically competent. Each claim needs a suitable measurement. A total protein assay cannot establish the presence of a particular enzyme, even if the total concentration is estimated accurately. A target-specific binding measurement may establish the presence of the protein but cannot necessarily establish catalysis. A functional assay might establish catalysis while leaving open which of several related enzymes is responsible. Combining these results is more informative than treating one of them as the definitive measure of every property.

This principle has an important implication for standardisation. The same named technique can answer different questions depending on sample definition and the reported unit. An assay result of milligrams per gram of leaf is a tissue-level composition estimate, whereas milligrams per millilitre of extract describes the prepared solution. Enzyme activity per milligram of total protein is a relative functional measure; enzyme activity per leaf may be closer to a whole-organ capacity. A biological comparison may change direction when a different denominator is selected, because the denominator is itself a variable that can respond to the experimental treatment. The choice must be made in relation to the biological claim.

Sampling, preservation and molecular integrity

The molecular composition of living tissue is dynamic. Enzymes continue to catalyse reactions while substrates and appropriate conditions remain available. Membranes maintain compartmental differences that disappear when cells are disrupted. Cellular responses can alter transcript abundance rapidly. Collection therefore establishes a time boundary between the biological state of interest and transformations occurring after collection. The interval between collection and preservation, as well as the conditions during that interval, can influence the final result. Identical analytical precision cannot compensate for systematic changes that occur before the analysis.

Structural and functional preservation

A representative sample reflects the population or tissue about which a conclusion is intended. In a leaf study, veins, mesophyll, epidermis and developing regions do not necessarily have the same biochemical composition. Collecting only the visibly largest leaves might select a developmental stage. If one experimental group is represented by young leaves and another by mature leaves, differences in macromolecule concentration cannot be attributed uniquely to the intended treatment. The biological sampling plan must specify the unit being compared, the tissue region and the developmental context. Independent plants are biological replicates; repeated aliquots from a single leaf extract are technical replicates. Both have useful roles, but one cannot substitute for the other.

Integrity refers to the preservation of the relevant molecular or structural state. In DNA studies it may refer to polymer length and absence of extensive chemical damage. For RNA it may include size distribution and preservation of the transcript population. For an enzyme it includes the capacity to retain its functional conformation and activity. For a membrane preparation integrity may involve retention of bilayer organisation or compartmental enclosure. A sample can be chemically pure and structurally unsuitable. Conversely, crude material may retain functional activity even when its purity is modest.

Temperature affects reaction rates, solubility and molecular organisation. Cooling can retard many unwanted enzymatic reactions, but it is not a universal guarantee of preservation. Some proteins are sensitive to cold, ice formation can concentrate salts in the remaining liquid, and freezing may disrupt membranes through mechanical and osmotic effects. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles can alter aggregation or release compartmental enzymes. The preservation strategy must be selected according to the measurement. For an investigation of organelle function, preserving a membrane may matter more than maximising release of every protein. For a denaturing measurement of total nucleic acid, maintaining intact organelles is not the main objective.

Hydrolysis breaks susceptible chemical bonds using water. Nucleases catalyse cleavage of nucleic acids, proteases cleave peptide bonds, and lipid-modifying enzymes can change lipid composition. RNA possesses a ribose 2′ hydroxyl group that contributes to its susceptibility to alkaline backbone cleavage; ribonuclease activity provides an additional route to degradation. DNA lacks this hydroxyl group and is less susceptible to that particular chemical mechanism, but it remains vulnerable to nucleases and chemical damage. Protein unfolding can occur without breaking the peptide backbone, so a size measurement alone may miss loss of function.

A useful conceptual control is to compare aliquots from the same initial benign sample exposed to different storage histories, with identical measurement conditions afterwards. If activity decreases while total protein signal remains essentially unchanged, the result supports functional deterioration or inhibition rather than simple removal of all protein. If both measures decrease, material loss or assay interference must also be considered. The comparison does not by itself specify the molecular cause. An independent assessment of solubility, fragmentation or inhibitory effects would be needed to distinguish these possibilities.

The choice of sampling unit also determines the level at which an investigation can generalise. Four leaves collected from a single plant provide information about variation within that plant but not four independent measurements of variation among plants. Pooling material from several plants may be a sensible way to obtain adequate sample quantity, but the pool then becomes the measured unit. Several measurements of that pool cannot reconstruct the independent variation lost during pooling. A student should ask whether the intended conclusion concerns individuals, groups, tissues or repeated measurements of one preparation.

A sample can be representative in one dimension and biased in another. For instance, collecting the same dry mass of leaf from each plant controls the amount of dry material entering the preparation, but it does not control the proportion of vascular tissue in that material. Collecting equal areas controls area while potentially changing mass. If a stress treatment reduces leaf thickness or water content, fresh-mass normalisation, dry-mass normalisation and area normalisation may all reveal different aspects of the response. These differences are not simply measurement mistakes. They reflect distinct questions about abundance and allocation.

Preservation must begin at the point when the property of interest could change. Imagine an enzyme in a plant cell that normally occupies a compartment separated from its substrate. Tissue damage removes that separation and permits an artificial reaction. Even if the enzyme concentration is later measured without error, the measured product concentration could be different from the product concentration in the intact tissue. Measuring the product after an uncontrolled delay is then a measurement of both biology and the preparation history. This problem is particularly significant for fast metabolic processes and for targets that respond to changes in cellular conditions.

The relationship between molecular structure and integrity can be clarified with protein denaturation. The peptide backbone connects amino acids through covalent bonds. Secondary and tertiary structures depend substantially on noncovalent interactions and, for some proteins, covalent disulphide bonds. An environmental change can disrupt the folded state without breaking the backbone. A denatured enzyme may retain its original molecular mass and respond to a protein assay, yet lack the geometric arrangement of residues required for catalysis. A procedure intended to estimate polypeptide mass can tolerate denaturation that would be unacceptable in a procedure intended to study active enzymes.

Aggregation differs from unfolding, even though the phenomena can occur together. A protein that exposes normally buried hydrophobic surfaces can associate with other protein molecules and form aggregates. Aggregated material may become insoluble or sediment with other particles. In the resulting supernatant, the concentration of soluble protein decreases. The protein has not necessarily been chemically destroyed; it may have changed physical state. A comparison of total and soluble fractions can reveal this distinction. If the activity of the soluble fraction decreases as well, further evidence is required to determine whether soluble molecules have also lost function.

Nucleic acids can change in integrity without losing many of their ultraviolet-absorbing groups. Each break in a long polymer creates shorter pieces, but most bases may remain present. An optical measurement can consequently remain similar even when the average molecular length has changed greatly. The interpretation depends on the required length: a small fragment may be sufficient for detecting a short sequence but not for obtaining information spanning a long region. The same degraded sample could therefore be fit for one application and unfit for another. A quality threshold is meaningful only when related to the intended application.

Another source of error is adsorption. At low target amounts, a fraction of a molecule can associate with the wall of a container or with suspended material, leaving less in the measured solution. The relative effect may be greater at low concentration if the surface provides a limited number of binding sites. This illustrates why a recovery assessment performed using a concentrated standard cannot automatically establish recovery near the low end of the analytical range. Exam questions that give a constant absolute loss at several initial concentrations exploit the same distinction: equal absolute losses correspond to unequal percentage losses.

The need for preservation is not a demand that all molecules remain in their original state. Preparation for denaturing electrophoresis intentionally disrupts many native protein interactions. A measurement of total sugar may deliberately alter polysaccharides. A measurement of intact organelles, on the other hand, requires the physical boundary to remain functional. It is the relationship between treatment and objective that distinguishes a suitable transformation from damage. When comparing two procedures, the one that releases more material is not automatically superior if it alters the property that the experiment is intended to measure.

Release, fractionation and the properties used for separation

Cell disruption is the loss of cellular boundaries needed to make particular components accessible. The word homogenate refers to the mixture produced when tissue is dispersed and cells are disrupted. It does not imply that all molecules are dissolved or that every cell is broken. A homogenate may contain dissolved proteins, organelles, membrane fragments, cell-wall material and intact cells. A lysate is material released by lysis, which may still contain insoluble components. The composition of either depends on the properties of the source and the disruption method.

Sedimentation, solubility and partitioning

Cell walls, lipid membranes and protein complexes present different physical barriers. Mechanical disruption can release material without relying on dissolution of all membranes, but shear and local heating may damage fragile structures. Detergents interact with lipids and hydrophobic protein surfaces, making some membrane-associated molecules accessible in an aqueous environment. A detergent that preserves one protein complex may disrupt another, and detergent residues can influence subsequent assays. Osmotic effects can alter cell volume and membrane integrity, although cell walls and other structural features influence how an organism responds. The correct conceptual choice follows from the target and the desired structural state.

An extraction medium is not just water with a label. Buffers resist pH change within a useful range; ions influence electrostatic interactions and protein solubility; osmotic support can help maintain organelle structure. The same reagent may assist one goal and interfere with another. Metal chelation may suppress some metal-dependent enzymes that would otherwise damage the sample, but it may also impair the activity of a desired metalloenzyme. Reducing conditions can protect some thiols from oxidation but may interfere with assays based on reduction reactions or alter disulphide-dependent structures. Thus preparation chemistry and measurement chemistry must be considered together.

Centrifugal fractionation separates suspended material according to sedimentation behaviour. A pellet is the material that collects at the bottom or outer end of a centrifuge tube under specified conditions; a supernatant is the liquid remaining above it. In differential centrifugation, successive separations can enrich fractions containing differently sedimenting particles. The resulting fractions are not pure simply because they occur at different stages. A large fragment may sediment with a smaller dense particle, and aggregation may change the apparent sedimentation behaviour of molecules. Marker assays are used to test which components occur in each fraction. Detailed centrifugal equations belong to node 14.A.04.

Solubility differences permit another kind of fractionation. Molecules can move between dissolved and condensed or precipitated states as the chemical environment changes. Precipitation enriches a constituent only when it is recovered selectively relative to unwanted material. Co-precipitation can carry impurities into the same fraction. Re-dissolution must also be considered: a pellet containing a large quantity of target is not necessarily useful if the target cannot be recovered in a suitable state.

Partitioning between immiscible phases relies on unequal affinities of molecules for those phases. Hydrophobic lipids often favour an organic-rich environment, whereas many charged molecules favour an aqueous-rich environment. Amphipathic molecules have both polar and nonpolar regions and can behave differently from neutral lipids. Interfaces can trap molecules and produce incomplete recovery. Chromatographic separation extends selectivity by allowing components to interact differently with stationary and mobile phases. Electrophoresis uses migration in an electric field, often combined with a molecular sieving medium. The full treatment of these methods is reserved for later nodes.

The practical consequence is that no named fraction identifies a molecule by itself. A pellet labelled nuclear fraction is a hypothesis about its enrichment, not a certificate of exclusive nuclear origin. A soluble protein fraction may exclude an important membrane-bound form of the same enzyme. When interpreting a comparison, ask which components were removed, which were retained, and what independent evidence supports their identity.

A biological membrane is a selective barrier formed primarily by amphipathic lipids and associated proteins. The interior of the bilayer is comparatively nonpolar. Hydrophobic domains of integral membrane proteins interact with this interior, whereas hydrophilic regions interact with aqueous compartments. Once membrane material is released into an aqueous homogenate, hydrophobic components may remain in fragments or form aggregates rather than behaving as freely dissolved solutes. In a study of membrane-associated molecules, the fraction that sediments or appears insoluble is therefore often scientifically important. Discarding it on the assumption that all interesting biomolecules are soluble can selectively remove the target.

The effect of a detergent follows from its amphipathic structure. By interacting with hydrophobic surfaces and water, detergent molecules can help disperse membrane components. This can make integral membrane proteins accessible to analytical methods, but the original interactions between proteins and lipids may be changed. Some analytical methods measure properties of the resulting complexes rather than the isolated polypeptide alone. For example, a molecule associated with detergent may behave differently in a separation based on hydrodynamic size. The lesson is not that detergents are inherently undesirable but that their effect must be interpreted relative to the target's native environment and the analytical principle.

Homogenisation creates a population of particles of many sizes. Large tissue fragments sediment differently from small vesicles, and dissolved molecules may remain in the liquid under conditions in which large particles sediment. A series of sedimentation steps changes the mixture progressively. It is tempting to visualise this as a set of sieves with exact size cutoffs, but the analogy is incomplete: sedimentation depends on density differences, size, shape, viscosity and the applied acceleration. Particles with different compositions can sediment at similar rates, and particles of one composition may have a distribution of sizes. The resulting fractions have overlapping compositions.

Markers provide a way to evaluate this overlap. A mitochondrial enzyme activity, a nuclear-associated marker and a cytosolic enzyme can be examined in several fractions. If a putative mitochondrial fraction is enriched in the mitochondrial marker but also contains substantial nuclear marker, it is reasonable to describe it as mitochondrial-enriched but contaminated with nuclear-associated material. It would be incorrect to claim absolute organelle purity. Furthermore, marker activity may be affected by damage or inhibition, so absence of measured activity is not conclusive proof of absence of the compartment. More than one marker or an independent structural observation can strengthen the inference.

Another separation principle is based on molecular charge. Proteins possess ionisable groups whose charge depends on pH and their chemical environment. Two proteins may carry different net charges at the same pH and therefore interact differently with charged surfaces or migrate differently in an electric field. At the isoelectric point the average net charge is zero, but individual charged groups can remain. A molecule with zero net charge is not chemically nonpolar. This distinction explains why predictions based exclusively on a single net-charge value can be incomplete when other interactions contribute.

Size-based separation also requires attention to the property being measured. Molecular mass describes the amount of matter in a molecule, while hydrodynamic size describes the dimensions relevant to its motion through a fluid. A compact globular protein and an extended polymer of equal molecular mass may have unequal hydrodynamic radii. Size-exclusion chromatography responds principally to access to pores and thus hydrodynamic behaviour, rather than directly measuring mass. This is why calibration with a series of globular proteins may yield an apparent molecular mass that differs from the true mass of an unusually shaped molecule.

In adsorption-based separation, a molecule associates with a surface through chemical interactions. Adsorption and absorption should not be confused: adsorption is association at a surface, while absorption can refer to entry into a bulk material or uptake of radiation. A surface may bind the target or preferentially bind impurities. In either case, a mass balance should account for material in the retained and unretained fractions. Binding capacity matters because a finite surface cannot retain an unlimited quantity. If a separation is overloaded, target material may appear in a fraction expected to contain only unwanted molecules. This is a selectivity and capacity issue, not necessarily evidence that the target has changed chemically.

The contrast between partitioning and adsorption is useful for interpreting lipid and nucleic acid preparation. In a partitioning system a solute distributes between environments according to its relative affinity under the specified conditions. In adsorption a solute associates with an available surface. Both processes can depend on pH, ionic conditions and solvent properties, but their descriptions differ. A solvent distribution measurement should not be interpreted as a surface capacity measurement, and a binding system should not be assumed to behave as an unlimited equilibrium partition.

Selection of a separation method often involves combining independent properties. If two proteins have nearly equal mass but different charge, a size-based separation may be ineffective while charge-based separation can be useful. If they also have similar charge, a selective binding interaction may distinguish them. The sequence of methods can matter: removing particles at an early stage may protect subsequent analytical operations from interference, but removing a particulate fraction is inappropriate if the target is a membrane complex. It is therefore more useful to reason from target location and molecular properties than to memorise a universal sequence of named steps.

Recovery, material balance and selective loss

Recovery expresses how much of a specified target is retained relative to the quantity initially available or measured at a defined starting stage. A material balance accounts for target distributed among products, discarded fractions and unmeasured losses. In a closed conceptual balance without chemical conversion, the starting quantity equals the sum of the quantities in all output fractions. Real measurements seldom close exactly because of analytical uncertainty, inaccessible material, transfer loss or chemical change. The balance is therefore a diagnostic tool rather than an assumption that every value must be exact.

Concentration alone does not determine recovery. Suppose the initial extract contains 2 milligrams of target per millilitre in a volume of 10 millilitres. Its total measured quantity is 20 milligrams. After separation the retained fraction contains 5 milligrams per millilitre in 2 millilitres, or 10 milligrams. Concentration has increased 2.5-fold, but total target recovery is 50 percent. Removing solvent can increase concentration without producing additional target. If the investigator reports only concentration, the loss of half the original quantity is hidden.

For sequential operations, the denominator is important. If a first stage retains 80 percent of the target from its own input and a second retains 75 percent of the material entering it, the cumulative recovery is 0.80 multiplied by 0.75, or 60 percent of the starting target. Adding the percentages or taking their average gives the wrong answer because the second operation acts on a smaller quantity. If both reported percentages were already calculated relative to the same original material, multiplication would also be inappropriate. Always identify the reference stage.

Activity recovery has the same mathematical structure but its numerator is catalytic activity rather than mass. Total activity is the activity per unit volume multiplied by volume. It can increase after removal of an inhibitor even if the number of enzyme molecules has fallen. A calculated activity recovery greater than 100 percent therefore does not necessarily violate conservation of matter. The assay describes a rate, which depends on molecular state and experimental conditions. A mass-based recovery greater than 100 percent may indicate a changing analytical response, matrix interference, calibration error or uncertainty. It cannot be treated as evidence that physical material was created during an ordinary separation.

Imagine that a target signal is found in both the retained fraction and the fraction that would usually be discarded. Measuring both reveals whether the chosen separation is selective. If much of the target is in the discarded fraction, changing the retained fraction's final volume cannot recover what has already been discarded. In contrast, if the target is missing from both fractions, incomplete extraction, adsorption to a surface, degradation or a suppressed assay response are plausible alternatives. An examination problem may ask which measurement best discriminates these explanations. The answer is generally a comparison that follows the target at the relevant stage, not another reading of the final concentration alone.

The distinction between extraction yield and purification recovery is worth examining more carefully. An extraction begins with a biological material whose true total target content may not be known. A purification often begins with a measured extract. Reporting recovery relative to the measured crude extract is possible even when the fraction of the original tissue target released into that extract is unknown. If the extraction liberated only half of the tissue target and later purification retained 80 percent of the liberated amount, overall recovery from tissue would be 40 percent. The purification recovery of 80 percent is correct for its stated denominator but says nothing by itself about the first extraction.

A mass balance can be closed only if the same target quantity is measured on a comparable basis in the input and all outputs. For example, consider an illustrative separation with 100 micrograms of DNA measured in its input and 60, 30 and 5 micrograms measured in three fractions. The total accounted amount is 95 micrograms and the closure is 95 percent. The retained fraction has 60 percent recovery if it contains 60 micrograms. Closure and recovery differ because closure describes all measured outputs, not just the desired product. If the 30-microgram fraction is discarded, it remains part of the balance but not part of product recovery.

Uncertainty influences apparent closure. Suppose the input amount is estimated from concentration and volume, each with modest measurement error. The errors of multiple outputs can accumulate. A balance closing at 98 percent is not automatically evidence of a precisely identified two percent loss. Whether the discrepancy is meaningful depends on the uncertainty of the measurements and the assumptions of the assay. The purpose of checking closure is to identify substantial discrepancies and guide further investigation, not to assign every small difference to a unique physical mechanism.

The total quantity is often calculated by multiplying two measurements, so a volume error can affect recovery even when concentration is accurate. If a concentrated fraction is assumed to contain 1.0 millilitre but actually contains 0.8 millilitres, the computed quantity will be 25 percent greater than the true quantity at the same concentration. Evaporation can produce a related effect: concentration increases while volume decreases. A sample whose mass of nonvolatile analyte is conserved can show a concentration increase without increased recovery. This simple calculation illustrates why volume is part of the analytical record.

Purification can preferentially remove one form of a target. Consider a protein existing in free and membrane-associated states. The total target is the sum of these forms if the biological question concerns all protein molecules, but the soluble fraction may represent only the free form. A procedure giving excellent recovery of free protein might recover a low fraction of the total. When a question compares conditions that change the proportion of membrane-associated protein, this distinction becomes particularly important. An apparent change in soluble target abundance might reflect redistribution rather than a change in expression.

Recovery experiments using added references must also be interpreted with the reference's physical state in mind. A dissolved protein added to a homogenate may already be accessible to the assay, whereas endogenous protein may be trapped within an organelle or associated with cell-wall material. High recovery of the added reference shows that the measured preparation and assay can recover that reference under those conditions. It does not establish full liberation of the endogenous target. A stronger experiment may include a marker for residual material in the discarded fraction, compare preparation methods or use a complementary estimate of original abundance.

The relationship between target recovery and concentration can be visualised by plotting total amount and volume separately over successive operations. A stage reducing volume tenfold and target amount twofold produces a fivefold concentration increase. A stage reducing both by the same factor produces no concentration change. A stage retaining target while diluting the preparation produces lower concentration but full recovery. Such examples are especially useful for Part C questions because intuitive rankings based on concentration often contradict the actual ranking based on conserved quantity.

One further complication arises when target mass itself is intentionally converted into a different measurable chemical species. For a polymer hydrolysis measurement, the original polymer mass and the mass of liberated monomers are not necessarily equal because water is incorporated during cleavage. A conversion factor may therefore be required to express the result on the original polymer basis. An examiner may supply such a factor explicitly. The correct procedure is to apply it after identifying the quantity actually measured, not to assume that every mass reported at different stages can be compared directly.

Purity, enrichment and functionality

Purity is the proportion of a defined target relative to other material on a specified measurement basis. A statement of 90 percent purity is incomplete unless it says what is being counted: mass, molar fraction, chromatographic signal or another quantity. Detector signals from different molecules may not be proportional to their masses in the same way. Therefore 90 percent of the recorded peak area does not automatically establish 90 percent by mass. A defensible measurement connects the detector response to the quantity being claimed.

Purification fold for an enzyme is often estimated using specific activity, which is total measured enzyme activity divided by total protein mass. The underlying assumption is that the desired activity reports the target and that the assay is comparable across fractions. Suppose a crude extract contains 100 milligrams of total protein and 200 activity units. Its specific activity is 2 units per milligram. A later fraction contains 10 milligrams of total protein and 100 units. Its specific activity is 10 units per milligram, giving a fivefold enrichment with 50 percent activity recovery. Neither number is an absolute percentage of target protein purity.

Homogeneity and functional quality

In a mixture containing target enzyme and unrelated proteins, removal of unrelated protein can increase specific activity even while some target is lost. An apparent plateau in specific activity across purification stages can be consistent with increasing homogeneity, but it can also reflect limits of the assay or loss of active enzyme in parallel with removal of other proteins. If the target requires another component for activity, separation may change the measured rate without changing target mass proportionately. Independent structural evidence is valuable when the conclusion concerns molecular purity rather than just activity.

The trade-off between purity and recovery has a physical basis. Real molecules share properties with other molecules. A separation that excludes more contaminants may also exclude part of the target, particularly when the target occurs in several states or interacts with matrix components. A strong purification criterion can therefore select only a subpopulation of the original target. For an investigation of native complexes this selection may be undesirable. For a structural measurement requiring a homogeneous sample, it may be essential. The objective of the investigation determines which trade-off is acceptable.

A single band on an electrophoretic gel does not establish absolute purity because molecules can co-migrate and minor contaminants may be below detection. A single chromatographic peak can likewise contain unresolved components. A protein with a correct apparent molecular mass may be inactive. Convergent evidence from size, selective detection and biological activity gives a stronger basis for interpreting a preparation than any one of these measurements in isolation.

In practical interpretation, a fraction may contain a very small proportion of a target yet account for most of the recovered target molecules. Imagine a crude mixture containing ten milligrams of a desired protein and ninety milligrams of other proteins. A separation yields a fraction containing eight milligrams of the desired protein and twelve milligrams of other proteins. The purity rises from ten percent to forty percent, a fourfold enrichment by mass, while recovery is eighty percent. If a second fraction instead contains only two milligrams of the desired protein and one milligram of contaminants, its target purity is approximately sixty-seven percent but recovery is only twenty percent relative to the original. Both fractions are enriched; one preserves much more of the original protein, and the other has a higher target mass fraction. The best choice depends on whether subsequent work requires greater purity, sufficient quantity, or both.

Specific activity is particularly useful when the desired molecule has a distinctive catalytic action and an independent estimate of total protein can be obtained. However, the unit of activity depends on the reaction definition and assay conditions. Measurements made at different substrate concentrations or temperatures may not be directly comparable. For an enzyme following a simple saturation relationship, the velocity can change when substrate concentration changes even if enzyme quantity remains fixed. The fraction with higher measured activity is not necessarily the fraction containing more enzyme if assay conditions differ. To interpret a purification table, compare total activities measured under the same relevant conditions before calculating fold enrichment.

An illustrative purification series starts with 50 milligrams of total protein and 500 units of target activity. The clarified extract contains 30 milligrams and 450 units; a subsequent enriched fraction contains 5 milligrams and 250 units; a final fraction contains 2 milligrams and 120 units. The specific activities are respectively 10, 15, 50 and 60 units per milligram. The cumulative activity recoveries are 100, 90, 50 and 24 percent. The fold enrichment values relative to the crude preparation are 1, 1.5, 5 and 6. The final operation increases specific activity only from 50 to 60 units per milligram while losing more than half the activity entering that operation. A scientist needing substantial enzyme quantity might reasonably prefer the preceding fraction. A scientist performing a measurement highly sensitive to impurities may require the final fraction. The numbers alone cannot choose the objective.

Apparent protein purity can be influenced by the analytical detection system. For example, a general stain on a gel may stain different proteins with unequal efficiencies. If the intensity of the main band accounts for most of the total staining signal, this supports enrichment but does not establish exact mass fraction unless the staining responses are comparable and the signal lies in an appropriate range. A target-specific detection method can establish that a band corresponds to the target, but it may not reveal other proteins that the recognition reagent does not bind. A general stain and a selective detection method therefore answer complementary questions.

Homogeneity adds another dimension to protein quality. A fraction may contain a single polypeptide sequence but exist as a mixture of monomers, oligomers and aggregates. The composition is relatively pure in terms of sequence but heterogeneous in terms of molecular assembly. An activity measurement may preferentially reflect the active assembly. A size-sensitive measurement may reveal multiple populations even when a denaturing gel shows one polypeptide band. For a mechanistic study, the native assembly and its distribution can be as important as the purity of the sequence. Protein-quality assessments therefore extend beyond simple chemical purity.

A contaminant may be biologically significant even when its mass fraction is small. Suppose a protein preparation is intended for an assay that depends on a nucleic acid substrate. A trace nuclease may substantially alter the substrate without contributing appreciably to the total protein mass. Conversely, a moderate amount of a chemically inert contaminant might have little effect on a particular functional measurement. The relevant criterion is not solely the number of contaminating molecules but whether their properties interfere with the scientific objective. This explains why downstream functional controls remain necessary even after a preparation appears highly enriched.

Concentration, dilution and calibration

Concentration is quantity divided by volume or another explicitly stated amount of material. Mass concentration may be given in milligrams per millilitre or micrograms per millilitre; molar concentration expresses moles per litre. A conversion between mass concentration and molar concentration requires molecular mass. For a heterogeneous mixture of polymers with varying molecular masses, there may not be a unique meaningful molecular mass. A total protein assay calibrated in mass units should not be casually converted to moles of protein molecules.

A dilution reduces concentration by increasing the final volume while retaining the amount of analyte in the aliquot. For a conserved solute, the relation C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ follows directly from equality of the solute amounts before and after dilution. If a 20 microlitre aliquot is diluted to 200 microlitres, the dilution factor is 10. A measured diluted concentration of 12 micrograms per millilitre corresponds to 120 micrograms per millilitre in the original solution, assuming the measured response is valid and the dilution introduces no selective loss.

Absorbance is defined from the ratio of incident to transmitted light intensity, A = log₁₀(I₀/I). Under the assumptions of the Beer–Lambert relation, A = εlc, where ε is an extinction coefficient, l is the optical path length and c is concentration. If c is molar concentration and l is measured in centimetres, ε has units of litres per mole per centimetre. Absorbance itself is dimensionless. The relation applies to an absorbing species within an appropriate range under conditions where scattering and other deviations are negligible. A mixture may contain several absorbing species, whose ideal absorbances add at a given wavelength. A single wavelength then cannot distinguish arbitrary mixtures.

Calibration establishes the relationship between measured signal and known analyte quantities. A standard contains a known amount or concentration, while a blank accounts for background attributable to reagents or other defined components. In an illustrative linear model S = b + mc, S is measured signal, b is the intercept and m is the slope. Solving for the unknown concentration gives c = (S − b)/m. If b = 0.05, m = 0.004 absorbance units per microgram per millilitre, and S = 0.25, the inferred concentration is 50 micrograms per millilitre. A preceding fourfold dilution gives 200 micrograms per millilitre in the original extract.

A high signal does not always imply an analyte concentration within the reliable range. Detectors can saturate, reagent response can become nonlinear, and high concentrations can change the physical properties of a sample. Extrapolation beyond the calibrated range is unsafe because the assumed signal model has not been tested there. Dilution into a validated range permits an appropriate estimate when dilution behaviour is itself consistent. If serial dilutions produce different back-calculated original concentrations, the assay may be affected by matrix-dependent response, saturation or measurement error.

A calibration curve with a high coefficient of determination can still be biased if standards do not reproduce the response of the unknown matrix or if the model is unsuitable at the low end. Inspect the intercept, residuals and concentration range. In particular, a small signal near a large background has high relative uncertainty. A result reported as below the limit of quantification is not proof of zero analyte; it states that reliable numerical estimation is not established under the conditions of measurement.

Dimensional analysis prevents many mistakes in concentration problems. One milligram per millilitre equals one gram per litre, because the numerator and denominator each change by a factor of one thousand. One microgram per millilitre equals one milligram per litre and is numerically equal to one nanogram per microlitre. If a purified protein has a molecular mass of 50,000 grams per mole, a mass concentration of 1 milligram per millilitre, or 1 gram per litre, corresponds to 1 divided by 50,000 moles per litre, which is 20 micromolar. For a mixture of unknown proteins, this conversion would not define the total molar concentration without further composition information.

A dilution factor is the ratio of final volume to sample volume for the dilution being described. Adding 90 microlitres of diluent to 10 microlitres of sample produces a final volume of 100 microlitres and a factor of ten. Adding 100 microlitres to the same sample produces a final volume of 110 microlitres and a factor of eleven. The distinction between volume added and final volume is a common examination trap. In successive dilutions, factors multiply. For two successive fivefold dilutions, the final concentration is one twenty-fifth of the original concentration, provided the solute remains conserved and each dilution is made from the preceding solution.

Calibration curves can be plotted with concentration on the horizontal axis and measured response on the vertical axis. An unknown sample provides a vertical coordinate; interpolation along a valid calibration relationship provides the horizontal coordinate. The slope expresses sensitivity in a mathematical sense, namely change in signal per change in concentration. A steeper slope does not by itself guarantee a lower detection limit, since background variation and instrumental noise also matter. An assay with a very steep response but highly variable background might detect low concentrations less reliably than an assay with a smaller slope and stable background.

Consider a hypothetical standard series with concentrations of zero, 20, 40, 60 and 80 micrograms per millilitre and corresponding raw signals of 0.04, 0.14, 0.24, 0.34 and 0.44. The intercept is 0.04 and the slope is 0.005 absorbance units per microgram per millilitre. A sample with raw signal 0.29 corresponds to (0.29 minus 0.04) divided by 0.005, or 50 micrograms per millilitre. If the assay was calibrated using raw signals, subtraction of the intercept is already part of inversion. If the sample signal had been blank-corrected first, it would be 0.25, and the blank-corrected calibration would have an intercept near zero. Subtracting 0.04 again would underestimate concentration. The mathematical treatment must be consistent between standards and unknowns.

A dilution series offers a simple check on the applicability of calibration to an unknown matrix. Suppose two aliquots of one extract are diluted twofold and fourfold, and their measured concentrations are 80 and 40 micrograms per millilitre. Multiplication by their respective dilution factors yields 160 micrograms per millilitre in both cases. Agreement supports proportional response over that range. If the fourfold diluted sample instead gives 55 micrograms per millilitre, the back-calculated value is 220 micrograms per millilitre. The disagreement indicates that at least one assumption of the simple concentration estimate may be violated. Possibilities include saturation, a matrix effect that changes upon dilution, pipetting error or instability. The appropriate response is to investigate rather than average incompatible estimates blindly.

A standard addition experiment uses known added quantities to examine the response in the sample matrix. If the response is linear and the original analyte and added reference behave equivalently, the increase in signal following addition can be compared with the expected increase. A smaller increase may indicate suppression and a greater increase enhancement. Standard addition is not a universal correction: a chemical form of the added reference may behave differently from the endogenous analyte, and the addition itself may change the matrix. Exam reasoning should identify which assumptions are being tested rather than equate the phrase standard addition with complete elimination of all errors.

Detection and quantification have different thresholds. Detection concerns whether the signal can be distinguished from an appropriate background with defined confidence. Quantification requires a range in which an estimate is sufficiently reliable for the intended purpose. A trace amount may be detectable but not accurately measurable. If one treatment gives a value below a quantification threshold and another gives a measurable value, the former cannot automatically be assigned a concentration of zero. A fold change involving an unquantified denominator may not be reliably calculable. Such limitations should be stated rather than hidden by reporting excessive significant figures.

Instrumental measurements also have uncertainty in the optical path. A shorter path gives smaller absorbance for the same concentration under ideal conditions. Some microvolume instruments adjust the path and report absorbance normalised to a standard path. A textbook concentration factor referring to a one-centimetre path must be applied to the correctly normalised signal. If a raw absorbance of 0.10 was measured at 0.2 centimetres, the one-centimetre equivalent would be 0.50 under the linear model. Applying a one-centimetre conversion factor directly to 0.10 would underestimate the concentration by a factor of five.

Analytical controls and contamination

Controls are comparisons designed to test an explanation or identify a source of error. A reagent blank contains the relevant reagents without intentionally added analyte and estimates background arising from those reagents. A process blank is carried through the preparation sequence without the biological specimen and helps detect material introduced by handling, containers or reagents. A negative control is expected to lack the target response under the specific hypothesis. A positive control contains a suitable known target or otherwise produces an expected response, confirming that the assay is capable of detection in the tested conditions. These categories overlap in some designs, but they answer different questions.

Selecting a control for each alternative

An internal standard is a known reference measured alongside or within a sample to help interpret variation. An added spike is a known quantity of analyte introduced into the sample to assess how the matrix or preparation influences its recovery. A spike introduced before extraction tests more stages than one introduced after extraction. If the early spike is poorly recovered while the later spike responds normally, preparation-related loss is supported. If both spikes respond poorly, an analytical matrix effect or another shared problem is plausible. A freely dissolved spike may nevertheless behave differently from target bound inside intact cells, so good spike recovery does not prove complete liberation of endogenous material.

Contamination is the unintended addition or retention of material capable of altering the preparation or measurement. It may originate in the biological sample, another sample, equipment or reagents. Cross-contamination makes one sample contain material from another. Carryover is the residual influence of an earlier measurement or operation. A blank measured after a concentrated sample can provide evidence of carryover, whereas a process blank can help locate contamination introduced before measurement. Contaminants need not produce a detectable signal themselves; they may inhibit or enhance the target's response.

A pigment-rich leaf extract illustrates interference. Pigments can absorb visible light near wavelengths used by colorimetric assays, and suspended particles can scatter light. A reagent blank prepared without leaf material will not remove all such sample-dependent effects. A suitable sample background correction, a matrix-matched comparison, dilution behaviour or an orthogonal measurement may reveal the problem. Correcting background is justified only when the assumed contributions can be separated in the particular measurement.

Accuracy refers to closeness to an appropriate reference or accepted value, whereas precision describes agreement among repeated measurements. A precisely pipetted but incorrectly calibrated assay may give a consistent biased result. Technical replication can reveal measurement variability, but it cannot eliminate a common bias shared by all measurements. Biological replication provides information about variation among independent biological units. Randomising the order of samples or distributing groups across batches can reduce confounding between experimental group and measurement order. If all controls fail, a biological comparison should not be interpreted simply because its numerical pattern matches expectations.

Control selection is easiest when each control is tied to a possible alternative explanation. A positive control answers whether a measurement system can produce the expected response. It does not prove that the sample is free from interference, because a positive reference in a clean matrix may behave normally while an unknown in a complex matrix does not. A matrix spike tests the response of a reference introduced into the sample. A process blank examines whether handling or reagents introduce a signal even without specimen. A technical replicate evaluates the repeatability of the specified analytical operations. Each comparison reduces uncertainty about a different stage.

Suppose a carbohydrate assay on a leaf extract shows a high signal, the reagent blank is low, and a glucose reference gives its expected response. The observations show that the clean reagent system is functioning, but they do not exclude leaf pigments as an additional contributor. A sample-specific background measurement may be useful if the background is separable from the assay response. A complementary glucose-selective measurement tests a different aspect of the problem. The result must be interpreted in light of which carbohydrate class each method recognises, since a disagreement can arise from legitimate specificity differences as well as interference.

The numerical meaning of spike recovery is the incremental measured amount divided by the known added amount, multiplied by one hundred percent. In an illustrative experiment an unspiked sample contains an estimated 30 micrograms, and an otherwise equivalent sample to which 20 micrograms were added gives an estimated total of 45 micrograms. The measured increment is 15 micrograms and the apparent spike recovery is 75 percent. If the spike was introduced immediately before measurement, the poor recovery suggests a problem at the analytical stage or in interactions occurring after addition. If it was introduced before preparation, losses during preparation are also plausible. It would be incorrect to divide 45 by 20, because 45 includes the endogenous analyte.

A comparison between pre-extraction and post-extraction spikes can provide more specific evidence. Imagine that identical added amounts yield increments of 12 and 18 micrograms respectively when the intended increment is 20 micrograms. The apparent recoveries are 60 and 90 percent. If the added reference behaves comparably at both stages and the same matrix response applies, the ratio of these recoveries, two-thirds, is consistent with additional loss during preparation. That interpretation remains conditional: the pre-extraction reference may associate with components that are absent after extraction. The comparison is informative because it isolates an additional stage, not because it supplies an exact universal correction for endogenous material.

A common source of batch confounding occurs when all samples of one biological group are processed on one day and all samples of another group on a different day. Reagent age, instrument drift and storage duration may then correlate with biological condition. If the groups differ, the difference cannot be uniquely attributed to the biological factor. Distributing samples from both groups across preparation batches, recording batch information and including suitable reference samples help distinguish these explanations. Repeating multiple measurements of the same batch does not by itself remove the confounding, because all those measurements may share the same systematic shift.

Contamination can generate a positive signal or suppress one. For example, residual molecules that absorb ultraviolet radiation may inflate an apparent nucleic acid concentration. Other compounds may inhibit an enzymatic reaction without contributing much absorbance at the measurement wavelength. This second category is especially important: a normal spectrum cannot establish compatibility with a downstream enzyme. A functional control examining whether a known substrate is processed in the presence of the sample tests a different property from an optical purity ratio. A negative result in a functional assay is interpretable only if the assay and relevant controls behave as expected.

Blinding and randomisation are related but distinct approaches to avoiding bias. Randomisation changes the order or allocation of samples so that systematic time-dependent effects are less likely to align with group membership. Blinding prevents knowledge of group identity from influencing discretionary decisions, such as excluding a weak signal or selecting a region for measurement. Neither guarantees sample integrity or accurate calibration. Their value is in protecting the comparison from specific classes of bias. Methodological quality depends on an appropriate combination of preparation, measurement and experimental-design safeguards.

Reproducibility requires an adequate record of what was measured and how it was interpreted. Relevant records include the original biological unit, collection context, sample storage history, fraction identity, dilution factor, extract volume, calibration standard, analytical range and controls. Reporting only a final concentration makes it difficult to evaluate whether differences arose during extraction or measurement. When an examination presents incomplete metadata, the scientifically strongest answer may be that a particular conclusion cannot be established from the available measurements. This is not evasiveness; it is recognition of the evidential boundary.

Representative DNA measurements

DNA is a polymer of deoxyribonucleotides whose phosphate backbone is negatively charged under ordinary biological conditions. The bases contain aromatic systems that absorb ultraviolet light, which makes absorbance near 260 nanometres a convenient method for estimating nucleic acid concentration. The measurement is not exclusive to DNA. RNA, free nucleotides and other ultraviolet-absorbing constituents can contribute. The extinction behaviour also depends on base composition and molecular state because interactions among stacked bases influence absorption.

Optical purity and selective detection

For relatively pure double-stranded DNA, an absorbance of one at 260 nanometres normalised to a one-centimetre path is commonly approximated as 50 micrograms per millilitre. For a tenfold diluted sample with A₂₆₀ = 0.24, the approximate original DNA concentration would be 0.24 × 50 × 10 = 120 micrograms per millilitre. If the extract volume is 0.15 millilitres, the total estimated DNA amount is 18 micrograms. This calculation assumes that most of the absorbance arises from double-stranded DNA, the instrument and blank are appropriate, and the signal is in range.

The ratio A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ near 1.8 is often used as an approximate indicator for relatively pure DNA. It is not an absolute purity test. The ratio can vary with pH, ionic conditions, low concentrations, baseline correction and contaminants. A second ratio, A₂₆₀/A₂₃₀, may provide additional information about substances that absorb at shorter ultraviolet wavelengths. Neither ratio establishes whether the DNA is intact or whether a downstream enzyme will work. A sample with an apparently satisfactory ratio may still contain inhibitors or fragmented DNA.

Fluorescent measurements can improve selectivity by using dyes whose fluorescence changes upon binding a defined nucleic acid class. They require calibration and an understanding of dye specificity, and they are not independent of all sample conditions. Comparing a DNA-selective fluorescence estimate with a UV estimate can help identify additional UV-absorbing material, although disagreement does not uniquely identify a contaminant. Electrophoretic examination provides information about molecular size distribution. A DNA sample may have normal bulk concentration while containing much shorter fragments than required for a length-sensitive analysis.

An important distinction is between total DNA and a particular sequence. An absorbance measurement estimates an aggregate optical property; it does not reveal which genes are present or whether a sequence is represented at an expected abundance. Sequence-specific methods require an appropriate recognition principle and controls. Their detailed treatment follows in node 14.A.02.

The optical properties of nucleic acids illustrate why molecular state can affect a concentration estimate. Stacked bases in a double helix interact electronically, producing a lower ultraviolet absorbance than would be expected if the same bases were unstacked in certain comparable conditions. Separation of strands and loss of stacking can increase absorbance, a phenomenon called hyperchromicity. No additional nucleotides are necessarily present. Consequently an absorbance increase caused by a change in nucleic acid structure cannot always be interpreted as a mass increase. Conversion factors are empirical approximations for specified classes and conditions.

A useful hypothetical comparison involves two preparations measured after identical dilution. Preparation A has a normalised A₂₆₀ of 0.4 and gives a DNA-selective fluorescence estimate of 19 micrograms per millilitre in the measured solution. Preparation B has the same A₂₆₀ but gives a fluorescence estimate of only 8 micrograms per millilitre. The ultraviolet estimate is 20 micrograms per millilitre for both if the double-stranded DNA factor is used. For A the two estimates agree approximately. For B the discrepancy suggests that the UV assumption, dye response or both need investigation. RNA, free nucleotides or other UV absorbers are plausible contributors, but the disagreement alone does not identify their chemical identity.

An electrophoretic profile can support an integrity assessment by revealing whether material occurs primarily as large molecules or smaller fragments. A broad distribution extending to small sizes may be consistent with fragmentation. The amount loaded and the detection chemistry influence band and smear intensity, so intensity is not automatically a direct measure of the original sample amount. Comparing profiles is most informative when comparable amounts and conditions are used, and when the size range relevant to the intended application is identified. A single visible band at a particular apparent length establishes a size-related observation, not the complete sequence or chemical purity of all material in that band.

Genomic DNA and plasmid DNA provide a useful conceptual preview. Both are DNA and both absorb near 260 nanometres, but their size, topology and biological organisation differ. A circular DNA molecule may occur in forms whose electrophoretic mobility differs even at identical nucleotide length. A UV concentration estimate does not distinguish these forms. A preparation intended to recover long genomic fragments has different integrity requirements from one designed to examine small circular DNA. Recognising the target's topology and length helps determine the appropriate quality measurement. The specific isolation principles and sequence-selective analyses are treated in the following node.

A nucleic acid preparation can also contain chemically damaged bases without extensive fragmentation. A length-based measurement may look satisfactory while the ability of an enzyme to read or modify the molecule is altered. Conversely, a sample containing short but chemically intact fragments may be suitable for a short sequence assay. These examples show why the word intact must be defined for the intended purpose. Structural integrity can refer to backbone continuity, base chemistry or higher-order topology; a measurement sensitive to one of these properties does not necessarily measure the others.

Representative RNA measurements

RNA shares the ultraviolet-absorbing bases and phosphate backbone of DNA but differs in sugar chemistry and biological diversity. The RNA population in a eukaryotic cell includes ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, messenger RNA and numerous other species. Total RNA concentration does not directly measure any one transcript. It is possible for two samples with equal total RNA mass to have different transcript abundances or very different RNA quality.

RNA integrity and biological representation

A conventional approximation for relatively pure single-stranded RNA is 40 micrograms per millilitre per unit of A₂₆₀ at a one-centimetre optical path. A fivefold diluted sample with A₂₆₀ = 0.30 gives an estimated original concentration of 60 micrograms per millilitre. In 0.2 millilitres this corresponds to 12 micrograms. If genomic DNA contributes appreciably to the absorbance, the estimate cannot be interpreted as RNA alone. RNA-selective fluorescence or independent assessment of DNA contamination can provide complementary evidence.

RNA integrity is especially important when the intended measurement depends on long molecules or the representation of transcript regions. Breakage can produce fragments that still absorb ultraviolet radiation. A degraded preparation can therefore have an acceptable concentration and approximate purity ratios. Size-based analysis, including electrophoretic profiles, can reveal a shift toward smaller fragments. The RNA integrity number described by Schroeder and colleagues in 2006 summarises features of an electrophoretic profile into a quality metric. Its interpretation depends on sample type and intended application; it should not be treated as a universal guarantee for every transcript or every organism.

In 1987 Chomczynski and Sacchi described an RNA isolation approach that combined chemical disruption and differential partitioning. Its historical significance is that the chemical conditions used during release can protect or selectively recover a nucleic acid class while separating other material. This does not mean every extraction yields the same RNA population. The physical and chemical characteristics of tissues, the presence of inhibitory compounds and the recovery of small versus long molecules may affect the composition of the final sample.

A comparison of transcript measurements must consider whether the observed difference is biological or attributable to preparation. If one sample has undergone greater degradation, short regions may remain detectable while long contiguous regions are depleted. An assay targeted to a short region may therefore provide a different picture from a length-sensitive measurement. Independent integrity information and comparable sample handling strengthen the interpretation. Detailed isolation and specific sequence detection are considered in the next chapter.

A ribosomal RNA profile often provides a convenient view of total RNA integrity because ribosomal RNA constitutes a substantial part of the total RNA mass in many cells. However, the abundance of ribosomal RNA is not equivalent to the abundance of messenger RNA. An integrity score based on prominent rRNA features does not directly prove that every messenger RNA has been preserved. Different organisms and sample types may also show different characteristic profiles. An examiner may provide a conventional profile from mammalian total RNA, but its interpretation cannot be transferred without qualification to every biological preparation.

The problem of comparing total RNA yield across tissues illustrates the importance of biological denominators. Two tissue extracts may contain equal RNA amounts per millilitre of extract but originate from different amounts of tissue. Even after normalisation to tissue mass, differences in cell number, cell size or ribosome abundance may influence total RNA. Equal total RNA input in a downstream comparison standardises one aspect of the analytical procedure but does not mean equal numbers of cells or equal amounts of every transcript. The normalisation basis must match the inference being made.

A degraded RNA sample may produce short fragments that still contribute to absorbance or dye-based fluorescence, depending on dye specificity and fragment properties. If a length-sensitive analysis is required, bulk concentration is insufficient. For an illustrative comparison, sample X and Y each contain an estimated 10 micrograms of RNA, but X has most of its material in large RNA species while Y has a broad distribution of small fragments. The two quantities are equal by the selected assay, but their usability for a measurement that depends on long continuous RNA differs. For a short region assay, the difference may be less severe, though representation bias can remain.

RNA measurements are also susceptible to DNA contamination because both molecules share related optical properties. If an experiment aims to measure a particular RNA using a downstream method that generates a DNA copy, pre-existing DNA can contribute to a signal under some assay designs. An appropriate conceptual control omits the reverse transcription step while retaining the downstream detection logic. Signal in this control supports a DNA-derived contribution or another background source. Absence of signal reduces that concern for the tested target but does not guarantee that all other targets are free of contamination. Detailed nucleic acid analysis follows in 14.A.02.

An inhibitor can make two equally concentrated RNA samples appear different in a downstream enzymatic measurement. If a defined reference performs poorly when measured in the presence of one extract but normally in a clean comparison, suppression by the sample matrix becomes a plausible explanation. A good integrity profile alone would not exclude this problem. The analytical quality assessment should therefore match the downstream goal, combining quantity, size-related evidence and compatibility controls when each is relevant.

Representative protein measurements

Protein analysis may ask how much protein is present, whether a particular protein is present, how large it is, or whether it is functionally active. These are different questions. Proteins vary in sequence, molecular mass, structure and chemical modification. A method based on interaction with amino acid side chains may respond differently to equal masses of different proteins. A mass estimate obtained using a reference protein is therefore a calibrated estimate, not a direct count of all peptide bonds or molecules.

Comparing mass assays and activity

Absorbance at 280 nanometres is influenced especially by tryptophan and tyrosine residues, with contributions from disulphide bonds. A purified protein with a known extinction coefficient can be quantified using Beer–Lambert reasoning. For crude material the extinction coefficient of the mixture is generally unknown, and nucleic acids or other chromophores can contribute. A protein lacking aromatic residues may produce little 280-nanometre absorbance relative to a protein rich in such residues. Equal protein mass does not necessarily produce equal UV response.

The biuret reaction depends on complex formation involving copper and peptide bonds under alkaline conditions. The Lowry method, described in 1951, combines copper-dependent protein chemistry with a colour-generating reaction involving the Folin reagent. Bradford's 1976 method uses the change in spectral properties of Coomassie Brilliant Blue upon association with proteins, with measurement commonly near 595 nanometres. The methods differ in sensitivity, chemical response and susceptibility to interfering compounds. Detergents can affect dye-based assays, while reducing substances can influence copper-based assays. Method choice requires compatibility with the preparation matrix.

In a calibration based on bovine serum albumin, an unknown protein whose dye response per unit mass is lower than that of albumin will be underestimated if the response difference is ignored. Replicate wells can yield closely agreeing values while retaining this systematic error. If the objective is to compare the same target in similar matrices, a relative comparison may still be useful, but changes in sample composition can alter the response. The reference protein and measurement basis should be reported.

Protein function is commonly investigated using an activity assay when a relevant biological reaction is known. An enzyme activity unit is conventionally the amount catalysing conversion of one micromole of substrate per minute under specified conditions. It is a rate-based measure. An inactive enzyme can still contribute to total protein assays and may remain visible on a gel. Conversely, removal of an inhibitor can increase activity without increasing target protein mass. These distinctions underpin the purification calculations developed earlier in the chapter.

The choice between protein mass assays is often determined by the matrix rather than by a simple ranking of sensitivity. An absorbance measurement can be convenient for a purified protein whose extinction coefficient is known and whose solution lacks interfering UV absorbers. A dye-binding assay can be useful for dilute mixtures, provided the response is calibrated and compatible with the solution chemistry. Copper-based assays provide another family of responses involving peptide-associated chemistry and, depending on the method, subsequent reactions. Different preparation buffers can interfere with these families of methods differently. The correct reasoning is to identify the chemical basis of the signal and the substances present in the sample.

Consider two purified proteins, P and Q, at the same mass concentration. P contains more tryptophan per unit mass than Q. Under comparable conditions P may show greater absorbance at 280 nanometres. If both are calculated using the same arbitrary mass extinction factor, their inferred mass concentrations can differ despite their true equality. The same principle applies to a dye-binding assay if the proteins differ in their interactions with the dye. A calibration made using P can be an excellent reference for P but a biased reference for Q. This limitation is especially relevant when comparing tissue extracts whose protein composition changes substantially.

An activity assay and a mass assay can be combined to estimate the functional state of a preparation. Suppose two samples contain the same amount of a target enzyme as measured by a suitable selective mass method. The first produces product at 20 micromoles per minute and the second at 10 micromoles per minute under identical assay conditions. The difference indicates a lower measured catalytic capacity per unit target mass in the second sample. Possible explanations include partial denaturation, inhibition, altered modification or a different proportion of active assembly states. The data do not establish a unique molecular mechanism without further evidence.

An enzyme may be present in several molecular environments in a cell. A fractionation that separates the enzyme from a necessary cofactor can lower activity while leaving the polypeptide intact. If the appropriate cofactor is restored under comparable assay conditions and activity increases, that response supports a cofactor-related explanation, although other effects of the added substance must be considered. If no increase occurs, irreversible damage or another limitation remains plausible. The control illustrates that biochemical function depends on the system in which the protein is measured, not merely on the presence of a band of the expected size.

Protein assays often use an aliquot of a fraction, while purification balances refer to the whole fraction. If an assay estimates 3 milligrams per millilitre in an original fraction of 4 millilitres, the total protein is 12 milligrams. If an activity measurement gives 15 units per millilitre, total activity is 60 units. The specific activity can be calculated either as total activity divided by total protein, 60 divided by 12, or as activity concentration divided by mass concentration, 15 divided by 3. Both give 5 units per milligram when they refer to the same solution. Mixing total activity with a protein concentration without accounting for volume produces a quantity with incorrect units.

Not all proteins are enzymes, and specific enzyme activity is not an appropriate universal indicator of protein purity. For a structural protein or a binding protein, a different target-selective measurement may be required. A binding assay can demonstrate an interaction under the tested conditions but may not establish every structural property. A size measurement may reveal aggregation but not biological specificity. The analytical framework remains the same: identify the relevant property, choose a measurement that responds to that property, and test alternative sources of signal.

Representative carbohydrate measurements

Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, oligosaccharides and polysaccharides. Their analysis must distinguish the target class from its constituent building blocks. A measurement of reducing sugar is not equivalent to a measurement of all carbohydrates. Reducing behaviour depends on whether an appropriate anomeric centre is available to participate in the relevant chemistry. Sucrose has both anomeric centres engaged in its glycosidic bond and is nonreducing under ordinary reducing-sugar assay conditions, whereas maltose has an available reducing end.

Chemical response and standard equivalents

Polysaccharides contain many sugar residues but can have relatively few reducing ends per unit mass. If hydrolysis converts a polymer into smaller sugars, the number of reducing molecules can increase even though the original carbohydrate material has not increased. The chemical conversion changes the relationship between mass and signal. An investigator seeking total polymer abundance must distinguish the measured hydrolysis products from the original polymer and account for conversion efficiency and the relevant mass basis.

Broad carbohydrate colour assays, such as the phenol–sulfuric acid chemistry described by DuBois and colleagues in 1956, generate coloured products from sugar-derived compounds. They can estimate total carbohydrate equivalents using a chosen standard, but different sugars may produce unequal responses. A glucose calibration does not establish that all detected sugars are glucose. A reducing-sugar method emphasises a different chemical property. Enzyme-linked glucose measurements offer a more selective route when the specific analyte is glucose rather than total carbohydrate. Each method therefore answers a distinct question.

Consider an illustrative sample in which a broad carbohydrate assay reports 100 micrograms of glucose equivalents per millilitre but a glucose-selective assay reports 20 micrograms of glucose per millilitre. The measurements are not necessarily contradictory. Other carbohydrates may contribute to the broad response. Nor is the numerical difference necessarily 80 micrograms per millilitre of other carbohydrate by actual mass, because their response factors may differ from glucose. The correct interpretation preserves the meaning of glucose equivalents.

Plant materials may contain pigments, soluble sugars, starch and cell-wall polysaccharides. A water-soluble fraction does not represent every carbohydrate originally present. A change in the fraction recovered could indicate altered extractability rather than changed total carbohydrate abundance. A comparison should specify which chemical fraction was measured, whether any polymer conversion was involved and what standard defines the reported result.

Hydrolysis of a disaccharide provides a simple example of how the mass basis of an analytical conversion can matter. Maltose consists of two glucose residues joined by a glycosidic bond. Hydrolysis adds the elements of water to generate two free glucose molecules. For a large starch polymer, the approximate mass per anhydroglucose residue is 162 grams per mole, whereas free glucose has a molecular mass of about 180 grams per mole. Under the idealised assumption of complete conversion of a long polymer to glucose and no other glucose source, 100 milligrams of measured glucose corresponds to about 90 milligrams of original anhydroglucose residues. The ratio follows from 162 divided by 180. End-group effects and incomplete conversion limit the approximation, especially for short oligomers.

The same result would not be justified when free glucose was already present in the original sample and its contribution was not distinguished. If the total glucose measured after hydrolysis was 100 milligrams, but 20 milligrams were originally free glucose, only the increase of 80 milligrams could be attributed to the converted material under the simplified assumptions. Multiplying by the anhydroglucose factor gives 72 milligrams of polymer residue mass. A student must therefore identify whether the measured quantity refers to total products, products newly released or pre-existing soluble sugars.

Reducing-sugar chemistry also illustrates the distinction between the number of reacting groups and the mass of a molecule. A long linear polysaccharide may contain many glycosidic linkages but only one reducing end per chain. Cleavage of that chain can generate multiple molecules with additional reducing ends. A reducing-sugar measurement can therefore be useful for following hydrolysis even when total carbohydrate mass is nearly unchanged. This logic underlies many conceptual enzyme-activity measurements in carbohydrate metabolism. It does not imply that reducing power uniquely identifies a particular monosaccharide.

A broad carbohydrate colour assay responds to chemical derivatives formed from carbohydrate constituents, and different sugar types can contribute unequal signal per unit mass. Suppose a calibration defines one response unit per microgram of glucose but an unknown sugar gives only 0.5 response units per microgram. A sample containing 40 micrograms of the unknown sugar would produce 20 response units and be reported as 20 micrograms of glucose equivalents under that calibration. The estimate is correct as an equivalent value but not as an actual mass of the unknown sugar. When a mixture is unknown, the equivalent unit is an honest description of the measurement rather than evidence of its exact composition.

Plant samples illustrate why a preparation may distinguish soluble and insoluble carbohydrate fractions. Sugars associated with the aqueous portion of an extract and structural polysaccharides remaining in insoluble material play different biological roles. A change in the soluble fraction during germination may reflect conversion of storage polymer into soluble products. To support this mechanism, the experiment should follow both fractions on comparable biological bases and distinguish newly produced sugar from differences in extractability. Measurements of a single fraction cannot establish the complete material balance.

Representative lipid measurements

Lipids are a chemically diverse group rather than a single family of polymers built from one repeating monomer. Triacylglycerols, phospholipids, sterols and other hydrophobic or amphipathic molecules differ in polarity and biological function. Their common analytical challenge is that many do not dissolve readily in water and may associate with membranes, proteins or other lipids. The term total lipid is consequently operational: it depends on the scope and effectiveness of the extraction and measurement.

Bulk and class-selective measurements

Folch, Lees and Sloane Stanley published a classical total-lipid isolation method in 1957. The historical importance of such approaches lies in selective partitioning of biological constituents between chemically different environments. The concept does not imply that every lipid class has identical partition behaviour. More polar lipids and molecules associated with proteins can behave differently from neutral storage lipids. Thus recovery should be interpreted for the particular lipid class and biological matrix.

Gravimetry estimates the mass of recovered material by weighing. A lipid-rich residue may contain residual solvent, nonlipid impurities or water. An apparent mass therefore need not equal the mass of pure lipid. Colorimetric total-lipid methods estimate a chemical response relative to an appropriate reference, and class-specific enzymatic assays report particular lipid constituents rather than all lipids. Chromatographic separation allows different molecular populations to be distinguished; the detailed principles of retention and resolution follow in node 14.A.05.

A seed's lipid content can be expressed relative to fresh mass, dry mass or total recovered extract. These denominators have different meanings. A dehydrated seed sample may contain more lipid per gram of fresh material merely because it contains less water. If a group comparison concerns allocation of carbon to storage lipid, dry-mass normalisation may be informative alongside the total mass of lipid per seed. Neither normalisation answers every question: a developmental change in seed size can alter total lipid per seed while leaving the dry-mass fraction stable.

Oxidation and enzymatic conversion can change lipid composition after collection. A total mass estimate may remain similar while the molecular identities of lipids change. When the purpose is to compare lipid species, a measurement that distinguishes chemical identity is needed; bulk extraction alone is insufficient. The wider lesson is that separation, recovery and analytical detection must be matched to the biological claim.

The structure of a triacylglycerol differs from that of a phospholipid. A triacylglycerol has three fatty acyl chains esterified to glycerol and is relatively nonpolar. Many phospholipids contain a polar head group and hydrophobic chains, giving them amphipathic character. Their biological association with membranes follows from this structure. During extraction these properties can influence the distribution of different lipid classes among phases, interfaces and residual material. A method that efficiently recovers neutral storage lipids is not automatically equally effective for every membrane lipid.

For a hypothetical distribution between two liquid phases, define a partition ratio K as the equilibrium concentration in the organic-rich phase divided by that in the aqueous-rich phase under specified conditions. If K is 4 and the phase volumes are equal, the organic-rich phase contains four times the amount in the aqueous-rich phase, corresponding to eighty percent of the combined quantity. If the aqueous phase is twice the volume of the organic phase, the amounts are proportional to 4 × 1 and 1 × 2, so the organic-rich phase contains two-thirds of the total. The partition ratio is unchanged but the recovered proportion is different because concentration must be multiplied by phase volume. This is an ideal equilibrium illustration; real mixtures can exhibit interfaces, multiple chemical states and nonideal behaviour.

A lipid mass comparison must identify what was actually weighed. An extract residue could contain compounds other than lipid. If a nominal lipid fraction has mass 30 milligrams and independent measurements identify 5 milligrams of residual nonlipid material, the corrected lipid estimate would be 25 milligrams under the simplified assumption that the remaining material is entirely lipid. If the starting dry tissue mass was 500 milligrams, the corrected percentage is five percent. Reporting the uncorrected residue gives six percent. A small contaminant can thus produce a substantial relative bias when the measured fraction is small.

Oxidative alteration of lipids is especially relevant to measurements concerned with unsaturated molecular species. Chemical conversion can alter the composition of a lipid population while leaving an appreciable amount of material recoverable by a bulk extraction. A mass measurement may therefore be relatively insensitive to changes in molecular species. Class-selective measurements or separations based on chemical properties provide information at a different level. The method should distinguish the species relevant to the question rather than infer molecular preservation from bulk yield.

Normalisation by the biological unit is again crucial. Consider two seeds with dry masses of 100 and 200 milligrams and lipid contents of 20 and 30 milligrams. The larger seed contains more lipid in total, but the smaller seed has a higher lipid fraction of dry mass, twenty percent compared with fifteen percent. Both observations are correct. The first is relevant to total resource content per seed, the second to relative allocation of dry material. If the seeds differed greatly in water content, fresh-mass percentages might give a different ranking again. Methods questions frequently test this distinction because a change in denominator can reverse a comparison without any error in the arithmetic.

A lipid-selective signal can measure a chemical group rather than the entire lipid molecule. For instance, an assay directed at a particular lipid class may not include other classes in its estimate. Two samples could contain the same total lipid mass but different proportions of storage and membrane-associated lipids. A total mass measurement would miss this compositional difference, while a class-selective measurement could reveal it. Conversely, one class alone cannot represent total lipid abundance. The most informative interpretation specifies the measured class, extraction basis and analytical standard.

Integrated interpretation: storage reserve mobilisation

An integrated comparison brings the principles together. Consider an illustrative study of storage reserve mobilisation in germinating seeds. The biological question is whether the soluble sugar pool increases as stored carbohydrate is mobilised, rather than whether a single extract gives a stronger colour response. Independent seeds from comparable developmental groups form the biological units. Their masses and sampling histories matter because water uptake during germination can alter fresh mass without a proportionate change in stored dry material. Measurements should therefore distinguish total amount per seed from abundance relative to dry mass.

Suppose the ungerminated group gives an average extract volume of 2 millilitres with 10 milligrams of measured soluble glucose equivalents per millilitre, while the germinating group gives 4 millilitres with 8 milligrams per millilitre. The measured concentrations suggest a decrease from 10 to 8, yet the corresponding totals increase from 20 to 32 milligrams of glucose equivalents per preparation. The concentration has fallen because the extract volume is greater, not because the total measured soluble carbohydrate necessarily fell. The distinction matters before any inference about carbohydrate mobilisation is made.

Now suppose that a glucose-selective assay estimates 4 milligrams per preparation in the ungerminated group and 14 milligrams in the germinating group. The increase supports an increase in measured glucose under comparable assay conditions. It does not prove that every molecule responsible for the broad carbohydrate response is glucose. If a separate estimate of polymer-derived carbohydrate decreases from 50 to 35 milligrams on a comparable residue-mass basis, the two observations are consistent with mobilisation of stored carbohydrate. They are not by themselves a closed carbon balance because other sugars, respiration and other metabolic destinations may contribute.

An appropriate reference spike might show comparable responses in both final extract matrices, while a process blank checks for introduced carbohydrate signal. These controls improve confidence in the analytical comparison but cannot prove that all carbohydrate was released from both tissues with identical efficiency. A comparison of the discarded solid fractions or an independent measurement of the original carbohydrate pool would address that remaining possibility. If the two biological groups differ systematically in tissue disruption or extraction behaviour, the measured difference could be partly methodological.

The strongest conclusion is therefore specific: under the defined illustrative preparation and validated analytical conditions, soluble carbohydrate equivalents and glucose increase per preparation while the measured polymer-derived fraction decreases. Establishing the responsible enzymatic mechanism would additionally require relevant enzyme measurements and experimental evidence linking their activity to the change. This integrated reasoning illustrates how sample preparation, quantitative interpretation and biological mechanism support one another without collapsing distinct measurements into a single claim.

Original examination practice

These original questions are designed for NET and SET preparation. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

Part B — Conceptual foundations

1. Which term denotes the particular chemical constituent that an assay intends to measure?

  1. Matrix
  2. Analyte
  3. Blank
  4. Aliquot
Answer and explanation

B. Analyte

The analyte is the substance of interest. The matrix consists of its surroundings and other constituents; an aliquot is a portion of a sample, and a blank provides a background comparison.

2. Which statement distinguishes fractionation from purification?

  1. Fractionation guarantees pure components.
  2. Purification necessarily increases total target mass.
  3. Fractionation produces portions with different compositions; purification enriches a defined target relative to impurities.
  4. The terms refer only to optical assays.
Answer and explanation

C. Fractionation produces portions with different compositions; purification enriches a defined target relative to impurities.

Fractionation separates portions by physical or chemical properties; purification has a target-specific enrichment objective. Neither guarantees complete purity nor creation of target.

3. What does a supernatant denote after sedimentation?

  1. The liquid remaining above sedimented material
  2. Only the target protein
  3. A pure nuclear fraction
  4. The precipitate alone
Answer and explanation

A. The liquid remaining above sedimented material

The supernatant is defined by its location after separation, not by its chemical composition. It can contain suspended or dissolved constituents.

4. Which is the most direct measure of polymer backbone continuity among these choices?

  1. Total UV absorbance
  2. Sample volume
  3. A260/A280 ratio
  4. A size-distribution measurement
Answer and explanation

D. A size-distribution measurement

A size profile can detect fragmentation. Bulk absorbance and purity ratios may remain acceptable when the polymer is broken; volume says nothing about length.

5. A protein denatures without peptide-bond cleavage. Which is possible?

  1. Its mass must become zero.
  2. Its catalytic function declines while a general protein mass signal persists.
  3. Its amino acid sequence must change.
  4. All protein assays become impossible.
Answer and explanation

B. Its catalytic function declines while a general protein mass signal persists.

Denaturation alters folding and can abolish activity without destroying the peptide backbone. Mass and function are different properties.

6. What is the dimension of absorbance?

  1. Moles per litre
  2. Centimetres
  3. Dimensionless
  4. Grams per litre
Answer and explanation

C. Dimensionless

Absorbance is the logarithm of an intensity ratio, which has no dimensions. Concentration and path length are separate terms in Beer–Lambert's relation.

7. Which pair of units is numerically equivalent?

  1. mg/mL and g/L
  2. µg/mL and g/L
  3. mg/L and mg/mL
  4. ng/µL and mg/mL
Answer and explanation

A. mg/mL and g/L

Both milligram and millilitre convert by the same factor to gram and litre, so mg/mL = g/L. The other listed pairs differ by powers of one thousand.

8. Which property is measured by specific enzyme activity expressed in U/mg?

  1. The mass fraction of DNA
  2. The number of cells
  3. Activity per unit volume only
  4. Catalytic activity per unit total protein mass
Answer and explanation

D. Catalytic activity per unit total protein mass

Specific activity normalises the reaction rate to total protein mass. It can support purification analysis but is not itself an absolute purity percentage.

9. Which is a common approximate one-centimetre A260 conversion for relatively pure dsDNA?

  1. 40 µg/mL per unit
  2. 50 µg/mL per unit
  3. 280 µg/mL per unit
  4. 1 µg/mL per unit
Answer and explanation

B. 50 µg/mL per unit

The conventional dsDNA approximation is 50 µg/mL per A260 unit; 40 is commonly used for RNA. The factor is conditional on optical and sample assumptions.

10. Which is a common approximate A260/A280 ratio for relatively pure RNA?

  1. 0.2
  2. 5.0
  3. 2.0
  4. 0.02
Answer and explanation

C. 2.0

Approximately 2.0 is a typical RNA indicator under suitable measurement conditions. It is not a universal acceptance criterion or an integrity measurement.

11. What causes the greater susceptibility of RNA to alkaline backbone cleavage relative to DNA?

  1. The ribose 2′ hydroxyl group
  2. The absence of all phosphate groups
  3. The absence of bases
  4. A peptide bond in RNA
Answer and explanation

A. The ribose 2′ hydroxyl group

The 2′ hydroxyl group participates in a chemical route to phosphodiester cleavage. DNA lacks that group but can be damaged by other mechanisms.

12. Which assay is based on the spectral change of Coomassie Brilliant Blue?

  1. Biuret
  2. Direct A260
  3. Reducing-sugar assay
  4. Bradford
Answer and explanation

D. Bradford

Bradford uses dye association with proteins. Biuret uses copper–peptide chemistry; A260 measures UV absorption; reducing-sugar assays test carbohydrate chemistry.

13. What chemical feature strongly contributes to protein absorbance near 280 nm?

  1. The number of lipid droplets
  2. Aromatic residues, particularly tryptophan and tyrosine
  3. The number of RNA molecules only
  4. The number of reducing sugar ends
Answer and explanation

B. Aromatic residues, particularly tryptophan and tyrosine

Aromatic amino acids contribute substantially to A280. The extinction coefficient depends on protein composition; other UV absorbers can interfere.

14. Which carbohydrate is ordinarily nonreducing because both anomeric centres participate in the linkage?

  1. Glucose
  2. Maltose
  3. Sucrose
  4. Lactose
Answer and explanation

C. Sucrose

Sucrose links both anomeric centres. Glucose, maltose and lactose have an available reducing form under ordinary assay conditions.

15. What does a glucose-equivalent total carbohydrate result indicate?

  1. Signal expressed relative to a glucose standard
  2. Proof that all carbohydrate is glucose
  3. A direct count of every polymer chain
  4. Exact mass regardless of sugar identity
Answer and explanation

A. Signal expressed relative to a glucose standard

The unit expresses calibrated response relative to glucose. Other carbohydrates may have different response factors; the method is not a glucose-specific identity test.

16. Which structural feature explains the amphipathic character of typical phospholipids?

  1. Only a neutral hydrocarbon chain
  2. Only a peptide backbone
  3. Only a DNA-binding site
  4. Polar head and hydrophobic chains
Answer and explanation

D. Polar head and hydrophobic chains

Distinct polar and nonpolar regions enable interactions with water and hydrophobic environments. Neutral storage lipids generally lack a comparable polar head.

17. Which control is best suited to detecting material introduced during sample preparation?

  1. A pure target standard alone
  2. A process blank
  3. A replicate of only the unknown
  4. A larger sample volume
Answer and explanation

B. A process blank

A process blank passes through the preparation without specimen. It can reveal contamination from handling or reagents that a clean positive standard cannot.

18. Which describes an independent biological replicate in a plant comparison?

  1. Two readings of one well
  2. Two aliquots of one homogenate
  3. A separately sampled independent plant
  4. Two calculations from one signal
Answer and explanation

C. A separately sampled independent plant

Independent plants represent biological units. Aliquots and repeated readings measure technical variation rather than adding independent biological observations.

19. What is a key limitation of a single electrophoretic band as proof of absolute purity?

  1. Different molecules may co-migrate or remain below detection.
  2. A band can never contain proteins.
  3. A band always proves catalytic function.
  4. Electrophoresis cannot separate any molecules.
Answer and explanation

A. Different molecules may co-migrate or remain below detection.

Co-migration and detection limits mean a single band is evidence of apparent homogeneity under the method, not exhaustive purity or functional evidence.

20. What does a value below the limit of quantification establish?

  1. The target is definitely absent.
  2. The target concentration is negative.
  3. The true value equals the calibration intercept.
  4. A reliable numerical estimate has not been established at that level.
Answer and explanation

D. A reliable numerical estimate has not been established at that level.

The threshold concerns quantitative reliability. A target may exist below it; the result cannot be replaced automatically by zero.

Part C — Analytical reasoning

21. An extract is 4 mg/mL in 5 mL. Its retained fraction is 10 mg/mL in 1 mL. What are recovery and concentration enrichment?

  1. 100%, 2.5-fold
  2. 50%, 2.5-fold
  3. 25%, 10-fold
  4. 200%, 0.5-fold
Answer and explanation

B. 50%, 2.5-fold

Input = 4 × 5 = 20 mg; retained = 10 × 1 = 10 mg. Recovery is 10/20 × 100 = 50%. Concentration enrichment is 10/4 = 2.5. Concentration and total amount are not interchangeable.

22. Three successive stages retain 80%, 75% and 50% of their respective inputs. What is overall recovery?

  1. 68.3%
  2. 205%
  3. 30%
  4. 50%
Answer and explanation

C. 30%

The retained fractions multiply: 0.8 × 0.75 × 0.5 = 0.30. Averaging or adding percentages ignores the changing denominator.

23. Input target is 120 µg. Product, wash and discard contain 60, 30 and 24 µg. What is product recovery and mass-balance closure?

  1. 50% and 95%
  2. 95% and 50%
  3. 50% and 75%
  4. 60% and 114%
Answer and explanation

A. 50% and 95%

Product recovery = 60/120 = 50%. Accounted total = 114 µg; closure = 114/120 = 95%. Closure includes the nonproduct fractions.

24. A crude extract has 200 U and 100 mg protein; a purified fraction has 90 U and 9 mg. What is the purification fold?

  1. 0.45
  2. 10
  3. 2
  4. 5
Answer and explanation

D. 5

Specific activities are 200/100 = 2 U/mg and 90/9 = 10 U/mg. The ratio is 5. Activity recovery is 45%, a different measure.

25. A purification step reduces enzyme mass by 20% but activity increases from 80 to 100 U. Which explanation is consistent with both results?

  1. Mass was necessarily created.
  2. The step may have removed an inhibitor.
  3. Activity equals molecule count regardless of conditions.
  4. Both mass measurements must be false.
Answer and explanation

B. The step may have removed an inhibitor.

Removal of an inhibitor can increase rate per enzyme molecule. The activity recovery is 125%, but this is not a mass balance.

26. A 50 kDa pure protein is present at 2 mg/mL. Its molar concentration is approximately:

  1. 4 µM
  2. 20 µM
  3. 40 µM
  4. 100 µM
Answer and explanation

C. 40 µM

2 mg/mL = 2 g/L; divide by 50,000 g/mol to obtain 4 × 10⁻⁵ M = 40 µM. The conversion needs a defined molecular mass.

27. A sample is diluted 1:5 (fivefold) and then fourfold. The final measured concentration is 7 µg/mL. What is the original concentration?

  1. 140 µg/mL
  2. 35 µg/mL
  3. 28 µg/mL
  4. 1.4 µg/mL
Answer and explanation

A. 140 µg/mL

The cumulative factor is 5 × 4 = 20. Original concentration = 7 × 20 = 140 µg/mL assuming conserved analyte and proportional response.

28. A raw calibration follows S = 0.03 + 0.006c. The diluted sample gives S = 0.27 and dilution factor 3. Original concentration?

  1. 40 µg/mL
  2. 135 µg/mL
  3. 45 µg/mL
  4. 120 µg/mL
Answer and explanation

D. 120 µg/mL

Measured c = (0.27 − 0.03)/0.006 = 40 µg/mL. Multiply by three for 120 µg/mL. Ignoring the intercept gives 135, which is incorrect.

29. A dsDNA preparation gives raw A260 = 0.12 at 0.2 cm path, without prior dilution. What is its approximate concentration under ideal assumptions?

  1. 6 µg/mL
  2. 30 µg/mL
  3. 12 µg/mL
  4. 60 µg/mL
Answer and explanation

B. 30 µg/mL

Normalise to 1 cm: 0.12/0.2 = 0.60. Apply 50 µg/mL per normalised unit to obtain 30 µg/mL.

30. A fivefold diluted RNA sample has normalised A260 = 0.25 and original volume 0.4 mL. What is approximate total RNA?

  1. 10 µg
  2. 50 µg
  3. 20 µg
  4. 100 µg
Answer and explanation

C. 20 µg

Original concentration = 0.25 × 40 × 5 = 50 µg/mL. Total amount = 50 × 0.4 = 20 µg, assuming the signal is predominantly RNA.

31. A DNA extract gives A260/A280 = 1.8 but shows only small fragments on a size profile. Which is justified?

  1. Optical purity may be satisfactory while long-fragment integrity is poor.
  2. Both methods must be wrong.
  3. The preparation is necessarily ideal for long-read measurement.
  4. DNA concentration must be zero.
Answer and explanation

A. Optical purity may be satisfactory while long-fragment integrity is poor.

The ratio is not a length measurement. Small fragments can retain UV absorption, so acceptable optical ratios and poor long-fragment integrity can coexist.

32. Two DNA preparations show equal A260; one has half the dsDNA-selective fluorescence signal. What is the strongest inference?

  1. The low-fluorescence sample contains exactly 50% RNA.
  2. Both have identical dsDNA concentration.
  3. The instrument must be broken.
  4. The UV and selective measurements disagree, requiring investigation of specificity or matrix effects.
Answer and explanation

D. The UV and selective measurements disagree, requiring investigation of specificity or matrix effects.

UV responds to multiple absorbers; selective dye response also depends on conditions. The disagreement does not uniquely identify or quantify RNA contamination.

33. An RNA sample has normal UV ratios but a downstream RNA-dependent test is weak. An added defined reference is also suppressed in that matrix. What does this support?

  1. RNA must have no phosphate.
  2. A matrix-dependent analytical inhibition is plausible.
  3. The reference proves there is no inhibitor.
  4. The total RNA mass necessarily doubled.
Answer and explanation

B. A matrix-dependent analytical inhibition is plausible.

Suppression of a defined reference in the extract supports an analytical effect. It does not rule out additional integrity problems, but a normal optical ratio cannot exclude inhibition.

34. A reference spike of 25 µg is added to a sample whose unspiked estimate is 40 µg. Spiked estimate is 55 µg. Apparent recovery?

  1. 220%
  2. 137.5%
  3. 60%
  4. 15%
Answer and explanation

C. 60%

Measured increment is 55 − 40 = 15 µg. Recovery = 15/25 × 100 = 60%. The total sample signal includes endogenous material.

35. Identical early and late spikes give recoveries of 50% and 100%. What is the most defensible interpretation?

  1. A loss associated with preparation is supported for the added reference.
  2. Exactly half of every endogenous target is missing.
  3. The late analytical response is necessarily invalid.
  4. No information can be obtained.
Answer and explanation

A. A loss associated with preparation is supported for the added reference.

Only the early reference experiences the entire preparation. The comparison supports preparation-associated loss, but a dissolved reference may not represent bound endogenous material.

36. Twofold dilution yields 70 µg/mL; fourfold dilution yields 50 µg/mL. What should be done with the original concentration estimates?

  1. Average 70 and 50.
  2. Report 120 without qualification.
  3. Report 35 because the sample was diluted.
  4. Investigate discrepancy: estimates are 140 and 200 µg/mL.
Answer and explanation

D. Investigate discrepancy: estimates are 140 and 200 µg/mL.

After back-calculation, the values should agree under linear proportional response. Matrix effects, saturation or error are possible; averaging does not resolve the disagreement.

37. An enzyme fraction contains 12 U/mL and 3 mg/mL protein in 8 mL. What are total activity and specific activity?

  1. 12 U and 3 U/mg
  2. 96 U and 4 U/mg
  3. 96 U and 32 U/mg
  4. 4 U and 96 U/mg
Answer and explanation

B. 96 U and 4 U/mg

Total activity = 12 × 8 = 96 U. Specific activity = 12/3 = 4 U/mg, equivalently 96/24. Volume cancels in the ratio when both concentrations refer to the same fraction.

38. A starch hydrolysate gives 120 mg glucose after subtracting pre-existing free glucose. Assume complete conversion of a long starch polymer. Approximate anhydroglucose residue mass?

  1. 133.3 mg
  2. 120 mg
  3. 108 mg
  4. 12 mg
Answer and explanation

C. 108 mg

Multiply by 162/180 = 0.9 to obtain 108 mg. The mass of free glucose includes water incorporated during hydrolysis.

39. A broad carbohydrate assay gives 60 glucose-equivalent µg/mL; a glucose-specific assay gives 20 µg/mL. Which claim is valid?

  1. Other responding carbohydrates may contribute, but their true mass is not established by subtraction.
  2. There are exactly 40 µg/mL of fructose.
  3. Both assays must be invalid.
  4. All carbohydrates are glucose.
Answer and explanation

A. Other responding carbohydrates may contribute, but their true mass is not established by subtraction.

Broad response factors differ among sugars. The difference in calibrated signal does not identify or establish actual mass of other carbohydrates.

40. For a solute with K = Corganic/Caqueous = 3, organic phase volume 2 units and aqueous volume 4 units, what proportion is organic at ideal equilibrium?

  1. 75%
  2. 25%
  3. 33.3%
  4. 60%
Answer and explanation

D. 60%

Organic and aqueous amounts are proportional to 3 × 2 and 1 × 4. The organic fraction is 6/(6+4) = 0.6. A concentration ratio is not an amount ratio.

41. Seed A contains 12 mg lipid per 60 mg dry mass; B contains 18 mg per 120 mg dry mass. Which statement holds?

  1. A has more total lipid and lower fraction.
  2. B has more total lipid but A has higher dry-mass fraction.
  3. B has higher fraction and more total lipid.
  4. The fractions are equal.
Answer and explanation

B. B has more total lipid but A has higher dry-mass fraction.

A = 12/60 = 20%; B = 18/120 = 15%. B contains more milligrams, but A allocates a larger fraction of its dry mass to lipid.

42. A fraction has strong mitochondrial marker activity and substantial nuclear marker signal. What is justified?

  1. Absolute mitochondrial purity
  2. No mitochondria are present.
  3. Mitochondrial enrichment with evidence of nuclear-associated contamination
  4. Nuclear marker is necessarily a mitochondrial protein.
Answer and explanation

C. Mitochondrial enrichment with evidence of nuclear-associated contamination

The pattern supports enrichment rather than exclusivity. Marker activity and possible redistribution require cautious interpretation.

43. All treated plants are processed Monday and all control plants Tuesday. Measurements differ. What is the main inference limitation?

  1. Treatment and processing day are confounded.
  2. Technical replication guarantees causal inference.
  3. Sample volume proves treatment causality.
  4. Calibration makes batch effects impossible.
Answer and explanation

A. Treatment and processing day are confounded.

Day and treatment cannot be separated in this design. Balanced processing and appropriate reference samples reduce the confounding.

44. A purified protein has one denaturing gel band, but two populations in a native size measurement. Which interpretation is compatible?

  1. The denaturing gel establishes native monodispersity.
  2. The protein must contain two unrelated genes.
  3. The native method cannot provide any evidence.
  4. One polypeptide may occur as different native assemblies.
Answer and explanation

D. One polypeptide may occur as different native assemblies.

Denaturing gels separate polypeptide states under denaturing conditions; native size methods can reveal oligomers or aggregates. A single denaturing band does not establish native homogeneity.

Assertion–reason, multiple statements and SET-style practice

45. Assertion: A protein preparation with high specific activity is necessarily 100% pure. Reason: Specific activity is measured activity per unit total protein mass.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion false; reason true.

Specific activity can support enrichment but depends on enzyme state and assay conditions. The ratio definition is correct; the claim of necessary absolute purity is false.

46. Assertion: RNA fragmentation can reduce usability without greatly reducing A260. Reason: Many absorbing bases remain in the resulting fragments.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion.

Fragmentation reduces polymer length without necessarily removing the UV-absorbing bases. The reason directly explains why bulk signal can persist.

47. Assertion: A post-extraction spike alone proves complete release of endogenous analyte. Reason: The post-extraction spike bypasses release from the original specimen.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion.
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion.
  3. Assertion false; reason true.
  4. Both false.
Answer and explanation

C. Assertion false; reason true.

A spike introduced after extraction cannot assess the preceding release step. Its response is informative about the later matrix and analytical stage.

48. Which statements are correct? I. A process blank can reveal contamination during preparation. II. A positive control proves every unknown is inhibitor-free. III. Technical replicates can assess analytical repeatability.

  1. I and II only
  2. I and III only
  3. II and III only
  4. All three
Answer and explanation

B. I and III only

I and III are valid. A positive control in a clean matrix cannot prove absence of inhibitors in a different unknown matrix.

49. Which statements are correct? I. Recovery and purity can move in opposite directions. II. A single UV wavelength can resolve any number of unknown absorbing species. III. Molecular mass is needed to convert pure-protein mass concentration to molarity.

  1. I only
  2. II and III only
  3. I, II and III
  4. I and III only
Answer and explanation

D. I and III only

I and III follow from target and denominator definitions. II is false because a single absorbance cannot generally identify the contributions of arbitrary mixtures.

50. A sample has a raw signal of 0.20; calibration is S = 0.05 + 0.003c. If undiluted, what is c?

  1. 50 units of concentration
  2. 66.7
  3. 0.15
  4. 150
Answer and explanation

A. 50 units of concentration

Subtract intercept 0.05 and divide the difference 0.15 by 0.003 = 50. Raw signal should not be treated as already blank-corrected.

51. A fraction contains 18 mg target in 30 mg total material. Initial target was 24 mg in 120 mg total. Calculate target purity, recovery and mass-based enrichment.

  1. 75%, 60%, 3
  2. 60%, 75%, 4
  3. 60%, 75%, 3
  4. 20%, 60%, 3
Answer and explanation

C. 60%, 75%, 3

Final purity is 18/30 = 60%; recovery is 18/24 = 75%; initial purity is 24/120 = 20%, so enrichment is 3-fold.

52. A pure protein gives A = 0.4 with ε = 20,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and path 1 cm. Molar concentration?

  1. 0.2 M
  2. 20 µM
  3. 2 µM
  4. 200 µM
Answer and explanation

B. 20 µM

c = A/(εl) = 0.4/20,000 = 0.00002 M = 20 µM, provided Beer–Lambert assumptions apply.

53. A nominal lipid residue weighs 45 mg, including 5 mg known nonlipid material. Starting dry mass is 800 mg. Corrected lipid percentage under the stated assumptions?

  1. 5.625%
  2. 0.5%
  3. 40%
  4. 5%
Answer and explanation

D. 5%

Corrected lipid mass = 40 mg. Divide by 800 and multiply by 100 to obtain 5%; the uncorrected residue would give 5.625%.

54. Which experiment best separates loss of enzyme function from loss of enzyme mass in an extract?

  1. Measure target-selective mass and activity under matched conditions.
  2. Measure only the total sample volume.
  3. Measure A260 twice.
  4. Measure activity at different substrate concentrations without controls.
Answer and explanation

A. Measure target-selective mass and activity under matched conditions.

Matched selective mass and functional measurements distinguish decreased amount from lower rate per target mass. Different assay conditions can confound that distinction.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. High concentration means high recovery

Correct concept: Concentration is amount per volume; recovery compares total quantities. Why it is confusing: Smaller volumes make a preparation look more concentrated. Examination reasoning: Given unequal volumes, multiply each concentration by its own volume.

2. Purity and recovery always rise together

Correct concept: A separation can enrich target while discarding part of it. Why it is confusing: Both words are treated as synonyms for successful preparation. Examination reasoning: Calculate the target fraction and total target recovery separately.

3. A pure molecule is necessarily intact

Correct concept: Chemical purity does not establish structural continuity or native folding. Why it is confusing: A purity label is mistaken for comprehensive quality. Examination reasoning: Use a size or functional measurement appropriate to the target.

4. A260 is exclusively DNA

Correct concept: RNA and other UV absorbers can contribute. Why it is confusing: A conventional DNA conversion factor is mistaken for specificity. Examination reasoning: Check selective fluorescence or independent contamination evidence.

5. A260/A280 is a purity percentage

Correct concept: It is a spectral ratio affected by composition and conditions. Why it is confusing: A familiar threshold looks like a direct chemical assay. Examination reasoning: Do not convert the ratio into percent purity; assess complementary evidence.

6. An RNA sample with good optical ratios is intact

Correct concept: RNA fragments can retain UV absorption. Why it is confusing: Spectral and structural quality are conflated. Examination reasoning: Inspect a relevant size profile and downstream compatibility.

7. A single band guarantees absolute purity

Correct concept: Co-migration and detection limits remain possible. Why it is confusing: Visible simplicity is mistaken for exhaustive detection. Examination reasoning: Distinguish apparent homogeneity from identity and absolute purity.

8. All protein assays respond identically to all proteins

Correct concept: Response varies with chemistry and composition. Why it is confusing: The standard curve seems like a universal mass conversion. Examination reasoning: Identify the reference and potential response-factor mismatch.

9. A positive control excludes sample inhibition

Correct concept: The unknown matrix may differ from the control. Why it is confusing: Assay capability is mistaken for universal sample compatibility. Examination reasoning: Use a suitable matrix spike or functional interference comparison.

10. Technical replicates are independent biological replicates

Correct concept: Aliquots of one specimen share its biology. Why it is confusing: Counting wells is easier than identifying sampling units. Examination reasoning: Count independent specimens for biological inference.

11. The pellet always contains all interesting molecules

Correct concept: Both pellet and supernatant may contain target. Why it is confusing: Sedimentation is imagined as a perfect sorting operation. Examination reasoning: Follow target and markers across fractions.

12. A reducing-sugar assay measures every carbohydrate equally

Correct concept: Reducing ends and response chemistry determine signal. Why it is confusing: All sugars are assumed to have identical reactions. Examination reasoning: Distinguish reducing equivalents, total carbohydrate and specific sugars.

13. Glucose equivalents prove glucose identity

Correct concept: The value is defined relative to a standard. Why it is confusing: The calibration standard's name is read as analyte identity. Examination reasoning: Use glucose-specific evidence if glucose itself is the target.

14. Lipid residue mass equals pure lipid mass

Correct concept: Residual nonlipid material or solvent may contribute. Why it is confusing: Gravimetry appears independent of chemistry. Examination reasoning: Check residue composition and extraction recovery.

15. A ratio of phase concentrations is a ratio of total amounts

Correct concept: Phase volumes also matter. Why it is confusing: The larger concentration is assumed to contain the same proportion of mass. Examination reasoning: Multiply each phase concentration by its volume.

16. An activity recovery above 100% is impossible

Correct concept: Activity is a rate and can increase after removal of inhibition. Why it is confusing: Activity is mistaken for conserved molecule mass. Examination reasoning: Use matched activity conditions and independent mass evidence.

17. Below quantification means zero

Correct concept: A reliable numerical estimate is not available at that level. Why it is confusing: A reporting threshold is mistaken for physical absence. Examination reasoning: Avoid substituting zero or calculating unsupported fold changes.

Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

Every biological measurement begins with a representative specimen and a defined analyte. Preparation should preserve the properties needed for the intended analysis while controlling interference. Fractionation distributes molecules; purification increases relative enrichment. Interpret total amount, concentration, recovery, purity and integrity separately. Combine selective measurements with suitable controls to support the biological inference.

High-yield equations

  • Amount = concentration × volume.
  • Recovery (%) = recovered amount ÷ initial amount × 100.
  • Cumulative recovery = product of conditional fractional recoveries.
  • Specific activity = total activity ÷ total protein mass.
  • Purification fold = final specific activity ÷ initial specific activity.
  • Conserved dilution: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.
  • Beer–Lambert: A = εlc, with validated optical assumptions.
  • Linear calibration: c = (S − b)/m.
  • Spike recovery (%) = (spiked estimate − unspiked estimate) ÷ amount added × 100.
