1. Living matter as a solution

A living cell is an organised chemical system immersed in water. Its proteins, nucleic acids and membranes retain the properties explained in the preceding chapters, but they operate in an environment whose composition continually changes. A protein that binds its substrate efficiently in one solution may bind poorly in another, even if its amino acid sequence has not changed. A red blood cell transferred from its usual surroundings into a dilute solution may swell and rupture although its membrane is initially intact. A bacterial culture can stop growing when the medium becomes acidic, despite the continued presence of nutrients. These apparently separate observations are consequences of the same principle: the physical and chemical state of the surrounding solution helps determine the behaviour of biological molecules and cells.

To understand a solution, distinguish its components from the properties of the mixture. Water is usually the solvent in the systems considered here. Dissolved salts, sugars, metabolites and small molecules are solutes. Macromolecules may be dispersed in the same medium but can participate in associations, form phases or display nonideal interactions. Calling cytoplasm a solution is therefore a useful starting approximation, not a claim that every molecule behaves independently in a homogeneous dilute liquid. Cellular compartments, macromolecular crowding and local electrostatic environments introduce further complexity that must be remembered when applying simple equations.

Two questions organise this chapter. First, how do dissolved particles change the physical behaviour of the solvent? This leads to concentration scales, colligative properties and osmotic pressure. Second, how does the transfer of protons alter the chemistry of dissolved molecules? This leads to acid and base equilibria, pH, titration and buffering. The questions are related because both require accurate accounting of substances and both depend on interactions with water. They are not identical: a neutral glucose molecule can exert an osmotic effect without contributing a strong acid, whereas a trace of strong acid can change pH greatly while making almost no difference to the total osmolality of a concentrated salt solution.

A useful mental experiment is to compare three preparations at the same temperature: pure water, a 0.10 molar glucose solution and a 0.10 molar sodium chloride solution. The glucose and sodium chloride preparations contain the same number of moles of formula units per litre. However, glucose remains largely as intact molecules, while dilute sodium chloride produces sodium and chloride ions. The solution properties that count particles will therefore differ. The molecular properties of those particles, including charge, will also influence their interactions. A valid calculation must state which aspect of solution composition the question is asking about before choosing an equation.

The methods developed below are also essential for reading an experimental graph. A statement that an enzyme was tested at pH 7 is incomplete unless the investigator identifies the buffer, its concentration, temperature and other relevant medium components. A stated concentration of 100 millimolar is incomplete if the substance and denominator are unclear. In an examination problem, these missing details often determine whether an approximation is permissible. The habit of naming assumptions is consequently part of scientific reasoning rather than a formality added after a calculation.

2. Counting solute: amount, concentration and dilution

Chemical reactions involve numbers of molecules or ions. A mole provides a bridge between microscopic counting and laboratory measurements: one mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. The entities must be named. One mole of sodium chloride formula units is not one mole of all ions after ideal dissociation; one mole of glucose molecules contains six moles of carbon atoms. The mass of a mole of a compound is its molar mass, expressed in grams per mole when masses are weighed in grams. Thus a mass divided by molar mass yields an amount of substance, not a concentration. Dividing that amount by an appropriate volume or mass produces one of several concentration scales.

Molarity, usually written c or M, is the amount of solute divided by the final volume of solution. Its customary unit is mol L⁻¹. For example, 5.844 g of NaCl corresponds to 0.1000 mol when the molar mass is taken as 58.44 g mol⁻¹. If the final solution occupies 0.500 L, the analytical NaCl concentration is 0.200 mol L⁻¹. The word final is crucial. Adding this mass to exactly half a litre of water does not guarantee that the resulting solution occupies half a litre. Mixing changes volume, and temperature affects volume. The formula c = n/V defines a property of the final mixture.

Biological experiments commonly use millimolar, micromolar and nanomolar units because the quantities of metabolites and signalling substances may differ by many orders of magnitude. One millimolar is 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹; one micromolar is 10⁻⁶ mol L⁻¹. A one millilitre aliquot of 20 mM solute contains 20 micromoles. The numerical identity arises because multiplying millimoles per litre by millilitres contributes a factor of one thousand in the denominator. Checking units independently is safer than memorising a conversion for every combination of volume and concentration.

A stock solution is often more concentrated than the working solution. If no solute reacts, precipitates, evaporates or is otherwise lost during dilution, the number of moles transferred remains fixed. For a stock of concentration c₁ and aliquot volume V₁, the amount is c₁V₁. After dilution to final volume V₂, it is c₂V₂. Equating these quantities gives c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. To prepare 250 mL of 12 mM solution from a 0.300 M stock, first express the concentrations in common units: 0.300 M equals 300 mM. The required volume is (12 × 250)/300 = 10 mL. The aliquot is diluted to 250 mL, not mixed with an additional 250 mL of solvent.

If two solutions of the same nonreacting solute are mixed, their amounts add. When the final volume is known, the concentration is (c₁V₁ + c₂V₂)/Vfinal. If volume additivity is assumed, mixing 40 mL of 150 mM solute with 60 mL of 50 mM solute gives (6 + 3) mmol in 100 mL, hence 90 mM. This is a volume-weighted mean, not the unweighted average of 100 mM. If volumes are not additive, use the measured final volume. If solutes react, a stoichiometric calculation must precede the division; the same algebra cannot directly predict the amount of free species.

Serial dilution is useful when the desired concentration is much smaller than a stock concentration. Transferring one part of solution into sufficient solvent to make ten parts of final mixture gives a tenfold dilution. Three successive tenfold dilutions of a 1 mM stock give 1 μM, because the factors multiply. Errors also propagate through the series. A calculation involving a final measurement should distinguish uncertainty in the stock from uncertainty introduced at each transfer. The purpose of a serial dilution is not to create or destroy solute but to distribute a known fraction of the initial amount into a new volume.

Mass concentration is another legitimate scale. A 1% mass/volume glucose solution means 1 g per 100 mL of final solution, or 10 g L⁻¹. With glucose molar mass about 180.16 g mol⁻¹, this is about 55.5 mM. The same mass concentration of NaCl corresponds to 171 mM formula units, illustrating why equal grams per litre need not mean equal numbers of dissolved particles. A mass/mass percentage instead uses total solution mass; a volume/volume percentage uses volumes under a specified preparation convention. These three definitions cannot be exchanged merely because each uses the percent sign.

3. Molality, mole fraction and density

Molarity is useful for biochemical reactions occurring in measured volumes, but volume is sensitive to temperature and mixing. For properties such as freezing-point depression, it is often more convenient to describe solute relative to a measured mass of solvent. Molality m is the number of moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. The denominator excludes solute mass. A mixture containing 0.200 mol glucose in 0.800 kg water has molality 0.250 mol kg⁻¹ regardless of its solution volume. In a closed sample with no change in composition, heating changes molarity if thermal expansion occurs but does not change molality. This distinction explains why thermodynamic data often use a mass-based scale.

Mole fraction counts the amount of one component relative to the total amount of all specified components. For an ideal two-component mixture, xₛ = nₛ/(nₛ + nw) and xw = nw/(nₛ + nw), so their sum equals unity. It is dimensionless. If 1 mol of a nonvolatile solute is mixed with 9 mol of water, xw is 0.90 and xs is 0.10. In an ionic solution a model must clarify whether it is counting analytical formula units or independently behaving species. The simple ideal-solution expression applies to the chosen components under its assumptions; inserting both a dissociation factor and already separately counted ions would count the same particles twice.

Density connects mass and volume. To derive a useful molality-to-molarity conversion, choose one kilogram of solvent as the reference. A solution of molality m contains m moles of a solute of molar mass W in kg mol⁻¹. The solute mass is mW kilograms, so the total solution mass is 1 + mW kilograms. If its density is ρ kg L⁻¹, the volume is (1 + mW)/ρ litres. Dividing the amount m by this volume gives c = mρ/(1 + mW). Rearranging yields m = c/(ρ − cW), provided that the solution contains the stated solvent and solute and the denominator represents a positive solvent mass. For mixtures of many solutes, the mass contribution of each must be included.

As an illustration, let a glucose solution contain 0.50 mol kg⁻¹ and have a measured density of 1.035 kg L⁻¹. With W = 0.18016 kg mol⁻¹, one kilogram of water plus the glucose has mass 1.09008 kg and occupies 1.0532 L. Its molarity is therefore about 0.475 M. The numerical closeness of molarity and molality in some dilute aqueous solutions follows from the density being close to one kilogram per litre and the solute contribution being small; it is an approximation rather than a definition. Concentrated solutions may differ substantially.

A related quantity, analytical concentration, identifies the total amount of an introduced chemical component whether or not that component changes form. A 20 mM total phosphate solution may contain several differently protonated phosphate species. Their concentrations sum to the analytical phosphate concentration, but the abundance of each form depends on pH. Free concentration, total concentration and activity are three separate concepts. An equilibrium constant concerns the activities of the participating species; the total concentration may appear only in a supplementary material balance. This distinction becomes central when solving weak acid and buffer problems.

4. Electrolytes and effective dissolved particle number

When an ionic crystal such as sodium chloride dissolves, hydration stabilises its separated ions in water. A strong electrolyte in dilute water is largely dissociated, whereas a weak electrolyte establishes an equilibrium between associated and ionised forms. A nonelectrolyte such as glucose normally remains molecular. If each dissolved formula unit of a solute yields ν particles upon complete dissociation, the ideal number of dissolved particles is ν times the initial number of formula units. NaCl has ν = 2; CaCl₂ has ν = 3. A solution with 0.10 mol L⁻¹ of CaCl₂ then contains nominally 0.10 M calcium and 0.20 M chloride, with an ideal osmolarity of 0.30 Osm L⁻¹.

For a simple dissociation model in which a fraction α of formula units separates into ν particles and the remaining fraction stays intact, one initial mole yields (1 − α) moles of intact units plus να moles of dissociated particles. The total is 1 + α(ν − 1). This defines the ideal particle multiplication factor i for that model. If a monoprotic weak acid is 5% dissociated, i is 1.05, not 2. Conversely, if two solute molecules associate into dimers and a fraction α of the original molecular units enters dimers, there are (1 − α) monomer moles and α/2 dimer moles per initial mole, giving i = 1 − α/2. The direction of deviation tells us how a simple association or dissociation process changes the count.

Measured van't Hoff factors may be smaller than the nominal ion count even for salts regarded as strong electrolytes. Charged particles do not behave as independent points at finite concentration. Ionic atmospheres, electrostatic correlations and association can change the solvent activity. A measured factor is then an effective representation of the deviation of the property from an ideal model. It cannot automatically be translated into a percentage of undissociated salt. If a freezing-point experiment yields i = 1.8 for NaCl, the result does not by itself establish that 20% of the salt exists as intact neutral molecules. Additional evidence and a suitable activity model are needed.

Osmolarity describes the concentration of osmotically active particles per litre of solution; osmolality describes it per kilogram of solvent. In ideal dilute calculations, sum each solute's molar concentration multiplied by its ideal particle factor for osmolarity, and use molality analogously for osmolality. Thus 80 mM glucose and 110 mM NaCl contribute 80 and 220 mOsm L⁻¹ respectively in the ideal model, for a total of 300 mOsm L⁻¹. The osmole counts the effective particles, not the magnitude of their electrical charge. A Ca²⁺ ion contributes one particle, not two because of its valence.

Electrical neutrality is a different accounting constraint. A macroscopic bulk solution has essentially equal total positive and negative charge, although small charge separations at interfaces are physically important. In the ideal 0.10 M CaCl₂ example, 0.10 M divalent positive ions carry 0.20 mol of positive elementary charges per litre, balanced by the chloride ions. Osmolarity counts 0.30 M particles; electroneutrality balances 0.20 M charge equivalents of each sign. Neither calculation substitutes for the other.

Ionic strength provides yet another description: I = ½ Σ cᵢzᵢ², where zᵢ is the signed charge number of each ionic species. In the same solution, the ideal ionic strength is ½(0.10 × 4 + 0.20 × 1) = 0.30 M. By contrast 0.30 M glucose has essentially no ionic strength contribution but the same nominal osmolarity. That coincidence demonstrates why an experiment that replaces a salt with an osmotically equivalent sugar does not necessarily preserve electrostatic screening or protein binding. It also prepares the distinction between concentration and activity used in the acid–base sections.

5. Solvent activity, vapour pressure and colligative behaviour

A sealed container of water at constant temperature contains both liquid water and water vapour. Molecules continually leave the liquid and return to it. At equilibrium, the vapour pressure reflects equality of the chemical potential of water in the two phases. Addition of a nonvolatile solute usually lowers the equilibrium vapour pressure of the solvent. This is not adequately explained by saying that solute particles form a rigid cover over the liquid surface. Instead, dissolved substances change the chemical potential of water in the bulk mixture. For an ideal solution the reduction is described by the entropy of mixing and quantified through the solvent mole fraction.

Raoult's law for an ideal solution relates solvent partial vapour pressure P to that of pure solvent P° at the same temperature: P = xwP°. For a nonvolatile solute in a binary mixture, ΔP = P° − P = (1 − xw)P° = xsP°. The relative lowering is therefore xs. If a sample contains 19 mol of water and 1 mol of an ideal nonvolatile molecular solute, xw = 0.95. At a temperature for which the pure water vapour pressure is 3.20 kPa, the predicted pressure is 3.04 kPa, a lowering of 0.16 kPa. This example uses a specified illustrative pure-solvent pressure; the actual value depends on temperature.

The more general formulation replaces the solvent mole fraction with its activity, aw, so that approximately P/P° = aw when the vapour can be treated ideally and the same reference state is used. In an ideal solution aw = xw. Real solute-solvent interactions can produce deviations from this relation. Water activity is dimensionless, and should not be equated to the percentage water by mass. A highly soluble solute can lower water activity even while the mixture still contains abundant water molecules. Biological relevance follows because microbial growth, hydration equilibria and molecular interactions depend on the thermodynamic availability of water, not solely on the mass of water present.

Vapour pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure are termed colligative because their ideal dilute limits depend predominantly on the number of solute particles relative to solvent rather than detailed solute identity. The qualifier ideal dilute is essential. Two solutions having the same analytical molarity may have different particle counts because of dissociation. Two solutions having the same nominal osmolarity may have different actual water activities because of nonideal interactions. These qualifications do not invalidate colligative laws; they specify when the simplified equations are appropriate.

An experimental comparison of glucose and NaCl solutions illustrates the reasoning. Suppose measurements show a nearly linear decrease in solvent vapour pressure with increasing dilute glucose mole fraction, while the corresponding NaCl preparation produces a larger effect at the same analytical amount. The natural interpretation is that more effective dissolved particles are present. If the salt data bend away from the predicted ideal line as concentration increases, the observation calls for a nonideal activity treatment. Neither result establishes that the two solutes have identical chemical effects on proteins. The measurement informs us about the solvent's thermodynamic state, which is one aspect of their biological action.

6. Boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression

Boiling begins when the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the surrounding pressure. At a fixed external pressure, a solution with lower solvent vapour pressure at a given temperature must usually be heated to a higher temperature to reach that condition. This is boiling-point elevation. For a dilute solution of a nonvolatile solute, ΔTb = iKb m, where ΔTb = Tb,solution − Tb,pure is positive, Kb is the solvent-specific ebullioscopic constant and m is analytical solute molality. The product i m is an ideal effective particle molality. For water Kb is about 0.512 K kg mol⁻¹. A 0.20 molal glucose solution therefore has an ideal boiling elevation of 0.1024 K.

Freezing has a different phase comparison. Consider a dilute solution that forms essentially pure solvent crystals on freezing. The solute remains mainly in the liquid phase. Its presence lowers the chemical potential of liquid solvent relative to that of pure liquid at the same temperature. Equality with the solid solvent is achieved at a lower temperature, so the freezing point decreases. The ideal dilute result is ΔTf = iKf m, where ΔTf = Tf,pure − Tf,solution is defined as a positive depression and Kf for water is about 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹. For the same 0.20 molal glucose sample, ΔTf = 0.372 K. This is not a negative depression; the solution freezing temperature is lower by that amount.

A conceptual derivation explains the shared concentration dependence. In the ideal liquid, the solvent chemical potential is μw° + RT ln xw. For a dilute solution ln xw is approximately −xs, so the shift is proportional to solute mole fraction. At the pure-solvent phase transition the chemical potentials of the two phases are equal. A small change in temperature changes their difference approximately in proportion to the entropy of transition. Equating this first-order temperature effect to the mixing term yields a temperature displacement proportional to the dissolved particle amount. The proportionality depends on solvent molar mass and the enthalpy of the phase transition, which is why Kb and Kf are solvent-specific.

The freezing-point method is particularly valuable for biological fluids because a small temperature difference can reveal total effective osmolality. In the ideal approximation a depression of 0.558 K in water corresponds to 0.300 Osm kg⁻¹. An instrument calibrated for aqueous samples uses the observed freezing behaviour to estimate osmolality. The quantity obtained does not specify which molecules are responsible. A mixture of salts, sugars and other metabolites can give the same reading as a different mixture with similar water activity. Additional chemical analysis is required to determine composition.

The distinction between equilibrium freezing and supercooling matters when interpreting data. A liquid may remain unfrozen below its equilibrium freezing point because crystal nucleation has not yet occurred. The lowest temperature reached before ice formation is therefore not automatically the thermodynamic freezing temperature. Once crystals form, latent heat and changing composition influence the temperature trace. A valid osmometric measurement controls or accounts for these phenomena. Also, because freezing excludes much of the solute, the remaining liquid becomes progressively more concentrated. The initial dilute formula should not be used unchanged throughout extensive freezing.

In molecular-mass determination, a known mass of a nonvolatile nonelectrolyte is dissolved in a known solvent mass and the depression is measured. For example, 0.90 g of an unknown molecular solute in 0.100 kg water produces ΔTf = 0.186 K. Assuming i = 1 and ideal dilution, m = 0.100 mol kg⁻¹, so the sample contains 0.0100 mol and its apparent molar mass is 90 g mol⁻¹. If the substance actually dissociates or associates, that apparent molecular mass is biased. For a completely dissociating two-particle electrolyte treated incorrectly as a nonelectrolyte, the inferred molar mass would be half its formula molar mass under ideal conditions. For large macromolecules, osmotic pressure is frequently more sensitive to small molar concentrations than the small freezing-point shift they produce.

7. Osmosis and the van't Hoff relation

Osmosis requires a selective barrier. Imagine a chamber split into two compartments by a membrane that allows water to cross but retains a dissolved substance. Both directions of water movement occur microscopically. If one compartment contains pure water and the other contains solute at the same hydrostatic pressure, the chemical potential of water is initially higher on the pure-water side. The net water movement is toward the solution. Water entry can raise the liquid level and hence hydrostatic pressure on that side. Equilibrium occurs when the pressure difference offsets the difference in solvent activity, not when the solute concentrations necessarily become identical.

Osmotic pressure π is the pressure increment that would have to be applied to the solution side to prevent net solvent transfer across the ideal semipermeable membrane when the other side is pure solvent. It is a thermodynamic property defined through a membrane equilibrium, rather than a pressure exerted by solute particles striking a physical cell membrane in a simplistic mechanical picture. The pressure contribution to water chemical potential is approximately its partial molar volume times the pressure increase when compressibility is neglected. At equilibrium, V̄w π + RT ln aw = 0, hence π = −RT ln aw/V̄w. Because aw is generally below one for the dilute solutions considered here, the resulting π is positive.

For ideal dilute solutions, −ln xw is approximately the ratio of solute particle amount to solvent amount. The product of solvent amount and solvent molar volume is approximately the solution volume. Substitution gives π ≈ nParticlesRT/V = i cRT, the van't Hoff equation. The symbol c denotes analytical concentration, and i accounts for particle multiplication if that has not already been included. For multiple solutes use RT Σ iⱼcⱼ. This form resembles the ideal gas equation mathematically, but it describes the solvent chemical potential change in a solution. It does not imply that hydrated molecules literally occupy the solution as a free gas.

At 298 K, a dilute 0.10 M glucose solution has π = 0.10 × 0.082057 × 298 = 2.45 atm if the gas constant is expressed in L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A nominal 0.10 M NaCl solution has twice that ideal pressure, about 4.89 atm, if completely dissociated and ideal. These relatively large numbers are not estimates of arterial blood pressure: the latter is a mechanical pressure in circulation, whereas the former compare solution to pure solvent across a hypothetical barrier that retains every solute particle. Comparing two biological fluids of nearly equal osmolality gives a much smaller difference than comparing either fluid against pure water.

In SI units the gas constant is 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A concentration of 0.10 mol L⁻¹ must then be converted to 100 mol m⁻³; multiplying yields approximately 247,800 J m⁻³, equivalent to pascals. This equals 248 kPa or 2.45 atm. Unit consistency makes it possible to detect thousandfold mistakes. When both chambers contain ideal solutions at the same temperature, the osmotic pressure difference is RT times the difference in retained particle concentration. The sign of the difference identifies which side requires increased hydrostatic pressure to oppose water flow.

For macromolecular solutes a mass concentration may be converted into molar concentration by dividing by molar mass. An ideal dilute solution containing 10 g L⁻¹ of a protein of molar mass 50,000 g mol⁻¹ has protein concentration 0.00020 M. At 298 K, its ideal osmotic pressure is about 0.00489 atm or 3.72 mmHg relative to solvent. In real protein solutions intermolecular interactions, excluded volume and counterions can alter the result. The calculation nevertheless explains why relatively high mass concentrations of large proteins yield small molecular particle concentrations compared with small salts. A membrane that passes small solutes but retains proteins selectively measures a different osmotic contribution from one that retains every dissolved species.

8. Osmolarity, tonicity and biological water balance

Osmolarity measures total effective dissolved particles. Tonicity describes the sustained effect of an external medium on cell volume, and depends on which solutes can cross the cell membrane on the relevant timescale. A solute that crosses freely can contribute to an osmometer reading while failing to maintain an enduring transmembrane osmotic difference. The distinction is indispensable because biological membranes are selectively permeable rather than perfectly impermeable to every solute.

Consider a cell whose initial contents contain 300 mOsm L⁻¹ of solutes that are effectively retained over the duration of an idealised experiment. If it is transferred into a large bath of 300 mOsm L⁻¹ of a retained solute, the initial chemical driving force for net water movement is approximately zero. If the outside contains 150 mOsm L⁻¹ of retained solute, the cell swells. If it contains 600 mOsm L⁻¹, the cell shrinks. Assume negligible hydrostatic pressure difference, no regulatory solute flux, constant external concentration and a cell with no rigid wall or osmotically inactive volume. Under these assumptions, the final retained intracellular concentration matches the external retained concentration. Because n = cV is conserved, Vfinal/Vinitial = cinitial/cexternal.

The idealised volume ratio in the 150 mOsm bath is 2, while in the 600 mOsm bath it is 0.5. A real red blood cell cannot necessarily double its volume without rupturing. Membrane area, cell shape and osmotically inactive material restrict these predictions. A cell wall in a plant cell supports a hydrostatic pressure difference; consequently a plant cell may reach equilibrium with a positive turgor pressure while its internal and external osmotic concentrations remain different. The same osmotic principle applies, but the mechanical boundary conditions differ.

Now compare an iso-osmotic urea solution with an iso-osmotic medium containing a retained solute. Urea is permeant through many biological membranes, with permeability depending on the cell type and transport pathways. If water moves rapidly and urea subsequently enters a cell, the intracellular total concentration increases transiently and water follows. A bath with permeant solute but little retained solute can therefore be hypotonic despite initially matching the cell's total osmolarity. Whether an early transient shrinkage or swelling is observed depends on the relative permeabilities and starting concentrations. The sustained tendency is determined by the effective retained solute difference, not simply by the starting osmometer readings.

A useful phenomenological refinement describes the osmotic effectiveness of a solute with a reflection coefficient σ ranging from approximately zero for a freely permeant solute to one for an ideally retained solute. Under simplified dilute transport conditions, the effective osmotic term may be written RT Σ σⱼΔcⱼ. This is not a universal equilibrium concentration formula for a living cell with active transport. Its purpose here is to make explicit that membrane selectivity matters. In an experiment, measuring water flux immediately after exposure and measuring final volume can produce different information about water movement and solute permeability.

Osmotic stress also explains why an organism may accumulate compatible solutes under dry or saline conditions. Increasing intracellular solute concentration can reduce the outward water-driving force, but solutes must be chosen or regulated so they do not disrupt protein function. The preceding chapter explained how hydration and electrostatic interactions influence protein stability. The present chapter supplies the quantitative basis for solvent availability and pressure. Membrane transport pathways, plant water potential and microbial stress responses are studied more fully in their respective later chapters.

9. Acids, bases and the transfer of protons

The physical counting of dissolved particles does not tell us how protons distribute among molecules. Water itself participates in proton-transfer equilibria. In the Brønsted–Lowry description, an acid donates a proton to a base, and a base accepts one. In the reaction HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻, HA is the acid, water is the base, A⁻ is the conjugate base of HA and H₃O⁺ is the conjugate acid of water. Members of a conjugate pair differ by one proton. This bookkeeping is especially useful in biology because a molecule can have several groups that gain or lose protons independently or interactively.

The Arrhenius description identifies acids and bases through their effects on aqueous hydrogen and hydroxide ion concentrations. It works for simple aqueous acid–base systems, but the Brønsted–Lowry language also captures compounds such as ammonia, which accepts a proton from water to form ammonium and hydroxide. A broader Lewis description considers electron-pair donation and acceptance; a metal ion coordinated by a ligand may act as a Lewis acid without itself donating a proton. In this chapter the emphasis is proton transfer because pH and buffers concern proton activity. The distinction prevents the incorrect assumption that every Lewis acid must release a proton directly.

Water is amphiprotic: it can accept a proton in the presence of an acid and donate one in the presence of a base. Two water molecules can exchange a proton with one another, 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. In ordinary aqueous equations H⁺ is shorthand for solvated proton activity, not a bare proton moving through the liquid without interaction. Hydration and hydrogen-bond networks make the microscopic environment complex, but the simplified notation correctly accounts for proton transfer in equilibrium expressions.

The charge of a conjugate base is one unit lower than that of its conjugate acid. The starting acid need not be neutral: NH₄⁺ loses a proton to become NH₃, while H₂PO₄⁻ loses a proton to become HPO₄²⁻. A protein carboxyl group can shift between a neutral protonated form and a negative deprotonated form; an amino group can shift between positively charged protonated and neutral deprotonated forms. For each group, protonation increases charge by one unit. Multiple groups collectively determine a protein's net charge and interactions.

A strong acid donates its proton extensively to water under ordinary dilute conditions. A weak acid establishes a measurable equilibrium between protonated and deprotonated forms. Strength is not the same as concentration: a dilute strong acid may supply fewer protons per litre than a concentrated weak acid. Similarly, the amount of acid required to neutralise a given sample is governed by the total titratable acid, not simply its initial free proton concentration. This explains why two solutions that initially have the same pH may behave very differently when a base is added.

10. Water ionisation, pH and pOH

Water autoionisation is represented by the equilibrium constant Kw = a(H₃O⁺)a(OH⁻) when the solvent activity is incorporated into the conventional constant. At 25 °C its familiar dilute aqueous value is approximately 10⁻¹⁴. Pure water has equal hydrogen and hydroxide ion activities under the ordinary ideal approximation, so each is approximately 10⁻⁷ relative to the standard state. This equality defines neutrality. A neutral sample does not necessarily have pH exactly seven at other temperatures because the equilibrium constant changes with temperature.

The pH scale uses a negative base-ten logarithm: pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺). Activities are dimensionless quantities relative to an agreed standard state. In dilute teaching problems, a(H⁺) is approximated by the numerical concentration in mol L⁻¹ divided by the standard concentration of 1 mol L⁻¹. IUPAC defines pH using activity, not merely concentration. In real ionic media, deviations from ideality, conventions for single-ion activities and electrode calibration affect its operational determination. Thus the common concentration expression is appropriate as an explicitly stated approximation.

The negative logarithm reverses the direction: a tenfold increase in proton activity lowers pH by one unit. A change from pH 7 to pH 5 represents a hundredfold increase in proton activity. The numerical difference of two units is not a twofold increase. A pH of 6.5 corresponds to proton activity 10⁻⁶⋅⁵ ≈ 3.16 × 10⁻⁷. At 25 °C, pOH is defined as −log₁₀ a(OH⁻), and taking negative logarithms of Kw gives pH + pOH = pKw ≈ 14.00. More generally the sum equals the pKw appropriate to the temperature and convention.

A strong acid calculation may be simple when the introduced acid concentration is much larger than the neutral-water proton concentration. A 0.0020 M HCl solution has approximate pH 2.699 if completely dissociated and ideal. If the concentration is only 10⁻⁸ M, the same shortcut predicts pH 8, which would misleadingly imply that adding acid makes initially neutral water basic. Water autoionisation cannot be neglected. Electroneutrality for a dilute strong monoprotic acid of concentration C gives h = C + [OH⁻]. Substituting [OH⁻] = Kw/h and multiplying by h yields h² − Ch − Kw = 0. The physical positive root is h = (C + √(C² + 4Kw))/2. With C = 10⁻⁸ M and Kw = 10⁻¹⁴, h ≈ 1.051 × 10⁻⁷ M, giving pH about 6.978. The result is slightly acidic, as it should be.

The familiar pH range of zero to fourteen is not a universal mathematical limit. A sufficiently concentrated acidic solution can have a negative operational pH, and very alkaline solutions can have pH above fourteen. Such cases require attention to activities rather than naive concentration substitutions. In cellular systems the important issue is not whether the numerical scale ends at a particular number but how proton activity changes molecular charge and chemical equilibria. A lysosome and the cytosol can maintain different pH values in part because of membrane transport and buffering. These differences have implications for enzyme function, but the regulation of organelle pH belongs to the cellular organisation chapters.

The pH of a solution cannot generally be averaged arithmetically when two samples are mixed. The logarithms represent activities, not conserved amounts. For example, equal volumes of ideal strong acid solutions of pH 2 and pH 4 contain approximate hydrogen concentrations 0.0100 M and 0.000100 M. Their mixed concentration is 0.00505 M and pH about 2.297, not 3. If an acid is mixed with a base or buffer, chemical reactions must be included before the final equilibrium is calculated. This is a recurring pattern throughout acid–base problem solving.

11. Weak acid and weak base equilibria

For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, the thermodynamic acid dissociation constant is Ka = a(H⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA). In sufficiently dilute solutions the corresponding concentration approximation gives Ka ≈ [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA], with the standard-state factors understood. The constant expresses equilibrium, not the speed of dissociation. The smaller the pKa = −log₁₀Ka, the larger the equilibrium constant and the stronger the acid in that medium. Comparing pKa values is a convenient way to reason about which proton transfers are favoured.

Consider a weak monoprotic acid of analytical concentration C with no added conjugate base and negligible water autoionisation. If x mol L⁻¹ dissociates, the equilibrium concentrations are C − x for HA and approximately x for both H⁺ and A⁻. Substitution gives Ka = x²/(C − x). Multiplication yields x² + Kax − KaC = 0. The positive root is x = (−Ka + √(Ka² + 4KaC))/2. When x is small relative to C, replace C − x with C to obtain x ≈ √(KaC). The approximation must be assessed rather than used automatically.

For an acid with Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ and C = 0.10 M, the square-root estimate is 1.34 × 10⁻³ M, corresponding to pH 2.872. This represents only about 1.34% dissociation. The exact positive root is about 1.333 × 10⁻³ M, and the corresponding pH is approximately 2.875. For C = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M, the same approximation gives x ≈ 4.24 × 10⁻⁵ M, which is already more than forty percent of the initial concentration. The assumption fails. Solving the quadratic gives about 3.44 × 10⁻⁵ M and pH about 4.46. The important result is not merely the changed pH: a larger fraction of the acid is dissociated at lower total concentration.

Dilution can therefore increase the percentage ionisation of a weak acid while decreasing the absolute proton concentration. A common error is to say that increased ionisation makes the diluted solution more acidic. These statements refer to different quantities. The fraction dissociated is x/C; pH depends on free proton activity. A graph of dissociation fraction versus concentration and a graph of pH versus concentration need not move in the same direction.

For a weak base B that accepts a proton from water, B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻, define Kb ≈ [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B] in the dilute approximation. The same quadratic structure applies with hydroxide as the quantity x. The conjugate acid BH⁺ has its own Ka. Multiplying the acid and base equilibrium expressions cancels B and BH⁺ and gives Ka(BH⁺)Kb(B) = Kw, so pKa + pKb = pKw for a conjugate pair at the same temperature and in compatible conventions. A strong conjugate acid corresponds to a weak conjugate base. The relation is not valid for arbitrary unrelated acid and base species.

Salt solutions can be acidic or basic because their ions may participate in proton-transfer equilibria. Sodium acetate provides acetate, which accepts protons from water and produces hydroxide. Ammonium chloride provides ammonium, which donates protons to water. Sodium chloride is approximately neutral in ordinary dilute aqueous treatment because neither ion hydrolyses appreciably in the sense relevant here. For a salt with both ions capable of acid–base chemistry, compare the competing equilibrium strengths rather than assigning neutrality simply because the substance is called a salt.

A general solution strategy uses three independent kinds of information: equilibrium expressions, material balances and charge balance. For an ideal monoprotic acid in water, total acid C equals [HA] + [A⁻]; electroneutrality gives [H⁺] = [A⁻] + [OH⁻]; Ka and Kw constrain the species ratios. If a salt introduces an additional spectator cation, it must appear in the charge balance. These equations identify the underlying assumptions and allow numerical solution even when a convenient approximation fails. For NET-level problems one often chooses a simplified analytic expression, but the full balance method explains why that expression is valid.

12. pKa, fractional protonation and polyprotic acids

The acid dissociation expression provides an immediate relationship between pH and the relative abundance of two conjugate forms. Rearranging gives [A⁻]/[HA] ≈ Ka/[H⁺] = 10^(pH − pKa) under ideal dilute conditions. When pH equals pKa the two concentrations are equal. One unit above pKa, the ratio is ten to one; one unit below it, the ratio is one to ten. The deprotonated fraction is the ratio divided by one plus the ratio, or fA = 1/(1 + 10^(pKa − pH)). The protonated fraction is 1 − fA. Thus at pH two units above pKa, approximately 99% of the molecules are deprotonated for a simple isolated group.

A weak acid with pKa 6.0 is 50% deprotonated at pH 6, about 90.9% deprotonated at pH 7 and about 9.09% deprotonated at pH 5. For a basic group such as a protonated amine BH⁺, exactly the same expression describes the fraction in its deprotonated form B when the pKa is that of BH⁺. The charge states differ from those of the neutral acid example: protonated amine is positive, deprotonated amine is neutral. The mathematical relationship does not change, but the biological interpretation does.

Polyprotic acids release more than one proton in successive equilibria. Phosphoric acid has three dissociation steps: H₃PO₄ ⇌ H⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻; H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻; HPO₄²⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + PO₄³⁻. Typical dilute-solution pKa values near room temperature are about 2.1, 7.2 and 12.3. Each step involves a different conjugate pair and has its own equilibrium constant. Near neutral pH the first step is largely complete and the third is limited; the dominant pair is H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻. This is why phosphate is useful as a biological buffer near neutrality.

Successive proton removal often becomes less favourable because the remaining species becomes more negatively charged, although molecular structure and local interactions also matter. For a simple diprotic acid H₂A, let h represent hydrogen ion activity in a dilute concentration model. From Ka1 and Ka2 we obtain [HA⁻] = Ka1[H₂A]/h and [A²⁻] = Ka1Ka2[H₂A]/h². The total analytical acid is the sum of these three concentrations. Multiplying the resulting denominator by h² gives D = h² + Ka1h + Ka1Ka2. The fractions are h²/D, Ka1h/D and Ka1Ka2/D, respectively. Their sum is exactly one in the model. These formulas are useful when the two pKa values are not sufficiently separated for a single conjugate-pair approximation.

An intermediate form such as H₂PO₄⁻ or HCO₃⁻ may both donate and accept a proton, so it is amphiprotic. For a simple dilute solution of an amphiprotic intermediate with well-separated neighbouring pKa values and conditions in which water and concentration corrections are small, its pH is approximately the average of those two pKa values. This is an approximation derived from balancing the small amounts of more protonated and more deprotonated species. It is not a formula for every polyprotic buffer, and it is not a rule for calculating the pH of a mixture of two unrelated acids.

Ionisation of groups within biomolecules is often influenced by the surrounding structure. A buried charged group may be less stabilised by water than the corresponding group at the surface. Nearby positive or negative charges can shift the relative stability of protonated and deprotonated forms. Hydrogen bonds and conformational changes may couple protonation to binding. Therefore the pKa of an amino acid side chain in a folded protein is not necessarily identical to that of the free amino acid in dilute water. The ideal independent-group model remains a useful baseline from which biological deviations can be understood.

13. The chemical mechanism of buffering

A buffer is a system that resists changes in pH when modest amounts of acid or base are introduced. It commonly contains appreciable quantities of a weak acid and its conjugate base. The two forms play complementary roles. When strong acid is introduced, A⁻ accepts the added proton to form HA; when strong base is introduced, HA donates a proton and is converted to A⁻ while water forms. In both cases most of the added strong reagent is consumed in a stoichiometric proton-transfer reaction. The new equilibrium is determined by the altered relative abundance of the conjugate pair.

A buffer does not keep pH mathematically constant. If acid is added, the conjugate base pool becomes smaller and the acid pool larger; the resulting pH falls. The fall is modest when the added amount is small compared with both pools. If the base pool is exhausted, further strong acid cannot be absorbed by the same mechanism and the pH may change sharply. Similarly, excessive added base exhausts the proton-donor pool. An effective buffer is therefore a finite chemical reservoir, not an unlimited source or sink of protons.

Rearranging the equilibrium expression for HA gives h ≈ Ka[HA]/[A⁻]. Taking negative logarithms of both sides produces pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]), the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation in concentration form. The thermodynamic version uses the activities of the two forms. The ratio direction is important: increasing conjugate base relative to acid raises pH. An equation written with acid in the numerator and a plus sign would predict the opposite behaviour. When the two concentrations are equal, the logarithm is zero and pH equals pKa.

Suppose a buffer contains 0.060 mol A⁻ and 0.040 mol HA per litre, with pKa 6.80. Its approximate pH is 6.80 + log₁₀(1.5) = 6.976. Adding 0.010 mol strong acid to one litre, assuming negligible volume change and complete reaction with A⁻, gives 0.050 mol of each form, so pH becomes 6.80. Adding the same amount of base instead to the initial preparation converts HA to A⁻, yielding 0.070 mol base and 0.030 mol acid. Its pH becomes 6.80 + log₁₀(7/3) ≈ 7.168. Both pH changes are modest despite the amount of strong reagent being many orders of magnitude greater than the free proton amount present at the initial pH.

The use of mole ratios in the example is justified because both species occupy the same final solution volume. If an added reagent substantially changes the volume, the ratio of the two remaining mole amounts still equals their concentration ratio, provided no other equilibrium process changes the amounts significantly. However, dilution changes total buffer concentration and hence buffer capacity. If the buffer becomes sufficiently dilute, water autoionisation and changes in dissociation become significant and the simple stoichiometric calculation loses accuracy.

Buffer preparation can start from a weak acid and its salt or from the weak acid partially neutralised with a strong base. If a target pH and total analytical buffer concentration Ct are specified, the required ideal ratio r = 10^(pH − pKa). Material balance gives [HA] = Ct/(1 + r) and [A⁻] = Ctr/(1 + r). For a target of pH 7.40 with pKa 7.20 and Ct = 50 mM, r ≈ 1.585. The acid component is about 19.34 mM and the base component about 30.66 mM. Actual laboratory preparation also requires attention to ionic strength, temperature, reagent form, final volume and the calibrated measured pH.

The equation is convenient but not a replacement for equilibrium reasoning. If a problem starts with only a weak acid, the equilibrium generates some conjugate base but its abundance is not known without solving the dissociation problem. If strong acid is added in excess, the remaining base pool may be zero; inserting a negative or zero calculated concentration into a logarithm is invalid. First establish which species remain after any stoichiometric reaction, then select the equilibrium treatment. This order of operations is particularly important in titration problems.

14. Buffer capacity and the effect of dilution

Buffer pH and buffer capacity answer different questions. pH describes the equilibrium proton activity at a given moment; capacity describes the amount of added acid or base required to cause a specified pH change. Two acetate buffers can have the same acid-to-base ratio and hence the same approximate pH, yet a hundredfold difference in total concentration. The more concentrated buffer contains a much larger reservoir of proton donors and acceptors. This distinction is easily missed when studying only the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation because the ratio does not show the absolute number of molecules.

For a simple closed monoprotic buffer with constant total concentration Ct, define buffer capacity β locally as the concentration of strong base added per unit increase in pH at essentially fixed volume. From the fraction of conjugate base f = 1/(1 + 10^(pKa − pH)), increasing pH converts a fraction of HA into A⁻. Differentiating gives df/dpH = ln(10)f(1 − f). The contribution of the weak acid pair is therefore βpair = 2.303 Ct f(1 − f). Expressing f in terms of h and Ka gives βpair = 2.303 Ct Kah/(Ka + h)². This result is valid within an ideal dilute, constant-volume, monoprotic equilibrium model.

The product f(1 − f) is maximal at f = 0.5. Thus the pair contribution reaches βmax = 2.303 Ct/4 ≈ 0.576 Ct at pH = pKa. For a 0.10 M buffer the ideal maximum is about 0.0576 mol L⁻¹ per pH unit. For a 0.010 M buffer it is one-tenth of that value. This is a local slope, not an exact prediction for adding enough strong base to shift the pH by one whole unit. Over a large pH interval the slope changes, so one should calculate the finite change from the initial and final species amounts.

Free hydrogen and hydroxide ions also contribute to the total acid/base capacity. In the ideal model their contribution is approximately 2.303(h + Kw/h) when h denotes molar hydrogen concentration. At strongly acidic or basic pH these free-ion terms can dominate. The familiar maximum near the pKa refers to the isolated weak-acid-pair contribution, not necessarily to the entire pH range of every solution. Polyprotic buffers have overlapping contributions from multiple protonation steps. Proteins additionally contain groups whose pKa values may interact.

A practical buffer range is often described as pKa ± 1 because within that interval both conjugate forms are present in meaningful amounts. At the edges the minor form is about one eleventh of the total pool. This is a rule of thumb, not a sharp limit imposed by nature. A highly concentrated buffer used outside that range may still have more absolute capacity than a very dilute buffer exactly at its pKa. Selecting a buffer involves both pH proximity and concentration, alongside compatibility with the biological system.

Consider two solutions initially containing equal concentrations of HA and A⁻, with total buffer concentrations 100 mM and 10 mM. Adding 2 mM strong acid at constant volume changes the first from 50/50 to 48/52 mM in the base/acid order. The pH decreases by log₁₀(48/52) ≈ −0.0348 units. The second changes from 5/5 to 3/7 mM, and pH decreases by about −0.368 units. The initial pH values were identical but their responses to the same acid input were not. This finite example makes capacity visible without calculus.

Dilution of a well-behaved buffer changes both concentrations proportionally, so their ratio and approximate pH are preserved. Yet its capacity per unit volume falls. If a whole undiluted sample is diluted while retaining all buffer molecules, its total number of buffering groups remains unchanged even though capacity per litre is lower. This distinction between capacity of a volume and the capacity of a complete sample helps prevent mistakes in comparisons where the final volumes differ.

15. Titration curves and stoichiometric regimes

A titration systematically introduces a reagent of known concentration and measures a property that reveals the chemical reaction. In an acid–base titration, pH is commonly measured as a function of added titrant volume. The equivalence point is determined by stoichiometry: the amount of base added matches the number of acid equivalents being neutralised for the specified step. An indicator endpoint is an observed colour transition, which ideally occurs near the equivalence point but need not coincide exactly with it. The difference between endpoint and equivalence is one reason that titration methods require appropriate indicator selection or instrumental measurement.

For a strong monoprotic acid titrated with strong base, first calculate initial acid moles and added hydroxide moles. Before equivalence, the difference is excess acid. Divide by the total volume to obtain approximate free hydrogen concentration. After equivalence, excess hydroxide determines pOH. At equivalence the remaining salt is approximately neutral for a simple strong-acid/strong-base system at 25 °C. If the initial acid is 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl, it contains 2.50 mmol. The equivalence volume of 0.100 M NaOH is 25.0 mL. At 20.0 mL base added, 0.50 mmol acid remains in 45.0 mL, so h ≈ 0.01111 M and pH ≈ 1.95. At 30.0 mL base added, 0.50 mmol hydroxide remains in 55.0 mL; pOH ≈ 2.04 and pH ≈ 11.96.

A weak acid titration has additional regimes. Initially the pH follows weak acid dissociation. After addition of strong base but before equivalence, some acid has been converted to conjugate base, forming a buffer. In a suitable region, Henderson–Hasselbalch applies using the remaining HA and generated A⁻ amounts. At half-equivalence, half the original acid has been converted, so HA and A⁻ are approximately equal and pH ≈ pKa. The half-equivalence point is not the equivalence point, and the pH at equivalence need not be seven.

Take 25.0 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid, with pKa about 4.76, titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. The starting amount is again 2.50 mmol and the equivalence volume 25.0 mL. At 10.0 mL titrant, 1.00 mmol acetate has been generated and 1.50 mmol HA remains. The buffer estimate is pH = 4.76 + log₁₀(1.00/1.50) ≈ 4.58. At 12.5 mL titrant the two pools are equal and pH ≈ 4.76. At 20.0 mL titrant the ratio is 2.00/0.50 = 4, yielding pH ≈ 5.36. The increasingly rapid rise in pH near equivalence is a consequence of depletion of the protonated reservoir.

At the equivalence point of this weak acid/strong base titration, acetate remains and can accept protons from water. The total acetate concentration is 2.50 mmol/50.0 mL = 0.050 M. With Ka = 10⁻⁴⋅⁷⁶, Kb = Kw/Ka ≈ 5.75 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 25 °C. The hydroxide concentration is approximately √(KbC) = 5.36 × 10⁻⁶ M, giving pOH ≈ 5.27 and pH ≈ 8.73. An answer of seven would ignore acetate hydrolysis. Beyond equivalence, enough excess strong base dominates the pH, though very close to equivalence a complete equilibrium treatment gives a smoother transition.

Polyprotic acid titrations can display multiple buffer regions and equivalence steps if their pKa values are sufficiently separated. A diprotic acid can consume two equivalents of strong base per mole when fully deprotonated. At the midpoint of a well-resolved first neutralisation region, pH approximates pKa1; at the midpoint of the next region, it approximates pKa2. If the pKa values are close together, the transitions overlap and it is misleading to assume the curve contains separately resolvable features for every chemically ionisable group. The number of visible steps is therefore not necessarily equal to the number of transferable protons.

In biological experiments, a titration curve can reveal apparent ionisation behaviour. A molecule with several groups may have broad overlapping regions rather than perfectly separated textbook steps. The slope depends on the total buffering contributions of those groups, and changes in protein conformation can alter apparent pKa values. A titration does not identify a particular residue solely from a pKa estimate; structural or mutational evidence may be required. The correct conclusion is proportional to the information actually supplied by the measurement.

16. Phosphate, bicarbonate and protein buffers

Phosphate is a central biological buffer because its second dissociation lies close to the pH range of many cellular systems. In an ideal dilute model near neutral pH, H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ dominate. With an illustrative pKa2 of 7.20, a solution at pH 7.40 has a base-to-acid ratio of 10^0.20 ≈ 1.585. About 61.3% of the pair is in the more deprotonated form. The actual apparent pKa depends on temperature and ionic conditions. Both forms carry charge, so changing their ratio may alter ionic strength as well as pH unless other solution components are adjusted.

Phosphate also interacts with biological molecules and metal ions. A buffer that is ideal for maintaining pH may not be inert toward every experimental system: phosphate can bind or precipitate with certain ions depending on composition, and it is itself involved in metabolic reactions. For an enzyme that uses phosphate as a substrate or product, a phosphate buffer might alter the reaction independently of pH. This is why pH matching alone cannot establish that two experimental media are equivalent.

The bicarbonate system illustrates a different principle, because it is coupled to a gas phase and biological removal of carbon dioxide. The reactions are CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. In physiological acid–base calculations, dissolved CO₂ and its hydration equilibrium are often represented together using an apparent pKa of approximately 6.1 at body temperature. The denominator used in the familiar expression is dissolved CO₂ (with the conventional combined acid-pool treatment), not solely the concentration of true hydrated carbonic acid molecules. Confusing the apparent and intrinsic dissociation constants gives incorrect numerical ratios.

Using the approximate solubility coefficient 0.03 mmol L⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹ for CO₂ at 37 °C, pH ≈ 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻]/(0.03 PCO₂)), with bicarbonate in mmol L⁻¹ and partial pressure in mmHg. At 24 mM bicarbonate and PCO₂ = 40 mmHg, the dissolved CO₂ term is 1.2 mM. The ratio is 20 and the estimated pH is 7.40. Holding bicarbonate constant while doubling PCO₂ to 80 mmHg halves the ratio and lowers the calculated pH by about 0.301 units. This is an isolated chemical perturbation, not a complete physiological prediction, because actual bicarbonate and other buffering responses may change simultaneously.

The bicarbonate buffer can operate effectively in extracellular fluid even though its apparent pKa lies below normal blood pH. It has a substantial base reservoir, and ventilation controls carbon dioxide while renal processes contribute to bicarbonate and acid balance. An open system can exchange material with its surroundings, which changes its effective ability to handle a sustained acid load compared with a sealed buffer of identical initial composition. Carbonic anhydrase catalyses reversible hydration and dehydration of carbon dioxide; it accelerates approach to equilibrium but does not independently change the equilibrium constant at the same conditions.

Proteins provide numerous protonatable groups. Histidine residues are particularly relevant because the imidazole group's pKa is often in or near a biologically useful range, though values vary with local structure. Amino and carboxyl termini and other ionisable side chains contribute according to their accessibility, abundance and pKa values. When the environmental pH changes, their protonation distribution can shift. A protein's buffer capacity is therefore distributed across a range of pH rather than always concentrated at a single pKa.

Haemoglobin provides an instructive link between proton binding and ligand binding. Oxygenation changes haemoglobin's conformational ensemble and its interactions with protons; protonation in turn affects oxygen affinity. The resulting Bohr effect connects acid–base equilibria to oxygen transport. This is not simply the behaviour of a free histidine solution, because several sites and coupled structural equilibria are involved. Detailed oxygen-binding cooperativity belongs to the protein structure–function chapter. Here the key principle is that protein protonation can both buffer the solution and respond to other molecular processes.

Biological pH stability relies on both chemical buffering and regulation. Buffers provide immediate resistance to perturbation by changing chemical form. Transporters and metabolic reactions can subsequently remove or redistribute acid/base equivalents, while gas exchange can alter dissolved carbon dioxide. A finite closed buffer cannot absorb a continuing load indefinitely. The existence of near-steady cellular pH therefore does not mean proton-producing reactions have stopped; it indicates that production, removal and buffering operate together.

17. Measurements, experimental interpretation and buffer selection

A pH meter provides an operational measurement calibrated against standard buffers. A glass electrode responds to hydrogen-ion activity through a potential difference involving the selective glass membrane and reference electrode. In an ideal monovalent response, the magnitude of the Nernst slope is 2.303RT/F, about 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C. The sign depends on electrode orientation and instrument convention. Calibration maps measured potentials to known pH values and accounts for electrode offsets and response characteristics. This is why measuring an unknown solution without checking the calibration is less informative than reporting a calibrated reading at a stated temperature.

Temperature can change both the electrode response slope and the actual solution equilibrium constants. Automatic temperature compensation of an electrode does not automatically convert the chemical pH of a sample at one temperature into the pH it would have at another. A buffer with temperature-sensitive pKa can shift appreciably on warming. For enzyme measurements at different temperatures, the actual pH at the reaction temperature should be considered. Otherwise an apparent temperature dependence of activity may partly reflect an unintended pH change.

Buffer selection depends on the pH range, required capacity and compatibility with the substances in the experiment. A buffer with pKa close to the target provides a useful starting point. However, ionic strength, metal binding, absorption of light at the measurement wavelength, effects on enzymes and permeability to membranes may also matter. An apparently inactive buffering compound can interact with a biological system. Good and colleagues developed criteria for biologically useful buffers in 1966; their work highlights that the buffer is an experimental component rather than a completely invisible background substance.

To test whether a pH effect on enzyme activity is actually due to pH, one could compare activity in different suitable buffer systems over their overlapping pH region while controlling temperature, ionic strength, substrate and enzyme concentrations. If the activities disagree at the same measured pH, the result suggests an additional buffer-specific or medium-dependent effect. If the activities agree, that supports—but does not conclusively prove—the interpretation that pH is a principal variable. Replicates, controls and uncertainty estimates matter because biological measurements can vary for reasons unrelated to the intended manipulation.

An osmometry experiment addresses a different question. Compare an ideal prediction from the solution composition to a measured freezing-point depression. If the observed depression is lower than predicted for completely independent ions, possible explanations include nonideal ionic interactions, association, incomplete composition accounting or measurement error. It is not legitimate to choose one cause solely because it is plausible. Repeated measurements, calibration with standards, and systematic concentration variation help distinguish possibilities. A plot of measured osmolality versus analytical concentration can reveal departures from a simple linear model without identifying the microscopic mechanism by itself.

A combined cell-volume and osmometry experiment can reveal the distinction between permeant and retained solutes. Prepare two baths with similar measured osmolality but different solute identity. Observe cell volume over time. If one sample shows sustained swelling while the other remains stable, the difference cannot be explained by total osmolality alone. Different membrane permeability, transport and regulation must be considered. An interpretation should specify the cell type, elapsed time and the limits of the assumed membrane model. Such a problem asks for evidence-based inference rather than memorisation of the labels isotonic and hypotonic.

The broad lesson is that a measured number is tied to a method and a model. pH reflects proton activity; freezing-point osmometry reflects solvent behaviour; concentration measurements identify an amount relative to a denominator; volume changes reveal water movement under a set of membrane and mechanical conditions. None of these quantities is interchangeable with the others. Their combination, together with explicit material and charge balances, permits a much stronger explanation of biological behaviour than a single isolated measurement.
