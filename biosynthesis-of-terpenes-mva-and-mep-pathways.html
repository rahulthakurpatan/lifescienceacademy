1. The biological problem: constructing a five-carbon building block

A young leaf needs pigments to capture light, electron carriers to move electrons between photosystems, and sterols to maintain the physical properties of cellular membranes. A flowering plant may also release volatile compounds that influence animal behaviour. These materials appear very different when we observe their functions, but many share an underlying biosynthetic logic: their carbon skeletons are assembled from small branched five-carbon precursors. Understanding how those precursors arise explains the connection between central carbohydrate metabolism and the extraordinary diversity of plant isoprenoids.

A terpene is conventionally a hydrocarbon constructed from isoprene-related units, whereas the broader word terpenoid includes oxygenated and otherwise modified derivatives. In biological usage the terminology can overlap. An isoprenoid carbon skeleton need not display a sequence of recognizable unchanged five-carbon fragments in its final structure, because cyclization, rearrangement, cleavage and oxidation can modify the original backbone. Therefore, biosynthetic origin is often a more reliable basis for classification than a simple inspection of the finished molecule.

Free isoprene, a volatile hydrocarbon with the formula C5H8, is not the general substrate from which plants assemble all longer terpenoids. Instead, the immediate universal precursors are isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP). These molecules both have five carbon atoms and a diphosphate group, but their double bonds occupy different positions. IPP usually provides the incoming carbon unit during chain extension, whereas DMAPP can initiate extension because it is an allylic diphosphate. This difference in chemical reactivity is crucial: the two isomers are not interchangeable in every individual enzyme reaction.

Plants use two routes to the five-carbon precursors. The mevalonate pathway (MVA) operates mainly outside plastids, in the cytosolic and associated endomembrane metabolic system. The methylerythritol phosphate pathway (MEP) operates in plastids. Both make chemically identical precursors, but the pools are not necessarily freely mixed. Consequently, the location of an enzyme and the supply of substrates to that location matter almost as much as the enzyme's catalytic properties. Plants can redistribute some isoprenoid intermediates between compartments, but the extent depends on physiological circumstances.

The carbon feeding both pathways ultimately comes from assimilated or stored carbon in a plant. Photosynthesis fixes inorganic carbon into organic compounds, but individual isoprenoid molecules are not made by direct addition of atmospheric carbon dioxide to growing terpene chains. Instead, sugars are metabolized into smaller intermediates such as glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, pyruvate and acetyl-CoA. The MVA and MEP pathways reorganize these intermediates, consume energy and reducing equivalents, and generate activated five-carbon units.

2. The mevalonate pathway: acetyl-CoA to IPP

Acetyl-CoA consists of a two-carbon acetyl group attached to coenzyme A by a thioester bond. The sulfur-containing linkage makes the acetyl group an effective substrate for transfer and condensation reactions. Acetyl-CoA participates in many metabolic processes, so its availability for one biosynthetic pathway depends in part on competition from other pathways and on the compartment in which it is produced. A mitochondrial pool of acetyl-CoA cannot simply be treated as identical to a cytosolic pool because membranes constrain exchange. In plant cells, cytosolic acetyl-CoA can be supplied in part by ATP-citrate lyase, which cleaves citrate to yield acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate.

The first MVA reaction joins two acetyl-CoA molecules into acetoacetyl-CoA, a four-carbon thioester. Acetoacetyl-CoA thiolase (AACT) catalyses this reversible condensation, releasing one CoA molecule. Although the equilibrium of the condensation is not inherently favourable in isolation, subsequent consumption of the product and appropriate substrate conditions support forward metabolic flux. The carbon balance at this stage is two plus two equals four; no carbon is lost.

HMG-CoA synthase (HMGS) subsequently combines acetoacetyl-CoA with a third acetyl-CoA. The product is 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA, abbreviated HMG-CoA. The net reaction includes hydrolysis of a thioester and release of another CoA. The six-carbon product contains the branched carbon architecture that will become characteristic of an isoprenoid unit. The branch is not added after IPP is formed. It is established by the chemistry of the precursor condensation.

HMG-CoA reductase (HMGR) then reduces the thioester functionality of HMG-CoA to a primary alcohol, yielding mevalonate and free CoA. This requires two NADPH molecules per mevalonate, reflecting the four-electron reduction of the thioester carbonyl to an alcohol. In plants HMGR is typically associated with the endoplasmic reticulum, with its catalytic region exposed to the cytosolic side. Thus an ER membrane association does not imply that the catalytic reaction occurs inside the ER lumen. HMGR is an important regulatory point, but the extent of control exerted by this enzyme can vary with tissue, metabolic state and downstream demand.

Mevalonate is a six-carbon compound, not an isoprenoid C5 unit. Mevalonate kinase (MK) consumes ATP to phosphorylate its terminal hydroxyl group, generating mevalonate 5-phosphate. Phosphomevalonate kinase (PMK) then transfers another phosphate from ATP, producing mevalonate 5-diphosphate. Neither reaction changes the number of carbon atoms. The two phosphates will be retained as the diphosphate group of IPP, providing chemical functionality necessary for subsequent prenyl transfer reactions.

Mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase (MDD, also called MPDC) consumes a third ATP molecule during the conversion of mevalonate 5-diphosphate to IPP. The enzyme transiently phosphorylates a hydroxyl group, and the resulting elimination is coupled to decarboxylation. Carbon dioxide is released from the carboxyl group, while inorganic phosphate is also released. The five-carbon product contains a double bond. Counting carbons across the full route gives six carbons in three acetyl groups and five retained in IPP; one carbon leaves as CO2. The ATP-dependent decarboxylation is therefore both a carbon-removal step and a chemical activation step, not an ordinary spontaneous loss of carbon dioxide.

The three direct ATP-consuming reactions are MK, PMK and MDD. HMGR is instead the major directly NADPH-consuming step in this conventional route. A useful accounting identity per IPP formed is three acetyl-CoA, three ATP and two NADPH. It excludes the energetic cost of producing acetyl-CoA, transporting precursors and replenishing cellular cofactor pools. Confusing the direct reaction cost with the total physiological energy cost is a common source of incorrect numerical comparisons between metabolic pathways.

3. The plastidial MEP pathway: two three-carbon precursors

The alternative route begins with pyruvate and D-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP), each containing three carbon atoms. These compounds can arise from carbohydrate metabolism; photosynthetically active plastids have direct connections to assimilated triose phosphates, while nonphotosynthetic plastids can obtain carbon and energy by other metabolic arrangements. The occurrence of the MEP pathway in plastids therefore does not imply that the pathway is restricted to illuminated green leaves.

1-Deoxy-D-xylulose 5-phosphate synthase (DXS) catalyses a thiamine diphosphate-dependent condensation of pyruvate with GAP. The carboxyl carbon of pyruvate is released as carbon dioxide, and the remaining two-carbon fragment is transferred to the three-carbon acceptor. The resulting product, 1-deoxy-D-xylulose 5-phosphate (DXP or DOXP), contains five carbon atoms. Unlike the conventional MVA route, which removes carbon at its final decarboxylation, the MEP route removes carbon during its first reaction. DXP also contributes to other metabolic routes in some organisms, so describing DXS as an important entry enzyme is more precise than treating all DXP formation as exclusively committed to isoprenoids in every biological context.

DXP reductoisomerase (DXR, also called IspC) rearranges and reduces DXP to 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate. NADPH supplies reducing power. This reaction reorganizes the carbon skeleton and forms the methyl-branched framework. MEP is still a phosphorylated polyol, not an activated isoprenoid diphosphate. Its formation represents a decisive transition into the MEP route and is inhibited by fosmidomycin, a useful biochemical tool for studying the pathway.

The next three enzymes transform MEP into a cyclic intermediate. MEP cytidylyltransferase (MCT or IspD) uses CTP to generate CDP-ME, or 4-diphosphocytidyl-2-C-methyl-D-erythritol. This step illustrates that nucleotide energy requirements are not limited to ATP. CDP-ME kinase (CMK or IspE) consumes ATP to phosphorylate CDP-ME, yielding CDP-ME 2-phosphate. MEP cyclodiphosphate synthase (MDS or IspF) then catalyses cyclization, producing 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 2,4-cyclodiphosphate (MEcPP) and releasing CMP. Throughout these steps the carbon skeleton of the pathway intermediate remains C5; the carbons in the transiently attached cytidine moiety do not become the isoprenoid backbone.

HMBPP synthase (HDS or IspG) converts MEcPP to (E)-4-hydroxy-3-methylbut-2-enyl diphosphate, abbreviated HMBPP. The reaction is a reductive transformation involving a catalytic iron-sulfur cluster and an external electron supply. HMBPP reductase (HDR or IspH), another iron-sulfur enzyme, catalyses reductive dehydroxylation of HMBPP to yield both IPP and DMAPP. Each HMBPP molecule forms one C5 product, not simultaneously one molecule of each. The product mixture often favours IPP, but its exact ratio depends on enzyme and conditions. In plastids, reduced ferredoxin can support the terminal reductive reactions; the ultimate source of reducing power may differ between green and non-green tissues.

Direct bookkeeping per C5 unit starts with one pyruvate and one GAP, and includes one CTP at the MCT step and one ATP at the CMK step. DXR consumes NADPH, and each of HDS and HDR requires a two-electron reduction. It is misleading to claim that the whole pathway uses only one ATP and is therefore necessarily cheaper than MVA. CTP must be regenerated, released CMP must be recycled, reducing electrons have energetic value, and precursor generation has costs. Compare explicit reactions before attempting any conversion into common ATP equivalents.

4. IPP and DMAPP: how chain extension begins

IPP and DMAPP share a molecular formula but differ in the position of the double bond. The diphosphate-bearing carbon in DMAPP is allylic, meaning that it neighbours the double bond. Cleavage of the carbon–oxygen bond can generate an allylic cation-like intermediate stabilized by resonance. This chemical property permits electrophilic addition to the double bond of an incoming IPP molecule within an enzyme active site. IPP has a different arrangement and ordinarily serves as the nucleophilic partner rather than the initial allylic donor in conventional trans-prenyltransferase reactions.

Isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase (IDI or IPPI) catalyses reversible interconversion of IPP and DMAPP by proton addition and removal, without changing the total carbon count. MVA makes IPP directly in the conventional route and relies on isomerization to provide DMAPP. MEP produces both directly at HDR, but isomerase activity can still help adjust the ratio of the two pools. Both pathways must ultimately provide the proportions demanded by downstream enzymes. The existence of two isomers is therefore not a trivial chemical detail: shortage of the starter can restrict product formation even when the extending substrate is abundant.

Geranyl diphosphate synthase (GPPS) joins DMAPP and IPP to form geranyl diphosphate (GPP), a ten-carbon allylic diphosphate. Farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FPPS) catalyses formation of farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), a fifteen-carbon product; GPP can arise as a reaction intermediate in this sequence. Geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase (GGPPS) produces the twenty-carbon geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP). Individual enzymes can differ in preferred substrates and products. For carbon accounting, each extension adds five carbons and releases diphosphate. The growing allylic product retains the diphosphate functionality derived from the incoming IPP, enabling the next round of condensation.

Chain termination depends on enzyme specificity, not depletion of a universal supply of free isoprene. The shape and volume of an active site influence which chain lengths can be accommodated and released. Related prenyltransferases may produce different chain lengths, and some plants use heteromeric enzyme complexes to alter product specificity. The basic progression GPP to FPP to GGPP is a useful carbon map, but it is not proof that GPP must be released into a common pool before each downstream reaction.

5. Compartmentation and the meaning of metabolic cross-talk

The dominant spatial arrangement in many plants places MVA-derived precursors upstream of sterols, triterpenes, many sesquiterpenes and the prenyl side chain of ubiquinone. Plastidial MEP-derived precursors commonly supply monoterpenes, diterpenes, carotenoids and the prenyl side chains of chlorophyll and plastoquinone. These are useful examination defaults rather than universally exclusive assignments. The localization of downstream enzymes and changes in precursor exchange can produce exceptions. Gibberellins, for example, arise from a diterpenoid precursor, whereas brassinosteroids arise from sterol metabolism. Their hormonal mechanisms are separate from the precursor synthesis discussed here.

The plastid envelope consists of membranes separating the plastid interior from the cytosol. Highly charged phosphorylated metabolites cannot simply diffuse across lipid bilayers as if those membranes were absent. Observations of shared precursor contributions therefore do not establish unrestricted movement of all intermediates. The identity and relative contributions of exchanged intermediates, directionality and physiological importance may depend on the species, tissue and experimental conditions. A scientifically accurate model allows constrained exchange while retaining the distinction between the two biosynthetic routes.

Compartmentation can make the concentration of a metabolite in whole tissue a poor guide to its local availability. A homogenate combines plastidial and nonplastidial material, potentially obscuring depletion in one compartment while another remains well supplied. A plastid-targeted enzyme introduced into the cytosol may fail to produce the expected compound if its substrates or electron donors are unavailable. This principle links pathway chemistry with organelle biology and explains why subcellular targeting is a central consideration in plant metabolic engineering.

6. Experimental evidence: inhibitors, tracers and causal reasoning

Historically, work on acetate incorporation and mevalonate established a route to isoprenoid biosynthesis. The discovery of a second route followed from carbon-labeling patterns in organisms and plant products that were inconsistent with derivation exclusively through the known MVA pathway. Carbon atoms derived from pyruvate and triose phosphate have a different biosynthetic history than those derived from three acetyl groups. Stable-isotope tracer experiments can reveal these patterns without relying on similarities of final molecular structure. The key insight is that chemically identical end products can preserve evidence of different precursor histories in their carbon atoms.

Laule and colleagues investigated pathway interactions in Arabidopsis seedlings using lovastatin to inhibit the MVA route and fosmidomycin to inhibit the plastidial MEP route (2003). These perturbations affected isoprenoid accumulation and provided evidence that the pathways are metabolically connected. This does not mean a single inhibitor establishes the identity of the molecule crossing the plastid envelope. Inhibitors are most informative when interpreted alongside independent biochemical, genetic and tracer evidence.

Lovastatin targets HMGR and fosmidomycin targets DXR. An immediate prediction is that inhibition of HMGR decreases direct mevalonate production, whereas inhibition of DXR decreases direct MEP production. Downstream pools respond with a delay that depends on their turnover, existing reserves and alternative inputs. If chlorophyll levels decline after a prolonged MEP block, pigment loss could be linked to limited prenyl supply, but secondary damage to plastids may amplify the phenotype. The observation is a physiological consequence, not an independent measurement of the instantaneous DXS or DXR catalytic rate.

Stable isotopes such as carbon-13 can be used to distinguish atoms originating from different substrate pools. Consider a simplified thought experiment in which only the carboxyl carbon of pyruvate is labelled, and the labelled molecule enters DXS without prior metabolic conversion. That label is predicted to leave as carbon dioxide rather than enter DXP. A label retained in another carbon of pyruvate could enter the product instead. In whole plants, labelled carbon may be redistributed by central metabolism or reassimilated as CO2. For this reason, an intact-plant tracer experiment needs appropriate interpretation of isotopic enrichment, substrate uptake and carbon recycling. A simple atom balance is a mechanistic prediction under controlled assumptions, not a complete description of an intact plant experiment.

An effective experimental design compares untreated and solvent-treated controls with pathway-specific perturbations and independent confirmation of target activity. Measurements can include relevant precursor pools, products, enzyme activity or isotope incorporation. A genetic complementation experiment can test whether restoration of an enzyme restores a phenotype. Even if a product is rescued by feeding an intermediate, uptake and compartment access must be considered. Failure of rescue is not proof that the proposed enzyme function is wrong, since the supplied intermediate might never reach the affected compartment.

7. Carbon and energetic accounting

For a conventional prenyl diphosphate of length 5n carbons made by repeated head-to-tail extension, the formal precursor count is one DMAPP plus n minus one IPP. Thus GPP requires one starter and one extender, FPP requires one starter and two extenders, and GGPP requires one starter and three extenders. The number of condensation steps is n minus one. This rule applies to the specified head-to-tail assembly process, not to every later terpenoid transformation. For example, two FPP molecules can contribute to a thirty-carbon squalene skeleton via a different coupling mechanism.

Suppose a pathway makes 10 micromoles of GGPP entirely from MVA-derived precursors. Four C5 units are needed per GGPP, so the demand is 40 micromoles of C5 precursors. Each originates from three acetyl-CoA molecules, requiring 120 micromoles of acetyl-CoA, 120 micromoles of directly consumed ATP and 80 micromoles of NADPH for the conventional MVA reactions. The carbon loss is 40 micromoles of CO2. Isomerization of the necessary IPP fraction into DMAPP does not require extra carbon. The calculation omits the cost of generating acetyl-CoA and cofactors and assumes no diversion into competing products.

For the analogous idealized MEP calculation, 40 micromoles each of pyruvate and GAP supply the 40 micromoles of C5 units. DXS releases 40 micromoles of CO2. Direct MCT and CMK requirements are respectively 40 micromoles of CTP and 40 micromoles of ATP, while DXR requires 40 micromoles of NADPH and the terminal two reductive reactions require a further 160 micromoles of electrons in total. The latter figure follows from two electrons per reaction, twice per C5 molecule. It does not specify the precise molecular donor pool because that depends on cellular context. Both pathways retain five of six directly entering carbon atoms; equal carbon retention does not imply equal cellular energy costs.
