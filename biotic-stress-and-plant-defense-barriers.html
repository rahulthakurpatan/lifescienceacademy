1. From contact to disease

A leaf is not sterile. Its outer surface supports microbial communities whose members may be harmless, beneficial or harmful to the plant. Even organisms capable of causing disease must first reach a suitable plant, encounter a favorable environment and overcome barriers that normally separate living plant material from the surroundings. An organism found on a leaf is therefore not automatically a pathogen of that plant. The observation of colonization and the demonstration of disease causation are different scientific claims.

Biotic stress is a disturbance imposed by a living organism or, in the conventional physiological treatment of plant disease, by an infectious biological agent such as a virus. A pathogen causes disease under suitable conditions. Infection refers to establishment of an infectious agent in a host; disease refers to a harmful disturbance of normal structure or function. A symptom is a host response, such as chlorosis, wilting or necrosis. A sign is evidence of the agent itself, such as fungal sporulation. Symptoms alone seldom identify the causal agent because unrelated infectious and noninfectious conditions can produce similar injury.

The barrier problem arises because the plant must maintain exchange with its environment. A fully impermeable shoot could reduce opportunities for microbial entry but would also impede carbon dioxide uptake for photosynthesis. Roots must take up water and ions while growing through soil populated by microorganisms. The plant therefore combines structural protection, selective entry routes, chemicals that limit microbial activity and inducible responses that become stronger after challenge. Protection is a regulated physiological process rather than a simple permanent wall.

A useful description of disease separates arrival, attachment, penetration, establishment, multiplication, movement and damage. These stages do not necessarily occur in a single rigid order. For example, a bacterium may enter through an already open stoma without digesting the cuticle, whereas a fungus may produce a penetration structure on the external surface. A virus introduced by an insect vector may bypass the intact surface entirely. The defense appropriate to one stage is not automatically effective at another stage.

Plants do not possess circulating antibody-producing lymphocytes. Individual cells and tissues participate directly in recognizing danger and in reinforcing barriers, producing inhibitory chemicals and transmitting signals. The same basic cellular machinery used in environmental stress responses, including calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species and transcriptional regulation, can contribute to defense. The particular sequence depends on the organism, the tissue and the physiological condition of the host.

An infection that remains localized need not produce a visible symptom, and a symptom may appear after the pathogen has already spread. Consequently, the absence of disease at the time of inspection is not sufficient evidence that no entry occurred. Defense experiments require measurements of both the pathogen and the host, ideally at several stages. Counting surface spores answers a different question from counting successfully penetrated epidermal cells or measuring pathogen biomass in a lesion.

2. The disease triangle and host outcome

The disease triangle expresses an essential idea in plant pathology: disease emerges from the interaction among a susceptible host, a pathogen with the capacity to infect that host, and environmental conditions that permit infection and subsequent disease development. A susceptible genotype is not necessarily diseased when it is growing in a pathogen-free environment. Similarly, abundant inoculum does not guarantee disease when the organism cannot establish infection in that host.

Environmental effects act on both organisms. A humid leaf surface may allow the spores of a particular fungus to germinate, while water films facilitate movement of some bacterial populations. Temperature changes the rates of both pathogen development and host metabolism. Nutrient supply can alter growth, barrier composition and the availability of resources to the attacker. No single temperature or humidity threshold is appropriate for all pathogens. The duration of conducive conditions matters as well as their intensity.

A host is a plant species or genotype in which the infectious organism can establish a biologically relevant association. In a compatible interaction the pathogen succeeds in establishing disease or substantial infection, whereas an incompatible interaction is restricted by effective defense or other barriers to colonization. Compatibility describes the interaction between partners under specified conditions, not an immutable characteristic of the plant. A cultivar that restricts one isolate may be susceptible to a different isolate of the same pathogen species.

Nonhost resistance describes the resistance of a plant species to pathogens that normally infect other host species. It may include lack of appropriate entry cues, constitutive antimicrobial conditions, incompatible nutritional requirements and effective inducible immunity. It should not be reduced to a single universal receptor or a single structural trait. A pathogen may occasionally breach one barrier in a nonhost but fail at another stage. That observation helps explain why counting penetration events is more informative than treating host range as a simple yes-or-no property.

Resistance is a host property that reduces infection or the amount of pathogen relative to an appropriate comparison. Tolerance is the capacity to maintain performance at a given pathogen burden. A resistant plant and a tolerant plant can both have high yield, but by distinct mechanisms. A genotype that restricts bacterial growth from a million cells to ten thousand cells is demonstrating a resistance phenotype in that comparison; a genotype that supports the same bacterial population as a susceptible genotype but sustains less damage is showing a tolerance phenotype. To distinguish them, measure the infectious burden and plant function independently.

Tolerance is not the absence of immunity. A tolerant plant may possess partial resistance as well. The distinction is clearest when the response of plant performance to increasing pathogen burden can be estimated in more than one treatment. A genotype with greater performance in the absence of infection may look better after inoculation simply because it began larger. A suitable comparison therefore considers its uninfected baseline as well as the relationship between loss of performance and pathogen load.

The disease triangle also provides a framework for understanding control. Changing the environment, limiting initial contact with the pathogen or altering the host through breeding may all reduce disease. However, success of an intervention does not by itself establish which physiological barrier it changed. A treatment that dries leaf surfaces can reduce fungal establishment without inducing any plant defense gene. A treatment that improves mineral nutrition can improve tolerance without reducing pathogen abundance. A causal explanation requires observations capable of distinguishing those alternatives.

3. Major classes of plant attackers

Plant pathogenic bacteria are prokaryotic cells. Many foliar bacterial pathogens establish populations in the apoplast, the extracellular continuum of cell walls and intercellular spaces, where they encounter host-derived nutrients and secreted defense proteins. The apoplast is not the cytosol: a bacterium may colonize spaces between cells while plant plasma membranes remain intact. Surface bacteria can gain entry through natural openings such as stomata and hydathodes, or through injuries. Some bacteria colonize vascular tissues and interfere with water transport. These different sites lead to different symptoms and barriers.

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms. Many filamentous plant pathogens grow as hyphae, which can branch and form a mycelium. Their spores may germinate on a host surface and differentiate specialized structures, such as an appressorium in certain fungi. An appressorium may concentrate mechanical force at a penetration site; secreted enzymes may assist in overcoming host surface material. Neither appressoria nor direct penetration are universal fungal traits. Some fungi enter through openings or wounds, and others exploit damaged or senescent tissues. Fungal cell walls commonly contain chitin and glucans, which are relevant to both recognition and enzymatic defense.

Oomycetes resemble filamentous fungi superficially but belong to a distinct evolutionary lineage. Many oomycete walls are rich in cellulose and beta-glucans; their composition is not identical to that of typical fungal walls. Some species produce motile, flagellated zoospores that disperse in water. Phytophthora infestans, associated with potato late blight, illustrates the severe disease that an oomycete can cause. A treatment or defense enzyme effective against one type of fungal wall cannot be assumed to have equivalent activity against an oomycete. The distinction between morphology and phylogenetic relationship is essential.

Viruses are infectious entities that depend on host cellular machinery for multiplication. Their genomes may be RNA or DNA, and they do not possess bacterial or fungal cell walls. Many plant viruses enter through damage to the plant surface, sometimes introduced by feeding vectors. Once established in a cell, they may move to nearby cells through plasmodesmata and spread over larger distances via vascular tissues. The strategy of a chitinase, which hydrolyzes chitin, cannot directly explain resistance to viral replication. Sequence-specific RNA silencing provides a more relevant example of antiviral defense.

Plant-parasitic nematodes are multicellular animals. Many use a stylet to puncture plant tissues and obtain nutrition. Some feed externally on roots, others migrate inside roots, and sedentary endoparasites establish specialized feeding sites. Root-knot nematodes can induce giant cells, while cyst nematodes induce syncytia through remodeling and fusion of host cell domains. These feeding sites act as strong sinks for plant resources. Root growth, vascular function and shoot performance may deteriorate even when the original infection site is below ground.

Different attackers therefore create different spatial problems. A foliar bacterium outside the plasma membrane is exposed to apoplastic antimicrobial substances. A virus replicating inside a living cell must be controlled through intracellular processes, and a nematode modifying host development may exploit sustained nutrient transport. The correct experimental question is not merely whether a compound is antimicrobial, but whether it reaches the biologically relevant site, at an effective concentration, at the stage when the attacker depends on that process.

Not every pathogenic organism causes disease by extensive multiplication. A small population that secretes potent compounds may inflict substantial injury, while a large population in a tolerant host may cause less damage than expected. Nor should infection be confused with every form of herbivory: chewing insects remove tissue directly and piercing insects extract fluids or transmit pathogens. Herbivore-specific signaling and indirect defenses are treated in 6.H.11; here their role chiefly illustrates the diversity of biotic entry routes.

4. Biotrophs, necrotrophs and hemibiotrophs

A pathogen's nutritional lifestyle describes how it obtains resources from the host. A biotroph exploits living host tissue. Successful biotrophy involves acquiring nutrients while preserving enough host viability to maintain the association. Certain biotrophic fungi and oomycetes produce haustoria, specialized feeding structures that interact closely with host cells. The haustorium is separated from the host cytoplasm by a host-derived interface; its formation does not imply that the pathogen has simply ruptured the plasma membrane and entered the cytosol without a boundary.

A necrotroph obtains nutrients from dead or dying host tissue. Necrotrophic pathogens may secrete cell-wall-degrading enzymes or compounds that promote host damage. Tissue breakdown can release carbohydrates, amino acids and other resources, producing a suitable nutritional environment for the attacker. Botrytis cinerea is a standard example of a necrotrophic fungus. The ability to exploit damaged tissue means that rapid host cell death is not universally protective. A defense response that limits one pathogen by eliminating living feeding cells may inadvertently provide suitable conditions for another.

A hemibiotroph has an initial phase associated with living tissue followed by a phase of extensive tissue killing. Representative species in the genus Colletotrichum illustrate this change. The transition is biologically significant because host processes that are beneficial during early infection may be less useful later. Timing of a defense intervention becomes a central variable. Treating a hemibiotroph as if it behaves identically throughout its infection cycle obscures the very property that defines its lifestyle.

The categories are explanatory models rather than exhaustive taxonomic classes. Fungi are not necessarily necrotrophs, nor are oomycetes necessarily biotrophs. The same broad group may contain pathogens of several lifestyles. Some organisms display different behavior depending on the host, organ or infection stage, and descriptions of lifestyle can be more nuanced than the introductory three-category division.

A classic reasoning problem contrasts resistance associated with localized host cell death and resistance dependent on protection of living cells. When a pathogen relies on living tissue, limiting the availability of that tissue at the site of entry may reduce establishment. When an attacker thrives on dead tissue, preventing damaging oxidation, preserving cell integrity and reinforcing walls may instead contribute to resistance. Nevertheless, the existence of these tendencies does not mean that all biotrophs are stopped by cell death or that every necrotroph benefits from it under every condition. The outcome depends on the mechanisms and the context.

The distinction has experimental consequences. Suppose a mutant develops larger lesions after challenge with a necrotroph but supports less growth of a biotroph. The phenotype could reflect increased propensity for cell death, but it could also reflect altered hormone signaling, surface chemistry or developmental changes. To establish a cell-death explanation, measure damage before the pathogen expands, compare pathogen growth independently of lesion area, and test whether restoration of the altered process reverses both outcomes. Such evidence is stronger than inferring mechanism solely from lifestyle labels.

The temporal sequence of measurements is especially important for hemibiotrophs. If a host is examined only after large necrotic lesions have appeared, events that determined early compatibility may already be complete. An experiment that samples early penetration, later colonization and the transition to necrosis can reveal when a barrier matters. A barrier that delays penetration may change the time of symptom appearance even if the final lesion area eventually becomes similar.

5. Preformed physical barriers: cuticle, walls and trichomes

The aerial epidermis is covered in many organs by a hydrophobic cuticle, a composite of cutin and waxes associated with the outer epidermal cell wall. Cutin is a lipid-derived polymer, while waxes comprise mixtures of hydrophobic compounds that may be embedded in the cuticular matrix or deposited at its outer surface. Their arrangement affects surface permeability, wettability and interactions with the environment. The cuticle performs both physiological and defensive functions.

By restricting uncontrolled water movement, a cuticle helps preserve leaf hydration. Its surface properties may also limit persistent water films needed by some pathogens. However, water repellency does not create an absolute barrier against infection. Some fungal spores respond to hydrophobic surfaces or to particular surface chemicals as cues for germination and differentiation. A pathogen equipped for direct entry may exploit the characteristics of an otherwise protective cuticle. Thus a single physical trait can both inhibit one attacker and provide information to another.

When the cuticle is breached, the attacker encounters the plant cell wall. A primary wall is a composite of cellulose microfibrils, matrix polysaccharides and proteins; its mechanical properties arise from interactions among these components, not from cellulose alone. A pathogen attacking the wall may need to overcome several substrates and structural arrangements. Many pathogenic fungi and bacteria secrete enzymes that modify or degrade wall polysaccharides. The plant may respond to wall damage and release of fragments, making the wall both a barrier and a source of information about injury.

Secondary walls, particularly those enriched in lignin, can provide additional mechanical and chemical resistance. Lignin is a complex aromatic polymer derived from phenylpropanoid metabolism. It is not a carbohydrate and cannot be equated with cellulose or callose. Deposition of lignin changes wall properties and can make it more difficult for some pathogens to advance through tissues. Lignification in water-conducting xylem is also important for normal physiological function, so manipulation of lignin content can affect growth and transport as well as disease resistance.

Suberin-rich barriers occur in tissues such as the root endodermis and periderm. They restrict movement of water and solutes and may contribute to protection. In a root, however, barrier development must be coordinated with growth and nutrient uptake. Young tissue, emerging lateral roots and sites of mechanical injury may present opportunities for pathogen entry. An anatomical map is therefore more useful than assuming that the entire root surface has an identical permeability.

Trichomes are epidermal outgrowths. Nonglandular trichomes may impede movement or access of certain attackers, whereas glandular trichomes may produce secretions that influence interaction with organisms on the surface. Their effects depend on density, shape, chemical composition and the size or behavior of the attacker. Some microorganisms may accumulate near a trichome base or exploit specialized surface microenvironments. A high trichome density should consequently be treated as a measurable trait, not a universal indicator of pathogen resistance.

Natural openings are essential components of plant physiology. Stomata permit exchange of gases and regulate transpiration, but they can also be routes of microbial entry. Hydathodes allow guttation and can provide access for certain vascular pathogens. Entry through these structures differs mechanistically from direct enzymatic or mechanical penetration. A comparison of surface inoculation with direct delivery into internal tissues can help determine whether an observed resistance phenotype acts before entry or after it.

Barrier-defective mutants often yield counterintuitive results. A cuticle with increased permeability may indeed allow easier entry by some attackers, but the associated changes in signaling or antimicrobial release may lead to enhanced resistance in particular interactions. Reviews of cuticle-dependent defense describe examples in which altered cuticles are associated with resistance to necrotrophs. A mutation in a structural component is not equivalent to removing a purely inert sheet from a building; it changes the physiology of a living system. A mechanistic conclusion requires evaluation of permeability, defense activation and pathogen outcome together.

6. Induced local structural reinforcement

The first wall encountered by a pathogen is usually already present before infection. A papilla is a localized wall apposition produced in association with an attempted penetration site. Its formation concentrates defense material where the risk is greatest. Papillae can contain callose, phenolic material, proteins and other wall components, with the precise composition varying between interactions. The spatial localization is crucial: a small quantity of a material deposited directly beneath an invading structure can have a different effect from the same quantity distributed throughout a leaf.

Callose is a beta-1,3-glucan deposited by plasma membrane-associated synthases in selected regions. In defense, it may contribute to wall appositions and alter transport through plasmodesmata. Its chemistry differs from that of cellulose, whose principal linkages are beta-1,4. Both polymers are composed of glucose, but the way in which glucose units are connected influences their physical organization and biological function.

The wall apposition is formed on the plant side of the pre-existing wall. Delivery of materials, local changes in enzyme activity and cytoskeletal organization can cooperate in reinforcement. The timing of deposition matters. A deposit made before penetration may contribute to its prevention; a deposit made after entry may instead help restrict subsequent spread or may simply mark a site of recognition. Observing a papilla in a successfully infected cell does not automatically establish whether it helped, failed or arose too late.

Oxidative chemistry can help reinforce walls. Reactive oxygen species generated during defense may participate in signaling and in reactions associated with wall cross-linking and lignification. Hydrogen peroxide can serve as a substrate for peroxidases involved in oxidative polymerization of phenolic compounds. The net outcome depends on location and regulation, because excessive oxidation can injure the host itself. The earlier chapter on oxidative stress provides the background for distinguishing a controlled defense-associated oxidative response from generalized oxidative damage.

Plasmodesmata provide communication channels through walls between adjacent living cells. Regulation of callose near these channels can modify intercellular movement. Reducing connectivity may limit spread of some infectious agents, including viruses that require cell-to-cell transport. Yet closing communication routes also affects movement of endogenous molecules and coordination of tissue functions. The physiological consequence depends on when, where and how extensively connectivity changes.

An important experiment challenges the assumption that more callose always means greater disease resistance. Nishimura and colleagues reported in 2003 that the Arabidopsis pmr4 mutant, deficient in pathogen-induced callose deposition, displayed resistance to certain pathogens rather than the expected susceptibility. Genetic analysis showed that interrupting salicylic-acid-associated defense signaling restored susceptibility in this mutant background. The result supports a role for defense regulation associated with loss of the synthase; it does not show that callose is universally useless. The comparison of single and combined genetic defects distinguishes a simple missing-material explanation from a signaling-dependent phenotype.

When studying a papilla-defective mutant, researchers can count attempted penetration sites and the fraction showing successful entry. Aniline blue fluorescence is commonly used to visualize callose, but fluorescence intensity is not automatically an absolute measure of polymer mass without calibration and appropriate imaging controls. The experiment should include a comparable wild type and, where possible, a complemented line. Measurements at the same developmental stage and under comparable illumination and staining conditions improve the interpretation of differences.

A conclusion should be limited to the tested interaction. If a mutant exhibits fewer callose deposits but normal penetration resistance, alternative materials in papillae or other defenses may compensate. If the mutant exhibits greater penetration, that supports a role for the altered process, but indirect effects should still be considered. A convincing interpretation links anatomy, timing, genetics and pathogen behavior rather than inferring defense effectiveness from the intensity of one stain.

7. Constitutive chemical defense and induced metabolites

A structural barrier can prevent contact, but organisms that penetrate it may still be exposed to chemical inhibitors. Plants synthesize a broad range of specialized metabolites, many of which influence their interactions with organisms. Their defensive roles depend on concentration, localization, stability and the sensitivity of the attacker. A molecule abundant in a whole-leaf extract may be absent from the microenvironment where a particular pathogen grows.

Phytoanticipins are antimicrobial compounds present in healthy plant tissues before microbial challenge, or compounds generated from pre-existing constituents by activation rather than requiring new synthesis of the active defense compound from distant metabolic precursors after infection. Phytoalexins are low-molecular-weight antimicrobial compounds synthesized and accumulated in response to challenge. These names describe the circumstances of defense production rather than a strict chemical classification. A molecule's role must be understood in its particular plant and interaction.

Oat roots contain avenacins, triterpenoid saponins associated with protection against certain fungi. Saponins can interfere with membrane organization in susceptible organisms, often through interactions with sterols. Fungal species adapted to particular hosts may produce enzymes capable of detoxifying such compounds. The defense is therefore not determined solely by whether the plant has a saponin, but by the balance between exposure and the ability of the attacker to withstand or modify it. This is a classic example of a constitutive chemical barrier contributing to host range.

Glucosinolates in brassicaceous plants are stored defense-related compounds whose hydrolysis by myrosinases can generate biologically active products. The separation of substrate and enzyme in healthy tissue can reduce uncontrolled exposure to reactive products. Disruption of tissue or regulated activation can bring these components together. The identity and amount of resulting products depend on the particular substrate and reaction conditions. Thus a defense system can be prepared in advance while its active products are generated in response to injury.

Phytoalexin accumulation commonly requires changes in metabolic flux and expression or activity of biosynthetic enzymes. Camalexin in Arabidopsis provides a standard example; pisatin in pea and resveratrol in grapevine are other classic examples in appropriate defense contexts. These compounds differ chemically, and not every species produces the same set. The production of an inducible antimicrobial may be concentrated near infected tissue, limiting the metabolic cost and possible self-injury associated with maintaining high concentrations everywhere.

An increase in phytoalexin concentration following infection is evidence of a response, but it does not alone establish that the compound is necessary for resistance. A biosynthetic mutant provides a way of examining necessity. If the mutant has impaired synthesis and supports greater pathogen growth, the result is consistent with a defensive contribution. A complemented mutant or restoration of the compound at the appropriate site can strengthen that inference. However, a metabolic mutation may alter other compounds in the pathway, so a single mutation does not always isolate the action of one metabolite.

In vitro growth inhibition is a useful but incomplete measure. A compound that inhibits fungal growth in culture may fail to reach the infection site at the required concentration in a living leaf. Conversely, a compound that shows modest direct inhibition may cooperate with wall reinforcement, other metabolites or defense proteins in vivo. For an examination, distinguish chemical capacity from demonstrated physiological function. Evidence from concentration, localization, mutant behavior and pathogen detoxification is more persuasive when the lines of evidence agree.

8. Pathogenesis-related proteins and antimicrobial enzymes

Pathogenesis-related proteins, often abbreviated PR proteins, include several families whose abundance or activity can increase during infection or other defense-inducing conditions. They are not a single class of enzymes with one substrate. Some are hydrolytic enzymes, some have antimicrobial activity through other mechanisms, and some are widely used as indicators of defense-associated gene expression. Their induction is informative, but it does not alone prove that infection has been prevented.

Chitinases catalyze hydrolysis of chitin, a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine. Since chitin is an important component of many fungal walls, particular plant chitinases may contribute to antifungal defense. The released fragments can also participate in recognition responses. A chitinase does not directly hydrolyze bacterial peptidoglycan simply because that polymer contains N-acetylglucosamine; the chemical linkage context and substrate specificity matter. Likewise, the presence of chitinase activity does not guarantee equivalent protection from every fungus.

Beta-1,3-glucanases can hydrolyze glucans with beta-1,3 linkages. Such glucans occur in walls of many microbial pathogens, including fungi and oomycetes, although wall organization and accessibility vary. These enzymes may also affect host callose, depending on their localization and substrate properties. This dual relevance illustrates why one cannot predict an entire infection phenotype from the name of an enzyme alone.

Different hydrolytic proteins can act cooperatively. One enzyme may expose a wall component that becomes more accessible to another, and mixtures of inhibitory proteins and metabolites can affect several pathogen processes simultaneously. An experiment testing a single protein against an isolated substrate is valuable for demonstrating its biochemical activity but does not reproduce the full environment of the plant-pathogen interface.

PR-1 accumulation is commonly used as an indicator of salicylic-acid-associated defense in model plant experiments. It should not be treated as a complete measure of resistance. Increased PR-1 expression can accompany defense activation even when the pathogen remains capable of multiplying. Conversely, a resistant genotype may rely on mechanisms whose activity is not captured by measuring that one gene. To interpret a marker, relate it to the biological question and compare it with outcome measurements.

Protein localization matters because many attackers are encountered outside the plasma membrane. Defense proteins secreted to the apoplast can reach organisms growing in extracellular spaces. Other proteins remain intracellular or act in particular compartments. A transcript measurement establishes the abundance of the measured RNA, not the location or catalytic activity of the corresponding protein. Comparing transcript levels with enzyme activity and disease phenotype helps bridge the gap between gene expression and function.

9. Antiviral defense through RNA silencing

Viruses present a distinct defensive challenge because they multiply through host cellular machinery rather than growing as extracellular cells with independent walls. RNA silencing is a sequence-directed mechanism that can reduce the accumulation of viral RNA. During infection by many RNA viruses, double-stranded RNA forms may arise from replication or other processes. These RNA structures provide substrates for plant Dicer-like enzymes, which produce small interfering RNAs derived from viral sequences.

A small RNA can guide an Argonaute-containing complex to a complementary RNA target. When pairing and the biochemical properties of the complex permit, Argonaute can cleave the target, contributing to reduction of viral RNA. Other modes of silencing can operate, and DNA viruses can also be affected by RNA-directed transcriptional silencing in appropriate contexts. The central principle is sequence recognition through nucleic-acid complementarity, rather than direct digestion of an extracellular viral wall.

RNA-dependent RNA polymerases can amplify silencing responses by generating additional double-stranded RNA substrates in relevant pathways. Different Dicer-like proteins and Argonaute proteins contribute according to the virus and host. In Arabidopsis, DCL4 and DCL2 are important examples of antiviral Dicer-like activities; their small RNA products can participate in antiviral defense. These roles should not be interpreted as an exclusive universal assignment for all plants and viruses.

Many plant viruses encode suppressors of RNA silencing. A suppressor may interfere with small RNA production, binding, Argonaute function or another stage of the response. The ability of a virus to multiply therefore reflects competition between its replication and movement functions and the host's silencing machinery. Detecting virus-derived small RNAs in infected tissue is consistent with recognition and processing, but a high abundance of such RNAs can also result from a large quantity of viral substrate. Small RNA abundance alone does not measure the effectiveness of the defense.

To distinguish antiviral replication control from restriction of movement, compare viral RNA or reporter expression in initially infected cells with the size and number of infection foci in tissue. A treatment that leaves initial cellular replication largely unchanged but reduces the size of foci may affect movement or intercellular communication. A treatment that reduces viral RNA in individual infected cells may act on replication or RNA stability. Viral burden in an entire leaf conflates these effects unless the spatial and temporal structure of infection is examined.

RNA silencing is sometimes described as an adaptive sequence-specific antiviral response in a broad molecular sense. That terminology must not be confused with antibody-based adaptive immunity in vertebrates. Plants do not make antibodies or use somatic rearrangement of immunoglobulin genes to recognize viruses. In this chapter, RNA silencing is distinguished from the receptor-based immune layers studied in 6.H.09 and 6.H.10, while recognizing that these mechanisms may interact in living tissues.

10. Experimental and quantitative reasoning

To determine the defensive stage affected by a plant mutation, compare surface inoculation with a treatment that bypasses the surface. In a hypothetical example, a wild type and a cuticle-altered mutant receive comparable initial bacterial challenges. At a specified time, the mutant has a higher internal bacterial population after surface exposure, but the two genotypes support comparable populations following direct introduction into the internal tissue. The pattern is consistent with a defect in surface-associated restriction rather than a large difference in internal bacterial multiplication under the tested conditions.

This inference depends on several assumptions. Direct introduction should deliver comparable initial internal populations, and the procedure itself should not differentially injure the genotypes. Both genotypes should have comparable developmental stages and growth conditions. The surface method should expose leaves to comparable inoculum without washing off one genotype more effectively than the other. Measuring bacteria immediately after entry and again later helps separate initial access from subsequent growth. A single final count is less informative than the trajectory.

Penetration efficiency provides a simple quantitative measure for filamentous attackers. It equals the number of successful penetration events divided by the number of assessed attempted penetration events, multiplied by one hundred when expressed as a percentage. The numerator and denominator refer to comparable sites observed under the same scoring criteria. If 18 of 120 attempted sites in a wild type and 42 of 120 in a mutant are successful, penetration efficiencies are 15% and 35%, respectively. The absolute difference is 20 percentage points. The relative ratio is 35 divided by 15, or approximately 2.33. This is a measure of entry in that assay, not a direct measure of final yield or the rate of growth of each successful infection.

For a bacterial population, a change from 10,000 to 1,000,000 colony-forming units per gram represents a 100-fold increase, equivalent to two base-ten logarithmic units. Colony-forming units estimate viable organisms capable of producing colonies under the chosen conditions; a cluster of bacteria may form a single colony. Normalizing to tissue mass can be useful, but differing water contents or tissue degradation can affect the denominator. The result must not be equated automatically with the number of individual living bacteria.

Disease incidence and severity answer different questions. Incidence is the proportion of examined units that meet a defined disease criterion. Severity quantifies the amount of disease in an affected or sampled unit, such as the fraction of leaf area showing lesions. If 12 of 40 plants are symptomatic, incidence is 30%. This does not state how much tissue is damaged on those plants. A treatment could reduce severity substantially while leaving incidence unchanged if it restricts the size of lesions rather than preventing their initial formation.

To examine tolerance, record plant performance at several measured pathogen burdens. Consider a hypothetical experiment where two genotypes have equal uninfected biomass and comparable microbial loads after challenge, but one loses much less biomass. This is consistent with improved tolerance. If the genotypes differ in their initial biomass, compare proportional or absolute losses with a clearly justified metric. If they differ in pathogen load, the data cannot isolate tolerance without considering the relationship between performance and burden across treatments.

The experimental unit is also important. Counting 100 infection sites on one leaf supplies information about that leaf but does not necessarily provide 100 independently grown plant replicates. Shared developmental conditions and host physiology can make observations within a leaf correlated. Biological replication across independently grown plants, a suitable control and an appropriate method of summarizing uncertainty strengthen the analysis. In exam questions, be cautious about drawing a genotype-wide conclusion from a single plant or from a single final time point.

The central result of this chapter is that plant defense must be studied as a sequence of interacting barriers. Resistance can arise because an organism never enters, because growth is restricted after entry, because movement is blocked, or because an induced mechanism changes the local environment. Tolerance can preserve plant function without necessarily reducing infection. Establishing which explanation is correct requires matching the observations to the relevant stage. The next chapter develops how surface receptors recognize biological patterns and activate many of these local defenses.
