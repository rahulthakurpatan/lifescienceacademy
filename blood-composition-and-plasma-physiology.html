1. Blood as a specialised connective tissue

Consider a skeletal muscle fibre that has just increased its rate of contraction. It requires more oxygen and metabolic fuel than it did a moment earlier. Its products include carbon dioxide, heat and metabolites that alter the local chemical environment. Although the cell membrane controls exchanges with its immediate surroundings, the surrounding fluid is not an inexhaustible source of substrates. Unless substances are continually supplied and removed, differences in chemical potential that drive exchange will progressively disappear. A large multicellular animal solves this problem by combining local diffusion with long-distance movement of a circulating fluid. Blood is the material that moves; the cardiovascular system supplies the vessels and pressure gradients that make its movement possible.

The movement of a solute with the bulk flow of fluid is called convection or advection. It differs from diffusion, in which random thermal motion produces a net movement down a concentration or chemical potential gradient. Diffusion is indispensable across very short distances, such as the thickness of a capillary wall or the distance from interstitial fluid to a neighbouring cell. But diffusion alone becomes inefficient over long distances because its characteristic time increases approximately with the square of distance. Blood continually renews the composition of fluid near exchange surfaces and links organs that would otherwise have little direct chemical communication. An oxygen molecule can be transported for a considerable distance inside a red cell before diffusing into tissue; a glucose molecule can travel dissolved in the fluid surrounding the red cell before entering a muscle cell.

Blood is classified as connective tissue because it has differentiated cellular components embedded in an extracellular matrix. Its unusual feature is that the matrix is fluid rather than organised into a persistent solid arrangement of collagen fibres and ground substance. Plasma is that liquid extracellular component. The cellular and cell-derived structures suspended in it are called formed elements. This organisation makes it possible to carry relatively large numbers of specialised particles through a continuous liquid phase, while permitting rapid exchange of smaller dissolved molecules. The tissue is thus neither simply a liquid organ nor simply a population of freely moving cells. Its physiological properties emerge from the interactions between the two phases.

Blood performs three broad categories of work: transport, regulation and protection. Transport includes respiratory gases, absorbed nutrients, metabolic waste products and chemical signals. Regulation involves the distribution of heat, buffering of acid–base changes and exchange of water and dissolved electrolytes with other fluid compartments. Protection includes immune recognition and removal of harmful material, and the limitation of bleeding after vascular injury. These roles overlap. For example, albumin is both a carrier for hydrophobic compounds and a determinant of water distribution, and leukocytes travel through blood before exerting many of their protective functions in other tissues. Classifying an element under one function does not mean that it lacks every other function.

The internal environment of an animal is not identical to its blood. Most cells are separated from circulating blood by an endothelial barrier and an interstitial space. Blood plasma and interstitial fluid belong to the extracellular fluid compartment, while the fluid inside cells constitutes the intracellular compartment. In an adult vertebrate, these compartments exchange materials continually. A blood sample therefore measures the composition of one compartment, not directly the composition of every tissue. Plasma sodium can be nearly constant while intracellular potassium is maintained at a very different concentration. Similarly, a normal plasma oxygen tension does not prove that oxygen delivery to every tissue is adequate, because circulation and tissue demand are also important.

The concept of a relatively stable internal environment became central to nineteenth-century physiology through the work of Claude Bernard. Walter Cannon later developed the idea of homeostasis, emphasising regulated stability rather than rigid constancy. The history matters because it changes how we interpret a blood test: stability at two sampling times is evidence of a net balance, not evidence that no exchange took place between them. The plasma protein pool is constantly subject to synthesis, exchange and degradation. Cells are continuously generated and removed. Water crosses vascular and cell boundaries. A stable concentration can therefore conceal large opposing rates.

At the level of a single component, the amount present in a compartment changes according to inputs minus outputs. If the concentration of a substance is C and the relevant compartment has volume V, its total amount is CV under the assumption of uniform mixing. A concentration can change when the amount changes, when the volume changes, or when both change. This elementary distinction runs throughout animal physiology. A sample of concentrated blood following water loss does not necessarily contain more erythrocytes in the animal as a whole. An apparently low concentration of plasma proteins after rapid fluid expansion need not indicate a sudden failure of protein synthesis.

The same reasoning applies to regulatory failures. Blood is useful only when its components can circulate and exchange material with the rest of the organism. Increasing the number of red cells indefinitely would increase oxygen-binding capacity per unit volume but would also alter resistance to flow. Increasing plasma protein concentration indefinitely would change not only ligand binding but also fluid distribution and viscosity. An efficient circulating tissue must balance several physical and biochemical requirements simultaneously. The following sections examine these requirements first through the visible components of blood and then through the properties of its liquid matrix.

2. Separating whole blood into its components

A drop of fresh blood appears uniformly red to the unaided eye. Microscopy reveals that the colour is largely associated with numerous erythrocytes, whereas the surrounding liquid is pale. This difference provides the first evidence that blood contains physically separable components. If clotting is prevented, a suitable centrifugation step concentrates the denser particles at the bottom of a tube. Most of the volume at the bottom is packed red cells. A thin intermediate layer, called the buffy coat, is enriched in leukocytes and platelets. The upper liquid is plasma. The exact separation depends on the centrifugal field, duration, sample condition and the properties of the particles.

A centrifuge does not identify cells by biological function. It generates sedimentation according to density differences and the hydrodynamic behaviour of particles. Erythrocytes are sufficiently dense and abundant that they form a substantial packed layer. Leukocytes and platelets form a much smaller visible fraction because they occupy relatively little of the normal total blood volume. The resulting arrangement is an operational separation, not three perfectly pure chemical compartments. Some plasma remains trapped between packed cells; different centrifugation conditions can leave different numbers of platelets in the upper fluid. Consequently, a preparation described as plasma must still be interpreted in light of how it was obtained.

In a representative adult human sample, an erythrocyte layer occupying roughly forty to forty-five percent of the total volume would be unremarkable, and plasma would occupy approximately the complementary fraction after allowance for the small buffy coat. These percentages are illustrative rather than fixed physiological constants. Biological sex, altitude, age, hydration and other physiological variables influence the proportion occupied by erythrocytes. A typical buffy coat may constitute less than one percent of the sample volume despite the indispensable functions of the cells and fragments within it. A component's volume fraction is a physical measurement; it cannot by itself indicate its functional importance.

The red cell fraction is called the haematocrit or packed cell volume. It may be expressed as a fraction such as 0.42 or as a percentage such as 42 percent. If a packed layer is 21 millimetres long in a uniformly narrow tube and the total sample column is 50 millimetres long, the volume fraction is 21 divided by 50, or 0.42. The ratio of lengths equals the ratio of volumes only because the cross-sectional area is constant. In a vessel with changing diameter, lengths cannot be compared without accounting for the geometry. The measurement is straightforward in principle, but the assumptions must remain visible.

A laboratory may instead estimate haematocrit using the measured red cell concentration and mean cell volume. Suppose each microlitre contains five million erythrocytes and each erythrocyte has a mean volume of 90 femtolitres. Their combined volume is 450 million femtolitres per microlitre. Since one microlitre contains one billion femtolitres, the predicted fraction is 0.45, or 45 percent. This calculation shows why haematocrit is neither a direct measurement of the number of cells nor an assay of the amount of haemoglobin. Both cell size and cell concentration contribute to its value.

The sedimentation of erythrocytes in a stationary tube is a different measurement. In an erythrocyte sedimentation test the rate or extent of settling under specified conditions depends partly on aggregation, plasma proteins and cell properties. A high sedimentation rate does not directly mean a high haematocrit. Centrifugal packing deliberately accelerates separation and measures a final fraction; sedimentation under gravity assesses a kinetic behaviour. A question that uses the word sedimentation without stating the measurement should therefore be read carefully.

Centrifugation also illustrates the difference between a sample and the entire circulation. A peripheral venous sample is usually treated as representative for many elementary calculations, but erythrocyte distribution is not perfectly uniform throughout every small vessel. Changes in vessel diameter and the radial migration of cells produce variations in local cell fraction. Moreover, a sample collected under conditions that alter local fluid exchange may differ from one collected under other conditions. The measured haematocrit is thus useful without being an exact inventory of the entire body's red cell volume.

To interpret the upper layer, consider what has been removed. Centrifugation separates intact particles from fluid but leaves ions, soluble proteins, sugars and other small dissolved substances in plasma. Because most plasma proteins are soluble, the pale colour of the upper layer does not mean that it is dilute water in a functional sense. The upper phase carries a large protein pool and a precisely controlled ionic composition. Its role in maintaining the circulation is not a passive consequence of occupying the remaining space after the cells have settled.

3. Formed elements and their division of labour

Erythrocytes are the most numerous formed elements in mammalian blood. Their principal function is to carry oxygen reversibly bound to haemoglobin, with additional roles in carbon dioxide carriage and acid–base buffering. The mature human erythrocyte has a flexible biconcave shape. It lacks a nucleus and conventional mitochondria, reducing the volume occupied by organelles and preventing it from consuming oxygen through mitochondrial respiration. It retains the enzymes required to obtain metabolic energy and preserve its chemical integrity. These characteristics reflect specialised differentiation, not a general property of all animal erythrocytes.

The biconcave geometry is often described as increasing surface area relative to volume. More precisely, a disc-like red cell has excess surface area compared with a sphere enclosing the same volume. The available membrane area supports deformation when the cell passes through narrow vascular spaces. The membrane and its underlying cytoskeleton must remain mechanically intact during these changes. If the cell lost membrane area without a proportional loss of volume, it would become more spherical and have less geometric freedom to deform. Shape, membrane mechanics and metabolic maintenance are therefore linked to the suitability of erythrocytes for circulation. Their detailed molecular organisation belongs to the later haemoglobin chapter.

Leukocytes are fewer in number but contain nuclei and can perform active cellular responses. The granulocytes include neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils, while lymphocytes and monocytes are conventionally classified as agranulocytes on the basis of their appearance in routine stained preparations. The term agranulocyte does not mean that these cells lack every intracellular vesicle or granule. Neutrophils contribute to rapid innate defence, eosinophils participate in selected responses including defence against some multicellular parasites, basophils release inflammatory mediators, monocytes can enter tissues and differentiate, and lymphocytes participate in diverse innate or adaptive immune activities depending on their class.

A leukocyte count in circulating blood reflects production, redistribution and loss. Certain leukocytes move readily between blood and tissues in response to molecular signals. A reduced number in blood could be caused by decreased production, accelerated destruction or a change in distribution; a single count does not distinguish these possibilities. The functional importance of circulating leukocytes is therefore not reducible to their fraction of blood volume. A small population can exert large effects through targeted recognition, signalling and release of soluble mediators. The chapter on blood-mediated immunity develops this point in more detail.

Platelets are small anucleate cytoplasmic fragments derived from megakaryocytes in mammals. Despite their origin as fragments rather than complete nucleated cells, they possess receptors, granules, a cytoskeleton and metabolic capacity. They respond to vascular damage by changing shape, interacting with adhesive molecules and recruiting additional platelets. Their contribution to primary haemostasis occurs in cooperation with the vessel wall and circulating plasma proteins. Platelet concentration and platelet function are different properties: a sufficient number of fragments can coexist with defective adhesion or aggregation. Conversely, a low concentration does not explain which stage of haemostasis is impaired.

These three categories are conventionally called formed elements because all can be identified as discrete structures in blood. Counting them and measuring the volume they occupy are distinct operations. An adult sample might contain millions of erythrocytes, thousands of leukocytes and hundreds of thousands of platelets per microlitre. The comparatively large platelet count relative to leukocytes does not imply that platelets occupy more volume than erythrocytes, since each platelet is much smaller. In general, the volume contributed by a class of particles equals its number multiplied by its mean volume, provided individual volumes are additive and measured under comparable conditions.

The distinction between number and mass also explains how a cell population can change without changing a simple bulk index. Consider a blood sample in which the red cell count decreases but the mean red cell volume increases proportionately. Haematocrit may remain almost unchanged even though the sample contains fewer erythrocytes. A student cannot infer that erythrocyte number is normal merely because the packed volume is near a representative value. Similarly, equal haematocrits in two species do not demonstrate equal oxygen transport capacity, because the amount and properties of haemoglobin, cell structure and cardiovascular flow may differ.

The formed elements depend on the liquid phase for nutrient delivery, pH regulation and the removal of metabolic products. Plasma in turn depends on the presence of formed elements for functions that cannot be achieved efficiently by dissolved solutes alone. Encapsulation of haemoglobin inside erythrocytes permits a high concentration of oxygen-binding protein without requiring it to be freely dissolved throughout plasma. Platelet surfaces help localise haemostatic reactions to regions of injury. Leukocytes carry specialised recognition and response machinery through a common fluid. Blood composition is thus an example of a tissue in which distinct material phases cooperate to solve different biological problems.

4. Plasma water, ions and extracellular composition

Plasma consists predominantly of water. Typical human plasma contains roughly ninety to ninety-two percent water by mass, with proteins and other dissolved substances accounting for much of the remainder. Approximate composition depends on the way concentrations and fractions are expressed and the physiological state of the individual. Water is the solvent in which many ions and small hydrophilic molecules move. It also acts as a thermal reservoir because of its relatively high specific heat capacity. But the abundance of water should not be confused with a lack of complexity: the behaviour of plasma is determined by the molecules dissolved or dispersed within it.

Water is polar, allowing interactions with charged ions and polar groups on biological molecules. A freely hydrated ion can be transported with the liquid phase and can diffuse relative to that phase. Many neutral hydrophobic molecules, by contrast, have low solubility in the aqueous environment and require association with carrier proteins or lipoprotein particles for efficient distribution. A plasma constituent's total concentration may therefore include physically different forms: freely dissolved, reversibly bound, or associated with a larger particle. The distinction becomes important when assessing its chemical activity or ability to cross a biological barrier.

Sodium is the major extracellular cation in typical vertebrate plasma, while chloride and bicarbonate are major anions. Potassium concentration is much lower in extracellular fluid than in the cytosol of many cells. This asymmetry is sustained by selective permeability and active transport, including the sodium–potassium ATPase of cell membranes. It is not produced simply by differences in the electrical charge of whole compartments. Both extracellular and intracellular solutions are approximately electrically neutral in bulk; a small separation of charge near membranes can generate a substantial membrane potential without requiring an appreciable departure from bulk electroneutrality.

Representative human plasma sodium concentrations are approximately 135–145 millimoles per litre. Potassium is commonly around 3.5–5 millimoles per litre, and chloride around 98–106 millimoles per litre, though reference intervals differ among laboratories and populations. Bicarbonate is commonly around 22–26 millimoles per litre. These values illustrate the relative abundance of extracellular ions rather than universal diagnostic boundaries. Calcium and magnesium occur at lower concentrations and participate in signalling, enzymatic activity, membrane stability and other functions. For calcium in particular, total concentration includes protein-bound and complexed forms as well as free ionised calcium.

An ion's concentration is not identical to its activity. Chemical interactions depend on effective activity, which reflects nonideal behaviour and interactions with other solutes. Introductory physiology often uses concentrations as reasonable approximations, but this assumption should be remembered when deriving equilibrium equations. A plasma sample containing abundant negatively charged proteins can have slight differences in its freely diffusible ion distribution compared with a protein-poor solution. Bulk electroneutrality is maintained by accompanying counterions; the system cannot simply accumulate an unlimited net electrical charge because some proteins are negatively charged.

At the level of cell volume, the plasma water and solutes constitute the surrounding medium for erythrocytes. The cell membrane allows rapid water movement but differs in its permeability to various solutes. If extracellular effective osmotic concentration changes abruptly, water redistributes and the volume of a cell can change before its solute content has appreciably adjusted. This is why plasma composition matters mechanically as well as chemically. A red cell that swells substantially or shrinks substantially has altered shape and circulation behaviour. A solution's ability to maintain cell volume is therefore a physiological property, not merely a statement about the quantity of dissolved material.

The kidneys, respiratory system, digestive tract and endocrine system contribute to maintaining plasma composition over time. The fluid is continually receiving absorbed substances, exchanging materials with tissues and delivering waste to excretory organs. Plasma does not independently regulate every ion concentration. Rather, its composition is a regulated outcome of transport across membranes, organ-level balances and changes in water content. To explain an abnormal plasma concentration, it is necessary to ask whether net solute balance, water balance, redistribution between compartments or measurement conditions have changed.

5. Nutrients, wastes and chemical signals

Glucose provides a clear example of a small polar nutrient transported in plasma. After absorption from the intestine, glucose enters the circulation and can be distributed to metabolically active tissues. Entry into a cell requires appropriate membrane transport pathways; glucose in plasma is not automatically equivalent to glucose available inside every cell. Hormonal regulation can change both the rate at which glucose is delivered into blood and the rate at which tissues take it up. During fasting, endogenous production helps support circulating glucose even when absorption from the digestive tract has ceased. A measured concentration is thus a result of simultaneous appearance and disappearance.

Amino acids are also distributed through plasma. They can contribute to protein synthesis or enter metabolic pathways after cellular uptake. Different amino acids have different concentrations, tissue transporters and metabolic fates. Plasma is not an inert storage tank containing a constant mixture of nutrients in fixed proportions. Its amino acid profile can change in relation to diet, tissue protein metabolism and organ-specific uptake or release. A nutrient that enters the circulation can also be temporarily incorporated into a larger molecule and reappear later in a different form. The circulation connects these processes without itself performing all the associated metabolic reactions.

The transport of lipids is more complicated because many lipids are poorly soluble in water. Fatty acids released into blood from stores may associate reversibly with albumin. Triacylglycerols and cholesterol are transported in lipoprotein particles whose surface composition makes them compatible with an aqueous environment. A lipoprotein is not simply a soluble protein carrying a single molecule; it is a molecular assembly containing proteins and lipids. These different transport strategies allow hydrophobic materials to circulate without forming uncontrolled bulk oily phases. Detailed lipoprotein metabolism is beyond the immediate scope, but the principle of organising hydrophobic substances in an aqueous medium is fundamental to plasma function.

Metabolic waste products illustrate why transport does not end with delivery of nutrients. Carbon dioxide produced by metabolism is carried in several forms, including dissolved gas and bicarbonate, with some bound to proteins. Nitrogenous waste arising from amino acid metabolism can be converted into urea in the liver and carried to the kidneys for excretion. Creatinine and other metabolic products are also present in plasma. Their concentrations reflect production, distribution and clearance. A high concentration of a waste product can therefore result from increased production or decreased removal, among other possibilities. It is a category error to equate concentration directly with production rate.

Hormones use the circulation as one route of long-distance communication. Some peptide hormones are water soluble and circulate substantially in the free form. Many steroid and thyroid hormones circulate with carrier proteins, creating a dynamic equilibrium between bound and unbound molecules. The bound fraction can act as a reservoir and can change the effective lifetime of a hormone in circulation. Target-cell responses depend on receptor availability and accessible hormone, not merely total measured hormone. The equilibrium can shift when carrier concentration changes, even when secretion is not immediately altered.

Plasma also contains enzymes and enzyme inhibitors, antioxidants, vitamins and diverse signalling molecules. Some proteins act directly in circulating blood, while others are detectable because they have entered the circulation from tissue. For example, an intracellular enzyme detected at an increased level in plasma may reflect release from cells rather than an adaptive increase in secretion for a plasma-specific purpose. The presence of a molecule in blood is not sufficient evidence that its normal function is performed there. To interpret a measured component, establish its source, usual compartment, target and route of removal.

The exchange of dissolved materials depends on blood flow as well as concentration. If a tissue receives a volume flow Q and the arterial concentration of a substance is Ca while its venous concentration is Cv, the net uptake from blood is Q(Ca − Cv), provided the units and compartments are consistent and accumulation within the vascular region is negligible. This elementary mass-balance relation is distinct from the claim that the tissue consumes all delivered material. If Q is 0.5 litres per minute and the arterial-to-venous concentration difference is 2 millimoles per litre, net uptake is 1 millimole per minute. A doubling of blood flow without a change in extraction difference doubles calculated net uptake; in living systems, the extraction difference may itself adjust as flow changes.

Care is needed when plasma concentrations are used in such a calculation. A substance confined to plasma is transported at the plasma volume flow rather than the full whole-blood volume flow. If haematocrit is 0.40 and the whole-blood flow is 0.50 litres per minute, the approximate plasma flow is 0.30 litres per minute, ignoring the very small buffy coat fraction. A two millimole per litre plasma concentration difference would correspond to a net plasma-carried uptake of 0.60 millimoles per minute under these assumptions. The choice of concentration denominator therefore changes the appropriate flow denominator. This provides a direct link between blood composition and quantitative physiology.

6. Plasma proteins: synthesis, classification and measurement

Plasma proteins represent a specialised extracellular population of macromolecules. Unlike the major inorganic ions, which are present at concentrations measured in tens or hundreds of millimoles per litre, the individual proteins generally have relatively small molar concentrations but substantial mass concentrations. Their molecular size, electrical charge, shape and binding properties determine how they interact with water, solutes, cells and vascular barriers. A molecule of albumin and a sodium ion both contribute to the osmotic properties of a solution, but they differ greatly in molecular mass and in the ease with which they cross different biological boundaries. These differences explain why the contribution of plasma proteins to fluid distribution cannot be inferred simply from their share of total plasma osmolality.

In a healthy adult human, total plasma protein is commonly of the order of six to eight grams per decilitre. A decilitre is one tenth of a litre, so this corresponds to roughly sixty to eighty grams per litre. Concentrations depend on the specimen type and measurement method. Total serum protein is usually slightly lower than total plasma protein because serum lacks most fibrinogen and some other constituents consumed or incorporated during clotting. The numerical overlap between typical reference intervals does not erase the biological difference between the specimens. Comparing total protein in two samples requires first establishing that the measurements refer to equivalent sample types.

Albumin, globulins and fibrinogen are convenient broad categories of plasma proteins. Albumin is usually the largest single component by mass. Globulins are a heterogeneous collection rather than a single molecular species, and fibrinogen is a soluble precursor of the structural protein fibrin. The traditional categories arose from observations of solubility and electrophoretic behaviour, not from the assignment of one universal function to each group. Plasma contains many other proteins with specialised roles, including complement components, protease inhibitors, binding proteins and enzymes. Their concentration may be low in comparison with albumin while their activity is physiologically significant.

The liver synthesises albumin, fibrinogen and many other circulating proteins. Antibody-secreting cells of the B lymphocyte lineage, especially plasma cells, synthesise immunoglobulins. The similarly named words plasma and plasma cell refer to different entities: one is the extracellular liquid of blood and the other is a differentiated antibody-secreting cell. The fact that a protein circulates in plasma does not establish that the liver synthesises it. A simple model that assigns all circulating proteins to the liver would fail to explain how specific immune responses generate new antibody populations.

Protein concentration reflects synthesis, degradation, distribution and loss. Albumin can move out of the vascular compartment in limited amounts and return through lymphatic pathways. Proteins may be broken down, and abnormal barrier function can allow excessive loss. In a regulated steady state, input approximately balances output for each relevant pool. A decreased plasma albumin concentration can result from reduced synthesis, increased degradation, increased loss, redistribution into extravascular compartments or dilution. Identifying a low concentration alone is not sufficient to determine the responsible mechanism.

A protein concentration expressed in grams per litre is not a count of molecules per litre. The conversion requires the molecular mass. For albumin, an approximate molecular mass of 66,500 grams per mole means that forty grams per litre corresponds to forty divided by 66,500 moles per litre, or about 0.60 millimoles per litre. For an immunoglobulin of approximately 150,000 grams per mole, the same mass concentration would represent only about 0.27 millimoles per litre. At equal mass concentrations, the lower molecular mass protein provides more individual molecules. This is one reason why mass-based protein fractions cannot be directly substituted for molar fractions in calculations.

The qualitative protein profile can be investigated using electrophoresis. A sample placed in an electric field contains charged molecules whose migration is influenced by the medium, molecular properties and the pH. Under common conditions used for serum protein electrophoresis, proteins separate into albumin and alpha, beta and gamma regions. The resulting pattern displays relative contributions to measured signal rather than identifying every individual molecule. An intense region may contain several distinct proteins. Mobility alone cannot determine a protein's exact biological identity; an appropriate immunochemical method or another independent identification method may be necessary.

When interpreting electrophoretic results, distinguish the height of a peak from the integrated area under the peak. A tall narrow peak and a lower broad peak may have comparable integrated areas. If the measured fraction is 60 percent albumin and total serum protein is 70 grams per litre, the estimated albumin concentration is 42 grams per litre, assuming the fraction and total protein measurements are compatible. If the albumin percentage decreases to 50 percent while total protein rises to 84 grams per litre, the estimated albumin concentration remains 42 grams per litre. A percentage change does not establish a corresponding absolute concentration change.

Globulin concentration is sometimes estimated as total protein minus albumin rather than measured by directly identifying each globulin. An albumin-to-globulin ratio therefore combines two quantities, and the ratio can change through movement of either numerator or denominator. If serum albumin is 42 grams per litre and total serum protein is 70 grams per litre, calculated globulin is 28 grams per litre and the ratio is 1.5. If albumin remains 42 but globulins increase to 42 grams per litre, the ratio falls to 1 despite unchanged albumin. A low ratio is not automatically evidence of reduced albumin synthesis.

One experimental approach to the role of proteins is to compare solutions with the same salt concentrations but different protein concentrations across a barrier that permits salts to equilibrate while substantially retaining proteins. Under those conditions, differences in water movement reveal a contribution of the retained macromolecules. Another approach compares the transport of a hydrophobic ligand in the presence and absence of its carrier. These experiments probe different functions of the same molecular class. A student should not treat every change caused by albumin as necessarily an osmotic effect; the experimental design must isolate the relevant property.

7. Albumin as an osmotic and molecular transport protein

Albumin is a soluble protein produced mainly by hepatocytes. Its high circulating concentration gives it a major role in maintaining the colloid osmotic pressure of normal mammalian plasma. Its relatively small molecular mass among abundant plasma proteins increases the number of molecules contributed per unit mass. At physiological pH, albumin has a substantial net negative charge, which influences its interactions with ions and surrounding water. These properties also contribute to the nonideal osmotic behaviour of concentrated protein solutions.

Albumin is retained more effectively than small inorganic ions by many vascular endothelial barriers. Retention is not absolute, and permeability varies between vascular beds. Because albumin can remain unequally distributed between plasma and the surrounding interstitial compartments, it can contribute to a sustained osmotic pressure difference across a selectively permeable interface. Albumin is not a mechanical pump that actively draws water into the circulation. Its contribution arises from equilibrium and transport physics: water movement depends on the balance between hydrostatic pressure and effective osmotic pressure differences. Where albumin is able to cross an interface freely, its concentration difference will not have the same persistent osmotic effect.

At the same time, albumin is a versatile molecular carrier. Its folded structure contains binding regions capable of associating reversibly with various fatty acids, bilirubin, hormones and other compounds. Noncovalent interactions permit association and dissociation. Binding is therefore a dynamic equilibrium rather than a permanent attachment. A molecule transported with albumin can be released when it encounters a different chemical environment or when the free ligand concentration is reduced by cellular uptake. The carrier makes transport through the aqueous plasma more favourable without requiring the bound molecule to become permanently part of albumin.

Consider a ligand L and a carrier protein P that form a reversible complex PL. For a single independent binding site, the equilibrium dissociation constant Kd can be expressed as the product of free protein concentration and free ligand concentration divided by complex concentration. A smaller Kd represents stronger equilibrium binding under specified conditions. If the protein has many unoccupied sites, a large share of ligand can be bound despite a very low free concentration. If available sites become occupied, the proportion of ligand remaining free may increase markedly as total ligand concentration rises. The distinction between free and total concentration is therefore central to interpreting carrier-mediated transport.

The fractional occupancy of a simple independent binding site is [L] divided by Kd plus [L], where [L] is the free ligand concentration. If Kd is 2 micromoles per litre and free ligand concentration is 2 micromoles per litre, half of the sites are occupied. At a free concentration of 8 micromoles per litre, occupancy becomes 8 divided by 10, or 0.8. This calculation does not tell us the fraction of total ligand bound unless the amount of carrier is also specified. Occupancy refers to protein sites, whereas bound fraction refers to ligand molecules. These are two different denominators, and confusing them is a frequent source of mistakes.

A complementary thought experiment holds total ligand constant while changing the concentration of available albumin binding sites. Under otherwise identical equilibrium conditions, a reduction in available sites generally increases the free ligand fraction. This does not necessarily mean that free ligand remains elevated indefinitely in a living organism. Changes in uptake, metabolism, distribution and secretion can subsequently alter the total amount of ligand. The immediate physicochemical prediction must be distinguished from the eventual regulated physiological state. A useful examination question may explicitly specify whether a sample is in a closed test tube or part of an intact animal.

Albumin also contributes to the binding of calcium. Total plasma calcium includes free ionised calcium, protein-bound calcium and calcium complexed with small anions. Ionised calcium participates directly in many physiological processes, including cell signalling and excitation–contraction relationships. In a simplified situation, reduced albumin binding capacity can lower total measured calcium without an equivalent fall in the free ionised fraction. The exact relationship depends on pH and other factors, so total calcium is not interchangeable with ionised calcium. This is an example of a general principle: measuring a substance after releasing it from all binding sites answers a different question from measuring its free activity.

The pH dependence of binding also illustrates that plasma properties are interdependent. Changes in protonation alter the charge of binding groups on proteins. A change in extracellular pH can therefore affect the availability of particular charged ligands even before the total concentration of the relevant chemical element has changed. This is one reason why a single total concentration may have limited physiological meaning without context. The biochemical equilibrium links the protein's physical state to the composition of the surrounding solution.

A reduction in circulating albumin can affect both fluid distribution and molecular transport. In an experimental model with an otherwise unchanged capillary barrier and hydrostatic pressure, lower plasma albumin tends to reduce the opposing colloid osmotic term and favour greater filtration. In a separate binding experiment, reduced albumin may change free ligand concentrations. These are parallel consequences of the same change in protein abundance, but they arise through distinct mechanisms. One must not explain the altered free ligand concentration merely by appealing to water movement, nor explain the osmotic effect merely through transport of a hydrophobic ligand.

The molecular properties that make albumin effective have limits. It is not impermeable across all endothelia, does not bind all ligands with equal affinity, and is not the only contributor to colloid osmotic pressure. Globulins and other macromolecules also contribute. Albumin concentration alone cannot establish the exact oncotic pressure of a sample because protein composition, ionic effects and nonideal interactions matter. A comparison of two plasma samples is most informative when albumin, total protein and other determinants are considered together.

8. Globulins, fibrinogen and other functional proteins

The globulin fraction includes many molecules with different biological origins and functions. Alpha and beta globulin regions in electrophoresis contain various transport proteins, inhibitors and components of immune defence. The gamma region is particularly enriched in immunoglobulins, although antibody migration is not absolutely confined to that region. A named electrophoretic fraction should not be treated as a single purified protein. Different proteins may have similar mobility under a given set of conditions and therefore contribute to the same broad region.

Transferrin is an example of a specialised carrier. Iron is required for haem synthesis and other cellular processes, but poorly controlled iron chemistry can promote harmful oxidative reactions. Transferrin binds ferric iron in plasma and contributes to its regulated distribution. Cells that express the appropriate uptake machinery can obtain iron from transferrin. The amount of iron in plasma and the amount of available iron-binding capacity are therefore distinct quantities. Transferrin saturation can be expressed as the proportion of measured binding capacity occupied by iron, with the assumption that compatible measures of iron and capacity are used. A change in saturation may reflect a change in iron concentration, carrier concentration or both.

Ceruloplasmin is another example of a protein associated with metal transport and metabolism. It contains copper and possesses ferroxidase activity, which is relevant to iron handling. These proteins show why the transport function of plasma does not consist solely of simple dissolution. Binding can limit undesirable reactions, coordinate delivery and influence the distribution of scarce or chemically reactive materials. The exact molecular mechanisms of metal metabolism are not needed here, but their physiological purpose explains why protein carriers occur in addition to the abundant nonspecific carrier albumin.

Immunoglobulins have a different kind of binding specificity. Their variable regions recognise particular molecular features of antigens, while other regions participate in biological effector mechanisms. Plasma can contain antibodies generated in response to earlier encounters or immunisation, as well as natural antibody populations. They provide one means by which circulating fluid can recognise foreign material. However, antibodies do not independently replace all the functions of leukocytes. Antibody-mediated recognition, cellular removal, inflammatory signals and complement activity can operate together. The distinction between soluble and cellular immunity is useful without implying that the two systems are independent.

Complement components circulate largely as regulated proteins, many of which are inactive precursors or require activation within an organised reaction sequence. Their products can contribute to opsonisation, inflammation and membrane damage to susceptible targets. Regulation helps restrict potentially harmful reactions. A protein that has not yet been activated may be abundant in plasma without exerting the same biological activity as its activated product. The presence of a precursor, the activity of a reaction pathway and the concentration of a final product must be distinguished when interpreting a functional assay.

Fibrinogen is a soluble plasma glycoprotein synthesised predominantly by the liver. During coagulation, thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptides from fibrinogen and enables fibrin monomers to assemble into a polymer network. Further stabilisation by factor XIII-dependent crosslinking contributes to clot integrity. The biochemical conversion provides an elegant solution to a physical problem: a circulating fluid must remain sufficiently mobile during ordinary flow, yet must be able to form an insoluble structural network at a site of injury. Keeping the precursor soluble and controlling its conversion allows structural material to be produced where and when it is required.

Fibrinogen is not itself equivalent to fibrin. The precursor circulates in soluble form; fibrin is a polymeric product of its conversion. Nor is a platelet plug identical to a fibrin network. Platelet interactions form an initial cellular structure, while coagulation reinforces haemostasis through protein reactions. Both processes interact, but they can be experimentally distinguished. A plasma sample containing fibrinogen but lacking adequate activation of the coagulation pathway will not necessarily produce a normal fibrin clot. A functional assay therefore reports more than the concentration of one precursor.

Fibrinogen influences blood rheology as well as haemostasis. Plasma proteins, especially fibrinogen, can promote reversible erythrocyte aggregation under suitable conditions. Aggregated red cells may form stacks called rouleaux and larger structures. Their hydrodynamic behaviour differs from that of dispersed individual cells. At low shear, aggregates can persist and contribute to a higher apparent viscosity; increased shear can disrupt them. This is why changing plasma protein composition may affect the flow behaviour of whole blood even when haematocrit is held constant.

Several circulating proteins change in abundance during inflammatory responses. Such changes are part of the acute-phase response and reflect alterations in synthesis and other aspects of protein balance. Fibrinogen and C-reactive protein are examples of proteins that may increase, whereas albumin is commonly described as a negative acute-phase protein. A fall in albumin and rise in fibrinogen need not be contradictory: the liver can regulate synthesis of different proteins in different directions. Equally, a change in blood protein concentrations during inflammation does not demonstrate that every altered protein directly recognises a pathogen.

The idea of functional diversity is essential when comparing two samples with equal total protein concentration. One may have a relatively high albumin fraction, whereas another contains more globulins. Their carrier capacity, immune properties, osmotic behaviour and tendency to promote cell aggregation may differ. Total protein is a useful bulk measurement, but the pattern of individual components matters. The biological meaning of the measurement lies in which proteins have changed and how those changes alter the relevant mechanism.

9. Osmolality, osmolarity, tonicity and red cell volume

The chemical composition of plasma determines its capacity to influence water movement across selectively permeable membranes. A useful starting point is the distinction between molarity and osmotic concentration. Molarity is the number of moles of a specified substance per litre of solution. Osmolarity is the total concentration of osmotically active particles per litre of solution, while osmolality expresses particles per kilogram of solvent. A molecule that dissociates into several ions can contribute more than one osmotic particle per formula unit. An ideal sodium chloride solution therefore has approximately two particles for every sodium chloride formula unit, although real solutions exhibit interactions that make the effective number somewhat different.

Plasma osmolality is typically of the order of 275–295 milliosmoles per kilogram of water in humans. The principal contribution comes from small solutes, especially sodium and its accompanying anions. Glucose and urea contribute additional particles. Proteins make relatively little contribution to total plasma osmolality measured across a hypothetical membrane that retains all solutes, because their molar concentration is small. This does not contradict their importance to capillary fluid exchange. A red cell membrane and a vascular endothelial barrier have different permeability properties, and the relevant osmotic gradient depends on which solutes are effectively retained.

For a dilute ideal solution containing a solute that does not cross the membrane, osmotic pressure can be approximated by the van't Hoff relation π = iCRT. Here π is pressure, i is the ideal number of particles per solute formula unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. If concentrations are expressed in moles per litre, a compatible value of R is 0.08206 litre atmospheres per mole kelvin. The result is then in atmospheres. Alternatively, using C in moles per cubic metre and R in joules per mole kelvin produces pressure in pascals. The equation describes a limiting ideal solution; it does not directly account for permeability, ionic activities or macromolecular interactions.

An approximate total osmotic pressure of an ideal solution with an osmolarity of 0.290 osmoles per litre at 310 kelvin is 0.290 multiplied by 0.08206 multiplied by 310, which is about 7.38 atmospheres. Converting using 760 millimetres of mercury per atmosphere gives approximately 5,610 millimetres of mercury. This is far larger than the typical net hydrostatic pressure differences in the microcirculation. If such a pressure were sustained across an endothelial wall, blood water would behave very differently from what is observed. The resolution is that most small solutes can exchange across many endothelial barriers, so their osmotic contributions on the two sides largely cancel. It is the difference in effective osmotic pressure, not total absolute osmotic pressure, that enters the water-flow problem.

Osmolality can be measured by a colligative property such as freezing point depression. In an ideal dilute aqueous solution, one osmole of dissolved particles per kilogram of water lowers the freezing point by approximately 1.86 degrees Celsius. A depression of approximately 0.54 degrees Celsius corresponds to about 0.29 osmoles per kilogram under this approximation. This method responds to the total concentration of particles rather than their identity. Two solutions with the same osmolality may contain different solutes and have very different effects on a biological membrane. The physical measurement provides one property of the sample, not a complete description of its interactions with cells.

A commonly used approximate calculated osmolality expression for human plasma is 2 times the sodium concentration plus glucose plus urea when all concentrations are expressed in millimoles per litre. The sodium term approximates the contribution of sodium and its associated anions. In an example with sodium 140, glucose 5 and urea 5 millimoles per litre, the estimated osmolality is 290 milliosmoles per kilogram, recognising that the expression is an empirical approximation using molar concentrations to estimate a mass-based quantity. If a question uses milligrams per decilitre, the appropriate conversion factors must be applied; glucose in milligrams per decilitre is divided by about 18 and blood urea nitrogen by about 2.8. Urea and blood urea nitrogen are not numerically interchangeable.

Tonicity refers to the capacity of a solution to cause a sustained change in the volume of a cell. It depends on effective nonpenetrating solutes across that particular cell membrane over the relevant time interval. A solution may be iso-osmotic with cell fluid but not isotonic if much of its osmotic content can penetrate the membrane. For example, an iso-osmotic solution of a readily penetrating solute can allow that solute to enter a cell, followed by water, changing cell volume. The cell membrane is a selective barrier, so the definition of tonicity must specify the biological system rather than the test tube alone.

Urea provides a useful conceptual contrast to extracellular sodium salts. Urea contributes to measured osmolality, but in many biological contexts it crosses cell membranes sufficiently readily that it exerts less sustained tonic effect than relatively impermeant extracellular solutes. The distinction is not absolute for every tissue or time scale, since permeability and transporter expression vary. A rapidly imposed urea gradient can cause transient water movements before urea equilibrates. Thus a question that asks about the immediate response may have a different answer from one asking about the final equilibrium.

For a simplified red cell containing a constant amount of impermeant intracellular solute, osmotic equilibrium requires its effective intracellular osmolarity to equal the effective extracellular osmolarity, neglecting hydrostatic pressure differences and nonideal effects. If the effective intracellular osmolarity is initially 300 milliosmoles per litre at volume V0, and the extracellular solution is changed to 375 milliosmoles per litre with solute retention, the new osmotically active volume is approximately 300 divided by 375 times V0, or 0.8 V0. This idealised prediction assumes constant intracellular osmole number and no inactive cell volume. Real erythrocytes contain substantial macromolecular material and have geometric limits, so the total cell volume need not change exactly in that proportion.

In hypotonic extracellular fluid, water enters erythrocytes and their volume increases. Initially, the excess membrane area of the biconcave cell allows shape change toward a sphere. Once the available membrane surface can no longer accommodate the increased volume without excessive tension, the cell may rupture and release haemoglobin. This phenomenon is haemolysis. Conversely, hypertonic extracellular fluid causes water loss and cell shrinkage, potentially producing a crenated appearance. The processes are consequences of water movement across a selectively permeable membrane, not direct evidence of increased or decreased synthesis of cell components.

Osmotic fragility experiments compare red cell responses in solutions of different effective tonicities. A concentration series can reveal the fraction of haemoglobin released relative to a reference condition that produces complete lysis. A useful control is a sample exposed to an approximately isotonic medium, which should have comparatively little osmotic haemolysis in intact cells. If a cell population has reduced surface area relative to volume, it can reach its spherical limit after a smaller volume increase and may lyse in less hypotonic conditions than a more deformable population. The experiment therefore links the geometry of the cell to its response to extracellular composition, although membrane integrity and other factors can also influence the result.

An important examination distinction concerns plasma osmolality versus plasma sodium concentration. Sodium is a dominant contributor to effective extracellular osmolality in many physiological situations, but it is not the only contributor. A change in another effective solute can alter tonicity and cause water redistribution between compartments, changing sodium concentration through dilution or concentration. The sodium measurement should therefore be interpreted as one property of a regulated fluid, rather than as a direct measure of total-body sodium content or cell hydration in every possible situation.

The osmotic behaviour of blood also connects to sample preparation. Adding a substantial volume of a liquid anticoagulant can dilute cells and solutes. If the additive is not osmotically appropriate, it may alter cell volume in addition to changing the sample's total volume. A haematocrit measured after such a change may differ from the original fraction for more than one reason. Experimental design should use compatible sample collection conditions and recognise when a measured property depends on sample processing. This is particularly important in questions comparing different treatments of otherwise identical blood.
