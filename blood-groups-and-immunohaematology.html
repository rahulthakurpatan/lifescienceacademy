1. Blood as an immunological identity

A transfused erythrocyte has the same basic task as a recipient erythrocyte: it transports respiratory gases through the circulation. Yet two cells that look alike under a microscope need not be equivalent to the immune system. The outer surface of each cell displays molecular structures that differ among individuals. Blood grouping emerged from the discovery that these differences could determine whether transferred erythrocytes remained intact. Immunohaematology connects this recognition problem to inheritance, membrane biochemistry and the physiological consequences of destroying circulating cells.

An antigen is a structure recognised specifically by an antibody or another adaptive immune receptor. The particular region recognised is an epitope. A blood group antigen is an inherited red-cell surface determinant; it may be a carbohydrate attached to a membrane molecule or a region of a membrane protein. In contrast, an antibody is a soluble or membrane-bound immunoglobulin produced by B-lineage cells. The names anti-A and anti-B identify the structures bound by the antibody, not the blood groups of the persons who produce it. An antigen does not attack an antibody. Instead, an antibody binds its target and may recruit mechanisms capable of damaging the target cell.

Two people with identical haemoglobin concentrations can have different red-cell antigen phenotypes. Likewise, the oxygen-binding ability of a cell does not predict its compatibility with another person's plasma. Blood group antigens should be distinguished from the globin proteins inside erythrocytes, from coagulation factors dissolved in plasma, and from the platelet receptors that initiate haemostasis. This distinction explains why immunologically incompatible blood may cause catastrophic consequences even when its erythrocytes were physiologically functional before transfer.

The word alloantigen describes an antigen that varies among genetically distinct members of one species. An alloantibody recognises an antigen present on another person's cells but absent from the antibody-producing individual. An autoantibody instead reacts with a structure of the individual's own cells. The distinction matters experimentally: a sample that reacts against many unrelated donors may contain an antibody against a widespread alloantigen or an autoantibody. Testing the person's own cells under suitable conditions and comparing specific reaction patterns can help separate these possibilities. Broad reactivity alone does not prove either mechanism.

Mature mammalian erythrocytes have no nucleus or normal Golgi apparatus. Their membrane identity is nevertheless genetically determined because erythroid precursors expressed the relevant genes and assembled membrane molecules during maturation. Thus the mature cell displays the biochemical result of earlier gene expression, without having to transcribe the corresponding genes while circulating. This establishes a bridge from haematopoiesis to cell recognition.

2. How serological evidence revealed blood groups

Before the discovery of blood groups, variability in the consequences of transfusion suggested that blood from different individuals could not always be treated as an interchangeable fluid. Karl Landsteiner's observations at the beginning of the twentieth century provided a way to analyse this variability outside the body. If serum from one individual is combined with red cells from several different individuals, some combinations exhibit visible aggregates and others do not. The pattern is reproducible rather than an unavoidable consequence of mixing two human samples.

A conceptual reconstruction of the experiment separates the cells and serum of several donors and compares every serum with every cell preparation, including each person's own pair. A negative autologous mixture makes the interpretation that all serum simply causes clumping less plausible. The use of the same serum against different cells shows that susceptibility resides partly in the cells. Conversely, using one cell preparation against different sera shows that the capacity to cause agglutination also varies in the fluid. The cross-comparison therefore requires two interacting variables. Such an experimental design is more informative than comparing only one successful and one unsuccessful transfusion.

Landsteiner's classification, reported in 1901, established the basis of the ABO system. The AB group was subsequently described. In the ordinary adult pattern, serum from a person with group A cells agglutinates group B cells, while serum from group B reacts with group A cells. Group O serum can react with both, and group AB serum normally lacks these two specific activities. These relationships became intelligible once antibody specificity and molecular antigen structures were understood. They are not explanations based on four intrinsically different colours or oxygen-carrying properties of blood.

The 1952 report by Bhende and colleagues identified a particularly instructive exception to simple ABO classification. Cells lacking both A and B determinants might appear to be group O, yet the serum of the newly described individual reacted even against normal O erythrocytes. A classification system based only on A and B could not explain the discrepancy. The missing piece was H, a precursor structure ordinarily present on O cells. The unexpected reaction provided evidence for an additional antigen and eventually a pathway in which two genetic loci interact.

The 1990 molecular study of the ABO system by Yamamoto and colleagues linked serological phenotypes to the sequences of genes encoding carbohydrate-modifying enzymes. This changed the level of explanation. Earlier classifications were based on a reliable pattern of antibody reactions; the biochemical model explains why a particular allele generates a particular surface sugar. These forms of evidence complement one another. An enzyme sequence can support a proposed mechanism, but a typing reaction still reveals the phenotype actually expressed by the sampled cells.

3. From membrane carbohydrates to ABO antigens

The plasma membrane is not a bare phospholipid sheet. Many membrane proteins carry carbohydrate chains and certain membrane lipids also have carbohydrate groups. A chain of covalently linked sugars is a glycan. Its chemical identity depends not merely on the types of sugars present but on their order, stereochemistry and linkage positions. The end of an exposed chain may contain a determinant recognised by an antibody. A and B erythrocyte antigens belong to this class of carbohydrate determinants.

During erythroid differentiation, glycosyltransferases transfer sugar residues from activated donor molecules to acceptor glycans. The enzyme product remains part of a larger glycoprotein or glycolipid. In the erythroid ABO pathway, a functional FUT1 gene encodes an alpha-1,2-fucosyltransferase. The addition of fucose to a suitable precursor produces the H determinant. The common A and B transferases subsequently use this H-bearing structure as an acceptor. The A transferase adds N-acetylgalactosamine, whereas the B transferase adds galactose. Both reactions form an alpha-1,3 linkage to the appropriate terminal galactose of the H structure. The resulting difference in chemical architecture permits discrimination by antibodies.

A common O allele does not encode an effective A or B transferase. Consequently much H remains unmodified. Group O is therefore not a state in which the membrane has no surface carbohydrates. It represents a specific result of a biosynthetic pathway: the precursor has formed, but the final A- or B-specific modification has not occurred. An ordinary O erythrocyte should be distinguished sharply from an H-deficient erythrocyte.

At the molecular level, inheritance of the ABO gene determines the repertoire of effective transferase activity. A person with one A and one B allele expresses both activities, and red cells display both classes of modified glycan. The enzymes need not act on the same individual sugar chain. Each modifies a subset of available acceptors; a single cell can carry many molecules terminating in A and many terminating in B. There is thus no requirement for a novel intermediate sugar halfway between the A and B determinants. This is the biochemical basis of codominance rather than incomplete dominance.

The amount of antigen available to a reagent is not solely determined by the name of the allele. Subgroups such as A1 and A2 differ in the extent and pattern of expression. Some A2 individuals have anti-A1. This illustrates that a broad group phenotype can conceal further structural diversity. A weak reaction may indicate reduced expression, but differences in reagent specificity, sample composition and testing conditions can also alter reaction strength. An unambiguous mechanistic inference requires evidence beyond one weak reaction.

FUT2 encodes a related fucosyltransferase important for soluble ABH substances in secretions. Secretor status and the erythrocyte H phenotype are related biochemical topics, but they are not equivalent. In the ordinary teaching model, FUT1 supplies the H substrate on erythroid cells, while FUT2 contributes to H structures in certain secretory tissues. A person may be a nonsecretor and still have perfectly ordinary red-cell ABO antigens. This is an example of tissue-specific gene expression and enzyme function, not a contradiction of ABO inheritance.

4. Multiple alleles, codominance and inheritance calculations

An allele is an alternative sequence at a genetic locus. The standard examination model uses three ABO alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. There are three common allele classes in the population, but each ordinary diploid person possesses only two alleles. The phenotypes are determined under the assumption that sufficient H substrate is present and that no unusual variant modifies expression. The genotypes IᴬIᴬ and Iᴬi give A; IᴮIᴮ and Iᴮi give B; IᴬIᴮ gives AB; and ii gives O. The notation i denotes an inactive common O allele in the simplified model, not a separate absence of the entire ABO locus.

At meiosis the two homologous copies segregate. A parent with Iᴬi produces gametes bearing Iᴬ or i with probability one half each in the absence of segregation distortion. If the other parent has Iᴮi, the four possible offspring genotypes are IᴬIᴮ, Iᴬi, Iᴮi and ii. Each occurs with probability one quarter because the independent probabilities of the maternal and paternal gametes multiply. The phenotypes AB, A, B and O are equally likely in this specified cross. That result cannot be generalised to all couples with A and B phenotypes because their genotypes might differ.

Consider a group A parent and a group O parent who have a group O child. Under the standard model the O parent is ii, so it always contributes i. To obtain ii in the child, the A parent must contribute i and therefore be Iᴬi. The observation of one O child changes the inferred parental genotype. Once that genotype is established, the probability of an A phenotype in the next child is one half. Previous births do not alter segregation probabilities when the parental genotypes remain fixed. This is different from revising uncertainty about those genotypes when an informative observation becomes available.

An AB parent crossed with an ordinary O parent gives Iᴬi or Iᴮi, each with probability one half. No ordinary AB or O offspring is expected because the AB parent lacks i and the O parent lacks Iᴬ and Iᴮ. In a simplified pedigree question, this result can exclude a proposed combination. It cannot establish parentage uniquely: many different individuals share the same phenotype, and rare phenotypic exceptions or sample discrepancies require investigation before any biological conclusion.

Population frequencies provide another kind of inference. Suppose p, q and r are the allele frequencies of Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i respectively. They are dimensionless proportions and sum to one. With random union of gametes and Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, genotype frequencies are p², 2pr, q², 2qr, 2pq and r² for the six genotypes. The phenotype probabilities are therefore p² + 2pr for A, q² + 2qr for B, 2pq for AB and r² for O. The factors of two occur because a heterozygote can arise with the alleles inherited in either parental order. This model describes a population at the ABO locus; it does not assert that the same frequencies occur in every population.

As an illustrative dataset, let p = 0.25, q = 0.15 and r = 0.60. The predicted proportions are A = 0.0625 + 0.30 = 0.3625, B = 0.0225 + 0.18 = 0.2025, AB = 0.075 and O = 0.36. The four results sum to 1.0000. In a sample of 400 independent people drawn under the model, the expected counts are 145, 81, 30 and 144. Actual sampled counts can vary around these expectations. A deviation is not automatically evidence of selection because sampling, migration, population structure, phenotyping errors and violations of random mating can all contribute.

5. Bombay phenotype and recessive epistasis

The ordinary ABO genotype–phenotype table silently assumes that an appropriate substrate is available for the enzymes encoded by the A and B alleles. If the upstream H-producing step fails, even an otherwise functional A transferase cannot construct its normal erythrocyte determinant from a missing acceptor. Similarly, the B transferase lacks its normal H substrate. In the classical Bombay phenotype, nonfunctional H-system alleles are associated with absence of red-cell H, A and B. The classical phenotype also lacks ABH substances in secretions, unlike some para-Bombay phenotypes. In simplified two-locus questions, the red-cell result is modelled as hh, without introducing secretor complications unless the question specifies them.

This is a case of recessive epistasis: the recessive genotype at the H locus masks the observable effect of another locus. It is not a new pattern of dominance among ABO alleles. Codominance still describes the relationship between functional A and B alleles when their substrate is available. Epistasis describes a relationship between different genes in the pathway. A cell may carry the genetic instructions for two active transferases and nevertheless lack both antigens because the precursor is missing.

Compare two cells with no detectable A or B. The ordinary ii cell with a functional H pathway expresses H. A classical Bombay cell does not. A and B typing reagents alone may not distinguish them, because both give negative reactions. Testing H expression and examining the serum reaction against ordinary group O cells offer independent information. A classical Bombay individual's plasma can contain anti-H in addition to anti-A and anti-B. An O donor's intact H determinant is therefore a target, even though the donor has neither A nor B. Ordinary group O red cells cannot be assumed compatible with Bombay blood.

The epistatic relationship predicts unusual pedigrees. For example, suppose an Iᴬi Hh parent mates with an Iᴮi Hh parent. Assume independent assortment and the simplified complete H-loss model. The chance of hh is one quarter regardless of the ABO outcome. Among the three quarters with H_, the four conventional ABO phenotypes each have conditional probability one quarter. Therefore the probabilities of conventional A, B, AB and O are each three sixteenths. The remaining four sixteenths have the H-deficient phenotype in this model. Adding the five classes gives sixteen sixteenths. Although the combined absence of A and B occurs in seven sixteenths, the four sixteenths that lack H should not be called ordinary O. In a clinically exact question the secretor genotype and para-Bombay variants may need separate consideration.

A useful way to check a two-locus problem is to calculate pathway availability first. Write the probability of a functional H phenotype, and only then distribute that subset into ABO classes. This avoids double counting the hh ii genotype. The latter already belongs to the H-deficient class and must not be counted again as conventional O. This method generalises to biochemical pathways in which a downstream phenotype is conditional on an upstream enzyme being present.

6. Naturally occurring ABO antibodies and specific recognition

An antibody molecule contains antigen-binding regions with structures complementary to particular epitopes. Affinity describes the strength of an individual binding interaction; avidity describes the combined strength of multiple simultaneous interactions. Pentameric secreted IgM can engage multiple determinants on cell surfaces, making it particularly effective at producing a network of cells. IgG molecules have two antigen-binding sites and can bind red-cell antigens without necessarily producing visible aggregates under ordinary saline conditions.

The usual anti-A and anti-B antibodies arise after birth as the immune system encounters environmental carbohydrate structures resembling ABO determinants. This is why they are called naturally occurring: a known transfusion or pregnancy is not required for their development. The phrase does not mean that a newborn already has the full adult antibody pattern, nor that their formation occurs without any antigenic stimulation. Exposure to structurally related microbial and other environmental glycans can stimulate cross-reactive responses. Self-tolerance helps prevent the ordinary production of strongly reactive antibodies against the individual's own expressed A or B determinant.

An adult with group A generally has anti-B, while group B has anti-A. Ordinary group O typically has both and group AB normally has neither. These regularities apply to ABO isoagglutinins, not to all antibodies in plasma. A group AB individual may form antibodies against unrelated red-cell antigens after exposure. A group O individual may have IgG as well as IgM ABO-reactive antibodies. Isotype cannot be inferred with certainty from the specificity name alone. This is important when considering passage across the placenta.

The distinction between sensitisation and effector injury prevents an important conceptual mistake. Antibody production may occur after exposure to antigen, yet tissue injury depends on subsequent antigen–antibody encounter and the mechanisms that the bound antibodies recruit. Antibody concentration, binding strength, antigen accessibility, complement activation and the capacity of macrophages to remove coated cells all affect the consequence. Detectable antibody binding is therefore neither identical to cell lysis nor a quantitative prediction of its severity.

During an infant's early development, naturally occurring ABO antibodies may be absent or weak. Maternal IgG can also be present in the infant's circulation. Hence the usual reciprocal association between forward and reverse adult grouping is not expected to operate identically in newborn specimens. This is a physiological exception based on immune development and passive transfer, rather than a departure from the inherited surface antigen phenotype.

7. Agglutination, haemolysis and complement

Agglutination is the formation of a visible or detectable aggregate of particles such as erythrocytes. An antibody that binds surface determinants on more than one cell can bridge those cells. Additional antibodies and cells can extend the structure into a lattice. Visible clumping therefore reports an interaction involving the geometry and abundance of binding sites as well as the specificity of the antibody. A negative visual result does not establish that no immunoglobulin has bound.

Haemolysis is the disruption or destruction of erythrocytes. Intravascular haemolysis releases haemoglobin into the plasma; extravascular haemolysis commonly involves removal of antibody- or complement-coated cells by phagocytes, especially in the spleen or liver. These are different endpoints. Agglutinated cells may remain intact during observation, and sensitised cells can be cleared without forming large visible clumps. Neither process should be confused with coagulation, which builds fibrin through enzymatic activation of clotting factors.

Complement provides a mechanistic connection between antibody recognition and membrane injury. Suitable antigen-bound antibodies activate the classical pathway through C1, initiating downstream proteolytic events. Formation of C3 convertase permits deposition of C3-derived fragments. These fragments can promote phagocytic clearance, and downstream activation may produce a terminal membrane attack complex. If membrane injury exceeds the cell's protective capacity, the erythrocyte loses integrity. Complement fragments can also amplify inflammatory responses; the effect is not restricted to the membrane of the original target.

IgM is especially effective at classical complement activation when bound appropriately to a cell surface. Some IgG subclasses can also activate complement, depending on their arrangement and other conditions. Rh antibodies are typically IgG and frequently cause predominantly extravascular removal of coated erythrocytes. These are useful characteristic patterns rather than absolute laws. One cannot determine the entire effector pathway solely from the letters ABO or Rh.

Consider a conceptual comparison of three samples containing the same target cells. The first contains a suitable agglutinating antibody but no active complement, the second contains that antibody with active complement, and the third contains complement without the specific antibody. A cell aggregate in both antibody-containing samples would support antibody-mediated bridging. Greater release of cell-free haemoglobin in the second sample would support an additional complement-dependent injury mechanism. The complement-only control helps test whether the injury can be attributed to antibody-independent damage. Equal cell numbers, medium composition and observation conditions are necessary for comparison. This is a teaching model rather than a clinical testing procedure.

Erythrocytes also carry negative surface charge, which contributes to mutual repulsion. The distance between cells can influence whether a particular antibody produces direct agglutination. Antiglobulin testing exploits the fact that an additional antibody against human globulins can connect cells already sensitised by human IgG. Thus immunological binding can be demonstrated even when direct aggregation was not obvious. The result identifies sensitisation under the test conditions, not necessarily the rate or anatomical site of destruction in the body.

8. Forward grouping, reverse grouping and experimental controls

In forward grouping the unknown is the antigen phenotype of the specimen's red cells. Reagents of known specificity provide the probes. Agglutination with anti-A but not anti-B supports A expression; reaction with anti-B but not anti-A supports B expression; reaction with both supports AB; and absence of reaction with either suggests no detectable A or B. The final interpretation assumes acceptable controls and adequate expression. It does not independently exclude a rare H-deficient phenotype.

Reverse grouping changes the unknown. The specimen is plasma or serum, and the known objects are reagent A and B cells. Reaction with B cells alone supports the usual group A antibody pattern. Reaction with A cells alone supports group B. Reaction with both supports the usual group O pattern, while reaction with neither is typical of AB. In both cases the reaction is observed between a cellular target and an antibody; it is the source of the unknown component that changes.

For an illustrative sample, forward results with anti-A and anti-B are positive and negative. Reverse results with A cells and B cells are negative and positive. The two parts support group A. If the same sample also reacts with reagent A cells, the result is discrepant. Possible explanations include an unexpected antibody, subgroup-associated reactivity, a sample issue or other interference. Labelling the sample as a new genotype purely from that one observation would exceed the evidence. A discrepancy is useful because two distinct measurements have failed to agree; its resolution matters more than choosing which result looks familiar.

In mixed-field reactivity, aggregates and a substantial population of unagglutinated red cells are observed together. One possible cause is the presence of two erythrocyte populations, as after transfusion with a different compatible ABO phenotype or following certain haematopoietic changes. Some antigen subgroups can also give mixed-field appearances. A molecularly uniform individual should not automatically be inferred from a mixed specimen. A serological phenotype describes the cells sampled, whereas germline genotype describes inherited sequence.

Controls establish that a reaction actually reflects the intended interaction. Known antigen-positive cells demonstrate that an antibody reagent can detect its target; appropriate antigen-negative cells test specificity and background. An unexpectedly reactive control can invalidate the inference from an otherwise convincing-looking test. A negative reaction is meaningful only when the detection system has worked under the conditions used. These principles apply not only to blood typing but to all antigen-based biological assays.

The direct antiglobulin test asks whether erythrocytes are coated with immunoglobulin or complement in the sampled state. The indirect antiglobulin test investigates antibodies capable of binding test erythrocytes under assay conditions, followed by detection of the bound human globulin. They differ in where sensitisation takes place, rather than simply in the presence or absence of an additional reagent. A positive direct test is not by itself a demonstration of active haemolysis, since coating and destruction are separate events. An indirect test can detect an antibody before exposure to the relevant donor cells in vivo.

9. Compatibility is a directional relationship

To reason about transfusion, first identify which component is transferred. Packed red-cell components carry many donor erythrocytes and relatively little residual plasma. Plasma components primarily deliver donor plasma and its soluble constituents. The crucial ABO question for erythrocyte transfer is whether the recipient has antibodies against the donor cells. For plasma transfer the direction reverses: could antibodies in the donor plasma recognise the recipient's red cells?

In a simplified ABO-only model, ordinary O erythrocytes carry no A or B determinant. A person with group A can therefore receive group A or O cells on ABO grounds, but B and AB cells carry B and are targets for anti-B. A group B recipient can receive B or O cells. AB recipients lack ordinary anti-A and anti-B and so can be compatible with all four ABO erythrocyte groups in the model. O recipients usually have both antibodies and can receive ordinary O cells only. The direction of transfer matters: A cells may be compatible with AB recipients, but AB cells are not ABO-compatible with ordinary A recipients.

For plasma, donor antibodies are the relevant agents. Group AB plasma normally lacks anti-A and anti-B, making it broadly compatible in this limited ABO model. Group O plasma contains both and may react against recipients with A or B antigens. A group A recipient can receive A plasma, which contains anti-B, or AB plasma, which normally lacks both. Group B recipients can receive B or AB plasma. Ordinary O erythrocytes lack A and B targets, so the simplified ABO model allows plasma from each ordinary group for an O recipient. These are explanatory relationships, not rules for selecting a component outside professional testing.

The common expressions universal donor and universal recipient omit crucial qualifications. The red cells of group O RhD-negative donors still carry many non-ABO antigens and usually H. A recipient with a relevant alloantibody or the classical Bombay phenotype may be incompatible with such cells. Conversely, an AB RhD-positive recipient may have other clinically significant antibodies. Whole blood contains both cells and plasma, so the two directions of possible reaction cannot be collapsed into one table. Products, antibody titres, residual plasma and circumstances matter. Actual transfusion decisions require validated compatibility testing and authorised clinical judgement.

An efficient examination technique is to write the two sets explicitly. Let D represent the A and B antigen set on donor erythrocytes and R the set of corresponding specificities present as antibodies in recipient plasma. In the ordinary ABO red-cell model, a necessary condition for compatibility is that D and R have no shared target. For group B cells D is {B}. For an A recipient R is {B}. Their intersection contains B, predicting incompatibility. For O cells D is empty in this restricted two-antigen model, so the intersection is empty. The set notation deliberately excludes H, Rh and other systems; it should never be interpreted as a general guarantee of safety.

An antibody screen asks whether a recipient's plasma contains unexpected red-cell antibodies using suitable test cell panels. A crossmatch asks about compatibility of the intended donor red cells and recipient plasma, or uses an electronic process in appropriately eligible circumstances. Matching ABO and D does not answer the unexpected-antibody question. A negative screening or compatibility result also has a defined analytical scope: uncommon antigens, weak antibodies and changing immune histories can matter. A previously identified antibody may decline below detection and become important again after renewed exposure.

10. Rh blood groups and sensitisation

The Rh system provides a contrast to the ABO pathway. Its important antigens are associated with membrane proteins rather than terminal carbohydrate modifications. RHD and RHCE are related genes encoding Rh proteins. The D antigen is the principal determinant of the positive or negative designation used in routine blood grouping. Other common Rh determinants include C, c, E and e. A D-negative person lacks ordinary detectable D expression but does not necessarily lack all Rh proteins or all Rh-system antigens.

For elementary inheritance exercises a D/d notation is often used, where D indicates an expressed D-associated allele and d represents lack of D in a simplified model. DD and Dd are treated as D-positive and dd as D-negative. There is no corresponding ordinary d antigen implied by this notation. Real RHD variation includes deletion and variant alleles, and some weak or partial D phenotypes complicate the simple model. The notation is useful for Mendelian predictions under stated assumptions, not a complete molecular account of Rh typing.

A D-negative person does not normally have anti-D merely because D is missing. Exposure to D-bearing erythrocytes through transfusion or fetomaternal cell transfer can lead to alloimmunisation. Antigen-responsive B cells, with appropriate supporting immune signals, expand and differentiate. Some become antibody-secreting cells and some contribute to immunological memory. Later exposure can lead to a faster and stronger antibody response. This contrasts with the usual ABO antibody pattern, where environmental antigens can stimulate cross-reactive antibodies before any transfusion.

A D-negative recipient may therefore lack detectable anti-D at the time of initial exposure and still be at risk of sensitisation. Immediate absence of a reaction does not prove that subsequent exposures are harmless. The time scale of primary immunity and the distinction between immunisation and effector injury explain how an apparently uneventful initial encounter can have later consequences.

Consider a D-negative mother represented as dd and a D-positive father represented as Dd. Under the simple inheritance model, paternal gametes carry D or d with equal probability. The mother contributes d. The chance of a D-positive fetus is one half in each pregnancy; if the father were DD all offspring would be predicted D-positive. The probability of D expression in the fetus is not the probability of haemolytic disease. The latter also depends on maternal exposure, sensitisation, antibody properties, placental transfer and preventive care. Conflating genetic probability with disease probability is a major reasoning error.

The existence of other Rh antigens and protein-based systems is a reminder that identical ABO and D classifications do not mean immunological identity. Kell antibodies can contribute to fetal disease, including suppression of erythroid production; Kidd antibodies can be associated with delayed transfusion reactions. These examples illustrate distinct physiological routes to incompatibility. A detailed catalogue of hundreds of antigens is unnecessary to understand the general principle that the relevant target and antibody must be considered together.

11. Maternal–fetal blood group incompatibility

The placenta supports exchange between the maternal and fetal circulations without normally allowing unrestricted mixing of their erythrocytes. Small numbers of fetal cells can enter the maternal circulation, especially around delivery but also during pregnancy. If these cells express an antigen absent from the mother, they may stimulate an alloimmune response. In a sensitised mother, IgG antibodies can cross the placenta through Fc receptor-mediated transport. This creates a direction of antigen and antibody exposure different from ordinary transfusion.

In a representative RhD mechanism, fetal D-positive erythrocytes enter the circulation of a D-negative mother. An adaptive response can generate anti-D and memory cells. When maternal IgG anti-D reaches the circulation of a D-positive fetus, it binds the fetal erythrocytes. Coated cells may be removed by macrophages, shortening their effective life span. Reduced erythrocyte survival lowers oxygen-carrying capacity. Compensatory erythropoiesis increases and, in severe cases, immature erythroid cells enter the fetal circulation, explaining the historical term erythroblastosis fetalis.

A useful physiological balance is red-cell population change = production minus loss. The equality is expressed as rates in cells per unit time. In a steady state, production approximately equals loss. If immune clearance increases faster than marrow and other fetal erythropoietic tissues can increase production, the circulating red-cell mass declines. Increased reticulocytes or immature cells can therefore coexist with anaemia. High production is a compensatory response rather than evidence that net red-cell mass must be increasing.

Severe fetal anaemia can increase circulatory workload and contribute to hydrops, a pathological accumulation of fluid in fetal compartments. Breakdown of haemoglobin also increases bilirubin production. Before birth, placental transfer helps clear fetal bilirubin through maternal handling. After birth, that route is lost and the newborn's own bilirubin processing capacity becomes important. A newborn with ongoing haemolysis may therefore develop substantial unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia. Anaemia and bilirubin accumulation are linked consequences of erythrocyte destruction but represent different physiological problems.

The familiar teaching narrative describes initial sensitisation during one pregnancy and greater risk in a subsequent antigen-positive pregnancy. It is a useful model of immune memory, not an absolute rule. A mother may have been sensitised before her first recognised pregnancy, including through a transfusion or prior pregnancy loss. Sensitisation can also occur early enough to affect the current pregnancy. Conversely, a later fetus may lack the target antigen. The relevant conditions are maternal antibody, fetal antigen, placental access and sufficient effector activity, not pregnancy number alone.

Passive anti-D immunoglobulin can help prevent active maternal sensitisation to D-positive erythrocytes in appropriately selected unsensitised D-negative individuals. The protective effect involves antibody-mediated immune suppression; clearance or altered handling of fetal cells and other inhibitory mechanisms may contribute. The passive antibody is not an active vaccine against D and does not erase established immune memory. Timing, eligibility and dosing are matters for professional care and are beyond this physiological chapter. The important concept is the difference between supplying temporary antibody and inducing a long-lived adaptive response.

ABO-associated fetal or newborn haemolysis has a different characteristic pattern. Most ABO-reactive IgM does not cross the placenta efficiently, but IgG anti-A or anti-B can. Group O mothers are more likely to have relevant IgG ABO antibodies. Consequently an A or B infant born to a group O mother may be affected even in a first pregnancy. ABO-associated disease is often less severe than classic RhD disease, in part because fetal erythrocytes express lower amounts of ABO determinants and other tissues also express related glycans. Severity can vary, and neither an ABO mismatch nor an Rh mismatch alone is proof of disease.

12. Haemolytic transfusion reactions and physiological consequences

An acute haemolytic transfusion reaction may arise when transfused erythrocytes encounter pre-existing recipient antibodies. Major ABO incompatibility can activate complement rapidly and produce intravascular haemolysis. The circulating red cells that were intended to improve oxygen transport are instead destroyed, and their released contents enter the plasma. Complement-derived mediators and other inflammatory responses may alter vascular function. Severe systemic injury can involve hypotension, activation of coagulation and kidney dysfunction. These consequences cannot be attributed simply to mechanical obstruction by erythrocyte clumps.

Free haemoglobin released into plasma binds scavenging proteins such as haptoglobin. Extensive release may exceed binding capacity. Haemoglobin and its breakdown products can contribute to renal injury; impaired perfusion and systemic inflammation can add to the effect. A fall in circulating red-cell mass, elevated cell-free haemoglobin and reduced haptoglobin are mechanistically related observations, but no single measurement independently identifies the cause. Nonimmune mechanical or thermal damage to erythrocytes can also release haemoglobin. Establishing an immune mechanism requires relevant immunohaematological evidence.

Delayed haemolytic transfusion reactions illustrate adaptive memory. A recipient sensitised during an earlier encounter may have an alloantibody whose concentration has declined below the detection threshold. Re-exposure to the relevant antigen can trigger a secondary response. Newly rising antibody coats donor erythrocytes, which are then cleared. Instead of an immediate reaction, the expected haemoglobin improvement may diminish over subsequent days. The time course is informative because it allows the development of a secondary immune response. It is not proof on its own: bleeding and nonimmune changes in red-cell survival can also cause falling haemoglobin.

The distinction between antibody detection and physiological outcome is illustrated by a positive direct antiglobulin test. It supplies evidence that immunoglobulin or complement is associated with sampled erythrocytes, but additional information is required to establish whether destruction is occurring. A blood count trend, biochemical evidence of haemolysis, and antibody specificity can strengthen the inference. Conversely, a negative test does not exclude every cause of haemolysis, especially if the relevant donor cells have already been rapidly removed.

The previous chapter on coagulation and fibrinolysis explains why uncontrolled coagulation can become a secondary complication of severe systemic inflammation and injury. The initiating lesion in an incompatible red-cell transfusion is immune recognition, not a primary defect in factor VIII or a spontaneous absence of fibrinogen. This causal ordering is important when interpreting integrated questions that present both haemolysis and disturbed haemostasis.

Finally, a compatibility analysis must distinguish red-cell reactions from other causes of adverse events associated with blood components. Not every fever, respiratory change or fall in haemoglobin represents an ABO reaction. In an examination problem, one should use the specific antigen–antibody relationship, temporal sequence and measured outcomes rather than assuming a transfusion reaction has a single universal mechanism. This completes the conceptual transition from inherited membrane molecules to whole-body physiological consequences.

13. Integrating genetic and serological evidence

A sound inference starts by stating what was measured. Genotyping examines sequence at defined loci; forward grouping examines surface expression; reverse grouping examines circulating antibodies; antiglobulin testing examines sensitisation or antibodies that can sensitise test cells. A haemolysis marker examines a consequence of red-cell injury. These methods overlap in the questions they address but are not interchangeable. Concordant results support a shared explanation, whereas discrepant results reveal the assumptions that need testing.

Consider three hypothetical samples X, Y and Z. X has no reaction with anti-A or anti-B, has H detectable and its plasma reacts with A and B reagent cells but not ordinary O cells. Y also lacks A and B reactions, but H is undetectable and its plasma reacts with ordinary O cells. Z reacts with both anti-A and anti-B, has no expected anti-A or anti-B activity and its cells display D. X is consistent with ordinary O. Y suggests classical Bombay-type H deficiency, requiring confirmation rather than merely the O label. Z is consistent with AB RhD-positive. None of these interpretations excludes the possibility of unexpected antibodies to unrelated antigens.

A second teaching dataset concerns an immunological response. Imagine a fixed dose of antigen-positive cells is encountered twice by an individual who initially has no detectable antibody to that antigen. In the first episode an assay shows a lag followed by a slow rise; later the antibody falls below the assay threshold. After the second encounter, a shorter lag and steeper increase are observed. The response is consistent with immunological memory. A negative measurement between episodes does not establish that memory B cells are absent. The assay detects circulating antibody, whereas memory describes a population of cells capable of mounting a response.

Antibody titre offers a simple quantitative description of reactivity under defined conditions. In a serial twofold dilution series, if 1:2, 1:4, 1:8 and 1:16 are reactive but 1:32 is not, the endpoint titre is conventionally expressed as 16. If a later measurement under comparable conditions gives a titre of 64, it is a fourfold increase in reciprocal dilution endpoint, corresponding to two twofold steps. It does not directly prove a fourfold increase in absolute antibody concentration or disease severity; reagent characteristics, avidity and assay conditions influence the endpoint.

The most reliable strategy for integrative questions is to follow a sequence of causes. An inherited variant changes an enzyme or protein, changing a surface determinant. Environmental exposure or transfusion can change the antibody repertoire. Encounter of antibody and target can lead to sensitisation, aggregation, complement activation or cellular removal. Changes in erythrocyte survival then affect haemoglobin concentration, oxygen delivery and haemoglobin breakdown. Distinguishing these links avoids attributing a change in antibody production to the erythrocyte itself or confusing a detectable antigen with an automatic pathological event.

Blood groups thus illustrate the way genetic variation can be physiologically silent until a new context creates a conflict. The same erythrocyte may be entirely functional in its original host and vulnerable in a recipient who carries a relevant antibody. In the next node, blood volume and its regulation, the focus shifts from the compatibility and survival of cells to the regulation of the quantity and distribution of circulating fluid. Both are necessary for oxygen delivery, but they answer different physiological questions.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Group O has no antigens

O has no A/B determinants in the standard model, but retains H and other antigens. The letter O is treated as absolute zero; check which antigen system is being discussed.

2. AB phenotype is intermediate

Both A and B determinants are expressed. A blended phenotype is incomplete dominance, not codominance.

3. Every O-like forward pattern is ordinary O

H-deficient cells may also lack A and B. Forward-only classification hides the precursor; inspect reverse and H evidence.

4. Anti-A is an A-cell antigen

Anti-A is an antibody targeting A. Similar naming reverses the roles; identify target and binder.

5. Natural antibodies need no exposure

Environmental cross-reactive structures stimulate their formation. Natural means no known red-cell transfusion required, not spontaneous fully developed immunity.

6. All ABO antibodies are IgM

IgG ABO-reactive antibodies also exist. Generalising the dominant class obscures fetal disease.

7. D-negative means no Rh antigens

Other Rh antigens can be present. D status is one antigen classification.

8. D-negative individuals automatically have anti-D

Anti-D generally follows sensitisation. Do not transfer the ordinary ABO reciprocal rule to RhD.

9. Clumping is coagulation

Agglutination involves cell bridging; coagulation forms fibrin. Both appear as masses; determine the molecular mechanism.

10. Agglutination proves haemolysis

Intact cells can aggregate; coated cells may be cleared without obvious clumping. Separate observed aggregate from membrane damage.

11. Red-cell and plasma compatibility run the same way

The source of harmful antibodies reverses. Always identify the component before using a table.

12. O negative is unconditionally universal

H and other antigens still matter. Restrict shorthand to the defined antigen model.

13. A positive DAT proves active haemolysis

DAT detects coating, not a destruction rate. Interpret together with physiological markers.

14. The first pregnancy is always safe

Prior or early sensitisation may affect a first recognised pregnancy. Use exposure history, not birth order alone.

15. Anti-D immunoglobulin creates permanent active immunity

It passively supplies antibody to prevent sensitisation. Distinguish prophylaxis from vaccination and from treatment of existing immune memory.

16. A D-positive fetus necessarily develops disease

Maternal antibody, transfer and effector conditions must also be present. Genotype probabilities are not disease probabilities.

Original examination practice

Questions use the ordinary simplified ABO and Rh inheritance models unless otherwise specified. They are original practice, not previous-year questions.

1. Part B — What is the immediate molecular target of an anti-A antibody?

  1. A determinant on a cell-surface glycan
  2. An ABO gene in the nucleus
  3. Intracellular globin
  4. Fibrinogen
Answer and explanation

A. The antibody recognises the exposed A carbohydrate determinant. DNA and globin are not the target of anti-A.

2. Part B — Which enzyme is upstream of the usual red-cell A and B transferases?

  1. FUT1
  2. Thrombin
  3. Heme oxygenase
  4. Erythropoietin
Answer and explanation

A. FUT1 generates H, the acceptor required by A and B transferases.

3. Part B — The A transferase adds which residue?

  1. Galactose
  2. N-acetylgalactosamine
  3. Fucose
  4. Glucose only
Answer and explanation

B. A transferase adds GalNAc; B transferase adds galactose, and FUT1 adds fucose.

4. Part B — The common O phenotype with functional H has which feature?

  1. No membrane glycans
  2. Unmodified H determinant
  3. Both A and B determinants
  4. Absence of all Rh proteins
Answer and explanation

B. Loss of effective A/B transferase activity leaves H available.

5. Part B — IᴬIᴮ illustrates which relation?

  1. Complete dominance of A
  2. Recessive epistasis at ABO itself
  3. Codominance
  4. Incomplete dominance
Answer and explanation

C. Both A and B determinants are expressed, rather than a blended intermediate.

6. Part B — A person with ordinary group B usually has which ABO antibody?

  1. Anti-A
  2. Anti-B
  3. Anti-D necessarily
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

A. Tolerance to self B and recognition of absent A give the ordinary reciprocal pattern.

7. Part B — Which immunoglobulin is generally most effective at direct saline agglutination?

  1. IgE
  2. IgM
  3. IgD
  4. Monomeric IgA only
Answer and explanation

B. Pentameric secreted IgM has high multivalent bridging capacity.

8. Part B — Forward grouping examines the unknown specimen's what?

  1. Plasma antibody specificity
  2. Red-cell antigen expression
  3. Haemoglobin oxygen affinity
  4. Platelet count
Answer and explanation

B. Known antisera probe antigens on the unknown cells.

9. Part B — Reverse grouping uses which material as the unknown?

  1. Known reagent cells
  2. Recipient plasma
  3. Donor DNA sequence
  4. A platelet lysate
Answer and explanation

B. Known A and B cells probe antibodies in the unknown plasma.

10. Part B — Which property defines D-negative in ordinary Rh typing?

  1. Absence of all Rh antigens
  2. Absence of detectable D expression
  3. Presence of anti-D from birth
  4. Absence of H
Answer and explanation

B. D is one Rh determinant, not the entire Rh system.

11. Part B — Which antibody class crosses the placenta efficiently?

  1. IgM
  2. IgG
  3. IgD
  4. All equally
Answer and explanation

B. Fc receptor-mediated transport transfers IgG efficiently.

12. Part B — What does a direct antiglobulin test primarily detect?

  1. Cell-associated globulin or complement
  2. ABO DNA sequence
  3. Plasma osmolality
  4. Fibrin crosslinks
Answer and explanation

A. The test detects coating of sampled erythrocytes, not genotype or haemolysis severity.

13. Part B — What is agglutination?

  1. Cell aggregation
  2. Fibrin synthesis
  3. Globin translation
  4. Inevitable cell rupture
Answer and explanation

A. Antibody bridging can aggregate intact cells without lysis.

14. Part B — Which is a typical feature of classical Bombay plasma?

  1. No antibodies to H
  2. Anti-H activity
  3. Only anti-D
  4. Absence of all IgM
Answer and explanation

B. H deficiency can permit production of anti-H; ordinary O cells carry H.

15. Part B — Which group normally lacks both anti-A and anti-B in adult plasma?

  1. O
  2. A
  3. B
  4. AB
Answer and explanation

D. AB expresses both self determinants and usually lacks both corresponding antibodies.

16. Part B — The Rh system is primarily associated with which molecular class?

  1. Membrane proteins
  2. Free haemoglobin
  3. Soluble plasma fibrin
  4. ABO terminal sugars only
Answer and explanation

A. Rh determinants are protein-associated, unlike ABO carbohydrate determinants.

17. Part B — Where are many IgG-coated red cells removed?

  1. Spleen and liver
  2. Alveolar airspace exclusively
  3. Bone mineral exclusively
  4. Glomerular filtrate exclusively
Answer and explanation

A. Macrophages in these organs recognise coated cells during extravascular clearance.

18. Part B — Which mechanism initiates classical complement in a suitable red-cell antibody reaction?

  1. C1 recognition of bound antibody
  2. EPO binding its receptor
  3. Fibrin crosslinking
  4. Oxygen loading
Answer and explanation

A. Antigen-bound suitable antibodies recruit C1 and initiate classical pathway activation.

19. Part B — What distinguishes an alloantibody from an autoantibody?

  1. The target is an antigen of another individual of the species
  2. It never binds a cell
  3. It is always IgM
  4. It is made by erythrocytes
Answer and explanation

A. Alloantibodies target nonself polymorphic antigens; autoantibodies recognise self.

20. Part B — Which gene contributes importantly to soluble ABH substances in secretions?

  1. FUT2
  2. HBB
  3. RHD only
  4. F8
Answer and explanation

A. FUT2 supplies fucosyltransferase activity in secretory tissues; FUT1 is the ordinary erythroid pathway.

21. Part C — Iᴬi × Iᴮi, with normal H expression: chance of an O child?

  1. 0
  2. 1/4
  3. 1/2
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

B. Each parent transmits i with probability 1/2, so P(ii)=1/4.

22. Part C — An A parent and O parent have an O child. Under standard assumptions, what is the A parent's genotype?

  1. IᴬIᴬ
  2. Iᴬi
  3. IᴬIᴮ
  4. ii
Answer and explanation

B. The A parent must carry and transmit i; phenotype A requires Iᴬ.

23. Part C — IᴬIᴮ × ii yields which ordinary phenotype distribution?

  1. All AB
  2. A and B in equal proportions
  3. O and AB in equal proportions
  4. All O
Answer and explanation

B. The AB parent supplies Iᴬ or Iᴮ and the O parent always supplies i.

24. Part C — In Iᴬi Hh × Iᴮi Hh, independent assortment, what is the probability of conventional AB with H expression?

  1. 1/4
  2. 3/16
  3. 1/16
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

B. P(IᴬIᴮ)=1/4 and P(H_)=3/4, so the joint probability is 3/16.

25. Part C — For the same two-locus cross, how many of 16 equally weighted combinations lack H regardless of ABO genotype?

  1. 1
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 7
Answer and explanation

C. hh occurs in 1/4 of conceptions. Seven of sixteen lack A and B under the model, but only four lack H.

26. Part C — Allele frequencies p=0.25, q=0.15, r=0.60; Hardy–Weinberg AB frequency?

  1. 0.0375
  2. 0.075
  3. 0.15
  4. 0.40
Answer and explanation

B. The heterozygote IᴬIᴮ has frequency 2pq=2×0.25×0.15=0.075.

27. Part C — With r=0.60 and ordinary H expression, expected O count among 400?

  1. 24
  2. 120
  3. 144
  4. 240
Answer and explanation

C. O=ii, so r²=0.36 and 400×0.36=144.

28. Part C — Forward anti-A +, anti-B −; reverse A cells −, B cells +. What is the interpretation?

  1. Ordinary A pattern
  2. Ordinary B pattern
  3. AB
  4. Bombay proven
Answer and explanation

A. A expression and anti-B activity agree; these results do not independently exclude all rare variants or other antibodies.

29. Part C — Forward A and B both negative; serum reacts with normal O cells and H is undetectable. What is the strongest hypothesis?

  1. Ordinary O alone
  2. H-deficient phenotype
  3. AB with normal H
  4. D positivity proven
Answer and explanation

B. Anti-H-like reactivity against O and absent detectable H explain the discrepancy; confirm with additional evidence.

30. Part C — The anti-A positive-control cells fail to react. Unknown cells are also negative. What follows?

  1. Unknown is definitely O
  2. Test cannot establish absent A
  3. Unknown is definitely B
  4. FUT1 is absent
Answer and explanation

B. A failed positive control invalidates confidence in the negative unknown reaction.

31. Part C — A recipient has group A. Which packed red-cell ABO donor is compatible in the simple model?

  1. B only
  2. AB only
  3. A or O
  4. All groups
Answer and explanation

C. A recipients usually possess anti-B, so donor cells must lack B.

32. Part C — A group A recipient receives plasma. Which groups are compatible on ordinary ABO grounds?

  1. A and AB
  2. A and O
  3. B and O
  4. O only
Answer and explanation

A. Donor plasma must lack anti-A; ordinary A and AB plasma satisfy this condition.

33. Part C — Why might ordinary O RhD-negative red cells react with classical Bombay plasma?

  1. They display B
  2. They display A
  3. They display H
  4. They necessarily display D
Answer and explanation

C. O is H-bearing when FUT1 functions. Anti-H is outside the usual A/B compatibility matrix.

34. Part C — An IgG-specific assay shows antibody on cells despite no direct saline clumping. What follows?

  1. Binding can occur without obvious direct agglutination
  2. No antibodies are present
  3. All cells have lysed
  4. Blood is definitely AB
Answer and explanation

A. Sensitisation and visible lattice formation are different endpoints; the test can reveal bound IgG.

35. Part C — Cells plus specific antibody clump with or without complement; haemoglobin release rises only with active complement. What is supported?

  1. Complement is essential for all aggregation
  2. Complement adds membrane injury beyond bridging
  3. The antibody is non-specific
  4. Clotting alone explains both
Answer and explanation

B. The controlled contrast separates antibody bridging from additional complement-dependent haemolysis.

36. Part C — A dd mother and Dd father: probability a fetus is D-positive in the simplified model?

  1. 0
  2. 1/4
  3. 1/2
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

C. The mother supplies d and the father supplies D half the time; this is not disease probability.

37. Part C — A D-negative mother has previously formed anti-D IgG. Which fetus is the relevant target?

  1. D-negative
  2. D-positive
  3. Every fetus regardless of antigen
  4. Only a group O fetus
Answer and explanation

B. The antibody requires its D epitope. ABO type alone does not identify the Rh target.

38. Part C — Why may haemolytic disease occur in the first recognised pregnancy?

  1. First pregnancy prevents IgG transfer
  2. Earlier exposure may have generated memory
  3. The fetus makes maternal anti-D
  4. IgM readily crosses placenta
Answer and explanation

B. Sensitisation may precede the pregnancy or occur during it. Maternal IgG can cross placenta.

39. Part C — A newborn has anaemia and increased circulating immature erythroid cells. How can both coexist?

  1. Production is necessarily zero
  2. Clearance may exceed increased production
  3. IgM converts to erythrocytes
  4. Anaemia means high haematocrit
Answer and explanation

B. Compensatory erythropoiesis can rise yet remain inadequate to replace the destroyed cells.

40. Part C — An antibody titre changes from 16 to 64 under comparable conditions. How many twofold endpoint steps?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. 48
Answer and explanation

B. 16→32→64 is two doublings; it is not a direct absolute antibody concentration measurement.

41. Part C — After transfusion, haemoglobin rises initially then declines as a previously undetectable alloantibody appears. Best immunological hypothesis?

  1. No immune response is possible
  2. Anamnestic delayed clearance
  3. Immediate IgE-mediated aggregation necessarily
  4. FUT1 changed in all cells
Answer and explanation

B. A memory response can restore antibody and promote donor-cell removal days after exposure; other causes need exclusion.

42. Part C — A plasma antibody screen is negative now, but an antibody was documented previously. What conclusion is justified?

  1. Memory definitely absent
  2. Historical specificity can remain relevant
  3. All future donors guaranteed compatible
  4. All red cells lack antigens
Answer and explanation

B. Antibody levels can decline below detection while immune memory persists.

43. Part C — An adult blood sample shows two erythrocyte populations in typing. Which explanation must be considered?

  1. Mixed donor and recipient cells after transfusion
  2. All cells necessarily have identical expression
  3. ABO antibodies are made by mature RBCs
  4. Every cell is haemolysed
Answer and explanation

A. A sample may include distinct red-cell populations; some subgroups may also show mixed field.

44. Part C — At steady state production is 10 arbitrary units/day and clearance 10. After immune injury production becomes 13 and clearance 18. What is net change?

  1. +5
  2. −5
  3. +31
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

B. Net production minus clearance is 13−18=−5 units/day.

45. Part C — A D-negative person lacks anti-D before exposure. What does this prove about later exposure?

  1. Permanent tolerance
  2. Nothing about future sensitisation
  3. D antigen is carbohydrate
  4. All Rh antibodies are absent forever
Answer and explanation

B. A primary alloimmune response may develop after D-positive exposure.

46. SET / integrated practice — Assertion: AB individuals are always compatible with any red-cell donor. Reason: Their ordinary plasma lacks anti-A and anti-B.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

C. ABO compatibility is not whole-system compatibility; other antibodies may be present.

47. SET / integrated practice — Assertion: A and B are codominant. Reason: Their transferases can both act on separate H acceptors in one individual.

  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false
  4. Reason false

48. SET / integrated practice — Assertion: Anti-D prophylaxis is active vaccination. Reason: Administered anti-D is passively supplied antibody.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

C. Passive anti-D helps prevent sensitisation; it is not an active vaccine.

49. SET / integrated practice — A positive direct antiglobulin test alone proves which statement?

  1. Erythrocyte-associated globulin or complement was detected
  2. Exact haemolysis rate known
  3. ABO mismatch proven
  4. No red cells were coated
Answer and explanation

A. Coating is measured; the cause and rate of destruction require additional evidence.

50. SET / integrated practice — An antigen-positive test cell yields a negative antibody reaction. What is an appropriate analytical limitation?

  1. Antibody may be below detection or absent
  2. All antigens must be absent
  3. The donor genotype is proven ii
  4. Only haemoglobin was measured
Answer and explanation

  3. Platelet count only
  4. RhD test alone

52. SET / integrated practice — A group O mother has an A newborn with IgG-mediated haemolysis. Is prior transfusion required by the ABO mechanism?

  1. Yes, always
  2. No
  3. Only if the infant is D-negative
  4. Only after two births

B. Naturally occurring ABO-reactive IgG may exist before pregnancy.

53. SET / integrated practice — A graph shows a faster, larger antibody response at second exposure. Which explanation fits?

  1. Immune memory
  2. Mature red-cell gene transcription
  3. Fibrin activation only
  4. Loss of all B cells
Answer and explanation

A. Memory cells support an anamnestic response to the antigen.

  1. Cell-free haemoglobin increases
  2. D allele changes
  3. FUT1 expression always stops
  4. A antibodies turn into B antigens

Revision and mastery

Follow antigen synthesis, recognition, antibody effector function and red-cell survival as distinct stages. Remember the conditions of the ordinary genotype and compatibility models: functional H, known red-cell population, typical adult antibody pattern and defined antigen systems.

High-yield relationships: FUT1 → H; A transferase → GalNAc; B transferase → galactose; IgM → strong direct agglutination; maternal IgG → placental transfer; immune memory → possible delayed response. For Hardy–Weinberg analysis, p+q+r=1 and ordinary O frequency=r².

  5. Explain why hh masks both A and B in the simplified model.
