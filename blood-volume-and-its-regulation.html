1. Why a circulating volume needs regulation

A multicellular animal cannot rely on diffusion alone to carry enough oxygen from its respiratory surface to every active cell. Diffusion becomes progressively slower as transport distance increases. A circulating fluid solves this problem by moving dissolved substances and specialised transport cells through an extensive vascular network. Yet this solution creates another problem: there must be enough fluid inside the vascular network to maintain flow when the animal changes posture, exercises, loses water, eats a salty meal or suffers an injury. A heart with a normal electrical rhythm cannot sustain adequate cardiac output if too little blood returns to fill its chambers. Conversely, a large volume of retained fluid is not necessarily useful if it accumulates outside the vessels. Blood volume regulation is therefore a problem of distribution, composition and feedback as well as total quantity.

Blood contains an aqueous phase, plasma, and formed elements. Erythrocytes contribute most of the volume occupied by the formed elements. Leukocytes and platelets are physiologically indispensable but make a small contribution to normal total blood volume. For routine physiological calculations, total blood volume is treated as the sum of plasma volume and red cell volume. Red cell mass, the total quantity of erythrocytes, must be distinguished from their concentration in a sample. When plasma water leaves the circulation but erythrocytes stay behind, the cell concentration rises even though no additional erythrocytes have been made. This distinction is central to understanding every later section of this chapter.

Volume helps determine the extent to which the circulatory system is filled. A significant fraction of blood resides in the compliant systemic venous circulation. Compliance means that a change in pressure can accommodate a change in volume; a highly compliant vessel can receive considerable volume without a large rise in pressure. The veins are consequently a variable reservoir, not merely passive return pipes. Sympathetic activity can alter the distribution of blood within this reservoir, changing cardiac filling even if total blood volume is unchanged. Changes in arterial pressure can therefore be rapid and need not imply an equally rapid change in blood quantity. Detailed pressure–flow relationships belong to cardiovascular physiology, but the distinction between filling and flow is needed here.

A stable blood volume does not mean that each water molecule stays in the circulation. Water crosses capillaries, enters cells, passes through lymphatics, is filtered by the kidneys, and returns from the gut. A stable volume represents a dynamic balance between flows. The body cannot simply close every exit from its vascular compartment: exchanges are required for nutrient delivery and waste removal. Instead, the rate of loss, replacement and redistribution is adjusted by a set of linked physical and biological processes. Short-term responses preserve circulation while slower responses replace salt, water and red cells. The mechanisms differ in their time courses and in the quantity they control.

2. Fluid compartments and the accounting of water

Total body water includes the water within cells and the water outside them. The intracellular fluid compartment, abbreviated ICF, is separated from extracellular fluid, abbreviated ECF, by cell membranes. The ECF includes plasma water, interstitial fluid between cells, and smaller specialised fluid spaces. Blood volume is not identical to ECF volume because erythrocytes contain intracellular water. Nor does the volume of plasma precisely equal the volume of plasma water: plasma contains proteins and other solutes. In simplified examination calculations these distinctions are often neglected, but they matter when deciding which marker measures which compartment.

For an illustrative adult of 70 kilograms with total body water equal to 60 percent of body mass, total body water is about 42 litres. A conventional model assigns two thirds, or 28 litres, to intracellular water and one third, or 14 litres, to extracellular fluid. If roughly one quarter of ECF is represented by plasma, its volume would be about 3.5 litres. These are deliberately rounded model values rather than a universal anatomical standard. Body composition alters the fractions because adipose tissue contains less water per unit mass than many lean tissues. Age, pregnancy, training and species differences also alter normal volumes. A student should use the values specified by a problem rather than combine numbers taken from incompatible reference models.

The boundary between intracellular and extracellular fluid has different selective properties from the boundary between plasma and interstitial fluid. Cell membranes restrict many dissolved particles and use transport proteins to establish different ionic compositions. Extracellular fluid is rich in sodium salts; intracellular fluid is rich in potassium salts and contains many negatively charged macromolecules. Water can move across many cell membranes much more readily than an effective extracellular osmole can cross them. The resulting movement of water tends to reduce the difference in effective osmotic concentration across the membrane. The sodium–potassium pump helps preserve the distribution of ions; it does not directly pump water into cells.

Osmolality measures the number of osmotically active particles per kilogram of solvent; osmolarity is the concentration per litre of solution. In dilute biological fluids their numerical values are often similar but the definitions are not interchangeable. Tonicity describes the effect of a solution on equilibrium cell volume and depends mainly on solutes that do not readily equilibrate across the relevant membrane during the observation. Urea can contribute to measured osmolality without having the same sustained influence on cell volume as extracellular sodium salts. A question asking whether an erythrocyte shrinks therefore requires information about membrane permeability, not merely a numerical osmolarity label.

Consider an idealised body with 28 litres of ICF and 14 litres of ECF, each initially at 300 mOsm/L. The total number of osmoles is 12,600 mOsm. If one litre of pure water is lost and solute quantities remain constant, the final equilibrium concentration is 12,600 divided by 41, approximately 307.3 mOsm/L. Intracellular osmoles are initially 8,400 mOsm, giving a final intracellular volume of about 27.33 litres. Extracellular osmoles are initially 4,200 mOsm, giving a final extracellular volume of about 13.67 litres. The lost litre has ultimately been shared by both compartments, even if it was initially removed from extracellular fluid. The calculation assumes ideal behaviour, equal final effective osmotic concentrations, negligible nonaqueous volume and no hormonal compensation during equilibration.

By contrast, removal of one litre of isotonic extracellular fluid removes both water and a proportional quantity of extracellular osmoles. In the same ideal model, final ECF volume becomes 13 litres but remains at 300 mOsm/L, so there is no sustained osmotic reason for ICF to change. This comparison is powerful because both disturbances decrease total body water by the same amount but their distribution and sensor signals differ. Water loss increases osmolality and activates osmoregulatory responses. Isotonic ECF loss can activate pressure and volume-related responses even if osmolality remains almost constant. Blood loss is a further distinct case because it removes red cells as well as plasma.

An increase in body sodium content generally favours expansion of extracellular fluid when water is available to accompany retained solute. Plasma sodium concentration, however, reflects a ratio between exchangeable solute and water rather than a direct inventory of sodium. A person or animal can retain both sodium and water and have a nearly normal plasma sodium concentration. Likewise, an increase in concentration during dehydration does not establish that sodium content increased. The kidney must regulate both water and sodium excretion, with partly separate signals for each task.

3. Haematocrit and the components of blood volume

Haematocrit, represented by H, is the volume fraction of a blood sample occupied by erythrocytes. A reported haematocrit of 45 percent is expressed as 0.45 in equations. Centrifuging an anticoagulated sample produces packed erythrocytes at the bottom, a thin buffy coat of leukocytes and platelets, and plasma above. A centrifuged packed cell volume may include a little trapped plasma. Automated methods often calculate haematocrit using the number and mean volume of erythrocytes. Both measurements are properties of the sample, and neither alone measures the amount of blood in the entire animal.

Let B denote total blood volume, P plasma volume and R red cell volume, all measured in the same units. Neglecting the small contribution from other formed elements, B = P + R and H = R/B. Substitution gives P = B(1 − H), B = P/(1 − H), and R = PH/(1 − H). These equations are algebraic consequences of the definitions, not independent physiological laws. They hold if H represents the relevant whole-body fraction and if the measurement of P and H refers to the same physiological state. A venous sample may differ from the whole-body haematocrit because erythrocytes and plasma are distributed differently through vessels of different sizes. An experimentally appropriate correction may therefore be needed. No single correction factor should be imposed on every species or method.

Suppose an indicator measures 2.75 L of plasma and the problem explicitly supplies a representative whole-body haematocrit of 0.45. Then B = 2.75/0.55 = 5.00 L and R = 5.00 × 0.45 = 2.25 L. A student can independently verify the calculation by adding the plasma and cell volumes. If haematocrit were entered as 45 rather than 0.45, the calculation would produce a nonsensical negative denominator. The dimensions are also useful: the denominator is a dimensionless fraction, so the result retains litres.

Now compare two animals with haematocrit 0.40. One has blood volume 5 L, the other 3 L. Their erythrocyte volumes are respectively 2 L and 1.2 L. A single concentration measurement cannot discriminate between these states. Conversely, the same 2 L erythrocyte volume yields haematocrit 0.40 at a total blood volume of 5 L and 0.50 at a total blood volume of 4 L. Relative erythrocytosis means that the sample shows a high erythrocyte concentration because plasma volume has fallen, not necessarily that total red cell volume increased. Dilution can produce a lower concentration with an unchanged cell inventory.

In small vessels, erythrocytes tend to migrate towards the centre of the flow, leaving a relatively cell-poor layer near the wall. This arrangement contributes to differences between microvascular and large-vessel haematocrit. Branching vessels can distribute plasma and cells unequally. These facts explain why the sample fraction is not automatically the average for every blood-containing space. They also show why plasma and erythrocyte volumes measured independently are valuable when precise component estimates are needed. The detailed hydrodynamics of small vessels will be considered with circulation rather than developed here.

4. Indicator dilution: from conservation to measurement

The simplest strategy for measuring an inaccessible volume is to distribute a known amount of a detectable substance through it and measure its final concentration. A container of unknown volume can be estimated this way without measuring its dimensions. Blood vessels, however, are not rigid containers. Their contents are in motion and their walls permit exchange. The chosen indicator must remain sufficiently confined to the target compartment over the measurement interval, mix adequately, and be measurable with a known relation between signal and concentration. These requirements explain why no single indicator can measure all body-fluid spaces.

Let Q be the net amount of indicator present in a compartment, C its uniform concentration above baseline and V the accessible volume. Conservation gives Q = CV, hence V = Q/C. If Q is in milligrams and C in milligrams per litre, V is in litres. If some indicator has already been lost, Q is the amount introduced minus the quantity removed, not automatically the injected dose. If an endogenous substance or background signal is present, the indicator-specific increase must be isolated. The derivation assumes a well-mixed accessible volume that does not change appreciably during measurement. A measured dilution space is always defined by the behaviour of the tracer on the selected timescale.

A plasma-associated indicator such as labelled albumin is useful because albumin is largely retained intravascularly over a short interval, although it is not permanently confined. Labelled autologous erythrocytes can measure red cell volume when the label remains cell-associated and the cells distribute normally. Conceptually, total body water is measured with a marker that exchanges with intracellular and extracellular water, and ECF is measured with a marker that accesses extracellular spaces but not most cells. Subtraction can estimate ICF or interstitial volume when the input measurements are compatible. No marker produces a perfectly geometric compartment boundary under all conditions. Some extracellular spaces are slowly accessible; labelled water can exchange with nonaqueous hydrogen; plasma labels can escape into tissues. These limitations must be considered before comparing dilution volumes.

Take a hypothetical plasma tracer dose of 20 mg. After mixing, the tracer concentration above baseline is 8 mg/L. Assuming negligible loss, P = 20/8 = 2.5 L. If 2 mg had left the measured plasma compartment before sampling but the same concentration were observed, the corrected amount would be 18 mg and the corrected volume 2.25 L. Using the uncorrected dose would overestimate the volume. The direction of this error follows from conservation rather than memorisation: concentration is lower than it would have been if all the injected indicator had remained in the compartment. Incomplete mixing has no universal bias because the sample may come from a locally indicator-rich or indicator-poor region.

An early sample can have a very high concentration if it is collected near a site where the indicator has not yet dispersed. A very late sample can show a low concentration because tracer leaves plasma. Serial observations can help distinguish an initial mixing phase from a later loss phase. When the post-mixing loss is approximately proportional to the remaining tracer, the resulting concentration follows C(t) = C0 exp(−kt), where k is the fractional disappearance constant in inverse time and C0 is the extrapolated initial mixed concentration. Plotting the natural logarithm of concentration against time yields a straight line for this ideal model. Extrapolating the fitted line to zero time can estimate C0, provided that the model and sampled interval are appropriate. This method does not mean that the indicator actually mixed instantly at the time of administration.

For example, suppose an illustrative post-mixing dataset gives 10 mg/L at time zero by extrapolation, 8 mg/L after one chosen interval, and 6.4 mg/L after two intervals. The ratio between consecutive observations is 0.8, consistent with exponential loss. With a net initial dose of 30 mg, plasma volume is 3 L. Using the last sample without correcting loss gives 4.6875 L, an overestimate of 1.6875 L. If concentration does not decline as a single exponential, a single straight-line fit to all samples may not be defensible. Multi-compartment exchange, sampling artefacts and changes in volume can produce curved or irregular plots. The purpose of the calculation is to understand the physical assumptions, not to prescribe a clinical measurement protocol.

5. Capillary exchange: pressure, protein and the barrier

A vascular compartment can lose water to the tissues even without external bleeding. The capillary wall offers a selective path for water and small solutes, while restricting many macromolecules. Hydrostatic pressure is the mechanical pressure of a fluid. Capillary hydrostatic pressure tends to push fluid from the lumen into the tissue if it exceeds interstitial pressure. Colloid osmotic pressure, also called oncotic pressure, describes the effective osmotic pressure produced by retained macromolecules such as plasma proteins. Albumin contributes substantially to plasma oncotic pressure because of its abundance and molecular properties. The balance of forces depends on both sides of the effective barrier, not solely on the amount of albumin circulating in blood.

The classical Starling formulation describes volume flux as Jv = Kf[(Pc − Pi) − sigma(πc − πi)]. Pc and Pi denote capillary and interstitial hydrostatic pressures. πc and πi denote capillary and interstitial colloid osmotic pressures, and sigma is the reflection coefficient for the relevant macromolecular solute. Kf combines hydraulic conductance with available exchange area. If pressure is expressed in mmHg and Kf in mL per minute per mmHg, Jv is in mL per minute. A positive result denotes outward filtration under the chosen sign convention. Sigma is dimensionless; an ideal fully reflecting barrier gives a value of one, whereas a freely permeable solute gives a value close to zero. This coefficient is solute- and barrier-dependent.

Suppose an illustrative classical model has Pc = 25, Pi = 0, πc = 25 and πi = 5 mmHg, with sigma = 1 and Kf = 0.2 mL/min/mmHg. Net filtration pressure is 25 − 20 = 5 mmHg, and flux is 1 mL/min. If capillary hydrostatic pressure rises to 30 mmHg with all other quantities held fixed, flux becomes 2 mL/min. If plasma oncotic pressure instead falls to 20 mmHg at the original hydrostatic pressure, flux also becomes 2 mL/min. The two disturbances yield the same flux in this simplified snapshot but have different biological causes. Equal numerical outputs do not establish that the underlying diseases or physiological changes are equivalent.

For much of the twentieth century, textbook diagrams commonly depicted filtration at the arterial end of a capillary and reabsorption at its venous end. This interpretation assumes that the outward hydrostatic force decreases along the capillary while the inward oncotic force remains high enough to reverse the direction of net flow. It is a useful starting model for understanding the competing forces, but it is not a universal description of steady-state fluid exchange. Experimental measurements at controlled microvascular pressures showed that a decrease in capillary pressure could cause absorption initially, with absorption subsequently diminishing. This time dependence demanded an explanation beyond a fixed bulk interstitial oncotic pressure. Levick and Michel reviewed the evidence and the revised interpretation in 2010.

The endothelial glycocalyx is a carbohydrate-rich layer on the luminal surface of endothelial cells. It contains membrane-associated proteoglycans, glycoproteins and associated molecules, and is involved in the restriction of macromolecular transport. In the revised Starling framework, the effective oncotic gradient is developed between plasma and the fluid immediately beneath the glycocalyx rather than simply between plasma and bulk interstitial fluid. In a simplified expression πi is replaced with πg, the subglycocalyx oncotic pressure. This local protein concentration can differ greatly from the surrounding interstitial protein concentration because flow through the narrow exchange pathway changes solute delivery and clearance. The model retains hydrostatic and oncotic forces but identifies a more appropriate location for the effective osmotic difference.

During outward filtration, relatively protein-poor filtrate can maintain a low protein concentration in the subglycocalyx region. The oncotic difference across the glycocalyx then opposes filtration. When the hydrostatic pressure falls enough for inward water flow, proteins may accumulate beneath the glycocalyx and diminish the effective oncotic gradient. The driving force for absorption consequently decreases as the local composition adjusts. In many continuous systemic capillary beds, steady-state exchange is a small net filtration rather than sustained venous reabsorption. A transient inward shift after an abrupt pressure fall is entirely compatible with this framework. Certain specialised absorbing vascular beds, including those in the kidney and intestinal mucosa, have distinct boundary conditions and can sustain absorption. Neither the classical diagram nor a blanket assertion that capillaries never absorb is an adequate generalisation.

Experimental reasoning can distinguish these models. If a vessel is held at a lower pressure and flux is recorded only immediately after the change, absorption may be observed. Repeated measurements after a stable interval test whether the absorption persists. An independent manipulation of bulk interstitial protein concentration can test how strongly it determines the effective oncotic gradient. A careful interpretation requires separating permeability changes from changes in hydrostatic pressure and controlling the time allowed for local solute distributions to adjust. A decrease in sustained absorption does not imply that proteins exert no osmotic pressure. Rather, the relevant protein concentration difference has changed. The cited reviews discuss experiments in isolated microvessels and the limitations of applying one model to every organ.

6. Lymphatic return and tissue fluid balance

The outward filtration of water and small dissolved substances does not normally cause indefinite tissue swelling. Lymphatic vessels collect extracellular fluid, including proteins that have escaped the blood vasculature, and return it to the venous circulation. Initial lymphatics have specialised endothelial junctions that can admit interstitial fluid when mechanical conditions favour entry. Collecting vessels possess valves and contractile elements; surrounding skeletal muscle and pressure changes associated with movement also help propel lymph. Valves reduce reverse flow. These features make lymphatic transport responsive to tissue mechanics rather than merely a passive drainage pipe.

The rate of interstitial fluid accumulation is approximately the difference between the rate of microvascular filtration and lymphatic removal, provided other local fluid sources and sinks are negligible. If both are 2 mL/min in an illustrative tissue, interstitial volume is stable even though fluid continuously passes through it. If filtration rises to 3 mL/min while lymphatic removal remains 2 mL/min, the initial accumulation is 1 mL/min. Multiplying by time gives the volume increase only if the rates remain fixed. Living tissues change pressure as they swell, and lymph flow may increase as interstitial pressure rises. A constant-rate calculation should therefore be described as a short-interval approximation rather than a prediction of indefinitely linear swelling.

Oedema is an excessive accumulation of interstitial fluid. It may arise through increased capillary pressure, reduced effective oncotic opposition to filtration, increased permeability, increased exchange area, impaired lymphatic transport, or a combination of these. An obstructed lymphatic vessel can allow interstitial proteins to accumulate, altering local exchange conditions. A rise in venous pressure can increase upstream capillary hydrostatic pressure even if plasma protein concentrations are unchanged. Loss of plasma albumin can reduce oncotic opposition to filtration. In inflammatory conditions, altered endothelial barrier properties may increase water and protein escape. Each mechanism changes a different term or boundary condition in the exchange problem.

The presence of oedema cannot establish that arterial filling is adequate. An animal may retain extracellular salt and water while having impaired effective perfusion because its heart cannot pump effectively or because vascular distribution is abnormal. Signals that favour sodium retention may therefore remain active despite excess fluid in tissues. This apparently paradoxical combination reveals the difference between actual fluid volume and the effectiveness with which blood fills and perfuses the arterial circulation. Conversely, a temporary shift of fluid from the vasculature to tissues during standing need not represent loss of water from the body. Distribution can change while total body water remains the same.

7. Sensory systems: effective arterial filling and osmolality

No single receptor counts the litres of blood present in an animal. Physiological control systems infer the need for adjustment from variables related to perfusion and composition. Arterial baroreceptors respond to deformation of the vessel wall. Stretch-sensitive endings in the carotid sinus and aortic arch convey information to brainstem circuits. Cardiopulmonary receptors respond to filling-related stretch in low-pressure regions of the circulation. Renal mechanisms respond to perfusion pressure, tubular sodium chloride delivery and neural input. The signals are complementary, not identical. Their outputs depend on vascular pressure, compliance, cardiac function and local flow as well as circulating quantity.

When arterial stretch declines, baroreceptor firing generally decreases. Brainstem reflex circuits adjust autonomic output: sympathetic activity to the heart and many vessels increases, while cardiac parasympathetic influence decreases. Increased heart rate and contractility can help maintain cardiac output; arteriolar constriction supports arterial pressure and venoconstriction shifts blood towards the central circulation. These responses occur rapidly and redistribute or use the remaining blood more effectively, but they cannot manufacture new plasma or red cells. Their action is constrained by the magnitude of volume loss and by the condition of the heart and vascular network.

Osmoregulatory neurons respond to changes in the effective osmotic concentration of the extracellular environment. Regions including the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and subfornical organ participate in sensing and integration, with connections to hypothalamic circuits controlling thirst and vasopressin. Effective hypertonicity removes water from osmosensitive cells and alters neuronal signalling. Drinking water and retaining water through the kidneys tend to reverse the stimulus. In contrast, a loss of isotonic blood can lower arterial filling substantially without a large primary increase in extracellular osmolality. A question comparing these disturbances must therefore identify which feedback system is being stimulated.

The two types of sensing interact. A severe reduction in arterial filling can stimulate vasopressin release even when osmotic conditions alone would favour suppression. Preserving perfusion may take priority over the immediate correction of osmolality. Thus a high vasopressin concentration cannot prove that plasma is hyperosmotic. Likewise, a low arterial pressure does not prove that blood volume is low: it may reflect reduced vascular resistance or poor cardiac pumping. A physiological prediction should trace the specific disturbance through its effects on the relevant sensors before assigning changes to hormones.

8. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system

The kidney is a major effector of long-term extracellular fluid balance. When sodium excretion is smaller than sodium intake, body sodium content increases. If water accompanies the retained sodium, ECF tends to expand. When excretion exceeds intake, ECF tends to contract. A kidney cannot generate new water or sodium, but it can alter the fraction of filtered material that is returned to blood, making urinary loss variable. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, or RAAS, is one important link between perfusion-related signals and renal retention.

Renin is a proteolytic enzyme released by specialised granular cells associated with the renal afferent arteriole. Major signals promoting release include reduced renal perfusion pressure, reduced sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa region and increased sympathetic signalling through beta-1 adrenergic receptors. The macula densa signal reflects salt delivery in tubular fluid and should not be confused with direct measurement of total body sodium. Altered filtration or upstream reabsorption can modify tubular delivery. These input pathways make renin secretion responsive to several aspects of renal circulatory function.

Renin cleaves circulating angiotensinogen, produced predominantly by the liver, to form angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), present on vascular endothelium in many tissues, converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Although the pulmonary vascular bed is an important site of conversion, ACE is not restricted to the lung. Angiotensin II acts through receptors, especially AT1 receptors for many of its classical pressure and volume effects. It can constrict blood vessels, stimulate thirst and vasopressin-related responses, enhance renal sodium reabsorption and promote aldosterone secretion by the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex. The effects operate in parallel and contribute differently to the time course of compensation.

Aldosterone is a steroid hormone that binds intracellular mineralocorticoid receptors in target epithelia, particularly cells of the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron. Its actions increase the capacity for sodium uptake across the apical membrane, including through epithelial sodium channels (ENaC), and support basolateral sodium extrusion by Na+/K+-ATPase. The electrochemical conditions associated with sodium transport and other aldosterone effects can favour potassium secretion. Because aldosterone primarily regulates sodium handling rather than directly installing water channels, its effect on water retention depends on osmotic conditions, water availability and the permeability of the relevant nephron segments. A common examination error is to describe aldosterone and vasopressin as interchangeable antidiuretic hormones.

The cascade can close a negative feedback loop. Improved renal perfusion, changes in distal salt delivery and the direct feedback effects of angiotensin II reduce the need for continued renin release. When extracellular expansion increases pressure and sodium excretion, additional compensatory mechanisms favour loss rather than indefinite retention. Aldosterone is also regulated by extracellular potassium, so an elevated hormone concentration is not unique evidence of reduced blood volume. The physiological meaning of any single measurement depends on the accompanying perturbation and the other available signals.

9. Vasopressin, thirst and water permeability

Vasopressin, also known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH), is a small peptide synthesised by hypothalamic neurons, including neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. It is transported along axons and released into the blood from the posterior pituitary. The release site is not the primary synthesis site. Raised effective plasma osmolality is a strong stimulus for secretion; reduced effective arterial filling also stimulates it, particularly during substantial circulatory disturbance. Water intake and a decline in effective osmolality generally reduce secretion.

In renal collecting-duct principal cells, vasopressin acts on basolateral V2 receptors. Receptor activation stimulates a Gs-linked signalling pathway involving adenylyl cyclase, cyclic AMP and protein kinase A. This promotes the accumulation of aquaporin-2 channels at the apical membrane, including insertion from intracellular vesicle pools. Water can then enter the cell from tubular fluid and leave through basolateral water channels into the surrounding interstitium. The channels facilitate movement down an osmotic gradient; they do not consume ATP to pump water uphill. An appropriate medullary osmotic gradient is essential for substantial concentrating capacity. The renal mechanisms establishing that gradient belong to the excretory physiology subunit.

Consider two isolated collecting-duct epithelia exposed to identical osmotic gradients. One is exposed to vasopressin and has functional V2 receptors and aquaporin-2 trafficking; the other has a defect in V2 signalling. An increase in transepithelial water movement is expected mainly in the first. If the gradient is removed, raising apical water permeability alone cannot produce sustained net water movement against equilibrium. This thought experiment separates a change in the availability of a pathway from a change in its driving force. The same reasoning applies to membrane transport generally: permeability and gradient are separate variables.

Thirst complements renal retention by promoting water intake. It cannot be replaced completely by reducing urine volume because respiratory and skin water losses continue even when urine is highly concentrated. Drinking also changes behaviour before the whole-body osmotic disturbance has necessarily equilibrated, through sensory signals associated with the mouth, gut and circulation. As absorbed water distributes between ECF and ICF, osmolality falls and further osmotic stimulation of vasopressin and thirst diminishes. A negative feedback loop should be drawn from disturbance to sensor, controller and effector, then back to the original variable.

10. Atrial natriuretic peptide and the opposition to retention

The heart itself participates in endocrine control. Increased atrial wall stretch promotes the release of atrial natriuretic peptide, ANP, from atrial cardiomyocytes. ANP acts through receptors that generate cyclic GMP and favours increased sodium excretion, known as natriuresis. It also opposes components of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and may modify renal haemodynamics and tubular reabsorption. Water excretion can accompany sodium excretion. The result is an integrated tendency to reduce an excessive extracellular volume load rather than preserve it.

Atrial stretch is influenced by filling and pressure; it is not a direct measure of total body fluid. In a simplified healthy circulation, isotonic volume expansion raises venous return and atrial stretch, favouring ANP release. Renin and aldosterone tend to decline and sodium excretion tends to increase. If water alone is added, the fall in osmolality suppresses vasopressin even before all aspects of circulatory filling have changed. The response to water loading and isotonic salt loading thus overlaps but is not identical. The physiological response must be inferred from the composition and distribution of the disturbance.

An instructive experimental approach is to compare urinary sodium excretion in matched animals given a control solution or an atrial peptide under otherwise similar conditions. Changes in filtration and tubular transport should be considered separately, because greater sodium excretion can arise from an increase in filtered sodium, a reduction in its fractional reabsorption, or both. Early studies of atrial peptides established a natriuretic effect and later work linked it to endocrine signalling. An increase in urinary sodium after peptide exposure supports a causal effect under the experimental conditions, but does not by itself identify the exact nephron site or every intermediate mechanism. Independent receptor and transport evidence is needed for that stronger conclusion.

11. Haemorrhage and the sequence of recovery

Acute haemorrhage removes whole blood from the vascular compartment. If plasma and erythrocytes are lost in the same proportion as in the circulation, the initial haematocrit need not change. Suppose an animal begins with 5 L of blood and H = 0.40. Its red cell volume is 2 L and plasma volume is 3 L. Loss of 1 L of well-mixed blood removes 0.4 L of red cells and 0.6 L of plasma. The remaining volumes are 1.6 L and 2.4 L, respectively. The new haematocrit is 1.6/4.0 = 0.40. A normal-looking haematocrit therefore does not exclude a major early reduction in blood quantity.

Reduced venous return tends to reduce cardiac filling and stroke volume. Reflex sympathetic activation increases heart rate, adjusts vascular resistance and redistributes venous blood. Hormonal signals promoting sodium and water conservation increase, and thirst may be stimulated. A decrease in capillary hydrostatic pressure can reduce outward filtration and can produce a transient inward movement of interstitial fluid. Ongoing lymphatic return also contributes to fluid redistribution. The available interstitial fluid is not equivalent to new blood cells, so a restoration of plasma volume cannot restore the erythrocyte mass immediately.

Extend the illustrative example by adding 1 L of cell-free fluid to the vascular compartment, without additional cell production or loss. The total volume again becomes 5 L, but red cell volume remains 1.6 L. Haematocrit falls to 0.32. This decline is not evidence of an additional haemorrhage in the model; it is the predicted effect of dilution during restoration of volume. In an actual animal, fluid is distributed among compartments and the exact changes depend on time, intake, renal handling and vascular exchange. The example isolates the accounting principle and should not be interpreted as a treatment instruction.

Recovery of erythrocyte mass requires production of new cells. Reduced oxygen delivery may reduce oxygen tension in renal interstitial regions. Under low oxygen conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor signalling, especially HIF-2 in renal erythropoietin-producing cells, promotes erythropoietin expression. Erythropoietin supports survival and differentiation of erythroid progenitors in marrow. This process requires adequate substrates such as iron and functioning marrow. Reticulocyte production increases over a period of days rather than seconds, and recovery of a depleted erythrocyte population takes longer. Erythropoietin is consequently a regulator of oxygen-carrying cell mass, not an immediate mechanism for restoring plasma water.

An important analytical distinction is that blood flow and oxygen content jointly determine oxygen delivery. A reduction in circulating volume can decrease flow, while a reduction in erythrocyte mass can lower oxygen-carrying capacity even after volume has been restored. Two animals with the same measured blood pressure may therefore differ in their tissue oxygen delivery. The compensation that maintains pressure is not sufficient proof of complete recovery. This distinction connects blood volume regulation to the study of cardiac output, respiratory gas transport and erythrocyte physiology without requiring the detailed equations of those neighbouring chapters.

12. Landmark evidence: testing the endocrine role of the heart and the exchange barrier

In the early development of cardiovascular physiology, the heart was primarily understood as a muscular pump. A distinct question arose when researchers found secretory features in atrial cells: could the heart release a substance that altered renal excretion? De Bold and colleagues tested the hypothesis in rats in 1981. They compared the renal response following administration of extracts prepared from atrial tissue with responses to ventricular tissue extract and to the preparation medium. The atrial preparation produced a marked increase in sodium and water excretion; the comparison preparations did not produce the same response. The experimental contrast was important because it connected the response specifically to the atrial preparation rather than simply to administration of fluid or cardiac tissue in general. This study is a landmark in the development of the concept of cardiac endocrine control.

The result did not, by itself, establish the precise chemical identity of the active substance or demonstrate that every effect was mediated by a single purified peptide. A tissue extract contains many molecules. Further biochemical and physiological work was required to isolate and characterise the active natriuretic factor, demonstrate its signalling properties, and establish how its release varies during normal changes in atrial filling. The distinction between demonstrating a biological activity and identifying its molecular mediator is central to experimental reasoning. An examination question based on the original experiment could reasonably ask what the control comparisons establish, but should not infer the entire modern ANP receptor pathway from the 1981 experiment alone.

The significance of atrial natriuretic signalling becomes clearer when compared with renin-mediated responses. A volume-related endocrine signal can originate outside the kidney and exert effects on renal handling. The heart thereby participates in the regulation of its own filling conditions through communication with another organ. Increased atrial stretch can signal that an expansion should be countered, while reduced renal perfusion can signal that retention is needed. These signals are not necessarily mutually exclusive in every pathological or experimental circumstance. An animal with impaired cardiac performance can have increased atrial pressure together with reduced effective arterial perfusion. A measured increase in both opposing endocrine systems is then understandable from their distinct sensing sites.

A second landmark question concerned the effective osmotic barrier of the capillary. In the classical model, increasing protein concentration in the bulk tissue fluid should substantially reduce the oncotic difference opposing filtration, provided other terms remain unchanged. The glycocalyx model predicts that the change in effective oncotic force may be much smaller because the protein concentration immediately beneath the selective barrier does not necessarily match the bulk interstitial value. Adamson and colleagues examined this problem in nonfenestrated rat microvessels in a study published in 2004. The evidence supported the idea that the selective endothelial surface layer and local protein concentration gradients are important determinants of the oncotic opposition to filtration. This is a test of competing physical descriptions, not a rejection of osmotic pressure as a biological force.

To understand the experimental logic, imagine two otherwise comparable vessels with the same intravascular pressure and albumin concentration. If the surrounding albumin concentration is increased in one, a model using the bulk extracellular concentration directly predicts a reduction in the opposing oncotic term. If the measured change is smaller than expected, it is appropriate to investigate whether the protein concentration adjacent to the effective barrier differs from the measured bulk concentration. It would be premature to infer from a single observation that the endothelial surface layer is the only possible explanation: changes in other barrier properties and uncertainties in the measurement also need consideration. Multiple complementary experiments and theoretical analyses support the revised interpretation. The simplified experiment described here is an explanatory reconstruction, not a detailed replication of the published methods.

These two historical examples share a useful feature. A conventional description was inadequate to explain a particular observation, leading to a testable alternative. The heart was found to have a secretory role in addition to mechanical pumping; the capillary wall was found to require a more spatially specific description of the osmotic gradient. In both cases, the revised view retains the useful parts of the earlier model. The heart still pumps, and capillary exchange still depends on hydrostatic and osmotic forces. Scientific progress does not require discarding a model wholesale whenever one of its assumptions fails. It requires identifying the assumption, testing its limitations and replacing it with a description that better explains the evidence.

A more quantitative approach to vasopressin response illustrates the same principle. Osmolar clearance is defined as the rate of solute excretion divided by plasma osmolar concentration. If urine flow is U in mL/min, urine osmolarity is Cu, and plasma osmolarity is Cp, then osmolar clearance is U × Cu/Cp in mL/min. The difference between urine flow and this clearance is conventional free-water clearance. In an illustrative system with urine flow 2 mL/min, urine osmolarity 600 mOsm/L and plasma osmolarity 300 mOsm/L, osmolar clearance is 4 mL/min and free-water clearance is −2 mL/min. A negative result indicates that the urine contains less water than would be required to excrete its solute load as an iso-osmotic solution. It is not a literal negative urine output. If urine instead has an osmolarity of 150 mOsm/L at a flow of 4 mL/min with the same plasma osmolarity, osmolar clearance is 2 mL/min and free-water clearance is +2 mL/min. The sign now indicates excretion of water in excess of that iso-osmotic reference. These calculations describe net renal handling; they do not directly measure vasopressin secretion or blood volume. The full physiology of urinary concentration is developed in 7.F, but this short quantitative bridge helps distinguish a hormone signal from its functional renal outcome.

13. Integration of disturbances across time

A change in posture redistributes blood before the kidney can change whole-body salt content. Water deprivation can increase osmolality before a large fall in arterial filling occurs. Isotonic volume loss can activate arterial and renal sensors with little initial change in osmolality. A large plasma protein loss can favour fluid filtration out of vessels even when the total quantity of extracellular water is high. Each case therefore begins with a different primary disturbance. The same hormones may appear in several responses, but the trigger, magnitude and time course need not be identical.

To analyse any examination problem, first specify the material moved: whole blood, cell-free isotonic fluid, water alone, or salt and water in a stated proportion. Next identify where the material moves and whether it leaves the body or is merely redistributed. Establish which quantities are conserved during the short observation interval. Calculate concentrations only after accounting for changes in both numerator and denominator. Predict effective osmotic gradients and changes in vascular filling separately. Then trace the likely responses of baroreceptors, osmoreceptors and renal sensors, followed by changes in sympathetic activity, RAAS, vasopressin and ANP. Finally distinguish the immediate physical redistribution from later excretion, intake and erythrocyte production. This sequence makes apparently complicated integrated physiology problems tractable without memorising a separate hormonal pattern for every possible scenario.

14. Quantitative discrimination between volume and concentration

Blood volume estimates are often interpreted alongside concentrations of haemoglobin, plasma protein and electrolytes. These measurements can be informative together because each is influenced by different processes. For a dissolved plasma constituent whose total intravascular amount remains fixed, concentration equals amount divided by plasma volume. Therefore its concentration increases as plasma volume decreases. For a substance distributed through both plasma and interstitial fluid, however, concentration depends on the wider accessible space and on ongoing exchange. Sodium concentration, in particular, cannot be treated as a simple indicator of plasma volume because sodium and water redistribute between extracellular regions. The conservation approach must specify the system boundary for every substance under consideration.

Consider a hypothetical plasma protein pool of 210 grams in 3 L of plasma, giving a concentration of 70 g/L. If 0.3 L of protein-free water leaves the vascular compartment while no protein leaves, the concentration increases to 210/2.7 = 77.8 g/L. If the barrier becomes permeable to protein and 21 g of protein also leaves, the concentration is 189/2.7 = 70 g/L. Identical protein concentrations can thus conceal different plasma volumes and different absolute amounts of circulating protein. This is a reason to avoid interpreting a stable concentration as proof of stable physiology. In real tissues protein synthesis, degradation and lymphatic return modify the pool, so the assumption of constant mass is most useful over appropriately short intervals.

A related principle applies to erythrocyte counts. If an animal has a red cell count of five million cells per microlitre and five litres of blood, multiplying these numbers requires converting litres to microlitres. One litre contains one million microlitres, so the illustrative total number of cells is 25 trillion. If plasma contraction reduces blood volume to four litres without cell loss, the concentration becomes 6.25 million per microlitre. No proliferation of erythroid progenitors is required for this rapid change. A comparison of red cell count, haematocrit and mean cell volume can help distinguish changes in cell number concentration from changes in cell size. If erythrocytes swell in a hypotonic extracellular environment, the haematocrit can rise even with a constant number of cells. A simple calculation that assumes fixed mean cell volume would miss this possibility.

When several markers are measured, an experimentally useful question is whether their changes are proportional. If the haematocrit rises along with haemoglobin and total plasma protein concentrations after short-term fluid loss, the pattern is consistent with haemoconcentration. The evidence is not conclusive on its own because each constituent can also be independently regulated. If erythrocyte concentration rises but plasma protein concentration does not, one should examine the time course, cellular mobilisation, protein escape and the possibility of changes in red cell size. The interpretation becomes stronger when direct measurements of the relevant compartments are available. A single variable rarely identifies a unique cause in a coupled physiological system.

15. Renal mass balance and feedback over longer intervals

The renal contribution to volume homeostasis can be examined by writing a whole-body sodium balance. The rate of change of exchangeable sodium equals dietary intake minus renal excretion and other losses, including those through the gastrointestinal tract and skin. In an animal with stable sodium content, total intake and total loss are equal on average over the relevant interval, not necessarily during every hour. Similarly, the rate of change of total body water equals absorbed water plus metabolic water minus urine, evaporative losses and other outputs. These equations do not specify how the kidney regulates transport; they define the net outcome that the regulatory system must achieve.

Take an illustrative animal receiving 150 mmol sodium each day. It loses 20 mmol through nonrenal routes and excretes 100 mmol in urine. Its net sodium balance is positive by 30 mmol per day. If this imbalance persisted for three days with identical inputs and losses, the net gain would be 90 mmol. It would be wrong to interpret that number directly as a 90 mmol/L rise in plasma sodium concentration. Some retained solute will distribute outside the plasma and water intake and excretion will change. If net retention is accompanied by proportionate water retention, extracellular volume can rise while sodium concentration remains near its initial value. This distinction between amount and concentration explains why natriuretic mechanisms are important for volume regulation even though osmoregulatory mechanisms can maintain concentration.

Renal sodium excretion is the filtered load minus the net quantity reabsorbed, ignoring the negligible role of tubular sodium secretion in a simple model. The filtered load is the glomerular filtration rate multiplied by the concentration of filterable sodium in plasma. For a hypothetical filtration rate of 0.1 L/min and a sodium concentration of 140 mmol/L, the filtered sodium load is 14 mmol/min. If 99 percent is reabsorbed, excretion is 0.14 mmol/min. If 98 percent is reabsorbed, excretion is 0.28 mmol/min. Thus a one-percentage-point fall in fractional reabsorption doubles the excretion in this example. A small change in tubular handling can have a substantial effect on net balance because most of the filtered sodium is ordinarily reclaimed.

This arithmetic illustrates a more general experimental problem: a higher urinary concentration does not prove a higher excretion rate. Urinary sodium excretion rate is urinary sodium concentration multiplied by urine flow rate. Suppose sample A contains 120 mmol/L sodium at a urine flow of 1 mL/min, while sample B contains 60 mmol/L at 3 mL/min. The rates are 0.12 and 0.18 mmol/min respectively. Despite its lower concentration, sample B represents faster sodium excretion. Similar reasoning applies to water output: a concentrated urine can have low volume while carrying a substantial solute load. Measuring both concentration and flow is essential for claims about renal balance.

The relationship between pressure and sodium excretion provides another feedback pathway. Increased renal perfusion pressure tends, under many conditions, to favour excretion of sodium and water. This phenomenon is called pressure natriuresis and diuresis. Over longer periods, an increase in extracellular volume and arterial pressure can therefore promote losses that oppose the initiating expansion. Hormonal activity, sympathetic input and renal function can shift the relationship, so one should not assume identical pressure-excretion curves under every condition. Nor should one infer that a sustained pressure increase is required for each normal adjustment of sodium intake; endocrine and renal mechanisms can adapt across varying circumstances.

When arterial filling is inadequate, renal sodium retention may improve filling if water is available and the circulatory pump and vascular network can use the additional fluid. In contrast, when pump performance is impaired, retaining fluid can increase venous pressures and tissue filtration without normalising effective arterial perfusion. The feedback signal may persist. The response is then understandable as a regulator acting on its sensed variable, even though the whole-body result includes excessive extracellular fluid. The concepts of feedback and compartment distribution must be used together rather than labelling each hormonal change as either intrinsically helpful or intrinsically harmful.

16. Testing causal links in hormonal regulation

Physiological experiments often distinguish a signal from the response by altering one component while keeping others comparable. For example, to test whether angiotensin II directly stimulates a target cell, an investigator could compare its response with and without a selective receptor inhibitor under matched extracellular conditions. A reduction in the response supports the involvement of the blocked receptor if target specificity, exposure and cell viability have been established. It does not demonstrate that angiotensin II is the only stimulus capable of causing the response. A second experiment changing the upstream production of angiotensin II would address a different question from receptor blockade. Both manipulations are needed to locate causality within the pathway rather than merely associate its components.

Consider a simplified hormone cascade in which a reduction in renal perfusion normally increases renin activity, angiotensin II and aldosterone. If ACE activity is selectively reduced, conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II through that route decreases. Loss of downstream negative feedback may increase renin activity even though the physiological effect of the cascade is diminished. Consequently, a high concentration of an upstream component is not proof of stronger downstream signalling. This is a common feature of negative-feedback systems. The prediction is conditional on the intervention, its selectivity and the other pathways remaining functional. Potassium-dependent aldosterone secretion, for instance, can persist independently of this renin-dependent sequence.

A comparable distinction applies to water conservation. If a hormone assay shows high vasopressin while the collecting duct remains poorly permeable to water, an impairment of the response pathway is one plausible explanation. The hormone may be high precisely because the original disturbance continues to stimulate secretion. A comparison of responses to a controlled hormone exposure in an isolated epithelium can help separate deficient hormone release from impaired target sensitivity. The osmotic gradient must also be controlled, since functional channels do not guarantee water movement when the gradient is absent. A complete inference combines hormonal input, receptor function, permeability and driving force rather than treating the endocrine measurement as a direct measure of urine concentration.

A useful design for examining ANP action compares an experimental group exposed to the peptide with a matched control and records both filtration and sodium excretion. If filtration remains unchanged but sodium excretion increases, the fractional amount reabsorbed must have declined under the simplified sodium balance model. If filtration increases alongside excretion, the effect on fractional reabsorption cannot be inferred without calculating filtered load. Adding a receptor-specific intervention can help distinguish direct receptor-mediated effects from nonspecific responses. A measurement of cGMP can establish activation of a signalling pathway, but it cannot independently prove that a particular transporter changed activity. Physiological evidence becomes more informative when the level of inference is matched to the measurement.

17. Reading dynamic compensation datasets

Suppose a teaching experiment follows three variables in a mammal before and after an isotonic blood loss: arterial pressure, haematocrit and reticulocyte fraction. Immediately after the loss, pressure falls, haematocrit remains similar to baseline and the reticulocyte fraction has not changed substantially. During subsequent fluid redistribution, arterial pressure may partly recover while haematocrit falls. Later, if erythropoietic activity increases, the reticulocyte fraction can rise before the total red cell volume returns to baseline. The pattern is consistent with rapid circulatory compensation, intermediate plasma replacement and slower cell production. These measurements reflect different aspects of recovery; no single one describes all three processes.

For an original hypothetical dataset, take baseline blood volume as 5 L with red cell volume 2 L. Immediately after loss of 20 percent of the well-mixed blood, the values are 4 L and 1.6 L. After partial plasma replacement, suppose blood volume is 4.6 L with unchanged red cell volume. Haematocrit is then approximately 0.348. After a later increase in red cell volume to 1.85 L, suppose blood volume is 4.95 L; haematocrit is approximately 0.374. The progression of haematocrit is not monotonic even though the total blood volume increases steadily after the initial loss. Graphing both component volumes and their ratio shows why a ratio alone cannot reveal the direction of the change in each component. These figures are teaching values, not observations from a published experiment.

One way to test the assumption of proportional initial loss is to obtain a direct component measurement before and shortly after the disturbance. If the measured haematocrit decreases immediately, possible explanations include nonuniform cell distribution, rapid fluid shifts or sampling from a compartment with a different cell fraction. The observation should not automatically be dismissed as incompatible with haemorrhage. The model of perfectly mixed blood, no redistribution and constant cell size is a limiting case. Actual samples take time to collect, and biological compensation can begin during the disturbance itself. Knowing when a sample was collected is therefore part of interpreting its meaning.

Compare the preceding sequence with a hypothetical water-deprivation study. Early in water deprivation, plasma and extracellular osmolality tend to rise; water shifts out of cells; vasopressin and thirst tend to rise; and urine flow can fall if renal concentrating mechanisms are intact. Haematocrit may increase through plasma contraction, although erythrocyte shrinkage and distribution complicate exact prediction. A lower urine flow does not stop evaporative losses. If the study continues, circulatory filling may decline and volume-related responses can join the initially osmotic response. The time course explains why studies of the same disturbance conducted at different stages may show different balances of hormonal activation.

Finally, compare isotonic extracellular expansion. A larger extracellular compartment can increase vascular filling, atrial stretch and capillary filtration. ANP may rise, renin may fall and sodium excretion can increase. If cell-free fluid expands plasma, haematocrit initially tends to fall. If the added fluid leaves plasma quickly for the interstitial compartment, the dilution may be less persistent. In an experiment, finding a low haematocrit, high ANP and increased sodium excretion would be compatible with expansion, but alternative explanations should still be considered. The combination of variables is more informative than any of them considered alone. Such comparative analyses are the basis of advanced physiological examination questions.

18. Measurement design: separating accessible spaces from anatomical spaces

The indicator dilution principle can be understood as a conservation experiment whose difficulty lies in defining the boundaries. When a marker enters plasma, it does not necessarily enter every vascular region at exactly the same speed. A poorly perfused region may receive marker more slowly than a region with high blood flow. If the interval is short, the experiment may underestimate the full volume that would ultimately become accessible. If the interval is very long, escape of marker from the intended compartment may instead exaggerate the measured volume. The best measurement interval represents a compromise between adequate mixing and sufficiently limited loss. Serial measurements are valuable because they permit an investigator to assess the plausibility of these assumptions rather than choosing a single sampling time blindly.

A useful distinction is between a marker that is attached to a physiological component and a marker that exchanges with water itself. A label associated with erythrocytes follows the distribution of labelled cells, assuming that the labelling does not alter their behaviour. A plasma-associated label follows the distribution of the substance carrying the label. A water marker, on the other hand, can cross cell membranes and therefore accesses a much larger space. These different accessible spaces are not competing estimates of a single volume. They answer different questions. In a comparative experiment, subtracting the water marker space from an erythrocyte marker space would not produce a meaningful compartment because the latter is not a subset of body water in a simple volumetric sense; erythrocyte volume includes water and cellular solids. Proper subtraction requires compatible definitions.

Consider two independently measured component volumes in an animal: labelled erythrocytes indicate 2.0 L of red cell volume and a plasma marker indicates 3.0 L of plasma volume. The calculated whole-body haematocrit is 2/5 = 0.40. A contemporaneous peripheral venous sample shows a haematocrit of 0.44. These measurements are not inherently contradictory. Their ratio, 0.40/0.44, is about 0.91 for this individual under these conditions. The whole-body value may differ because of microvascular plasma skimming and unequal cell distribution. If the venous value were used without correction, plasma volume of 3 L would give a total blood volume of 3/0.56 = 5.36 L, overestimating the independently measured value. The appropriate correction is an empirical property of the measurement system, not an arbitrary adjustment to force agreement.

The precision of a dilution experiment depends in part on the precision of dose and concentration measurements. If a dose is known very accurately but the measured concentration has appreciable uncertainty, the calculated volume inherits that uncertainty. Suppose a dose of 30 mg is distributed through plasma and the measured concentration is 10.0 mg/L with a plausible measurement range of 9.8 to 10.2 mg/L. Using the same dose, the corresponding estimates are approximately 3.061 and 2.941 L. The relationship is reciprocal, so the uncertainty range is not perfectly symmetric. A small concentration error near a low concentration can yield a large relative error if the signal approaches the detection limit. Choosing a suitably measurable indicator concentration is therefore part of experimental design, even though dosing procedures themselves are outside the scope of this chapter.

A further source of error is changing volume during observation. If plasma volume expands while the tracer disappears, a fall in concentration can arise both from loss of tracer and dilution by newly entering fluid. The simple exponential model used earlier implicitly assumes that the accessible volume is reasonably stable. A curved graph of logarithmic concentration against time may indicate that this assumption is failing, although other explanations are possible. An investigator may need independent observations of compartment stability to interpret the tracer curve. The important examination lesson is that a fitted rate constant describes the measured signal under the chosen model; it is not automatically a direct measure of a biological clearance process.

Controls are especially important for a measurement based on an optical or radioactive signal. Background signal must be determined so that the measured increment can be attributed to the tracer. Instrument calibration establishes the relation between signal and concentration. Replicate measurements help reveal random variation. Measurements of a reference standard of known concentration can detect drift or systematic bias. These controls do not establish complete mixing or biological confinement, so separate physiological evidence remains necessary. A highly reproducible measurement can still be systematically wrong when a marker escapes or fails to access part of its intended compartment. Precision and validity describe different properties of the experiment.

A student can often identify the most defensible marker choice in an examination by asking what must cross the relevant boundary. A water marker that rapidly crosses cell membranes cannot distinguish extracellular from intracellular water without an independent measurement. A marker excluded from cells but distributed through interstitial fluid cannot measure plasma specifically because it leaves the vascular space. A marker that stays with circulating erythrocytes estimates cell volume rather than plasma volume. If a labelled plasma protein escapes abnormally because the endothelial barrier is disrupted, an estimate based on prolonged sampling becomes less trustworthy. These conclusions can be derived from transport properties rather than memorised as an isolated list of tracer names.

19. Capillary beds, permeability and the spatial organisation of exchange

The capillary wall is not structurally identical in every organ. Continuous endothelium in many tissues offers relatively restricted pathways for large solutes. Fenestrated endothelial arrangements allow increased exchange of certain substances in specialised organs, while discontinuous or sinusoidal vessels in other tissues have different structural constraints. Even where the endothelial layer is fenestrated, additional structures may contribute to the overall barrier. The glomerular filtration system, for instance, should not be treated as an ordinary systemic capillary with a uniformly open wall. Its specialised filtration barrier and associated pressure relationships belong to renal physiology. The variation is important here because it prevents a student from assuming that one reflection coefficient or one filtration rate applies throughout the circulation.

The hydraulic conductance of a vascular bed depends on the permeability of individual exchange pathways and the total available surface area. Two beds can have similar permeability per unit area but different total filtration coefficients because they have different exchange areas. Recruitment of additional perfused microvessels can modify the effective exchange surface. This means that a rise in filtration need not result solely from a rise in capillary hydrostatic pressure. Changes in the number or area of exchange pathways can contribute independently. When a question explicitly holds Kf constant, it is isolating pressure and osmotic forces; when it describes vascular recruitment or increased permeability, Kf may change and must be considered as an additional variable.

A pressure difference across a capillary wall is also different from the pressure gradient along the vessel. Blood flows along the lumen in response to longitudinal pressure differences and vascular resistance. Fluid filtration crosses the wall in response to the combination of hydrostatic and effective osmotic forces across it. Both involve pressure, but their directions and relevant boundaries are distinct. A high longitudinal pressure gradient can increase flow without necessarily increasing mean capillary hydrostatic pressure by the same proportion. In an idealised network, changing resistance before the capillaries and changing resistance after the capillaries can have different effects on the capillary pressure. This distinction is developed further in cardiovascular physiology, but it already helps explain why arteriolar and venous disturbances can change fluid exchange differently.

For example, a constriction upstream of a capillary bed can reduce the pressure transmitted from an artery into that bed, depending on the rest of the network. A rise in downstream venous pressure can raise capillary hydrostatic pressure and promote filtration. The local effect is not determined by the systemic arterial pressure alone. An animal can have a normal arterial pressure measurement yet an elevated venous pressure in a particular region, increasing filtration there. Similarly, a change in gravitational position changes local hydrostatic pressures relative to the heart. In a standing animal, dependent vascular regions experience different pressures from elevated regions. These physical effects are important even when whole-body sodium content and total water remain unchanged.

A simple calculation illustrates the independent role of the reflection coefficient. Let the hydrostatic difference be 20 mmHg, the effective oncotic difference 18 mmHg, and Kf = 0.1 mL/min/mmHg. If sigma equals 1, the net filtration pressure is 2 mmHg and flux is 0.2 mL/min. If sigma falls to 0.5 with all other quantities fixed, the opposing effective oncotic term becomes 9 mmHg and flux becomes 1.1 mL/min. This is a 5.5-fold increase in the calculated flux, even though neither hydrostatic pressure nor plasma protein concentration changed. The example describes an instantaneous comparison with fixed gradients. In a living tissue, changing macromolecular permeability can subsequently alter local protein concentrations, so the final steady state will not necessarily show the same fold change.

The difference between water and macromolecular movement matters in inflammatory responses. A barrier that allows more macromolecules to leave can change both vascular protein inventory and the protein composition of the interstitial space. A barrier that becomes more hydraulically conductive can increase fluid flux even if macromolecular selectivity changes little. The two changes may occur together but need not be identical. An experiment using only a small diffusible solute cannot necessarily establish a change in protein permeability. Conversely, leakage of a labelled macromolecule provides evidence about its own transport and the barrier conditions, not a complete quantitative description of water flux. Independent measurements improve interpretation.

Fluid filtration and solute transport must also be distinguished. Many small solutes exchange by diffusion down concentration gradients even if net fluid movement is small. When water flows through a permeable pathway, some solutes can be carried with it by convection. A large retained macromolecule may be poorly transported by either mechanism through a selective barrier. Nutrient exchange does not therefore require that large volumes of plasma leave the circulation continuously. A student should avoid interpreting a drawing of filtration as if it represented all exchange of glucose, gases or metabolites. The regulation of interstitial volume and the delivery of particular solutes are related but separate transport problems.

20. How fluid redistribution modifies the apparent volume deficit

The interstitial compartment represents an exchangeable reservoir, but its contribution to restoring vascular filling is limited by tissue mechanics and microvascular properties. When capillary hydrostatic pressure declines, outward filtration often decreases. In some conditions a temporary reversal of fluid movement occurs before local oncotic gradients adjust. Simultaneously, previously formed tissue fluid continues to enter lymphatic vessels. Both processes can shift fluid towards the circulation relative to the previous balance. The resulting plasma volume change depends on the amount of fluid available in the tissues, the pressure gradients and the continuing external or renal losses. It is not a universal fixed fraction of the lost blood volume.

A simplified compartment model can represent these interactions with three rates: capillary filtration from plasma to interstitial fluid, lymphatic return from interstitial fluid to plasma, and external loss from plasma. If an animal has filtration of 3 units per time and lymphatic return of 3 units per time, the exchange makes no net contribution to plasma volume. If a disturbance reduces filtration to 1 unit per time while lymphatic return temporarily remains 3, the net effect of tissue exchange is a gain of 2 units per time in plasma. This gain can occur without requiring sustained net absorption across the capillary wall. It follows directly from the difference between opposing rates. The model simplifies the changing interstitial pressure and lymph flow but highlights a mechanism that is often omitted in an overly simple capillary diagram.

The distinction is particularly important in revised-Starling questions. The absence of sustained venous reabsorption in many tissue beds does not imply that the vasculature is incapable of recovering fluid after a volume disturbance. A fall in filtration allows ongoing lymphatic drainage to reduce the interstitial reservoir. Transient capillary absorption can contribute as well. Over time, the tissue may reach a different steady state where filtration and lymphatic return again balance. The net inward transfer integrated over the recovery period can be appreciable even though a sustained negative capillary flux is not maintained indefinitely. An answer that equates plasma recovery exclusively with permanent venous-end reabsorption misses the time-dependent nature of this exchange.

Interstitium contains a matrix of proteins and polysaccharides that influences how fluid is held and how pressure changes when fluid accumulates. The relation between added interstitial volume and interstitial pressure is not necessarily linear across a large range. At first, increasing pressure may oppose further filtration and promote lymphatic entry. As tissue volume increases further, mechanical conditions and effective compliance may change. The capacity of the lymphatic network also has limits. These properties help explain why an initially compensated increase in filtration can later become visible oedema. The appearance of swelling is not simply a direct visual measure of a constant filtration rate.

A thought experiment illustrates how two disturbances can produce similar swelling but require different explanations. In the first, capillary pressure increases with a functional barrier and intact lymphatics. Filtration increases and may eventually exceed lymphatic capacity. In the second, the filtration force remains similar to baseline but lymphatic removal is impaired. Tissue fluid accumulates because the outflow is reduced. A measurement of swelling alone does not distinguish increased inflow from reduced outflow. A direct or indirect assessment of lymphatic transport, capillary pressure and protein movement would help discriminate the hypotheses. This is the same logic used to analyse the level of fluid in a container with separately adjustable entry and exit rates, although tissue pressure and permeability add biological complexity.

21. Why the circulation uses multiple sensors instead of a single volume gauge

The regulatory system faces several disturbances that cannot be distinguished by a single concentration or pressure measurement. In an idealised animal, loss of whole blood lowers vascular filling without necessarily altering plasma osmolality immediately. Loss of pure water raises osmolality and may initially draw water from cells, partially supporting the extracellular volume. An increase in vascular capacity due to widespread vasodilatation may lower arterial filling pressure even though the amount of blood has not changed. Reduced cardiac pump function can limit arterial perfusion while venous filling pressure rises. A sensor responding only to osmotic concentration would miss important pressure changes, while a sensor responding only to vascular stretch could miss an early water imbalance.

A receptor must respond to a local physical or chemical signal. The stretch of an arterial wall depends on the pressure inside and outside the vessel, its geometry and its material properties. The same arterial pressure may produce different stretch if wall compliance changes. In the normal baroreflex, however, changes in pressure produce sufficiently informative changes in afferent signalling to support rapid adjustment of autonomic activity. Baroreceptors do not directly report the number of litres in the body. They report mechanical information relevant to circulation. This distinction explains both their usefulness and their limitations as volume-related sensors.

The neural response to reduced stretch provides rapid compensation because synaptic signalling and autonomic effector activity can change without waiting for a substantial change in whole-body fluid content. The kidneys provide a slower route because changing sodium and water excretion must accumulate into a meaningful net balance over time. The endocrine signals are not simply slower duplicates of the neural response. Angiotensin II can have rapid vascular effects as well as longer effects on fluid balance. Vasopressin changes renal permeability over a shorter interval than a change in erythrocyte mass. The different mechanisms operate on overlapping timescales. Describing them as strictly consecutive switches would be misleading, but distinguishing their dominant effects is useful for examination reasoning.

The renal sensors provide an additional advantage because renal sodium and water handling can be adjusted in response to local filtration and perfusion conditions. A reduction in filtered sodium delivery does not necessarily mean the blood sodium concentration is low. The delivery depends on filtration rate, tubular flow and upstream reabsorption. A system that directly sensed only plasma concentration would not capture these variables. The macula densa and juxtaglomerular apparatus provide feedback relevant to local nephron and vascular function. Detailed tubuloglomerular feedback is treated with renal physiology; here the key point is that local renal conditions contribute to endocrine signalling about the state of the circulation.

A common mistake is to classify every hormone into a rigid pair of opposites. For example, ANP favours sodium loss and aldosterone favours sodium retention, but their stimuli, receptor pathways, target cells and additional effects differ. Vasopressin primarily changes water permeability, while RAAS affects both vascular and renal functions. In severe circulatory disturbances, hormones normally associated with different homeostatic goals may rise together. A change in one hormone does not predict an exact reciprocal change in every other hormone. The integrated output depends on the relative strengths of osmotic, mechanical and chemical stimuli and on the ability of target organs to respond.

22. Interpreting water and sodium disturbances with tonicity models

A useful exercise is to compare two equal volumes of intake in an ideal compartment model. Suppose the same 42 L body water system described earlier initially has 12,600 mOsm of effective solute. Addition of one litre of pure water gives a final equilibrium concentration of 12,600/43, approximately 293.0 mOsm/L. If intracellular osmoles remain at 8,400 mOsm, the new ICF is about 28.67 L. The ECF becomes approximately 14.33 L. In the model, about two thirds of the additional water distributes intracellularly and one third extracellularly. Only a fraction of the extracellular gain appears as plasma expansion. This is why addition of water to the body is not equivalent to adding the same volume directly and permanently to blood.

Addition of one litre of isotonic extracellular fluid at 300 mOsm/L increases extracellular osmoles by 300 mOsm and total body water by one litre. Final concentration remains 300 mOsm/L because the added water and effective solute are proportional. ICF remains 28 L and ECF becomes 15 L under the model assumptions. The additional extracellular fluid distributes between vascular and interstitial regions according to exchange forces and tissue properties. The calculation cannot specify a permanent plasma fraction without further information. Many introductory problems use an approximate extracellular partition for convenience, but the biological division is dynamic rather than dictated by a universal fixed ratio.

Now consider a hypothetical loss of one litre of extracellular fluid containing 150 mOsm/L of effective solute, half the initial effective concentration. The remaining system contains 12,450 mOsm in 41 L, yielding approximately 303.66 mOsm/L. Intracellular volume becomes 8,400/303.66, about 27.66 L, while ECF becomes about 13.34 L. Because the lost fluid was hypotonic relative to the initial body fluids, the remaining fluids become more concentrated. Water moves out of cells, so both ECF and ICF contract. This is a simplified description of why hypotonic fluid loss, such as certain forms of sweating, can produce both osmotic and volume-related stimuli. Actual sweat concentration varies with circumstances and species, and renal and behavioural responses modify the final state.

Conversely, if an extracellular loss is hypertonic relative to the body fluids, the remaining extracellular effective solute concentration can fall. Water tends to move into cells, potentially intensifying extracellular contraction even as intracellular water expands. In a hypothetical system initially containing 42 L and 12,600 mOsm, loss of one litre carrying 600 mOsm leaves 12,000 mOsm in 41 L, or about 292.68 mOsm/L. The intracellular volume becomes 8,400/292.68, approximately 28.70 L, and extracellular volume falls to about 12.30 L. The ECF has lost more than the one litre removed externally because approximately 0.70 L shifted into cells. The direction of water redistribution can therefore differ from the direction of net whole-body water change.

These calculations use effective osmoles confined to compartments and assume that the system reaches osmotic equilibrium before hormonal correction. They also assume volume additivity and neglect changes in cell solute content. In biological cells, some solute transport and volume-regulatory responses occur over time, so the final intracellular and extracellular volumes may differ from these simplified values. The models are valuable because they isolate the primary physical tendency. An exam question that specifies a short interval and no compensation invites this type of calculation; a question describing a later steady state requires consideration of renal and behavioural feedback as well.

23. Biological variation in blood volume and adaptation

Typical human adult blood volume is often represented in physiology problems as several litres, with estimates related to body mass. This scale is useful for numerical practice, but blood volume per kilogram varies with body composition, age, sex, physiological condition and species. Body mass is not a direct measure of metabolically active tissue or vascular capacity. Two animals of equal body mass can have different plasma and erythrocyte volumes. A calculated expected volume based on body mass is therefore not the same as a measured volume. This distinction is important in comparative animal physiology, where simply applying a human reference value to an unrelated species can produce a misleading estimate.

In mammals, pregnancy provides a useful physiological example of unequal changes in blood components. Plasma volume commonly expands, while red cell mass can also increase. If plasma expansion is proportionately larger than red cell expansion, haematocrit falls despite an increase in the absolute red cell mass. This is a dilution effect, not necessarily a decrease in cell production. The example demonstrates why changes in sample concentration should not be interpreted without considering the relative changes in both compartments. Exact magnitudes vary with the stage of pregnancy and individual circumstances; the principle is more important for this chapter than numerical clinical ranges.

Endurance training can also alter circulating plasma volume and, over time, oxygen transport characteristics. A relative increase in plasma volume can reduce haematocrit even if red cell mass is maintained or increases. Improved filling may contribute to cardiovascular performance, while oxygen-carrying capacity depends on haemoglobin quantity and distribution. A concentration measured after a training adaptation is not equivalent to the same concentration measured after an acute haemorrhage. Time course, red cell inventory and the direction of plasma volume change distinguish the conditions. The detailed adaptations of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems are reserved for later chapters.

Exposure to reduced oxygen availability illustrates another distinction. Erythropoietic signalling may increase red cell production during sustained hypoxic exposure. This response is related to oxygen delivery rather than direct detection of blood volume. A mammal can have adequate circulating fluid yet stimulate erythropoietin because environmental oxygen availability is reduced. Conversely, an animal that loses plasma water without a reduction in oxygen availability may show haemoconcentration without a new erythropoietic stimulus. Environmental changes can also affect fluid balance, so measured haematocrit during adaptation represents the combined outcome of several processes. The correct interpretation depends on distinguishing red cell mass from plasma volume and identifying the dominant stimulus.

Comparative physiology also demonstrates that vascular fluid need not be organised identically across all animal groups. Mammalian erythrocytes lack nuclei at maturity, whereas many other vertebrate erythrocytes retain nuclei. This affects cellular structure and can contribute to differences in cell size and blood measurements, but does not eliminate the distinction between plasma and cell volume. In animals with open circulatory systems, haemolymph can directly bathe tissues in body spaces and may not be separated into plasma and interstitial fluid in the same manner as in a closed vertebrate circulation. The indicator dilution principle still follows conservation, but the accessible compartment and anatomical interpretation must be defined for the organism. It would be inappropriate to import a mammalian plasma volume formula into an open circulatory system without establishing the relevant compartments.

24. Recovery is a multi-variable process, not a single return to baseline

A useful way to organise the whole chapter is to regard blood volume, extracellular volume, intracellular volume, arterial filling, sodium inventory, effective osmotic concentration and red cell mass as related but distinct variables. Every perturbation affects a particular subset directly; the others change through redistribution or compensation. Every receptor responds to a particular local variable; its response influences several effectors. Every effector changes particular fluxes or aspects of distribution. Confusion occurs when a student compresses all these variables into an undifferentiated idea of hydration. Proper reasoning begins with the variable that changed and then traces the consequences.

For instance, restoration of arterial pressure after a brief disturbance may result from a change in vascular resistance without replacement of lost fluid. Restoration of plasma volume can occur through cell-free fluid movement without restoration of lost erythrocytes. Restoration of effective osmolality can occur through water intake without correction of a deficit in total body sodium. Restoration of sodium balance can take place while tissue fluid distribution remains abnormal because of venous congestion or altered barrier properties. A measured return to baseline in any one variable therefore provides evidence for recovery of that variable, not universal proof that the whole physiological system has returned to its previous state.

Experimental reasoning should respect these distinctions when defining an endpoint. An investigator testing rapid circulatory compensation might measure arterial pressure, heart rate and indices of filling over minutes. An investigator testing plasma recovery would instead need an estimate of plasma volume or a suitable combination of markers. An investigator testing erythropoietic recovery would examine reticulocytes, red cell mass and the availability of erythropoietic substrates over a longer interval. An investigator testing renal sodium regulation would measure intake, urinary excretion and nonrenal losses. These endpoints can complement one another but cannot be substituted without changing the scientific question.

Consider a hypothetical experiment comparing two mammals after the same proportionate blood loss. Both initially develop the same reduction in total blood volume. In the first, autonomic compensation is intact and pressure partly recovers rapidly. In the second, the rapid autonomic response is impaired, so pressure remains lower at the same volume. An observed pressure difference at this stage does not establish that the second animal lost more blood. Later, if renal and behavioural mechanisms operate, the two animals may accumulate different amounts of water and sodium because their perfusion-related signals differ. The early difference in neural control can therefore influence the later endocrine and renal outcomes. The comparison demonstrates how feedback mechanisms interact across time rather than independently restoring fixed targets.

The final physiological question is not merely whether the volume becomes normal, but whether the circulating tissue can again support adequate delivery and removal of substances while preserving normal exchange with tissue fluids. That goal requires a functioning pump, sufficiently filled vessels, a suitable plasma composition, an adequate erythrocyte population, a selective microvascular barrier and intact renal and endocrine feedback. The earlier chapters of this subunit explain the composition and cellular functions of blood. This chapter brings those concepts together by examining the regulation of the compartment in which they operate. The next subunit can now develop the mechanics of the heart and vascular circuit in detail, using blood volume and effective filling as essential starting variables.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Haematocrit equals red cell mass

Correct principle: Haematocrit is a fraction; direct cell volume or count is an absolute quantity.

Exam reasoning: Students conflate concentration with inventory. Compare two blood volumes with identical H before inferring cell production.

2. Plasma equals total ECF

Correct principle: Plasma is one part of ECF, whereas interstitial fluid is another.

Exam reasoning: A marker that exits capillaries but not cells measures ECF, not plasma.

3. Blood volume equals plasma volume

Correct principle: Red cells account for a substantial portion of blood volume.

Exam reasoning: Convert with representative H; do not simply label a plasma dilution result as blood volume.

4. Venous H always equals whole-body H

Correct principle: Vessel-size effects and unequal cell distribution can make the sample fraction different.

Exam reasoning: Check whether the question supplies whole-body H or a justified empirical correction.

5. A late tracer sample is always best

Correct principle: Tracer loss can lower concentration and inflate the estimated volume.

Exam reasoning: Check mixing and loss separately, and use a justified extrapolation model if appropriate.

6. Every capillary reabsorbs continuously at its venous end

Correct principle: Many continuous beds filter at steady state; temporary absorption and specialised absorbing beds exist.

Exam reasoning: Identify the model and time interval rather than imposing the classical drawing universally.

7. Oedema proves excessive effective arterial filling

Correct principle: Excess tissue fluid can coexist with inadequate arterial perfusion.

Exam reasoning: Examine pump function and distribution before predicting suppression of RAAS.

8. Osmolality and tonicity are interchangeable

Correct principle: Tonicity depends on effective membrane-impermeant osmoles over the relevant time.

Exam reasoning: Check solute permeability when predicting cell shrinkage or swelling.

9. Renin is a steroid hormone

Correct principle: Renin is a proteolytic enzyme that cleaves angiotensinogen.

Exam reasoning: Trace substrates and products rather than treating all RAAS components as similar hormones.

10. ACE exists only in the lungs

Correct principle: ACE is present in multiple tissues and endothelial beds.

Exam reasoning: The pulmonary bed is important but not the exclusive conversion site.

11. Aldosterone directly inserts AQP2

Correct principle: Aldosterone chiefly regulates sodium handling; vasopressin promotes AQP2 trafficking through V2 signalling.

Exam reasoning: Match ENaC to aldosterone and AQP2 to vasopressin, without ignoring osmotic coupling.

12. ADH is synthesised in posterior pituitary

Correct principle: Hypothalamic neurons synthesise ADH; the posterior pituitary releases it.

Exam reasoning: Examinations often contrast synthesis and secretion sites.

13. ANP is a sodium-retention hormone

Correct principle: ANP generally promotes natriuresis and opposes RAAS.

Exam reasoning: Read the stretch signal and direction of feedback before choosing a hormone.

14. Normal initial H excludes major haemorrhage

Correct principle: Proportional whole blood loss can preserve H initially.

Exam reasoning: Compute R and B after the loss; consider later plasma dilution separately.

15. EPO immediately restores lost plasma

Correct principle: EPO promotes erythroid production over days and longer.

Exam reasoning: Separate rapid redistribution and conservation from new cell production.

16. High urinary Na concentration means high Na excretion

Correct principle: Excretion rate equals concentration multiplied by flow.

Exam reasoning: Use matched units and calculate quantity per time rather than reading concentration alone.

47. Assertion–reason — Assertion: High vasopressin always proves hypertonicity. Reason: Reduced effective arterial filling can stimulate vasopressin.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion false reason true
  3. Assertion true reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

B — Assertion false reason true. Nonosmotic stimulation is important in substantial circulatory disturbance, so hormone concentration alone does not identify the stimulus.

48. Numerical — An animal gains 150 mmol Na/day, loses 20 mmol nonrenally and excretes 100 mmol in urine. Three-day retained Na under constant rates?

  1. 30 mmol
  2. 90 mmol
  3. 50 mmol
  4. 130 mmol
Answer and explanation

B — 90 mmol. Net daily balance is 150 − 20 − 100 = 30 mmol. Three days yields 90 mmol. This is an amount, not plasma concentration.

49. Experimental — Atrial extract increases urinary sodium and water output, but ventricular extract and preparation medium do not. What is supported?

  1. The exact receptor sequence is proved
  2. Atrial tissue contains a factor with natriuretic activity
  3. All atrial cells release equal peptide
  4. The kidney synthesises ANP exclusively
Answer and explanation

B — Atrial tissue contains a factor with natriuretic activity. The controls support specificity to atrial tissue extract. Purification and receptor studies are needed for the stronger molecular conclusions.

50. Experimental — A study of capillary exchange changes tissue protein concentration but sees a smaller change in effective oncotic opposition than predicted by the classical model. Best follow-up?

  1. Ignore the discrepancy
  2. Conclude lymphatic vessels do not exist
  3. Assume no hydrostatic force exists
  4. Measure or model local subglycocalyx protein and control barrier properties
Answer and explanation

D — Measure or model local subglycocalyx protein and control barrier properties. The alternative model locates the effective gradient at the glycocalyx. Other variables must be controlled before attributing the discrepancy to that mechanism.

Revision and mastery

Blood volume is the sum of plasma and red cell volumes to a close approximation. Concentrations are ratios, while volume and mass describe absolute quantities. Indicator dilution estimates an accessible space through conservation. Tissue fluid balance reflects microvascular filtration and lymphatic return. Baroreceptors and osmoreceptors distinguish mechanical and osmotic disturbances; RAAS, vasopressin, ANP, thirst and the kidney alter subsequent balance. EPO acts on the slower restoration of erythrocyte mass.
