1. An organ emerges from a field of potential cells

A developing organ must occupy an appropriate place, contain suitable numbers of cells, establish a recognizable shape and connect with the tissues it serves. These requirements are related, but none is identical to the others. A patch of epidermal cells can receive a signal to divide without acquiring the identity of an organ. An induced cell may acquire the correct identity but fail to move into the correct position. Even a correctly shaped organ may be nonfunctional if it does not connect to an internal passage. The vulva of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans provides an unusually clear way to separate these developmental problems and discover how they are coordinated.

The animal is a small, transparent roundworm. Its body has a longitudinal axis extending from the anterior head to the posterior tail and a dorsoventral axis distinguishing its back from its belly. The hermaphrodite reproductive system produces eggs that must pass through the uterus and leave the animal through a ventral opening. The vulva develops as an epithelial passage between the internal reproductive tract and the exterior. It is not the tissue that produces oocytes, nor is it the whole gonad. Its position is coordinated with that of the reproductive system, even though the cells of the vulval epithelium originate in the epidermal lineage rather than in the gonad.

At the beginning of the relevant developmental period there is no mature opening. Instead, a set of epidermal precursor cells is available along the ventral side of the larva. Only a subset of those cells will produce the organ. Their potential is broader than their normal contribution. This difference between what a precursor can do under suitable experimental conditions and what it actually does during ordinary development is the distinction between developmental potency and normal cell fate. It is central to interpreting virtually every classical experiment on the vulva.

Two kinds of information converge on these cells. One originates in a nearby gonadal cell and informs the epidermis of the position at which organ formation is required. Another passes between the epidermal cells themselves, coordinating neighboring developmental outcomes. The first interaction is inductive because it redirects the prospective behavior of responsive cells. The second is lateral because the communicating precursors lie beside one another in the same tissue. An organ with a central domain and two flanking domains can therefore be produced by signals that differ both in their spatial origin and in their molecular consequences.

The developmental question is not simply why three cells divide. It is why one of those three cells executes a central program, why the other two execute flanking programs, and why precursors just beyond them do not contribute vulval cells at all. A solution based only on the total amount of growth would be inadequate. Growth must be partitioned into distinct patterns of descendants, and these descendants must then remodel their epithelial geometry. In this system, genetic analysis and direct observation can connect each step of the process to particular molecular activities.

C. elegans is especially suitable for such an analysis because individual cells can be identified by position and lineage in living animals. Normal development is highly reproducible, so an abnormal division or fusion can be assigned to a particular precursor rather than inferred from an indistinct tissue mass. Animals also possess experimentally tractable genetics. A mutation can be associated with a changed cellular outcome, and combinations of mutations can help locate the affected gene in a signaling pathway. These properties make the worm an experimental model of how multicellular development solves problems that are also encountered in larger animals, without implying that every organ uses the same genes or geometry.

2. Precursor identity, lineage and developmental timing

The relevant precursor cells are members of a ventral epidermal series named Pn.p, where n indicates position in the numbered P-cell lineage. The suffix p identifies a posterior daughter in the naming convention, not the posterior end of the adult animal. Six cells, P3.p through P8.p, constitute the canonical vulval competence group. They can be placed in a single anterior-to-posterior sequence: P3.p, P4.p, P5.p, P6.p, P7.p and P8.p. P6.p is generally positioned nearest the gonadal anchor cell at induction. This relative geometry is essential; the numerical names are labels for cells, not instructions directing their eventual fates.

In nematodes a cell lineage records ancestry and division history. For example, when a precursor divides, its daughters are designated by extending the name of the mother with suffixes indicating their relative positions. A lineage allows an observer to follow a prospective organ from one cell to a set of differentiated descendants. Fate, in contrast, describes the normal developmental contribution or experimentally observed outcome of a cell. Two precursors may be related by ancestry yet take different fates. Conversely, a precursor that normally forms epidermis can be induced experimentally to generate a lineage similar to that of a different precursor.

The larval stages are designated L1, L2, L3 and L4, with molts separating stages. During early larval life the ventral precursors must remain available for subsequent induction. Fate patterning takes place principally during L3, and the characteristic invagination and shaping of the epithelium occur later, especially during L4. An adult egg-laying phenotype is consequently the combined result of events spanning several larval stages. Recording only the adult outcome cannot identify which of those stages was disrupted.

The gonadal anchor cell develops within the somatic gonad. It is distinct from the germline, which gives rise to gametes. The anchor cell lies on the internal side of the ventral epidermis near the prospective vulval center. Its role initially includes the production of an inductive signal, and later it helps coordinate the physical linkage between the vulva and uterus. These roles occur at different times and can be experimentally distinguished. The label anchor cell refers to the cellular source, whereas LIN-3 refers to a molecular product; removing the cell is not identical to removing only one product it makes.

The normal fate sequence, read from P3.p to P8.p, is tertiary, tertiary, secondary, primary, secondary and tertiary. The terms primary, secondary and tertiary refer to distinct developmental programs; they are not ordinal names for successive mitotic divisions. The primary precursor generates eight vulval descendants. Each secondary precursor generates seven, giving a total of twenty-two cells in the canonical vulval lineage before the final epithelial fusion events reorganize the descendants. Tertiary precursors do not contribute to this count. Most divide once and their daughters fuse into the large epidermal syncytium hyp7. P3.p is a variable case: it frequently fuses without dividing, and its competence is not equally persistent in every individual.

A syncytium is a region of cytoplasm containing multiple nuclei that are not separated into individual cells by intervening plasma membranes. Fusion with hyp7 is therefore more than a change in the name of a precursor. It changes the physical independence and developmental possibilities of the cell. A cell that has merged with the surrounding epidermis cannot simply act as an ordinary separate vulval precursor when the later inductive signal arrives. This observation introduces a temporal constraint: the organ-forming field has to be established and preserved before its members can be patterned.

The pattern of descendants also explains why it is insufficient to count nuclei in a late larva without following their origins. A cell may execute an abnormal number of divisions while retaining a marker of its earlier fate. A secondary lineage can be malformed without being converted into a primary lineage. A rigorous diagnosis uses division patterns, appropriate molecular markers, tissue morphology and developmental timing together. Each readout supplies a different piece of evidence about the pathway from precursor to organ.

3. Establishing and maintaining competence

Developmental competence is the ability of a cell to respond to an appropriate inductive stimulus at a particular time. It does not mean that the cell is already differentiated into the tissue that the stimulus can produce. A precursor can have a normal nonvulval fate while retaining the ability to form vulval descendants if the spatial arrangement of its neighbors is changed. Competence is also a conditional property. It depends on the state of the receiving cell and on the time at which the stimulus arrives, rather than merely on whether a receptor gene occurs in its genome.

The classical six-cell equivalence group is best understood in this operational sense. Its members share an overlapping range of potential fates, demonstrated by interventions in which normally induced cells are removed and other precursors contribute in their place. Equivalence does not require identical concentrations of every protein, identical exposure to signals, or identical probabilities of adopting each alternative. In particular, P3.p displays variability and can fuse earlier than other cells. Describing the group as strictly molecularly identical would confuse a useful experimental definition of potential with a stronger claim about biochemical uniformity.

One important early function is supplied by the Hox gene lin-39. Hox genes encode transcriptional regulators that help establish region-specific developmental programs. In this context LIN-39 supports the persistence of the ventral epidermal precursors as separate cells. A major consequence of reduced LIN-39 activity is premature fusion with hyp7. The fusogen EFF-1, which promotes membrane merger, is normally restrained in these precursors. The suppression of premature fusion creates a population that can subsequently respond to vulval induction. It is a permissive role because it maintains the physical and molecular opportunity for a later fate decision.

Wnt signals also contribute to maintaining precursor competence. In the larva, signaling from surrounding tissues helps preserve the prospective organ-forming cells and influences their developmental state. This signaling must not be conflated with the later central induction by LIN-3. A reduction in competence can produce fewer induced cells despite the presence of an anchor cell and an intact source of LIN-3. Thus an apparently simple vulvaless phenotype may arise because the signal was absent, because the receptor pathway failed, or because the target tissue ceased to be competent before the signal arrived.

The distinction can be explored experimentally by asking when the abnormality first becomes visible. Suppose a mutant precursor disappears as a discrete cell during early larval development, well before the normal time of induction. Later failure to express a vulval fate marker is then expected even if the marker gene and the inductive pathway are structurally intact. By contrast, if all six precursors remain independent until induction but fail to activate a response marker, a defect in signaling or in its immediate interpretation becomes a more plausible explanation. Time-resolved observations constrain the causal models that can explain a phenotype.

An important biological consequence of competence maintenance is economy. The animal does not need to specify the final fate of every member of the organ-forming field as soon as the cells are born. It can establish a field first and then use information from the developing gonad to select the appropriate contributors. This arrangement permits coordination between two tissues whose lineages are distinct. It also permits limited compensation when the expected contributors are missing. Compensation is constrained by time, position and the remaining molecular capabilities of the cells, not an unlimited ability of any cell in the worm to form any organ.

This framework is useful when interpreting sufficiency experiments. Adding an inductive signal to a region that lacks competent precursors may yield no new organ. That negative result does not establish that the signal is irrelevant. Conversely, exposing maintained precursors to an unusually strong signal can reveal their potential without proving that the same signal level is normally present. Necessity and sufficiency are conclusions about specified conditions. The relevant controls include the developmental age of the animals, the existence of the target cells and whether the intervention changes only the variable under investigation.

4. Classical experiments: separating position from ancestry

Direct observation of a normal lineage establishes reproducibility but cannot by itself show why the lineage occurs. Early nematode developmental research combined the ability to identify cells with targeted laser ablation. By removing a particular living cell and following the rest of the animal, investigators could distinguish outcomes that require that cell from outcomes that merely occur near it. In the vulva, the decisive question concerned whether epidermal fates were fixed internally or depended on interactions with the gonad and neighboring epidermal cells.

The classical removal of the anchor cell before vulval induction disrupts vulval fate specification. Without its normal inductive input, available precursors tend toward nonvulval development rather than producing the characteristic central and flanking lineages. This establishes that the anchor cell is necessary for the standard inductive process. The inference is stronger when neighboring tissues remain viable and comparable untreated animals retain the normal pattern. It remains an inference about the cell's requirement, not yet a molecular identification of LIN-3. A later experiment must separate signal production from all other functions of that cell.

A different experiment removes the normal prospective vulval precursors and leaves members of the surrounding competence group. If a remaining precursor can be induced to produce vulval descendants, its original position in the lineage does not irreversibly dictate a tertiary fate. Such substitution experiments revealed regulation of fate within the field. Their logic is particularly important because the lineage of the untreated animal is so consistent. An invariant normal lineage can result from reproducible interactions rather than from an entirely autonomous program.

Sternberg and Horvitz's 1986 work on pattern formation provided evidence for the multiple developmental possibilities of the precursors and the dependence of their outcomes on inductive and neighboring interactions. Subsequent genetic studies distinguished the pathways contributing primary and secondary fate. In 1989, their analysis of the combined actions of two intercellular pathways linked the secondary program to lin-12. Later experiments on sequential signaling refined the model by demonstrating how the induced central cell helps communicate secondary fate to neighbors. These findings are complementary, not an arbitrary succession of mutually exclusive textbook theories.

A critical experimental distinction is that between the direct effect of the anchor cell on a neighbor and the indirect effect mediated by a different epidermal cell. If removing the anchor cell eliminates both primary and secondary lineages, this result alone cannot decide whether both fates depend directly on the signal. The anchor cell may normally induce the primary cell, which in turn supplies a secondary-inducing signal. Removal of the initial source destroys both branches in either model. To distinguish them, experiments must manipulate or monitor the intermediate cell and the molecular pathway carrying lateral information.

An appropriate experimental reasoning sequence begins with a hypothesis that predicts which precursors will change when a specified cell or gene is perturbed. The observer follows the remaining lineages and assesses whether the predicted cells adopt an alternative fate or merely fail to develop. A sham manipulation can help control for nonspecific effects of handling and injury. Stage matching matters because an intervention performed after a fate is established need not have the same effect as an intervention before the relevant signal. A positive result identifies a causal dependency only as specifically as the intervention permits.

For example, if removal of P6.p changes a neighbor's eventual fate, the explanation might involve loss of a lateral signal, altered exposure to the anchor-cell signal, or compensatory changes in position. The changed outcome is evidence against a fully autonomous program, but it does not automatically identify which one of these mechanisms is responsible. Molecular genetic experiments and cell-specific rescue tests can then discriminate among the possibilities. This combination of anatomical manipulation with genetics turns an observed pattern into a mechanistic account of how the pattern is produced.

5. Induction by LIN-3 and the LET-23 receptor

LIN-3 is an epidermal growth factor-like signaling protein. During the relevant larval period, the gonadal anchor cell supplies the principal inductive LIN-3 input to the ventral precursor field. A signaling molecule that travels from one cell to nearby cells is often described as a paracrine signal. The term distinguishes local communication from endocrine signaling through a distant circulation and from communication that absolutely requires contact between adjacent membranes. In the worm, the relative positions of the anchor cell and responding epidermis provide a spatial framework for local induction.

LET-23 is the receptor tyrosine kinase through which much of the inductive signal is interpreted. Receptor tyrosine kinases have an extracellular ligand-binding region, a membrane-spanning region and an intracellular catalytic region. This arrangement allows information from the extracellular environment to change the activity of proteins inside the receiving cell without requiring the ligand to enter the cytoplasm. Ligand-dependent receptor activation promotes tyrosine phosphorylation and creates binding opportunities for intracellular signaling proteins. The receptor thus serves as the first molecular link between local extracellular information and changes in gene expression.

Receptor placement matters in an epithelial tissue. Membrane domains exposed toward the source of a ligand may not be equivalent to domains facing another tissue surface. The vulval precursor receives LIN-3 from its basal side, and proteins that help maintain appropriate LET-23 localization support normal induction. The LIN-2, LIN-7 and LIN-10 proteins participate in this localization mechanism. A receptor that remains catalytically functional in an artificial assay but is misplaced within the precursor can be inefficient at receiving the physiological signal. This illustrates why a mutation can alter signaling without directly changing a ligand-binding pocket or catalytic active site.

P6.p normally encounters the strongest effective inductive input because it lies near the anchor cell. The adjacent P5.p and P7.p cells can also receive LIN-3, although their responses differ from that of P6.p. More distal precursors generally experience weaker effective induction under ordinary conditions. The phrase effective induction is important: a cell's response depends on ligand availability, receptor location, receptor abundance and the state of downstream regulatory proteins. A spatial difference in pathway output cannot always be translated directly into an extracellular concentration measurement.

The consequences of LIN-3 signaling are mediated by an intracellular cascade rather than by LIN-3 serving as a structural component of the mature organ. Activated LET-23 leads through adaptor and guanine nucleotide exchange functions to activation of the small GTPase LET-60/Ras. It promotes the primary differentiation program when the downstream signaling state is appropriate. If the receptor is inactivated in a precursor, adding extra extracellular ligand may not restore that precursor's normal response. Conversely, artificially activating a downstream component can sometimes bypass loss of ligand or receptor, provided the remaining machinery and developmental competence are intact.

This logic distinguishes the signal from the response. A reporter of lin-3 transcription in the anchor cell measures production-related gene expression, not successful reception by the epidermis. A reporter of a target gene downstream of the MAP kinase pathway measures an aspect of intracellular response, not necessarily the extracellular distribution of LIN-3. To infer how a mutation changes the system, the student should identify which compartment is monitored, which step of the mechanism it reports and whether the outcome is an early signaling event or a later morphological consequence.

6. From receptor activation to a primary-fate program

A kinase is an enzyme that transfers a phosphate group to an appropriate substrate. In the vulval induction pathway, protein phosphorylation changes the activity and interactions of signaling proteins. The small GTP-binding protein LET-60 is a Ras-family molecular switch. Its GDP-bound state is generally less active than its GTP-bound state for signaling to effectors. A guanine nucleotide exchange factor promotes replacement of bound GDP with GTP. This is not phosphorylation of the GDP molecule by the receptor; it is an exchange of one nucleotide for another in the binding site of Ras.

Adaptor proteins couple the activated receptor to the exchange machinery. SEM-5 performs an adaptor function related to that of Grb2 in other animals, and SOS-1 promotes Ras activation. These interactions place LET-60 in a position to recruit or activate effectors near the membrane. Ras does not act as a protein kinase. Its activity is controlled in part by GTP hydrolysis, which converts its GTP-bound state to the GDP-bound state. Regulatory proteins can accelerate that hydrolysis and limit signaling. The cycle can therefore encode the presence or absence of continued upstream stimulation.

For the primary vulval fate, an important effector is the Raf-family kinase LIN-45. It activates the kinase MEK-2, which in turn activates MPK-1, the nematode ERK-family MAP kinase. The sequence LET-23, LET-60, LIN-45, MEK-2 and MPK-1 is a useful central pathway map. The arrows represent causal activation through specific molecular interactions rather than direct binding of each gene to the next gene. The three-kinase cascade provides several points at which activity can be amplified, restrained or integrated with another signal.

ATP supplies phosphate groups for the kinase reactions, whereas the Ras switch uses a guanine nucleotide cycle. The distinction matters because the two processes are often lumped together as phosphorylation. The hydrolysis of GTP returns Ras toward an inactive signaling state; the transfer of phosphate from ATP can activate or inhibit a target protein depending on the particular site and mechanism. Phosphatases remove phosphate groups from protein substrates. Continuous regulation makes pathway activity dynamic even when the total amount of a given kinase changes little.

MPK-1 activity influences nuclear regulators of gene expression. One important example is LIN-1, an ETS-family transcription factor whose regulation by the pathway helps relieve repression of vulval differentiation. LIN-31 also participates in transcriptional regulation in the precursor cells. An altered balance of transcriptional regulators changes which downstream genes are expressed, eventually affecting division behavior, identity and the ability to communicate with neighbors. The induced state is therefore a regulatory state of the cell, not just a transient increase in one enzyme activity.

The pathway also explains why mutations can cause opposite phenotypes. A loss of activity in a required positive component can reduce induction. Persistent activation of a positive signaling component can promote ectopic induction in otherwise competent cells. Removal of a negative regulator can sometimes increase pathway output without increasing LIN-3 production. A common examination error is to classify all mutations in a named pathway as equivalent. The direction of the change depends on whether the gene product normally promotes or restrains the outcome and on whether the specific allele reduces or increases its activity.

The pathway is conserved in its broad organization across animals, but the developmental output is specific to cell context. A similar receptor-Ras-MAPK module can support proliferation in one tissue, differentiation in another, or other cellular responses depending on the regulatory targets and competing inputs. The worm vulva is valuable precisely because an evolutionarily familiar signaling module can be connected to a defined set of developmental fates. The molecular name of a pathway alone does not predict the identity of the resulting organ.

7. Lateral communication through LIN-12/Notch

A central cell that simply responds to an extracellular gradient would not necessarily establish the correct identities of its immediate neighbors. The cell must also participate in the process of patterning those neighbors. In the vulval field, a strong primary-fate-inducing signal changes both the internal state of P6.p and its capacity to send a new extracellular signal. This change creates a second source of developmental information. The original source lies in the gonad; the new source lies in the epidermal field itself. A distinction between those sources explains why the developmental state of P5.p and P7.p cannot be inferred from their distance from the anchor cell alone.

The LIN-12 protein is a receptor of the Notch family. In a receiving cell, it detects ligands belonging to the Delta/Serrate/LAG-2 family, commonly abbreviated DSL. During vulval patterning, expression of several such ligands is promoted in the prospective primary cell. LAG-2, APX-1 and DSL-1 are important examples. Their functions overlap, so eliminating a single ligand need not abolish the entire signal. Some DSL proteins are membrane-associated, whereas DSL-1 can act as a secreted ligand. The familiar drawing of two touching cells is helpful for understanding the major local interaction, but it should not be taken to mean that every molecular form of the lateral signal is an immobile membrane protein.

Notch-family proteins are unusual in that the receptor is also a precursor of an intracellular transcriptional regulator. The intact receptor crosses the plasma membrane. Its extracellular region recognizes an appropriate ligand, while its intracellular region can contribute to the regulation of nuclear transcription after regulated proteolytic cleavage. Ligand engagement and associated molecular events permit cleavage, including a cleavage mediated by a gamma-secretase complex within the membrane. The released intracellular domain can enter the nucleus and associate with DNA-binding and transcriptional regulatory proteins. In the nematode, LAG-1 is a representative DNA-binding partner. This is fundamentally different from the relay of kinase activation initiated by a receptor such as LET-23.

The two mechanisms convey different biological information. The RTK pathway senses an extracellular inductive input and changes the activity of intracellular kinases. The Notch pathway converts an appropriate extracellular ligand interaction into a transcriptional response through release of part of its own receptor. Notch need not use a freely diffusing intracellular second messenger to reproduce the essential transcriptional response. An allele encoding a receptor whose intracellular region is active independently of normal extracellular ligand engagement can therefore alter the developmental pattern even when the upstream signal is weak.

In the normal vulval field, P5.p and P7.p become recipients of the lateral signal associated with the prospective primary cell. Activation of LIN-12 promotes the secondary program. This state includes a characteristic pattern of later divisions and differentiation, but these outcomes are not all instantaneous consequences of receptor cleavage. Transcriptional changes occur before the complete lineage has been executed. The effects of a receptor mutation should thus be examined using a readout early enough to assess specification and another late enough to assess execution of the program.

The primary cell is not simply a passive piece of epithelium located between two independent secondary cells. Its response to induction makes it a signaling center within the field. This arrangement offers a way to place secondary domains in relation to the chosen primary domain. If the strongest inductive input shifts to another competent precursor, the positions of cells receiving a strong lateral signal can also shift. The pattern is therefore capable of limited adjustment rather than being restricted to a single prewritten set of cell names.

LIN-12 has additional developmental functions elsewhere in the worm, including a role in the earlier decision that establishes the anchor cell rather than a ventral uterine precursor. It is essential to distinguish this earlier somatic gonadal decision from LIN-12 action within the ventral epidermal field. A whole-animal mutation can change both the number or identity of the inductive cells and the ability of epidermal cells to receive lateral signals. A simple fate prediction is safest when the question explicitly confines the perturbation to the receiving epidermal cells after the anchor-cell decision has occurred. Otherwise, indirect effects on the source must also be considered.

A receptor that is present but not active is not equivalent to an activated receptor. Protein staining may reveal LIN-12 at the membrane of a precursor that has not received a sufficient lateral signal. A transcriptional reporter of a LIN-12-dependent target supplies different evidence about the pathway. Comparing receptor abundance with downstream transcription can help distinguish a defect in ligand delivery, receptor cleavage or transcriptional interpretation. These measurements also reveal why a map of gene expression is not automatically a map of cell fate.

8. How induction and lateral signaling sharpen the pattern

The vulval field is a coupled system: a change within one precursor affects the signals that reach others. In an uncoupled system, each cell would make an independent choice based on its own local measurement of the anchor-cell signal. Such a mechanism can generate spatial patterns, but it places a heavy burden on precision in ligand concentration and cellular thresholds. In the coupled vulval system, the emerging primary cell supplies a local signal that promotes a different fate in its nearest neighbors. The resulting network integrates position with the developmental state of adjacent cells.

An important aspect of this integration is mutual antagonism between pathway outputs. Strong EGF/Ras/MAPK signaling in the prospective primary cell favors primary specification and reduces its responsiveness to LIN-12-mediated secondary-inducing signals. Downregulation of LIN-12 in the primary precursor is one mechanism contributing to this separation. The primary cell therefore tends to act as a source of lateral information rather than switching to a secondary program in response to the signals that emerge in the field. This does not require that every molecule of LIN-12 disappear from the cell instantaneously.

In the adjacent prospective secondary cells, activation of LIN-12 supports expression of negative regulators of the primary-inducing pathway. A familiar example is LIP-1, a phosphatase that restrains MAP kinase activity. Phosphatases act by removing phosphates from appropriate substrates. Consequently, the same amount of extracellular EGF can produce a lower downstream MAP kinase output in a cell with stronger phosphatase activity than in a cell with weak phosphatase activity. The secondary state is strengthened not only by positive regulation of its own program but also by suppression of a competing program.

This arrangement explains why describing LIN-12 exclusively as an inhibitor can be misleading. From the perspective of the primary program, lateral signaling inhibits or discourages the neighboring cell from also adopting that program. From the perspective of the secondary program, the same interaction is a positive specification signal. A student should always ask what outcome is being activated or inhibited. A signal that suppresses one identity can actively promote another rather than merely making the recipient inactive.

The term lateral inhibition is used in many developmental systems to describe the prevention of equivalent neighboring outcomes. In the vulval example it captures an aspect of the competition between primary and secondary identities, but lateral induction is also appropriate for the positive promotion of a secondary fate. The two terms refer to different consequences of the same interaction network. This dual role is why the simple idea of Notch as a universal inhibitor of differentiation is incorrect. In some contexts Notch helps maintain an undifferentiated state; in others it promotes a specialized identity. The particular downstream transcriptional program determines the result.

P5.p and P7.p need not be absolutely devoid of EGF signaling to become secondary cells. They can experience a lower degree of inductive signaling while receiving a strong lateral input. Their eventual fate reflects integration rather than a complete absence of one ligand. Experiments and reviews support contributions from graded EGF signaling as well as from sequential signaling. A compact examination model often assigns high effective EGF signaling to primary fate and strong LIN-12 signaling to secondary fate. That model is useful if it is remembered as a representation of the dominant interactions, not a claim that every cell receives precisely one molecular input.

At the two outer sides of the induced group, P4.p and P8.p ordinarily do not receive a combination of signals sufficient for vulval specification. P3.p is farther from the usual center still. These precursors adopt tertiary outcomes under ordinary conditions. Their nonvulval fate is an organized developmental response, not necessarily a pathological failure. Where they divide, their daughters ordinarily become incorporated into hyp7. The tissue retains epidermal continuity beyond the vulval region. Restricting the organ to the correct subset of cells is as important to normal development as inducing those that contribute.

The stability of the central and flanking pattern emerges from the combination of local geometry and regulatory feedback. A modest fluctuation of the inductive input need not move a cell across a fate boundary if the secondary-promoting and primary-restraining mechanisms remain effective. In a mutant that weakens the coupling, a similar fluctuation could have a more pronounced consequence. This provides an explanation for why a mutation may increase the frequency of variable or mixed fates rather than converting every affected animal into a single new, uniform phenotype. Biological robustness refers to resistance to such perturbations within a range; it does not imply absolute insensitivity to changes in signal strength.

The network also illustrates why measuring one component cannot always reveal the state of the system. An increase in the amount of MPK-1 protein does not necessarily imply increased MPK-1 activity. An increase in ligand transcription does not guarantee effective ligand presentation. A cell that expresses both an inductive-response reporter and an early lateral-response reporter may occupy a transient state before the final fate is resolved. Time, molecular activity and the sequence of interactions matter. A good mechanistic explanation therefore specifies which stage and which readout it addresses.

9. Graded induction and sequential induction: related explanations

A graded-induction model begins with the idea that effective signaling differs with position. The cell nearest a signal source experiences the strongest input, cells nearby experience intermediate input and distant cells experience weaker input. If the responding precursors have appropriate thresholds, a continuous spatial change in effective signal can yield discrete developmental identities. This is a classic mechanism of positional information. It explains why the relative position of the anchor cell is associated with the location of the primary fate.

The simplest version of this model assigns three outcomes to two thresholds. Above a high threshold a precursor becomes primary, between the high and low thresholds it becomes secondary, and below the low threshold it becomes tertiary. The threshold model is helpful for organizing an initial problem, but it is not a complete account of the real network. The contribution of LIN-12 cannot be inferred from a single curve of EGF activity, and experimental outcomes may reflect variation in receptor localization, signaling duration or competence. The model is a hypothesis about how input relates to output, not a direct measurement of the input in each cell.

In a sequential-induction model, strong anchor-cell signaling first favors primary specification. The responding epidermal cell then sends a secondary-inducing signal to neighbors. The second spatial pattern is produced by the arrangement of the responding cells around the initially selected cell rather than by an independently read threshold of the anchor-cell signal alone. The two interactions form a chain of developmental causation. An intervention that blocks the second interaction can preserve the initial primary response while impairing the normal flanking identities.

The logic of distinguishing the models becomes clearer in a controlled thought experiment. Imagine two otherwise competent adjacent precursors exposed to comparable intermediate EGF inputs. One receives a lateral signal from an induced neighbor and the other does not. If the two take different fates in a manner dependent on LIN-12, EGF input alone cannot explain the difference. Conversely, if changing the EGF input alters the probability of secondary fate under conditions in which the lateral contribution has been controlled, the EGF pathway contributes information beyond the first induction of a central cell. Neither conclusion follows simply from observing the normal symmetric arrangement.

Classical studies of the vulva helped reveal both aspects of the mechanism. The 1986 experiments on precursor fate and the later studies of lateral inhibition and sequential signaling provided distinct kinds of evidence. Genetic changes in the components of the pathways, together with cell-specific interventions, showed that the fate choices are not adequately described by either an immutable lineage map or a single uncontrolled gradient. More refined explanations retain the spatial bias of EGF while accounting for the interaction between EGF and Notch outputs in neighboring cells.

The graded and sequential explanations should not be treated as contradictory slogans. A molecule can create a graded input that initiates an asymmetric response, and that response can create a second signaling event that refines the pattern. In the real animal, several processes may overlap in time, so sequential does not imply that the first pathway shuts off everywhere before the second starts. The key causal meaning of sequential signaling is that induction of the lateral signal depends on an earlier or concurrent inductive response in the prospective primary cell.

A useful analogy to other developmental systems is the distinction between prepattern and refinement. A prepattern provides a broad spatial bias, such as the relative proximity of precursors to a source. A refinement mechanism resolves neighboring identities, stabilizes boundaries or coordinates the appropriate relationship between cells. The word prepattern must not be interpreted as a completed mature fate map. In this case, precursor competence and gonadal position help constrain the possible outcomes, and the coupled signaling network selects and stabilizes the normal arrangement within those constraints.

The distinction matters in experimental design. A fate map drawn from untreated animals can be consistent with both a graded and a sequential explanation. An experiment must perturb a feature on which the models make different predictions. For example, a manipulation that changes lateral reception while preserving normal inductive signaling is informative about the lateral contribution. Measuring only whether the final adult can lay eggs would not be sufficiently discriminating, because many unrelated developmental defects also affect egg laying. The meaningful observation is which of the six precursors acquired which fate and whether the resulting lineages retained the expected architecture.

One can also ask what a quantitative model is intended to explain. A fitted spatial curve can summarize the relationship between distance and pathway output, but it cannot establish whether the relationship results from free diffusion, ligand capture, polarized receptor localization, altered degradation or other processes. A simple model may correctly predict a fate arrangement while leaving the transport mechanism uncertain. Similarly, a graph showing a correlation between the level of Notch activity and secondary fate does not by itself prove that Notch causes that fate. Causal perturbations are needed alongside measurements. This discipline is valuable for Part C questions, where a graph is often evidence for one inference but insufficient to support a stronger claim.

10. From specified precursors to a shaped epithelial organ

The specification of three vulval lineages is a major developmental achievement, but the animal still lacks a completed organ at that point. A lineage pattern is a sequence of divisions and identities. Morphogenesis is the process through which the descendants acquire an organized three-dimensional arrangement. The vulva is particularly instructive because the later events can be described in relation to the earlier primary and secondary decisions. The descendants of P6.p contribute centrally, whereas those of P5.p and P7.p contribute to the flanking regions.

The three induced precursors undergo characteristic divisions. Primary and secondary lineages differ in their final division behavior, including whether particular descendants divide longitudinally, transversely or not at all. Longitudinal divisions are oriented along the anterior-posterior axis, whereas transverse divisions occur in a different orientation across that axis. Such differences affect which descendants lie beside one another and how they can subsequently assemble into rings. The number of descendants is therefore not merely a measure of cell proliferation. It is linked to spatial organization.

The primary lineage ordinarily contributes eight cells. The two secondary lineages contribute seven each. Their combined twenty-two descendants undergo remodeling in the developing epithelial tissue. The cells differentiate into seven vulval cell types, commonly designated vulA, vulB1, vulB2, vulC, vulD, vulE and vulF. The repeated letters with numbered forms identify distinct epithelial types, not different stages of a single signaling cascade. The central region includes the vulE and vulF contributions, whereas the secondary lineages provide the other types. Later cell fusion helps establish the characteristic stack of ring-like structures.

A toroid is a ring-shaped three-dimensional structure with a central opening. In the mature vulval epithelium, a stack of such ring-like arrangements provides a path through the tissue. The term is useful for visualizing the relationship between cells at different levels of the passage. It does not mean that each precursor independently generates a complete donut-shaped organ. Descendants migrate and organize with one another, establish junctions and, in appropriate cases, fuse into syncytial structures. The ring geometry is an emergent property of coordinated epithelial remodeling.

The primary-lineage descendants occupy the inner portion of the developing organ and contribute to the upward invagination toward the gonadal tissues. The secondary lineages help shape the outer regions and establish the connection of the developing epithelium with the ventral body surface. Differences in adhesion, cell shape, polarity and position must be coordinated. A cell that correctly expresses a fate-specific gene but fails to alter its shape or adhesion can still disrupt the lumen or the arrangement of the rings. This distinction separates an identity defect from a morphogenetic defect.

Apical and basal domains are essential to understanding an epithelial passage. An apical surface generally faces a lumen or the exterior, while the basal domain interacts with the supporting tissues or extracellular matrix. Junctions between epithelial cells help maintain tissue integrity and organize the boundaries between domains. In the developing vulva, the cells must rearrange without losing the coordinated epithelial organization needed for a functional passage. The same general principle operates in many animal organs, even though their geometries and molecular regulators differ.

The anchor cell has an additional later role in connecting the developing reproductive passage to the vulval epithelium. Invasion of the intervening basement membrane and coordination with uterine tissues help establish a physical linkage. This is conceptually distinct from its earlier production of an inductive EGF signal. The basement membrane is a specialized extracellular matrix separating or supporting epithelial and other tissues. Local remodeling of this barrier is needed where the tissues become connected. The animal must coordinate a change in extracellular architecture with the remodeling of the epithelial descendants.

The distinction between an early and a late function can be understood through a temporal intervention. If the anchor cell is removed before induction, the expected initial primary and secondary fates fail to be specified normally. If a perturbation instead selectively disrupts a later connection-forming process after the vulval lineages have already been specified, the cells may still execute much of the normal lineage but fail to establish a functioning passage. These outcomes should not be classified as identical simply because both animals may have difficulty laying eggs.

A well-formed vulva is also not sufficient for every aspect of egg-laying behavior. The mature physiological process requires coordinated muscles, neurons and reproductive tissues, which are outside the main scope of this chapter. The distinction is important in phenotype analysis because adult egg retention can result from abnormalities in any of those components. To establish that a mutation specifically affects vulval organogenesis, one must examine the developing epidermal structure, its lineage and its connection with the reproductive tract, not infer it solely from an adult behavioral defect.

11. Spatial organization, division polarity and biological mechanics

The growth of an epithelium creates geometrical constraints that are not solved merely by making enough cells. Cells are physically adjacent, and each division introduces new cell interfaces. The orientation of the division influences where daughter cells are placed relative to neighboring cells and the prospective lumen. A longitudinal division creates daughters at different positions along the main body axis; a differently oriented division can change the arrangement across that axis. These consequences are easiest to understand by drawing the two daughter cells in relation to the surrounding tissue rather than memorizing letters in a lineage chart.

The need for different division patterns becomes apparent when comparing a flat sheet with a tube. A tube has a lumen surrounded by a continuous epithelial boundary. Cells that are to form successive portions of the tube must be positioned and shaped appropriately relative to that lumen. If all divisions and movements occurred in the same direction, the expanding cell sheet would not automatically produce the proper ring architecture. The secondary lineages contribute peripheral structures that differ in topology from those supplied by the primary lineage. The fate programs thus constrain not only gene expression but also the spatial behaviors of descendants.

Cell adhesion helps determine which cells stay connected while they change shape. A junction is not an immutable weld; its component proteins can be reorganized as tissues develop. Appropriate remodeling of contacts permits coordinated movements without requiring cells to lose epithelial identity entirely. Cell fusion represents a more extensive change than junction remodeling because the plasma membranes between two cells cease to separate their cytoplasm. The resulting syncytial architecture is a distinct form of organization. The fusion events associated with normal vulval morphogenesis should not be confused with premature fusion of a precursor into hyp7, which removes its opportunity to participate in the vulval lineage.

Changes in cell shape require forces. The cytoskeleton supplies internal structural organization, and the interaction of cells with neighbors and extracellular matrix constrains how these forces can change tissue geometry. ATP-dependent actin-associated processes and membrane trafficking contribute to the dynamic behavior of epithelial cells in general. For examination purposes it is important to understand the distinction between biochemical control of a developmental program and physical execution of that program. There is no requirement to assign a numerical force or energy value to every cell movement without experimentally measured material properties.

When a gene involved in the control of fate also changes adhesion or cytoskeletal behavior, the resulting phenotype may involve both specification and morphogenesis. A carefully designed analysis can distinguish the two. First assess early molecular markers and precursor lineages before visible invagination. Then examine whether the expected cells occupy their correct relative locations and whether the epithelial rings and lumen are continuous. A mutation that leaves early identity markers and lineage counts normal but disrupts later epithelial arrangement is more directly implicated in execution of shape than in the initial assignment of fate.

The problem of cell number likewise needs careful interpretation. The normal count of twenty-two descendants is measured at a particular stage, before subsequent fusions change the number of independent cytoplasmic compartments. A count of twenty-two nuclei after fusion does not necessarily mean there are twenty-two separate cells. A nucleus can remain present in a syncytium. When a question reports a number, it should specify whether it counts nuclei, separate cells, precursor lineages or structures such as rings. Without that distinction a numerical comparison can imply a developmental defect where there is none.

One can reason about an abnormal lineage without memorizing every name in a complete nematode cell atlas. If the central precursor executes only two rounds of complete binary division rather than the usual production of eight descendants, the immediate expectation is a shortfall of central epithelial cells. The effect on organ shape will depend on which descendants are missing and on any compensation, not simply on the arithmetic difference. If an adjacent precursor instead changes its identity from secondary to primary, both cell number and topology can change. This is why equal totals do not guarantee equivalent organs. The distribution of lineages and identities is a separate biological quantity.

The anterior and posterior secondary lineages normally execute mirror-related patterns of divisions and differentiation. Wnt-dependent regulation helps orient these lineages, and the Wnt receptors LIN-17 and LIN-18 are important examples in the control of posterior secondary-lineage polarity. A precursor can therefore be correctly specified as secondary yet execute an abnormal orientation of its lineage if the polarity machinery is defective. The distinction between selecting a fate and orienting the behaviors associated with that fate is necessary for interpreting mutants with altered symmetry.

The primary lineage itself contains more than one terminal identity. Four vulE and four vulF descendants are normally generated; local signals from the anchor cell and Wnt-dependent regulation help pattern the primary descendants. The vulF cells contribute to the innermost region and contact the uterus, while vulE cells contribute to lateral structural attachments. This additional level of patterning shows that the primary label is not equivalent to a single terminal cell type. Once a precursor is specified, its descendants can receive further information that differentiates their functions within the organ.

Kimble's 1981 laser-ablation study provides historical context for the role of the gonad in induction. Later work showed that disrupting the anchor cell at an appropriate postinduction stage can impair morphology even when the expected number of vulval cells develops. These experiments distinguish the earlier inductive function from later tissue organization. They also establish why an accurate organogenesis analysis must record the developmental stage at which ablation occurs and not simply list the name of the removed cell.

12. Genetic perturbations and the meaning of Vul and Muv

A genetic screen starts with a phenotype and asks which genes contribute to the biological process that normally prevents or produces it. The vulva provides two contrasting classes of abnormal development. A vulvaless phenotype, abbreviated Vul, indicates a failure to form normal vulval structures. A multivulva phenotype, abbreviated Muv, indicates the production of ectopic vulval-like structures or induction beyond the normal group. These labels describe visible outcomes rather than a unique molecular cause. Different mutations can produce a similar gross phenotype, and the exact cellular effects of a particular allele may vary with its activity and genetic background.

A loss-of-function allele reduces the normal activity of a gene product. When a positive component of the inductive pathway loses function, its effect can be a reduced ability of otherwise competent cells to acquire vulval fate. For example, an appropriately strong reduction in LIN-3 production prevents the ordinary input to LET-23. A reduction in LET-23 activity can prevent reception even when the anchor cell is present and produces its ligand. A defect in a necessary component of the intracellular Ras/MAPK cascade can impair the response even after ligand binding. Each defect can therefore yield underinduction, but they disrupt different steps in the causal sequence.

A gain-of-function allele increases activity or produces activity in a new manner or location. A classic way to alter Ras-family signaling is to reduce the ability of the active nucleotide-bound form to return to its inactive state. Persistent Ras activity can then promote induction even when upstream stimulation is reduced. In the nematode, activated forms of LET-60 can cause excessive vulval induction. The outcome depends on the allele, the context of the affected cells and the remaining pathways. The term activated Ras does not mean that a new group of competent precursors has been created throughout the entire animal; it means that a signaling switch is unusually active in cells where the relevant developmental machinery exists.

A negative regulator can produce the opposite genetic logic. If the product of a gene normally restrains vulval induction, removing its activity may increase induction. One example of this general principle is regulation of the Ras GTPase cycle by proteins that promote GTP hydrolysis. With less effective inactivation, signaling can persist for longer after a stimulus. A phosphatase that restrains an activating phosphorylation can similarly influence the duration or amplitude of a pathway output. The absence of a negative regulator need not always produce a conspicuous phenotype on its own because other restraints may compensate. This is why genetic combinations are often more informative than the phenotype of a single mutation.

The synthetic multivulva genes illustrate the difference between independent-looking mutations and a combined effect. The term synthetic phenotype refers to a phenotype that becomes evident or more severe when mutations are combined, even though the individual mutations have little or less pronounced effect under the same conditions. In the vulval system, classes of synMuv genes contribute to restricting inappropriate inductive signaling. Certain combinations of mutations allow ectopic expression of lin-3 in the surrounding epidermis, providing an abnormal source of inductive input. Competent cells can then receive signals that are not restricted to the normal region near the gonadal anchor cell.

An ectopic source of ligand is not identical to an activated receptor or an activated Ras protein. The ectopic ligand still needs access to an appropriate receptor and the machinery downstream of it. If receptor activity is eliminated in the responding cells, merely producing the ligand from an additional tissue may not rescue induction. In contrast, an active downstream Ras allele may bypass an upstream deficiency under suitable conditions. These distinctions allow apparently similar Muv phenotypes to be divided into mechanisms involving abnormal signal production, altered reception, altered intracellular regulation or failure to restrict the developmental field.

The lateral pathway produces another kind of phenotypic complexity. If LIN-12 cannot signal in prospective secondary cells, their normal secondary differentiation can fail. This does not justify assigning a single replacement fate to both cells in every genotype. The amount of inductive signaling reaching them and the state of other regulatory components influence whether they acquire a competing identity or remain nonvulval. Conversely, activated LIN-12 in competent epidermal precursors can favor secondary specification independently of normal ligand delivery. For an unqualified whole-animal LIN-12 mutation, changes in the earlier anchor-cell versus ventral uterine decision can complicate the outcome. A good examination problem either states the stage and tissue of manipulation or supplies additional evidence about the gonadal cells.

The adult appearance of extra protrusions is useful for screening, but a protrusion is not proof of an additional complete functional vulva. An ectopic group of induced epidermal cells may form vulval-like tissue without connecting to the uterus or acquiring the normal central and flanking arrangement. The correct interpretation of a Muv phenotype is excessive or ectopic vulval development, not necessarily multiple fully functional reproductive passages. Likewise, the absence of a recognizable adult opening may reflect abnormal morphogenesis even when early induction occurred. This motivates direct observation of individual precursors and their descendants in addition to scoring adult morphology.

13. Epistasis, rescue and locating an affected developmental step

Genetic epistasis is the modification of the phenotypic effect of one genetic change by another. In a simple linear pathway, combining an upstream loss-of-function mutation with a downstream constitutively active allele may reveal which component can bypass the other. The reasoning starts from an explicit model: the upstream component is needed to activate the downstream component, while the downstream component controls the response. If artificial activation makes the downstream component independent of the upstream signal, then the upstream requirement for that particular response may be bypassed.

Consider a simplified pathway with LIN-3 upstream of LET-23 and LET-60, followed by a functional MAP kinase cascade. An allele that prevents LIN-3 production is expected to reduce induction, while an allele that supplies constitutively active LET-60 in competent precursors may increase induction. If combining the two restores an inductive response, the result supports the idea that LET-60 can act downstream of the missing signal. It does not prove that every biological function of LIN-3 is mediated exclusively through that Ras activity. The conclusion concerns the assayed outcome under the conditions of the experiment.

The reverse experiment tests another logical relationship. Suppose a positive kinase downstream of Ras is made inactive in the relevant precursors. Increasing upstream receptor activity should not restore a response that requires that missing kinase, unless another route can supply the necessary output. The failure to rescue is consistent with a downstream requirement. However, it can also occur because the constitutively active allele was not sufficiently expressed, because the cells lost competence, or because the assay does not actually measure the anticipated response. These alternative explanations are why the genotypes and the controls matter.

The same strategy can be adapted to lateral signaling, but it cannot simply collapse the network into one linear chain. The central epidermal cell responds to induction and produces ligands that act on a different cell. In the recipient, the LIN-12 pathway influences a different fate and restrains competing EGF output. A whole-animal double mutant can alter both sending and receiving functions simultaneously. To interpret such a result, it is necessary to specify which cell carries each altered gene product. A mutation in the sending cell and the same mutation in the receiving cell may have very different effects on the pattern even when the gene is expressed in both.

Cell-autonomous action means that the function of a gene is required within the cell whose particular behavior is being examined. Non-cell-autonomous action refers to an effect mediated through other cells or their products. A gene required to produce LIN-3 in the anchor cell affects epidermal fates non-cell-autonomously with respect to those epidermal cells. LET-23 reception is a function within the responding precursor. A gene promoting expression of a lateral ligand in P6.p can affect P5.p and P7.p non-cell-autonomously. These definitions are relative to a specified outcome; a protein can have autonomous roles in one context and influence another cell's behavior in another context.

Rescue experiments provide a means of testing the compartment in which a gene is required. If an animal with impaired receptor function is provided with a functional receptor specifically in the prospective responding precursors, restoration of their response supports a receptor requirement in those cells. Expressing that receptor only in the anchor cell would not generally be expected to substitute for its necessary receptor function in the receiving epidermis. The interpretation depends on the specificity and timing of expression. A promoter presumed to act only in one tissue must actually do so for the inference about autonomy to be justified.

Similarly, rescue of an inductive ligand deficiency by restoring LIN-3 expression in the anchor cell supports a role for that source. If ligand expressed from an alternative tissue induces ectopic cells, this can demonstrate a degree of sufficiency in the new location without establishing that the new tissue normally produces the signal. A rescue may use an expression level higher than the normal level and could therefore bypass a physiological control. Restoration of an endpoint is valuable evidence, but does not automatically prove that the rescued animal uses precisely the wild-type molecular mechanism.

Mosaic analysis exploits differences in genetic constitution among cells of an animal. A mosaic can reveal a requirement for a gene in one tissue while other tissues retain a different genotype. In a simplified examination example, a mutant anchor cell is paired with genetically normal epidermal precursors; induction fails. A second mosaic has a normal anchor cell but mutant precursors; induction also fails. The two results suggest distinct requirements in source and target, but to assign particular molecules to these requirements one must know which gene is altered in each case. If the same mutation affects multiple cell properties, additional assays may be needed to decide whether it disrupts signal transmission directly or the development of the cells themselves.

A strong genetic argument combines predictions rather than interpreting one endpoint in isolation. For instance, a mutant displays normal precursor maintenance, normal anchor-cell LIN-3 expression, but weak early MAP kinase output in epidermal cells. Tissue-specific restoration of LET-23 corrects the reporter and lineage pattern. These observations together support a defect in reception more specifically than an adult Vul phenotype alone. The strength of the conclusion derives from the correspondence between the predicted location of a defect and independent measurements of source, target and developmental response.

14. Quantitative reasoning: spatial input and response thresholds

A useful quantitative model of positional information must specify both the variable measured and the assumptions that connect it to cell behavior. In the vulval field, one might represent the effective inductive input by a quantity S(x), where x is distance from a localized source along a simplified axis. For a classroom model, assume that the source is fixed, the surrounding medium is uniform, and the signal undergoes first-order removal. The model is intended to illustrate a spatial decay, not to reproduce measured molecular concentrations in the worm.

Diffusion tends to move a substance from regions of higher concentration toward regions of lower concentration. In a one-dimensional description the diffusive flux is proportional to the negative spatial gradient of concentration, with proportionality constant D. If distance is measured in micrometres and time in seconds, D has units of square micrometres per second. First-order removal is described by a rate constant k with units of inverse seconds. Its contribution to the local rate of change of concentration is minus k times concentration. The two terms together give a diffusion-removal equation away from the source.

At steady state the time derivative is zero, so D times the second spatial derivative of concentration equals k times concentration. Trying a solution proportional to the exponential of minus x divided by a length lambda gives a second derivative equal to the original concentration divided by lambda squared. Substitution yields D divided by lambda squared equal to k, and therefore lambda equals the square root of D divided by k. The units are micrometres, since D divided by k has units of square micrometres. The decaying exponential is selected on a semi-infinite domain where the concentration does not diverge far from the source. The source boundary determines the concentration at the origin.

The resulting expression S(x) = S0 exp(-x/lambda) is a useful representation when the measured variable is proportional to the idealized concentration. In a symmetric one-dimensional approximation with a source at x = 0, the distance is the absolute value of position. A large characteristic length produces a shallow decline; a small characteristic length produces a steeper decline. Doubling D while keeping k fixed increases lambda by the square root of two, not by two. Doubling k decreases lambda by the square root of two. These relations distinguish a parameter that controls spatial range from the value of the signal at the source.

In the real nematode, LIN-3 delivery and LET-23 reception occur in a structured tissue with finite boundaries, oriented membrane surfaces and receptor-dependent interactions. The relationship between extracellular ligand and intracellular activity may be nonlinear. Consequently, the classroom exponential should not be used to claim a particular physical diffusion coefficient or measured LIN-3 half-life. It illustrates how a spatial input could be converted into a pattern and how a change in range could shift the set of cells above a threshold.

Consider a hypothetical field in which the central cell lies at distance zero in the one-dimensional approximation, the other cells are spaced five micrometres apart, S0 is 100 arbitrary signal units and lambda is five micrometres. The central value is 100. At five micrometres the value is 100 times exp(-1), approximately 36.8. At ten micrometres the value is 100 times exp(-2), approximately 13.5. At fifteen micrometres it is 100 times exp(-3), approximately 5.0. The distances are illustrative axial distances and do not imply that the actual anchor cell touches P6.p or that the biological field has these exact dimensions.

Suppose a simple graded threshold model assigns primary fate to values of at least 60 units, secondary fate to values of at least 25 but below 60 units, and tertiary fate to values below 25 units. With the assumed positions of P3.p to P8.p relative to P6.p, the distances are fifteen, ten, five, zero, five and ten micrometres. The resulting signal sequence is about 5.0, 13.5, 36.8, 100, 36.8 and 13.5 units. The threshold rule reproduces the normal tertiary, tertiary, secondary, primary, secondary, tertiary arrangement. This establishes only that a particular model is capable of reproducing the observed pattern, not that the real cells apply the stated thresholds.

Now increase lambda to ten micrometres without changing S0 or the thresholds. The values at five, ten and fifteen micrometres become about 60.7, 36.8 and 22.3 units. Under the same independent-cell rule, the cells immediately beside P6.p would now cross the primary threshold, and the cells ten micrometres away would cross the secondary threshold. The outermost P3.p remains below the secondary threshold. This prediction demonstrates sensitivity to signal range. It does not establish that such a change in an actual animal would necessarily create three adjacent primary cells because the lateral network could suppress some of those competing outcomes.

A second simple quantitative relationship concerns receptor occupancy. If a ligand binds independently to identical receptors at equilibrium with one binding site per receptor, the fractional occupancy can be represented by theta = L/(Kd + L), where L is the free ligand concentration and Kd is the equilibrium dissociation constant in the same concentration units. This follows from the definition Kd = concentrations of free receptor times free ligand divided by the concentration of bound receptor, with total receptor equal to free plus bound receptor. Substituting these relations and solving for the fraction bound yields the expression. The assumptions exclude cooperative binding and complex receptor activation kinetics.

For a hypothetical Kd of ten nanomolar, a ligand concentration of ten nanomolar gives occupancy of one-half. At thirty nanomolar, occupancy is three-quarters. A threefold increase in concentration therefore does not give a threefold increase in fractional occupancy. At high concentrations the fraction approaches one, illustrating saturation. If two precursors have different receptor abundance, the same fractional occupancy can still correspond to different absolute numbers of bound receptors. Receptor localization and the downstream kinase network can further alter the response. Thus occupancy is a molecular input to signaling, not a direct equation for final vulval fate.

These models illustrate the proper place of mathematics in the chapter. The calculation of a threshold crossing develops reasoning about position; the calculation of receptor occupancy develops reasoning about molecular sensitivity. Neither should be presented as a fitted description of this animal without appropriate data. For experimental questions, it is important to separate variables inferred from a mathematical model from measurements obtained directly through a reporter or a lineage observation. This prevents a numerical answer from being mistaken for a biological proof.

15. Reading perturbation data and distinguishing competing explanations

A developmental experiment can be summarized at several levels. One investigator may record the proportion of animals with normal vulval patterning, another may record the number of induced precursors per animal, and a third may record the frequency of each fate at each position. These summaries are not interchangeable. Two samples can have the same mean number of induced precursors but very different spatial patterns. A robust developmental analysis retains information about the identity and location of cells, especially when the scientific question concerns lateral signaling.

Imagine two hypothetical groups of forty animals. In the control group thirty-eight display the standard six-position fate arrangement and two show another arrangement. In the experimental group twenty-four show the standard arrangement and sixteen show another. The frequency of the standard pattern is thirty-eight divided by forty, or ninety-five percent, in the control and twenty-four divided by forty, or sixty percent, in the experimental group. The difference is thirty-five percentage points. This measures a change in pattern fidelity, not necessarily a change of the same size in average MAP kinase activity or the number of cells formed.

The underlying cell-level distributions would provide essential context. If the sixteen abnormal experimental animals retain one primary precursor but lack one or both normal secondary fates, a lateral-signaling defect becomes a plausible hypothesis. If their induced cells are missing altogether, a reduced inductive response or competence defect becomes more plausible. If the correct fate markers appear but the ring geometry is abnormal, a later morphogenesis defect deserves attention. Each interpretation predicts different observations that can be collected at earlier stages.

Suppose a second hypothetical experiment uses a reporter of early EGF pathway activity in the prospective primary cell and a reporter of LIN-12-dependent transcription in the two neighboring cells. A control animal shows both reporter classes. In a test animal the primary reporter remains strong, whereas the neighboring reporter is weak. This observation indicates that an early aspect of primary signaling is preserved while the lateral response is impaired. Possible mechanisms include insufficient production of lateral ligands, ineffective LIN-12 receptor function, or impaired transcriptional response downstream of LIN-12. The reporter pattern alone cannot choose among these alternatives.

A tissue-specific rescue can sharpen the interpretation. Restoring a functional LIN-12 receptor in the receiving cells while preserving the same ligand-producing cell might correct a receptor-deficient phenotype. If no rescue occurs, it could be because the ligand is missing or because downstream functions remain defective. A constitutively active intracellular Notch domain would test a different relationship because it can bypass the need for ordinary extracellular activation. The comparison is informative only if the introduced constructs are expressed in the intended cells at the relevant stage and if their activity is sufficient to test the hypothesis.

A reporter measures a chosen molecular event. A fluorescent transcriptional reporter, for example, can integrate expression over time because fluorescent protein maturation and degradation may be slower than the signaling events being studied. Brightness at a late stage need not correspond exactly to the current activity of the promoter. A localization reporter and an activity reporter likewise have different meanings. An experiment that seeks to establish the order of two pathway activations must use readouts with temporal resolution adequate for the biological question. This is an example of the general principle that the method imposes limits on the inference.

Biological variability also requires attention. If three of forty mutant animals show an unexpected fate conversion, the observed frequency is seven and a half percent. It does not establish that the remaining animals lack all molecular effects of the mutation. The phenotype may show incomplete penetrance, meaning that not every individual carrying the relevant genotype displays the scored phenotype. Expressivity describes differences in how a phenotype is manifested among individuals. These ideas help interpret developmental mutants whose outcomes depend on small variations in pathway activity, developmental stage or genetic background.

For a test of pathway interaction, include appropriate single-perturbation controls as well as the combined perturbation. If each individual change produces a mild effect and the combination produces a severe effect, redundancy or cooperation becomes a plausible explanation. Redundancy does not imply that the molecules perform precisely the same biochemical task. Different mechanisms can compensate for one another at the level of a shared outcome. A quantitative departure from an assumed independent-effect model can support an interaction, but the definition of independence must be specified before a statistical comparison can establish it. At examination level, identifying the appropriate control and explaining the direction of the result is usually more useful than applying an unjustified significance calculation.

16. Developmental timing, signal duration and commitment

The sequence of events in vulval development gives the organism a means of separating the creation of a responsive field from the assignment of identities within it. A competent cell in L2 has not necessarily received the same instructive information as that cell during L3. The eventual fate can be changed by a perturbation during the appropriate interval even though its normal developmental outcome is predictable. Once the cell and its descendants have entered a more stable program, the same perturbation may have a weaker or different effect. This difference between early responsiveness and later stability is the basis of an experimental investigation of commitment.

A pulse of signal is not always equivalent to continued signal exposure. At the molecular level, receptor activation, nucleotide exchange, phosphorylation, gene transcription and protein turnover occur on different timescales. A very short input may activate a kinase without allowing its downstream transcriptional effects to accumulate. A longer input can produce additional consequences even if the maximum kinase activity is not higher. Conversely, negative feedback may reduce pathway output during continued stimulation. The concentration of an inductive ligand at one instant is therefore not necessarily a complete description of the information received over a developmental interval.

For a simple illustration, suppose an immediate reporter rises to the same maximum value in two hypothetical precursors. In one cell the reporter stays high for two minutes, whereas in the other it stays high for ten minutes. If a downstream response requires an accumulated signal over time, the second cell can have a larger effective exposure despite the identical peak. An integral of activity over time would distinguish the two idealized inputs. If activity is reported in arbitrary units, the integral has units of arbitrary units multiplied by time. This is not a claim that vulval fate is calculated by a single known integration rule. It clarifies why a developmental experiment that records only peak activity can miss information that depends on duration.

The notion of commitment is also distinct from the persistence of a reporter. A fluorescent protein may remain detectable after a signaling pathway is no longer active, because the protein has not yet been degraded. A developmental identity can instead persist through continuing interactions among regulatory genes or through changes in cellular architecture. To establish that a fate has become stable, one needs to test the ability of the cell to maintain its outcome under an altered environment at a specified developmental stage. Reporter persistence alone does not establish irreversible determination.

An informative timing experiment would compare an equivalent perturbation delivered before primary signaling, after primary signaling but during lateral communication, and after the lineages have entered their characteristic division programs. If the earliest perturbation blocks all induced lineages and the intermediate perturbation selectively disrupts secondary identity, the result supports a difference in temporal requirements. If the late perturbation alters morphology without changing the original fate distribution, it identifies a later developmental function. The experiment must distinguish the time of intervention from the time at which the intervention actually changes protein activity. A mutation switched on at a particular time may not remove existing protein instantaneously.

There is a further complication in interpreting experiments that remove the anchor cell late. The anchor cell participates in later connection of the reproductive passage to the vulval epithelium. An animal with a normal early fate pattern but a defective late connection cannot be taken as evidence that induction was independent of the anchor cell at every stage. The early requirement may have been completed before the intervention, while the later requirement was still active. This example captures a general principle of developmental genetics: a cell or gene may be necessary at more than one stage, and a stage-specific experiment must assign the outcome to the appropriate role.

The timing of cell divisions also affects communication. A precursor cell has a different geometry and set of neighbors after it divides than before division. A lateral signal sent during initial fate selection need not reach precisely the same membrane contacts as one sent later. Development thus alters the geometry through which further development proceeds. A causal account of organ formation should specify the cell names and contacts at the stage being considered rather than treating the larva as an unchanging diagram of six boxes.

17. Conservation and evolutionary variation in organ formation

C. elegans is a model of development partly because many components of its signaling pathways have recognizable relatives in other animals. EGF-like ligands, receptor tyrosine kinases, Ras-family switches, MAP kinase cascades and Notch-family receptors are familiar molecular systems in comparative developmental biology. Conservation does not mean that all organs use these components in identical sequences. It means that related molecular machinery has been retained and adapted across evolutionary history. In the vulva, those systems can be examined in the context of a small and well-defined group of precursors.

The use of a receptor-Ras-MAPK pathway in the nematode illustrates the distinction between biochemical conservation and developmental identity. A vertebrate cell can use a related pathway to regulate a different process from that of a vulval precursor. The signaling cascade supplies a controlled means of changing intracellular regulatory activity. The identity of the cell, its accessible regulatory genes, its signaling environment and its stage of development determine which consequence follows. A claim that the human counterpart of a signaling gene directly specifies a nematode-like vulval lineage in a vertebrate would therefore be biologically meaningless.

Notch provides an equally useful comparison. During development of the Drosophila nervous system, interactions involving Notch help resolve different identities among neighboring cells. In the worm vulva, related signaling promotes secondary fates beside a primary fate and suppresses competing primary specification. The common principle is coordination of cellular choices through local communication. The concrete outcomes are different. A comparison should identify the shared regulatory logic without substituting the vocabulary of one tissue for the cell types of another.

Related nematodes provide an even closer comparative test. Studies of species related to C. elegans reveal differences in the positions of vulval precursor contributions, the relative dependence on gonadal induction and details of lineage execution. Some nematodes develop a vulva in a more posterior region and use different arrangements of signaling and cell migration. An adult anatomical feature can remain recognizable while the developmental route used to produce it changes. This observation is relevant to the idea of developmental system drift: comparable outcomes can be maintained despite evolutionary changes in their underlying developmental mechanisms.

An evolutionary comparison therefore requires more than showing that two species possess the same named gene. The first question is whether the genes are evolutionarily related. The next is whether their products function in comparable biochemical reactions. A further question concerns where and when the genes act during development. A homologous signaling pathway might be activated by a different tissue, used at another stage or connected to different fate regulators. Conservation of the core pathway is compatible with changes in the geometry, cellular source and regulation of its activity.

The normal worm pattern should also be distinguished from a rigid ideal of developmental invariance. The six named precursors do not all contribute to the vulva in the wild-type arrangement, and P3.p can vary in its early division and fusion behavior. Such variation does not necessarily destroy the final organ. It is possible for one feature of a developmental system to be variable while a functionally important feature remains stable. The observation invites the student to ask which outcome is under strong developmental constraint rather than assuming that every cell behaves identically in every healthy animal.

Comparative biology also clarifies why compensatory developmental potential is not equivalent to unlimited regeneration. A distal precursor may replace an absent prospective vulval contributor during the competence interval. That capacity does not imply that all adult vulval tissues can reconstruct themselves after arbitrary injury or that every differentiated epidermal nucleus can freely produce a new vulva. Developmental regulation within an available precursor field differs from regeneration of a damaged mature organ. The next relevant comparisons in this unit involve induction of the vertebrate lens and, later, limb development and regeneration, where competence, geometry and signal sources must again be considered in their specific biological context.

18. Using the vulval model to investigate developmental regulation

The central value of a model organism is that it allows a complex biological question to be divided into experimentally approachable components. In the worm vulva, the position of the responding cells is known, the lineages can be followed and different signaling perturbations produce distinguishable patterns. This makes it possible to evaluate the relationship between a change in a molecular pathway and a particular developmental choice. The system can be used to teach gene function, intercellular communication and organ formation within a single coherent example.

A genetic screen for altered vulval development can identify components of a pathway that would not be apparent from sequence comparison alone. An altered gene may encode a ligand, a receptor, a signaling enzyme, a transcriptional regulator or a protein needed for cellular architecture. Examining the affected stage and testing its relationship to known components can narrow the possibilities. A suppressor screen starts with an existing phenotype and seeks additional changes that reduce it. An enhancer screen seeks additional changes that intensify it. Both strategies can reveal regulatory relationships, but suppression is not automatically proof of direct molecular binding. Two genes may act in distinct pathways that converge on a shared outcome.

Fluorescent reporters and tagged proteins can connect a molecular event to a known precursor. For example, one type of reporter can indicate transcription driven by a promoter responsive to the inductive pathway. Another can indicate the localization of a receptor at a particular membrane domain. A third can report the identity of a differentiated descendant. Combining these observations with the cell lineage can show whether a molecular change precedes, accompanies or follows a particular fate outcome. The most informative interpretation uses a reporter that is suited to the causal question rather than assuming any fluorescent signal is a universal measurement of pathway activity.

Genetic rescue with related proteins can also be informative about conservation, provided the experimental context is carefully specified. If a related protein can restore a molecular or developmental function in an appropriate test, the result supports functional similarity for the measured process. A failure to rescue is harder to interpret because differences in expression level, cellular localization or interactions with other components may interfere. Such experiments help distinguish functional conservation from the weaker observation that two DNA sequences are similar. They do not establish that the entire developmental programs of the two organisms are interchangeable.

The relationship of Ras and receptor tyrosine kinase signaling to uncontrolled growth in other contexts is another reason the worm system is scientifically valuable. It permits researchers to examine the consequences of excessive signaling and failure of negative regulation at the level of identified cells. Nevertheless, an ectopic vulval protrusion in a nematode is not equivalent to a human malignant tumor. Cancer involves additional features including the behavior of transformed cells within tissues, and often invasion and other acquired capabilities. The worm phenotype is useful as a model of signaling regulation, not as a complete representation of human disease. This boundary keeps the developmental example connected to, but distinct from, the Academy's cancer and cell signaling units.

The most general research lesson is that a pathway diagram is an experimentally testable explanation, not merely a list of proteins. Its arrows imply consequences of interventions. If the diagram places ligand before receptor and receptor before Ras, it predicts that receptor impairment affects response to ligand and that suitable downstream activation can sometimes bypass an upstream failure. A lateral arrow between two cells predicts that a change in the sender can affect the identity of the receiver. When an observed phenotype violates a prediction, the model needs refinement or the assumptions of the intervention need examination. This is the scientific reasoning that makes the vulval system valuable for examination preparation as well as for developmental research.

19. Integrating identity, tissue architecture and causal reasoning

The completed conceptual account can be approached from the viewpoint of a single precursor. P5.p begins as a member of the ventral competence group. Its persistence as an independent cell depends on the developmental machinery that prevents premature fusion. During induction it is exposed to local signals from the gonad, but its position is not the same as that of P6.p. The latter receives a strong primary-inducing input and begins to communicate with its immediate neighbors. P5.p can interpret the lateral input through LIN-12 and alter its own regulatory state, favoring the secondary program while restraining a competing primary program.

The subsequent lineage of P5.p depends on the successful execution of that program. Its descendants contribute to the outer parts of the vulval epithelial structure. They must coordinate their divisions, positions, contacts and fusions with descendants of other precursors. The fate decision provides a developmental starting point for these behaviors but does not perform them mechanically. If the initial LIN-12 response is preserved and the later epithelial behavior fails, the defect lies beyond the mere acquisition of secondary identity. This single-cell perspective connects signaling and morphogenesis without confusing the two.

A complementary view begins with the organ's required function. An opening must connect the internal reproductive passage with the exterior at the appropriate position. To achieve that topology, the animal needs central and flanking contributions rather than an arbitrary cluster of extra induced cells. The positioning of the anchor cell helps coordinate the site of induction with the internal gonad. The neighboring signaling interactions coordinate the epithelial domains around that site. Later tissue remodeling and connection convert those domains into a passage. Each level of organization imposes requirements that cannot be inferred merely by counting early molecular signals.

A common analytical task is to work backward from a phenotype. If a question states that all competent precursors remain present but none displays an inductive response, the earliest plausible defect lies in production, reception or intracellular interpretation of the inductive signal. If one primary lineage is formed normally and the flanking lineages fail to express a secondary marker, attention shifts toward lateral communication or its execution. If the normal twenty-two descendants are produced but their rings fail to organize, attention shifts toward morphogenesis. If the epithelial organ is correctly arranged but the connection to the uterus is absent, a defect in late tissue linkage is plausible. These inferences are conditional rather than absolute, and each can be refined by a specific molecular assay.

When several abnormalities occur together, the developmental sequence helps distinguish primary and secondary consequences. Loss of early competence can prevent later induction and morphogenesis simply because the required responding cells no longer exist. A primary fate defect can also reduce the lateral signal and therefore impair neighboring secondary fates. A mutation that affects all three readouts need not act independently at all three stages. The earlier defect may be causally sufficient to account for the later failures. Testing for preservation of intermediate steps is therefore more informative than compiling a long list of endpoints affected by the mutation.

An examination problem may also present a normal pattern after a molecular perturbation. This does not necessarily show that the perturbed component never contributes to normal development. Its role may be redundant, the perturbation may be partial, or the system may compensate within the range tested. Conversely, a dramatic phenotype caused by forcing a protein to be active at an unnatural level does not demonstrate that the same level occurs physiologically. Both positive and negative results must be interpreted in the context of the specific intervention and the outcome being scored.

The vulval example connects earlier principles of cell potency, induction, positional information and gene regulation to a complete organ-forming system. It also prepares for the study of vertebrate lens induction, where the identities and competence of responding tissues again constrain the outcome of signals from neighboring tissues. The next example uses a different geometry and different molecular regulators, but it asks the same fundamental developmental question: how can interactions among tissues convert available cellular potential into an organ with a reproducible identity, location and architecture?
