1. Why a leaf needs a carbon-concentrating mechanism

A maize leaf and a wheat leaf can both use sunlight to reduce carbon dioxide into organic compounds. They contain chloroplasts, use water as an electron source and possess the Calvin–Benson cycle. Yet their responses to temperature and atmospheric carbon dioxide are not identical. This distinction emerges from a problem at the interface between the atmosphere and an enzyme. A plant must acquire inorganic carbon while limiting water loss, and the enzyme that incorporates carbon into the Calvin cycle also reacts with oxygen. C4 photosynthesis is a solution that changes the local chemical environment of that enzyme rather than replacing the entire photosynthetic apparatus.

Carbon dioxide enters a typical terrestrial leaf through stomata and diffuses through intercellular air spaces before crossing aqueous and membrane barriers. The concentration near an enzyme is therefore not necessarily the concentration measured outside the leaf. When stomata partially close, diffusion of carbon dioxide into the leaf becomes more restricted. Water conservation can consequently come at the expense of carbon acquisition. The balance depends on humidity, leaf temperature, stomatal behavior, boundary layers and metabolic demand. It is useful to separate the physical problem of delivering a substrate from the biochemical problem of consuming it.

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, abbreviated Rubisco, catalyzes the addition of carbon dioxide to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). The transient six-carbon intermediate breaks down to form two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate, each containing three carbons. Rubisco can instead add molecular oxygen to RuBP. This produces one 3-phosphoglycerate and one 2-phosphoglycolate. The latter is not directly usable as a normal Calvin-cycle intermediate. The photorespiratory salvage pathway recovers much of its carbon, but consumes resources and releases some carbon dioxide. Oxygenation thus imposes an opportunity cost as well as a biochemical recovery cost. Photorespiration itself will be developed in the respiration and photorespiration subunit; here its importance is the selective pressure that makes a carbon-concentrating mechanism beneficial.

The two gaseous substrates compete at Rubisco. For an unchanged enzyme and other fixed conditions, increasing the concentration of carbon dioxide relative to oxygen favors the carboxylation reaction. Rising temperature can favor oxygenation in C3 leaves because of changes in substrate solubilities and Rubisco specificity. A C4 leaf is able to operate Rubisco in an internal compartment where carbon dioxide availability is relatively high even when the concentration in the mesophyll is comparatively low. This does not require a Rubisco molecule incapable of oxygenation. It requires coordinated transport, metabolism and energy input to sustain a favorable ratio around Rubisco.

The crucial word in this explanation is sustain. If an internal compartment contains more carbon dioxide than its surroundings, diffusion tends to dissipate that difference. Building up a gradient once would not solve the problem of a photosynthetic leaf that operates for hours. A carbon pump must continuously deliver carbon, regenerate its acceptor and replace carbon that leaks from the concentrating compartment. As with other cellular gradients, a persistent nonequilibrium state depends on energy. The benefits of lowering photorespiration must be weighed against the costs of operating the pump.

A carbon-concentrating system does not create additional carbon atoms. It transfers inorganic carbon from an entry compartment to a second compartment, where fixation into the Calvin cycle occurs. In the usual C4 arrangement, an initial carboxylation in a mesophyll cell captures inorganic carbon in a four-carbon organic acid. This acid moves to an adjacent bundle-sheath cell, where a decarboxylation reaction releases carbon dioxide. The released carbon dioxide is then available for Rubisco. The three-carbon skeleton left after decarboxylation returns to the entry compartment and is regenerated into the initial acceptor. The arrangement can be regarded as a biochemical shuttle with a reusable carrier, provided that the analogy is not mistaken for a membrane protein carrying gaseous carbon dioxide directly.

The term C4 refers to the four-carbon nature of the initial stable carboxylation product, oxaloacetate. It does not mean that the carbohydrate produced by the leaf has four carbons. Nor does C3 refer to the number of carbon atoms in the carbon dioxide acceptor: RuBP has five carbons. C3 and C4 designate different routes by which recently acquired inorganic carbon first appears in stable organic metabolites. Both ultimately use the Calvin cycle to make triose phosphates that can contribute to sugars and other biomass.

2. The anatomy of spatial carbon concentration

A conventional C4 leaf contains two photosynthetic cell populations with contrasting roles. Mesophyll cells lie in a position favorable for acquiring carbon dioxide from the internal air spaces. Bundle-sheath cells surround vascular bundles. In many C4 species the sheath forms a conspicuous ring of relatively large photosynthetic cells, surrounded in turn by mesophyll cells. This arrangement is called Kranz anatomy, from the German word for a wreath. Its functional importance is not its appearance alone; the arrangement creates repeated short transport paths between the primary carbon-capturing cells and the cells that concentrate and assimilate carbon dioxide.

A vascular bundle contains xylem and phloem. Xylem contributes to water delivery and phloem participates in the redistribution of assimilates. Neither vascular tissue is the site of the principal C4 carboxylation shuttle. Bundle-sheath cells are living photosynthetic cells positioned around the vascular tissue. In a section through a maize leaf, it is important to distinguish the ring of bundle-sheath cells from the conducting elements inside it. The former encloses the main site of Calvin-cycle carboxylation; the latter carries water or solutes over larger distances through the plant.

The mesophyll cytosol contains phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC), while Rubisco is concentrated in bundle-sheath chloroplasts in the conventional two-cell system. Decarboxylation takes place in the bundle sheath, but the precise organelle differs among metabolic types. A model showing malate decarboxylation in a bundle-sheath chloroplast is appropriate for the NADP-malic-enzyme route; it should not be generalized to mitochondrial decarboxylation in an NAD-malic-enzyme species. Anatomy, cellular enzyme location and organelle location are three distinct levels of organization.

The diffusion distance between the two cell populations must be small enough for the required transfer of organic acids and return metabolites. Plasmodesmata, cytoplasmic connections that traverse adjacent plant cell walls, provide routes for exchange. Within a cell, transport across organellar envelopes is also necessary. For example, an oxaloacetate molecule made in the cytosol may have to enter a chloroplast before reduction to malate. Thus a diagram containing one arrow from mesophyll to bundle sheath summarizes several physical steps. It represents net metabolite movement, not a claim that membranes impose no transport constraints.

At the same time, the bundle sheath must retain sufficient carbon dioxide for efficient recapture by Rubisco. Diffusion resistance of the cell and tissue architecture helps limit escape. A sheath that completely prevented all chemical exchange would be useless, because organic acids and their return products could not circulate. The functional architecture combines relatively effective movement of shuttle metabolites with restricted gas leakage. No living bundle sheath is a perfectly sealed vessel. A portion of released carbon dioxide can return to the mesophyll and may be captured again, at the cost of additional energy.

Chloroplasts in the two cell types may differ in thylakoid organization. In many NADP-malic-enzyme grasses, mesophyll chloroplasts have prominent grana, whereas bundle-sheath chloroplasts show reduced grana and diminished photosystem II activity relative to mesophyll chloroplasts. This difference can be understood from the redistribution of reducing power through malate and the need to supply additional ATP. It is not a defining property of every C4 species. Some bundle-sheath chloroplasts have substantial photosystem II activity, and the relative allocation of photosystems depends on the biochemical route and species. A photograph alone is not sufficient to determine the complete flux pattern.

A minority of C4 plants demonstrate that two separate cells are not absolutely essential. Certain species achieve spatial separation between distinct intracellular regions with differently distributed chloroplasts and metabolic enzymes. These single-cell C4 systems retain the key requirement of local carbon dioxide concentration near Rubisco while using a different arrangement of compartments. They should be distinguished from CAM plants, in which the characteristic separation of primary capture and subsequent carbon dioxide release is temporal rather than the typical daytime spatial separation of C4 photosynthesis. The general principle is compartmentation, whereas Kranz anatomy is a frequent implementation of that principle.

3. Capturing inorganic carbon with PEPC

The first enzymatic carboxylation in the conventional C4 shuttle occurs in the cytosol of a mesophyll cell. Carbon dioxide entering the aqueous phase participates in the reversible equilibrium between carbon dioxide and bicarbonate. Carbonic anhydrase accelerates the interconversion of these forms. Its catalytic function does not alter the equilibrium position; it allows the substrate pool to respond rapidly to flux through downstream reactions. At physiological pH, bicarbonate can represent a substantial part of dissolved inorganic carbon. The distinction is important because the immediate inorganic substrate of PEPC is bicarbonate rather than gaseous carbon dioxide itself.

Phosphoenolpyruvate, or PEP, is a three-carbon phosphorylated organic compound. PEPC catalyzes its reaction with bicarbonate to form oxaloacetate and inorganic phosphate. The carbon balance is straightforward: a three-carbon acceptor and one inorganic carbon yield a four-carbon product. The phosphate group of PEP is released during the reaction. This step does not directly consume a molecule of ATP or NADPH. It uses the chemical potential already present in PEP, which must subsequently be regenerated. Any claim that the entire C4 pump requires no ATP because the PEPC reaction contains no ATP would ignore that regeneration cost.

PEPC differs from Rubisco both in substrate specificity and in the identity of its organic acceptor. PEPC uses bicarbonate and PEP; Rubisco uses carbon dioxide and RuBP. PEPC does not carry out the competing Rubisco oxygenase reaction. A mesophyll environment with restricted carbon dioxide entry can therefore support a capture reaction that is not directly subject to the same oxygenation competition as Rubisco. This does not mean that PEPC is active at any arbitrarily low inorganic carbon concentration. Substrate supply, enzyme abundance and regulatory state can all limit the rate of capture.

Oxaloacetate is the first stable four-carbon product in the usual pathway. It can be reduced to malate by a malate dehydrogenase, or undergo transamination to aspartate. In the reduction reaction, oxaloacetate accepts reducing equivalents and becomes malate. In the transamination reaction, the amino group from an amino donor such as glutamate is transferred to oxaloacetate, producing aspartate and the corresponding keto acid. The carbon atoms of oxaloacetate remain in the four-carbon skeleton during either transformation. These transformations facilitate transport and, in the case of malate, permit reducing power as well as carbon to be redistributed.

PEPC is not exclusive to C4 plants. Many organisms possess reactions that add inorganic carbon to a three-carbon skeleton, often to replenish intermediates used in respiration or biosynthesis. A C3 leaf can contain PEPC and can make malate. Its presence is therefore not a sufficient diagnostic test for C4 photosynthesis. To establish that a leaf has a C4 pump, one must show that the reaction operates at substantial photosynthetic flux, that four-carbon intermediates are decarboxylated in a compartment associated with Rubisco, and that the acceptor is regenerated. The distinction between the presence of an enzyme and its functional deployment within a pathway is a recurring theme in physiology.

The rate of initial capture must be matched to the rates of acid movement, decarboxylation and Calvin-cycle assimilation. If the pump were activated strongly while the Calvin cycle was unable to consume carbon dioxide, internal concentration could rise temporarily but diffusion losses would also increase. Excess organic acids might accumulate until feedback or substrate shortage slowed the pump. Efficient C4 photosynthesis requires coordination between two cycles rather than the maximization of an isolated enzyme activity. Regulation of photosynthetic PEPC by metabolites and reversible phosphorylation contributes to that coordination. The details vary among species; the key exam-level distinction is between the catalytic reaction and the control of its rate.

4. Following one carbon atom through the NADP-ME route

Consider a molecule of carbon dioxide that enters the mesophyll of a maize leaf. Carbonic anhydrase accelerates its equilibration with bicarbonate, and PEPC incorporates the inorganic carbon into oxaloacetate. In the conventional NADP-malic-enzyme model, oxaloacetate enters the mesophyll chloroplast and is reduced by NADP-dependent malate dehydrogenase, consuming NADPH. Malate contains the three carbons originally present in PEP plus the newly added inorganic carbon. It can move to the bundle sheath and enter a bundle-sheath chloroplast.

Within that chloroplast, NADP-malic enzyme oxidatively decarboxylates malate to form pyruvate, carbon dioxide and NADPH. Pyruvate has three carbon atoms. The fourth carbon is released as carbon dioxide, in the vicinity of Rubisco. The NADPH generated in this compartment can contribute to Calvin-cycle reduction. Combining the reactions in mesophyll and bundle sheath illustrates why malate can function as a shuttle for reducing power: NADPH is consumed in one cell and regenerated in the other. NADPH molecules themselves do not need to travel intact from one chloroplast to another for reducing equivalents to be redistributed.

Rubisco fixes the released carbon dioxide into RuBP in the Calvin cycle. ATP and reducing equivalents subsequently allow 3-phosphoglycerate to be converted into triose phosphate. Most of the carbon moving through the Calvin cycle is used to regenerate RuBP, while a net fraction can leave the cycle for carbohydrate production. Meanwhile, the pyruvate released from malate returns to the mesophyll. The two destinations of the carbon skeleton must not be conflated: the transferred inorganic carbon can enter Calvin-cycle products, while the original three-carbon acceptor skeleton is recycled.

A chloroplastic enzyme called pyruvate phosphate dikinase (PPDK) regenerates PEP from pyruvate, ATP and inorganic phosphate. Its products include PEP, AMP and pyrophosphate. Pyrophosphate can be hydrolyzed to inorganic phosphate, favoring forward operation. Because ATP is converted to AMP rather than ADP, restoring the adenylate to ATP requires two phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. This accounts for the familiar additional energy requirement of the simple C4 route. PEP can then return to the mesophyll cytosol, where it acts as the acceptor for the next inorganic carbon molecule.

The name Hatch–Slack pathway is often used for this biochemical pump. It is cyclic in the sense that the three-carbon acceptor skeleton is regenerated, but its purpose differs from that of the Calvin cycle. The latter incorporates inorganic carbon into net organic products; the former increases substrate availability at Rubisco. If the C4 shuttle is viewed in isolation, the inorganic carbon enters through PEPC and leaves through decarboxylation. There is no net accumulation of organic carbon in the carrier cycle when its intermediates return to their starting amounts. This is why adding a second cycle does not imply doubling the number of net carbon atoms fixed.

A defect in malate movement would interrupt the coupling between initial capture and delivery even if both carboxylases remained catalytically normal. In a short experiment, oxaloacetate or malate could accumulate on the capture side while the Calvin cycle became carbon limited. Over longer periods, depletion of recycled acceptor and feedback inhibition might reduce initial capture too. A prediction depends on when the observation is made. Immediate changes in a metabolite pool reflect differences between production and consumption rates; a new steady state may have a very different pool size. Examination questions that ask what happens immediately after inhibition should be distinguished from those asking about final photosynthetic rate.

5. Alternative decarboxylation routes and shared logic

C4 plants do not all use the same route to release carbon dioxide. The classical biochemical classification recognizes NADP-malic enzyme, NAD-malic enzyme and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) as major decarboxylating activities. The classification refers to the predominant enzyme, not necessarily the complete absence of the other activities. All three routes solve the same problem: capture inorganic carbon in the mesophyll, deliver a four-carbon compound to the bundle sheath and release carbon dioxide near Rubisco. Differences concern which metabolite carries the carbon, where release occurs and how the energy and redox budgets are distributed.

In the NAD-ME route, aspartate is commonly an important transport metabolite. Oxaloacetate formed in mesophyll cytosol can be transaminated, and aspartate reaching the bundle sheath is converted back to oxaloacetate through a reverse transamination. In bundle-sheath mitochondria, oxaloacetate is reduced to malate by an NAD-dependent malate dehydrogenase; NAD-dependent malic enzyme then produces pyruvate, carbon dioxide and NADH. The carbon dioxide must reach Rubisco in the bundle-sheath chloroplast. Return of three-carbon material may involve alanine instead of free pyruvate, with transamination restoring pyruvate in the mesophyll for PPDK.

Transamination reactions in this arrangement must be balanced. Transferring an amino group to form aspartate creates a corresponding keto acid; converting a three-carbon keto acid into alanine uses an amino group. The aspartate and alanine exchanges can therefore help redistribute nitrogen groups while the carbon skeletons shuttle between cells. The pathway is not a net source of nitrogen. Nitrogen is neither created nor consumed simply because two amino acids appear as transport intermediates. In a closed accounting cycle, the amino groups and the associated keto acid pools must be regenerated.

In the PEPCK route, oxaloacetate in bundle-sheath cytosol can undergo ATP-dependent decarboxylation to PEP and carbon dioxide. Because the resulting organic product already has the identity of the initial acceptor, this branch need not regenerate that particular molecule through pyruvate and PPDK. However, PEPCK itself requires energy, and actual leaves often operate this route alongside malic-enzyme activity. A simple assertion that PEPCK plants universally spend exactly one ATP less per carbon dioxide than other C4 plants overlooks the energetic coupling of the branches, mitochondrial metabolism and the costs of transport.

The diversity of routes matters for data interpretation. Detection of label in aspartate in a maize leaf does not invalidate the classification of maize as predominantly NADP-ME type. It may show that transamination and a subsidiary transport route participate alongside the major malate route. An enzyme assay reporting the presence of PEPCK likewise cannot by itself establish the fraction of carbon flux carried by that enzyme. Enzyme capacity measured under favorable assay conditions differs from in vivo reaction rate, which depends on concentrations, compartmentation and regulation.

One useful way to compare the types is to track the decarboxylation compartment first, the four-carbon input second, and the three-carbon output third. NADP-ME characteristically releases carbon dioxide in bundle-sheath chloroplasts with malate as substrate and pyruvate as product. NAD-ME characteristically operates in mitochondria with malate as the direct substrate even when aspartate is the transported compound. PEPCK operates in the cytosol using oxaloacetate as substrate and producing PEP. Rubisco remains a chloroplastic enzyme across these examples. The location of carbon dioxide production need not coincide precisely with the organelle where it is consumed, provided local diffusion and retention support the concentrating function.

6. Energy accounting and the cost of leakage

The ideal Calvin cycle requires three ATP and two NADPH for each net carbon dioxide incorporated into carbohydrate precursors. This average follows from the standard accounting for three carbon dioxide molecules: six molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate must be phosphorylated and reduced, consuming six ATP and six NADPH, and regeneration of three RuBP molecules consumes a further three ATP. The net product is one triose phosphate. Dividing nine ATP and six NADPH by three gives the familiar per-carbon dioxide requirement. These figures assume ideal carboxylation without additional losses through photorespiration, maintenance or export.

In a simple malic-enzyme C4 scheme, one molecule of PEP is regenerated for each carbon dioxide delivered by the pump. PPDK converts ATP to AMP, requiring two ATP equivalents to restore the high-energy adenylate state. Adding this idealized two-equivalent cost to the three ATP of the Calvin cycle gives five ATP equivalents per net carbon dioxide assimilated, while the baseline Calvin-cycle reducing requirement remains two NADPH. Redistribution of reducing power between cells must be accounted for separately from whole-leaf consumption. This distinction avoids counting the same reducing equivalents twice when malate transfers them.

For six carbon dioxide molecules, the ideal C3 accounting becomes eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH. In the simplified C4 model, six pump turnovers add twelve ATP equivalents, giving thirty ATP equivalents and twelve NADPH. Six fixed carbon atoms correspond to the carbon content of a hexose equivalent, not necessarily the immediate formation of one free glucose molecule in a chloroplast. The immediate Calvin-cycle net products are triose phosphates, and their subsequent conversion and export involve other reactions. The arithmetic is intended to compare carbon assimilation costs, not the complete energy budget of carbohydrate transport and storage.

Leakage adds a further cost. Define the pump flux Vp as the amount of inorganic carbon fixed by PEPC per unit area per unit time and L as the amount of carbon dioxide escaping from the bundle sheath back toward the mesophyll per unit area per unit time. Their ratio, phi = L/Vp, is dimensionless. Assume a simplified steady state in which all pump-delivered carbon either leaks or is fixed by Rubisco, and ignore respiration, photorespiration and other inorganic carbon sources. Net assimilation A then equals Vp minus L, or Vp multiplied by (1 minus phi). This equation states a carbon conservation relationship under explicitly restricted conditions; it is not a comprehensive model of a living leaf.

Suppose an illustrative leaf has Vp = 25 micromoles per square metre per second and a leakage flux of 5 in the same units. Net assimilation under the assumptions is 20, and phi = 5/25 = 0.20. Each unit of net assimilation requires 25/20 = 1.25 pump turnovers on average. With two ATP equivalents per turnover, the pump requires 2.5 ATP equivalents per net carbon dioxide rather than two. Including ideal Calvin-cycle demand gives 5.5 ATP equivalents per net carbon dioxide. The calculation demonstrates why a moderate leakage fraction has a measurable energy consequence without implying that every C4 plant has a leakage fraction of 0.20.

If leakage increases while capture flux stays fixed, net assimilation falls. If net assimilation stays fixed, capture flux must increase. These are different experimental constraints. For the preceding example, maintaining A at 20 while increasing phi to 0.40 would require Vp = 20/0.60 = 33.33. The simplified pump energy cost becomes 2/0.60 = 3.33 ATP equivalents per net carbon dioxide. The new total is about 6.33. As phi approaches one, the required gross pump turnover becomes extremely large in this model. Real leaves would encounter enzyme, light and transport limits before such a state could maintain the assumed net assimilation.

The higher ATP-to-NADPH requirement of C4 photosynthesis connects carbon assimilation to photophosphorylation. Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I can contribute to ATP generation without net NADPH production by that branch. Its relative importance varies with cell type and biochemical subtype. Malate decarboxylation supplies reducing equivalents directly to bundle-sheath chloroplasts in the NADP-ME route, while PPDK imposes ATP demand in mesophyll chloroplasts. A whole-leaf ATP total cannot establish the distribution of ATP production across these compartments. A successful system must satisfy both the aggregate energy budget and local metabolic requirements.

7. How experiments distinguish capture from assimilation

The C4 pathway emerged from experiments in which the movement of newly acquired carbon could be traced. In a carbon-labeling experiment, radioactive carbon dioxide provides a chemically similar but detectable form of carbon. Researchers can expose a photosynthesizing leaf to labeled carbon dioxide for short intervals, rapidly terminate metabolism and separate the organic compounds that contain the radioactive carbon. Early labeled metabolites provide information about the first destinations of the tracer, while later labeled metabolites reveal subsequent redistribution. The sequence alone does not establish every intervening enzyme reaction, but it can discriminate between competing hypotheses.

The landmark sugarcane study by Hatch and Slack in 1966 documented rapid incorporation of label into four-carbon acids and developed an alternative account of carbon delivery into sugar formation. Earlier observations by Kortschak, Hartt and Burr helped identify the unusual labeling pattern. If sugarcane simply operated the same initial fixation route as a typical C3 leaf, one would expect rapid appearance of labeled 3-phosphoglycerate without a required initial four-carbon shuttle. Early labeling of four-carbon compounds followed by transfer to Calvin-cycle products instead suggested another route of initial capture.

A pulse–chase experiment strengthens this interpretation. During a brief pulse, labeled carbon dioxide is supplied. During the chase, the supply is replaced with unlabeled carbon dioxide while metabolism continues. If an early labeled pool donates carbon to a later pool, the label in the early pool can decrease while label increases in the later one. Because unlabeled carbon continues to enter the system, label abundance is not identical to total metabolite concentration. A small pool with rapid turnover can acquire and lose substantial label without changing its total amount. Interpreting isotope experiments requires distinguishing flux, pool size and isotope enrichment.

To test the compartmental model, one can combine anatomical observations with enzyme localization or measurements from separated cell preparations. A result showing high PEPC activity in mesophyll material and high Rubisco activity in bundle-sheath material supports the expected division of labor, provided the samples are sufficiently pure. Contamination can generate apparent overlap. An appropriate control is to use established cell-type markers or microscopy to assess the preparation. Enzyme localization and radiolabeling together constrain the pathway more strongly than either type of evidence alone.

Leaf gas-exchange measurements provide another independent window into carbon concentration. Changing external carbon dioxide, oxygen, temperature or irradiance allows comparison of assimilation responses. A low carbon dioxide compensation point and comparatively weak oxygen inhibition under many conditions are consistent with suppression of Rubisco oxygenation. They do not prove absolute absence of photorespiration. The compensation point is defined operationally as the carbon dioxide level at which net exchange is zero under a specified set of conditions. It depends on the balance of carbon fixation and release and should not be confused with the affinity of one purified enzyme.

Consider two hypothetical leaves measured at a fixed irradiance and temperature. Leaf X assimilates 16 units of carbon dioxide at normal oxygen and 23 units at reduced oxygen. Leaf Y assimilates 24 units at normal oxygen and 25 units at reduced oxygen. Under these particular conditions, reducing oxygen relieves a substantial limitation in X but only a small limitation in Y. The pattern is consistent with a C3-like leaf X and a carbon-concentrating leaf Y, assuming other relevant characteristics are comparable. It would not be conclusive by itself because changes in oxygen can affect other processes and the comparison might be confounded by different temperatures, stomatal conductances or light saturation. Adding anatomical and early-labeling data makes the inference more defensible.

8. Environmental advantages, trade-offs and exceptions

C4 physiology is particularly useful when high temperature, strong illumination and restricted carbon dioxide availability would impose large photorespiratory costs on a C3 plant. A C4 plant can often maintain a given assimilation rate with lower stomatal conductance because the pump allows relatively effective capture at reduced internal carbon dioxide. If other factors are similar, lower stomatal conductance can reduce transpiration. This relationship explains the frequently observed improvement in intrinsic water-use efficiency of C4 plants. It does not guarantee that a whole C4 plant always uses less water over a season: canopy size, growth duration, root access to water and climate also matter.

Rubisco abundance represents a substantial investment of leaf nitrogen. By increasing carbon dioxide near Rubisco, a C4 plant can often achieve a given carboxylation rate with less Rubisco than a comparable C3 plant, contributing to photosynthetic nitrogen-use efficiency. The carbon pump nevertheless requires other enzymes and cellular structures, which themselves cost resources. It is therefore more accurate to describe a changed allocation of nitrogen than to assert that all C4 leaves contain less total nitrogen. Leaf nitrogen content reflects many functions beyond Rubisco.

The extra ATP requirement becomes especially relevant in dim light, where the supply of photochemical energy is limited. Low temperature or high atmospheric carbon dioxide can also reduce the relative disadvantage of C3 Rubisco oxygenation. Under some such conditions, the C4 pump offers less benefit for the energy invested in it. The crossover between C3 and C4 performance depends on multiple variables and species acclimation. One cannot predict a universal ranking from the number of carbon atoms in the first product. The pathway evolved because it can improve performance in particular environments, not because the ideal biochemical energy cost of C4 assimilation is lower.

Maize, sugarcane and sorghum are familiar C4 examples; wheat and rice use C3 photosynthesis. Comparing these crops helps connect cellular mechanisms to agricultural observations, but yield is a property of the whole plant interacting with its environment and management. Differences in phenology, nutrient uptake, allocation to harvestable organs and stress tolerance can outweigh a photosynthetic advantage measured at a single leaf. Similarly, introducing PEPC into a C3 crop does not automatically produce a functioning C4 system. Coordinated enzyme localization, metabolite exchange, decarboxylation, acceptor regeneration, gas retention and appropriate energy supply would all have to be established.

C4 and CAM share the idea of initial PEPC-mediated inorganic carbon capture followed by decarboxylation supplying Rubisco. They differ in the organization of those reactions. The usual C4 arrangement moves organic acids between distinct daytime spatial compartments. CAM commonly captures carbon at night, stores organic acid in vacuoles and releases carbon dioxide during the day when stomata may be relatively closed. The physiological advantage of CAM is closely tied to water conservation through temporal organization. A plant with abundant malate cannot be identified as C4 or CAM from that measurement alone. Timing and localization of flux are necessary to distinguish the strategies. The next chapter develops that distinction in detail.
