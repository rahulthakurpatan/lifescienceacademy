1. The biological problem: converting inorganic carbon into organic matter

A green leaf can increase its dry mass even when it receives no ready-made organic food. This observation separates photosynthesis from the ordinary digestion of food. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf from the atmosphere, while water and mineral nutrients reach the leaf from the roots. The carbon atoms of newly synthesized sugars, amino acids and many other organic compounds ultimately come from the assimilated carbon dioxide. The conversion is not merely a physical absorption of gas: carbon dioxide contains carbon in a highly oxidized state, and its carbon must be incorporated into bonds in an organic skeleton and then reduced. This chemical challenge explains why the production of ATP and NADPH by illuminated thylakoids is only part of photosynthesis.

The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, often shortened to the Calvin cycle, is a connected series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions that solves this problem. In the chloroplast stroma of a C3 plant, the cycle first attaches CO₂ to an existing five-carbon acceptor, then changes the resulting three-carbon acids into phosphorylated sugars, and finally reconstructs the acceptor. The last step is essential because every new carboxylation needs another acceptor molecule. If an acceptor were consumed without replacement, the rate of CO₂ uptake would collapse as soon as the initial supply was exhausted. The cycle therefore invests most of its immediate product in maintaining the capacity for subsequent carbon assimilation.

Carbon fixation and carbon reduction are related but distinct. Fixation here means incorporation of inorganic carbon into an organic molecule. Rubisco, the fixing enzyme, forms two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate from a single CO₂ and ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Reduction takes place afterward, when another enzyme transfers reducing equivalents from NADPH to an activated derivative of 3-phosphoglycerate. The immediate stable carbon-fixation product is therefore not glucose, and the first fixation reaction itself does not directly consume ATP or NADPH. Both carriers are indispensable for continued overall turnover because the product must be reduced and the acceptor regenerated.

A cyclic pathway is not a system that creates matter from nothing. Its intermediate pools may remain approximately constant even while carbon atoms continuously enter and leave. A useful analogy is a circulating collection of reusable molds: each passage produces some new material, but the molds have to be restored for the next passage. The analogy is incomplete because the actual cycle does not preserve an individual RuBP molecule as an unchanged object. Carbon atoms are redistributed among intermediates, so an incoming carbon atom can remain inside the cycle for several rounds rather than immediately appearing in an exported sugar. What is conserved at metabolic steady state is the size and composition of the cycling pools on average.

The designation C3 refers to the three carbon atoms in the first stable product of the main carboxylation reaction. Wheat, rice and many other plants use this basic mode of assimilation. The same Calvin cycle also operates in C4 and CAM plants, although those plants use additional mechanisms to increase CO₂ availability to Rubisco. Understanding the unmodified C3 pathway gives a reference against which the energetic cost and physiological advantage of those additional mechanisms can be judged.

Carbon acquisition is a different problem from energy acquisition

A plant may be described as a photoautotroph because light provides the energy for its autotrophic assimilation. The word does not imply that every molecule incorporated into a plant is made by the Calvin cycle. Mineral ions provide nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and other elements, and water provides hydrogen and oxygen as well as the physical medium of life. In the cycle, carbon dioxide provides a new carbon input that can be distributed into many different organic skeletons. It is useful to separate these materials when interpreting an experiment in which only one element is labeled. Radioactive carbon dioxide can reveal carbon movement, but it cannot independently identify the source of the oxygen released by photosystem II or the ultimate source of the electrons used to reduce NADP⁺.

The chemical state of carbon provides a more precise reason that light energy is needed. Assign oxygen an oxidation number of minus two and hydrogen a number of plus one in carbon dioxide and a simplified carbohydrate unit CH₂O. Carbon in CO₂ is then at plus four, while the average carbon oxidation number in CH₂O is zero. The difference of four units represents a net gain of four electrons per carbon in the formal conversion. A single NADPH molecule can provide a two-electron reducing equivalent; hence a requirement of two NADPH per assimilated CO₂ is consistent with the overall oxidation-state difference. This argument does not tell us which intermediate is reduced or how ATP is spent. The actual biochemical pathway is needed to determine those features.

For a triose phosphate, individual carbon atoms do not all have the same formal oxidation number. The aldehyde carbon, hydroxyl-bearing carbon and phosphate-bearing carbon have different bonding environments. The useful calculation is the average oxidation state across the carbon skeleton, not an assertion that every carbon individually changes from plus four to zero in a single enzyme reaction. Many of the carbon atoms in an exported triose have passed through previous cycle intermediates. A formal overall electron balance remains valid at steady state even though the identity of each atom in a particular molecule has a more complex history.

Fixing CO₂ is not by itself sufficient to classify an organism as carrying out C3 photosynthesis. Heterotrophic cells also possess carboxylating enzymes, and several autotrophic microorganisms use carbon-fixation pathways different from the Calvin cycle. What defines the C3 context of this chapter is the direct major incorporation of atmospheric carbon by Rubisco and the formation of 3-PGA in the photosynthetic chloroplast. The pathway can be recognized by its characteristic acceptor-regeneration logic and not merely by the observation of any carboxylation reaction. This distinction is especially important when a question presents a metabolic reaction in isolation rather than describing an entire organism.

2. Where carbon fixation occurs and how light supplies its energy

The chloroplast has an envelope surrounding the stroma, and a membrane system of flattened thylakoids enclosing a lumen. The two compartments have different chemical roles. Photosystem II, photosystem I and the intervening electron carriers reside within or beside the thylakoid membrane. ATP synthase releases ATP on the stromal side, and reduction of NADP⁺ by ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase also supplies the stromal pool. The major enzymes of the Calvin cycle operate in this same stromal environment. This proximity couples the reactions without requiring all carbon intermediates to cross the thylakoid membrane.

ATP is used to drive group-transfer reactions, especially phosphorylation. NADPH is a reduced electron carrier; it delivers reducing equivalents in chemical transformations. Neither carrier contributes the carbon atom that is fixed by Rubisco. Consider a chloroplast supplied with abundant ATP but almost no NADPH. Phosphoglycerate kinase can activate 3-phosphoglycerate while the ATP supply lasts, yet the subsequent reduction cannot be sustained. Conversely, NADPH cannot compensate for the absence of ATP needed for the activation step or for phosphorylation of ribulose-5-phosphate. Energy supply and reducing power are consequently separate constraints.

The historical expression dark reactions describes the lack of direct photon absorption by the enzymes of the carbon-fixation pathway. It does not mean that the pathway normally functions only at night. In illuminated chloroplasts, the light reactions continuously replenish ATP and NADPH. Illumination also changes stromal pH, magnesium availability and the redox state of regulatory proteins, favoring activation of several carbon-assimilation enzymes. After darkness begins, existing metabolite pools can support some transient reactions, but a sustained normal daytime rate cannot continue without replenishment and enzyme activation.

The carbon reactions also influence the light reactions. During productive assimilation, oxidation of NADPH returns NADP⁺, and ATP consumption returns ADP. These compounds become available again for photosynthetic electron transport and photophosphorylation. A sudden reduction in carbon demand, such as that produced by stomatal closure, may leave insufficient electron acceptor capacity relative to incoming excitation. Protective dissipation of excess light and adjustments in electron flow become important under such conditions. The cycle is therefore not a downstream machine entirely independent of its energy supplier; the two subsystems communicate through shared metabolic pools.

The route from the atmosphere to the active site

Before carbon dioxide can react with RuBP, it must move through a sequence of physical barriers. The atmosphere outside a leaf is separated from the intercellular air spaces by the leaf surface and stomatal pores. Within the leaf, CO₂ moves through gas spaces, dissolves in aqueous phases and passes through the mesophyll cells toward chloroplasts. Each resistance can sustain a difference in concentration when net assimilation is occurring. Even when the concentration outside the leaf is unchanged, a change in stomatal aperture or internal conductance may change the concentration experienced by Rubisco.

A high external CO₂ concentration therefore does not necessarily mean that carbon fixation is free from diffusion limitation. At a fixed metabolic demand, narrowing the stomatal pathway can increase the difference between atmospheric and intercellular CO₂. At a fixed stomatal aperture, a sudden rise in metabolic demand may itself draw down internal CO₂. A leaf is an interacting transport and reaction system, so concentration differences are produced by both the supply and the consumption of substrate. An experiment that compares leaves at the same external concentration without measuring stomatal behavior may miss this interaction.

In an aqueous environment carbon dioxide can interconvert with bicarbonate through hydration and acid–base reactions. Carbonic anhydrases accelerate interconversion without changing the equilibrium that is ultimately set by the chemical conditions. Rubisco uses molecular CO₂ as its carboxylating substrate, whereas PEPC in C4 and CAM metabolism uses bicarbonate. The distinction is not a matter of whether the carbon was originally present as atmospheric CO₂; it concerns the chemical species actually used at the enzyme active site. A reaction scheme should identify the correct substrate for the particular enzyme.

The envelope and thylakoid membrane separate different metabolic spaces. Stromal reactions and cytosolic reactions can use similarly named intermediates but maintain distinguishable pools. A defect in a transport protein may prevent two pools from equilibrating, even though the same molecular formula occurs on both sides of the membrane. The importance of this compartmentation becomes clear when one asks why ATP generated on the stromal side is useful for regeneration whereas ATP located elsewhere in the cell cannot be assumed to have identical availability. Transport, production and consumption determine each local metabolic pool.

3. How experiments revealed the path of carbon

A major historical difficulty in studying photosynthesis was that the products appeared inside a complicated mixture of compounds. Measuring the total amount of carbohydrate formed after a prolonged period of illumination could not reveal the order of individual reactions. A label was needed that would follow a particular carbon atom through the network. Radioactive carbon-14 made such experiments possible because a molecule containing radioactive carbon could be detected even among a much larger amount of chemically identical unlabeled material.

The investigations associated with Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson and James Bassham used photosynthetic material, short exposures to labeled carbon dioxide, rapid interruption of metabolism and separation of extracted compounds. Autoradiography could locate radioactive spots after chromatographic separation. Comparing samples collected at different labeling times provided a way to distinguish early incorporation from later redistribution. When exposure was brief, 3-phosphoglycerate was a prominent early stable labeled compound; with longer exposure, radioactivity appeared in other organic intermediates and sugar phosphates.

A time series is much more informative than a single late sample. If labeled carbon first appears in compound X and subsequently in compound Y, X is a plausible precursor of Y. Nevertheless, appearance order alone cannot prove a direct enzyme-catalyzed conversion: different pool sizes and reaction rates alter labeling kinetics. Identification of compounds, carbon positions, changes after experimental perturbations, and biochemical tests of enzymes are necessary to establish a cycle rather than a simple list of radioactive metabolites. The 1954 publication on cyclic regeneration of the carbon dioxide acceptor is an important historical record of this reconstruction.

An unlabeled pre-existing RuBP pool helps explain how the experiment works. A newly supplied labeled CO₂ molecule can enter a carboxylation reaction with RuBP containing unlabeled carbon. One of the immediate products can then carry the newly incorporated label while the other consists of previously present carbon atoms. After recycling and further turnover, the label is redistributed into other positions and compounds. Thus, seeing radioactivity in six metabolites at a later time does not imply that six independent initial carbon-fixation mechanisms are operating.

Controls matter when interpreting this method. Comparable samples should differ primarily in the duration of exposure or in the chosen perturbation, rather than accidentally differing in temperature, illumination or starting physiological state. A reliable identification of a chromatographic spot requires comparison with known compounds or additional chemical evidence. Rapid metabolic interruption is required because the very reactions being studied can continue during sample collection. The experiment measures movement of a tracer, not necessarily an immediate change in the total concentration of each metabolite.

From label appearance to cyclic regeneration

Imagine a set of rapidly sampled cultures exposed to labeled CO₂ for one, five and thirty seconds. The first sample may show labeling concentrated in a small number of rapidly labeled compounds, the intermediate sample in a larger set of sugar phosphates, and the last sample in an extensive network of metabolites. Such a sequence is compatible with a pathway in which carbon enters at one reaction and then passes through many different reactions. A tempting but incorrect approach is to rank metabolites solely by the amount of label recovered in the final sample and identify the most radioactive as the first product. A large slowly turning pool can contain substantial total label even if its molecules were not the first to receive carbon.

Specific radioactivity is the radioactivity relative to the amount of a chemical compound. If a pool contains a large quantity of pre-existing unlabeled molecules, a fixed number of newly labeled molecules will give a relatively low specific activity. The same number of labeled molecules in a smaller pool would yield a greater specific activity. This distinction explains why time-dependent labeling, absolute compound amounts and chemical identification together provide more insight than a picture of dark and light spots alone. It also helps students distinguish a graph showing radioactivity from a graph showing total metabolite concentration.

Pulse–chase reasoning extends the method. In a pulse, labeled substrate is supplied briefly. In a chase, a larger source of unlabeled substrate is supplied, so further entry of labeled carbon is reduced. If radioactivity decreases in one intermediate and increases in a downstream product, the change supports transfer of labeled carbon. The total pool of the original intermediate may remain constant because its labeled molecules are replaced with unlabeled ones. A decrease in the tracer signal therefore need not be caused by destruction or net depletion of the metabolic pool.

Changing light or carbon dioxide independently is another way to probe relationships among intermediates. In a simplified prediction, suddenly removing CO₂ while light and ATP generation continue reduces the use of RuBP by carboxylation, potentially allowing RuBP to accumulate while 3-PGA production falls. Removing light instead impairs the energy-dependent processing of 3-PGA and regeneration of RuBP, so a different transient distribution can result. These are conditional mechanistic predictions, not universal measured changes. Oxygenation, feedback, enzyme activation and the time after perturbation may modify them. Combining several kinds of evidence supports the interpretation that RuBP is regenerated as part of a metabolic cycle.

It is important to distinguish the discovery of the carbon-fixation pathway from demonstration of particular catalytic activities. A network inferred from tracer experiments should also be chemically feasible and supported by enzymes capable of performing each required transformation. A short-lived intermediate can be proposed on mechanistic grounds even if it cannot be isolated in large amounts from a functioning leaf. Conversely, an extract might catalyze a reaction under artificial conditions that contribute little flux in the intact cell. The network is best supported when independent forms of evidence converge.

4. Phase I: Rubisco and the chemistry of carboxylation

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) is a five-carbon ketose carrying phosphate groups at carbon positions 1 and 5. Its name distinguishes it from ribulose-5-phosphate, which has only one phosphate group. The enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase is abbreviated Rubisco. In higher plants, its usual form I complex includes eight large subunits and eight small subunits. The catalytic sites are associated with the large-subunit architecture, while the small subunits influence the properties of the assembled enzyme. These structural facts explain why alterations of the enzyme's function may involve nuclear as well as chloroplast gene products.

At an activated catalytic site, magnesium participates in organizing the reactive substrate and its chemical environment. RuBP undergoes enolization, allowing reaction with CO₂. The initially formed six-carbon carboxylation intermediate is not accumulated as a stable six-carbon sugar. Through hydration and cleavage, the enzyme produces two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Each product has three carbons. The arithmetic is direct: five carbons in the acceptor plus one entering carbon equal six carbons in the two products. This bookkeeping is necessary for distinguishing a C3 first product from the fleeting six-carbon intermediate.

There is no ATP term in the carboxylation reaction itself. The energy needed to generate RuBP and transform 3-PGA is supplied in the other phases. Similarly, NADPH is not a direct substrate of Rubisco. A student who assigns NADPH consumption to carboxylation may have remembered that photosynthesis is reductive but placed the reducing event at the wrong step. Locating each energy-consuming transformation prevents this error. The reaction is also distinct from the phosphorylation of ribulose-5-phosphate by phosphoribulokinase, which directly uses ATP.

Rubisco has to be activated before it can efficiently catalyze either reaction. Carbamylation of a specific active-site lysine by a CO₂ molecule, followed by Mg²⁺ binding, helps establish the active configuration. This activating CO₂ molecule is conceptually different from the substrate CO₂ molecule that enters the carbohydrate carbon skeleton. Therefore, counting two newly assimilated carbons for each carboxylation would be incorrect. Activation changes catalytic competence, whereas the carboxylation reaction changes the carbon inventory of organic metabolites.

Sugar phosphates can occupy Rubisco active sites in inhibitory configurations. Rubisco activase is an ATP-dependent regulatory protein that helps remove inhibitory ligands and restore access to catalytically competent sites. It does not catalyze the production of 3-PGA in place of Rubisco. Its ATP consumption is also outside the simplified core cycle budget of three ATP per fixed CO₂. This distinction becomes particularly useful when comparing an enzyme assay performed after maximal activation with actual assimilation in a heat-stressed leaf: the amount of Rubisco protein may be unchanged even though a smaller fraction is active.

Why the acceptor has two phosphate groups

A phosphate attached to a sugar changes its charge, solubility and binding properties. In RuBP the two phosphate groups occupy opposite ends of the carbon skeleton. The arrangement helps the enzyme position the substrate for chemical transformations, and it also ensures that cleavage yields two phosphorylated three-carbon products rather than requiring immediate phosphorylation of two free molecules. The existence of the phosphate groups is therefore not simply an accounting convention. They are part of the molecular recognition and reaction chemistry that connects the carboxylation product to the next enzymes.

The name ribulose identifies a five-carbon ketose. Ribose, by contrast, is a five-carbon aldose. Both are pentoses but their carbonyl groups occupy different positions. Ribulose-5-phosphate has the chemical skeleton required for formation of RuBP when phosphate is transferred to carbon 1. Ribose-5-phosphate first undergoes an isomerization; it is not converted into RuBP by a direct phosphate transfer that leaves its carbonyl position unchanged. Molecular terminology can thus predict the necessary transformations if the underlying structures are understood.

The enediolate intermediate formed in the Rubisco reaction provides a chemical explanation for the enzyme's two alternative substrates. Enolization produces a reactive arrangement in which the carbon skeleton can react with CO₂ or O₂. The protein establishes a particular catalytic environment that makes carboxylation favorable relative to oxygenation, but it does not eliminate the alternative reaction. A catalyst can accelerate a particular route without being perfectly exclusive. The physiological problem in C3 leaves arises because the atmosphere contains oxygen and the enzyme does not distinguish its gas substrates absolutely.

When Rubisco is considered as a soluble enzyme complex, students sometimes assume that the number of large subunits directly determines the observed carboxylation rate. The number of catalytically competent sites, their turnover rate, substrate availability and the degree of activation all contribute. For a fully saturated and activated preparation, a maximum rate can be approximated by the amount of active catalytic sites multiplied by turnover per site. If only a portion of the sites is active, multiplying the total protein amount by the turnover of a fully active site overestimates the actual rate. Distinguishing quantity from activity is as relevant in plant physiology as in any enzyme-kinetics problem.

Consider two leaf extracts that each contain the same amount of Rubisco protein. If one extract has seventy percent of its potential sites catalytically active and the other has thirty-five percent, the active-site abundance differs by a factor of two. If both are assayed at equal saturating substrate concentrations and the catalytic properties of active sites are identical, the predicted initial rate of the first is twice that of the second. This result does not require altered gene expression or protein synthesis. It demonstrates how regulation of activation state can cause substantial changes in flux without a proportional change in protein abundance.

An isolated activity assay should specify whether enzyme activation is measured before or after a deliberate activation procedure. A change in the initial activity measured immediately after sampling can reflect the state of the enzyme in vivo. A maximal activity measured after appropriate activation reveals a different quantity: the potential catalytic capacity. The ratio of initial to maximal activity is informative about activation state, provided the two measurements are performed comparably and the sampling and extraction procedure does not itself change the state in an uncontrolled manner. In a reasoning question, this comparison can distinguish a reversible regulatory defect from loss of enzyme protein or intrinsic catalytic capacity.

5. Phase II: phosphorylation and reduction of 3-phosphoglycerate

Three carboxylations form six 3-PGA molecules. Each is converted by phosphoglycerate kinase into 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, using one ATP and releasing one ADP. This reaction adds a phosphate group to the molecule, creating an activated acyl phosphate derivative. The significance of this activation is that direct reduction of an ordinary carboxylate is chemically difficult. Coupling reduction to prior ATP-dependent activation allows the cell to reach an intermediate whose conversion is more favorable when NADPH is available.

NADP-dependent glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase then converts 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP, also called G3P). The reaction consumes one NADPH per product, oxidizes NADPH to NADP⁺ and releases inorganic phosphate. The acyl carbon is reduced to an aldehyde-level carbon. The other phosphate group remains attached to the triose phosphate. Both 3-PGA and GAP contain three carbon atoms, so the phase changes chemical state and energy content without changing the total number of carbon atoms.

These steps resemble reactions known from glycolysis, but the direction and physiological setting differ. In glycolysis, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate oxidation and subsequent substrate-level phosphorylation participate in the harvesting of energy from sugar. In the Calvin cycle, related transformations run in the direction of sugar phosphate formation with expenditure of ATP and reducing power. Shared chemical intermediates do not mean that the two pathways perform the same metabolic function. The relevant isoenzymes and cofactor preferences are also important in maintaining compartment-specific metabolism.

Triose phosphate isomerase interconverts GAP and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). The two compounds have the same carbon number and molecular formula but differ in the placement of the carbonyl group. They provide complementary substrates for aldolase reactions during regeneration and downstream carbohydrate formation. A count of total triose phosphates therefore includes both forms. One should not treat GAP and DHAP as two additional products created from a single triose without an accompanying change elsewhere in the carbon balance.

For three entering CO₂ molecules, the reduction phase consumes six ATP and six NADPH because six molecules of 3-PGA need to be processed. If a question specifies only the reduction phase, the correct ATP answer is six for that scale, not nine. Nine ATP is the combined requirement after regeneration has been included. This distinction illustrates a general examination technique: identify the system boundary before applying a familiar stoichiometric number.

Following phosphate and electron transfer separately

A molecule of 3-PGA possesses a carboxyl group, a hydroxyl group and a phosphate ester. Phosphoglycerate kinase transfers the terminal phosphoryl group of ATP to the carboxyl-derived position, creating 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate. This adds a chemically reactive acyl phosphate group to the already phosphorylated carbon skeleton. The activation is not equivalent to making 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate through a simple isomerization. It depends on the transfer of a group and thus on a source of chemical free energy. When ATP becomes scarce, a pool of 3-PGA can accumulate because this step is unable to keep pace with its formation.

The subsequent NADP-dependent dehydrogenase reaction changes the oxidation state of the activated carbon. The transferable hydrogen equivalent of NADPH carries two electrons in the redox reaction; other proton transfers are accounted for by the solvent and enzyme chemistry. The acyl phosphate is replaced by the aldehyde functionality in GAP, while inorganic phosphate is released. The product still has the phosphate originally present on the three-carbon skeleton. The two phases thus cooperate: ATP provides a chemical activation route and NADPH supplies the reducing equivalent needed to form a more reduced carbon compound.

Enzyme nomenclature may appear confusing because the word dehydrogenase frequently accompanies oxidation rather than reduction in textbook examples. Many oxidoreductase reactions can be described in either direction depending on substrates, products and physiological context. The name glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reflects a familiar reaction of glyceraldehyde phosphate, but in the Calvin cycle the relevant NADP-dependent chloroplastic form contributes to its synthesis in the reverse direction. Looking only at the enzyme name without considering reactants and products can lead to a reversed prediction about NADP⁺ and NADPH consumption.

Metabolic reaction direction cannot be inferred solely from the existence of an enzyme. A catalyst changes the rate at which equilibrium is approached; it does not create a new equilibrium. In a functioning chloroplast, continuous input of ATP and NADPH, removal of products by subsequent reactions and control of substrate concentrations favor the required net directions. The overall network maintains a non-equilibrium steady state through external energy input. This is the physical basis for understanding a biochemical cycle as a flow rather than a closed collection of reversible reactions that have already equilibrated.

The stoichiometry can be checked by counting transformations rather than by memorizing the name of each stage. Each carboxylation forms two 3-PGA, each 3-PGA needs one ATP for activation, and each activated derivative needs one NADPH. For any gross carboxylation count n in the idealized pathway, reduction uses 2n ATP and 2n NADPH. The regeneration phase later adds n ATP. If a perturbation blocks reduction while Rubisco briefly remains active, the demand for both carriers from this phase falls. This decrease in consumption is capable of changing stromal nucleotide and redox balance even though the direct site of the defect is not in the light reactions.

6. Phase III: rearranging sugar phosphates and regenerating RuBP

The cycle cannot continue if all six triose phosphates leave for carbohydrate synthesis. Five of the six contain fifteen carbon atoms, which exactly match the carbon content of three five-carbon acceptors. Regeneration redistributes these fifteen carbon atoms through enzyme-catalyzed reactions that form and rearrange sugar phosphates. One net three-carbon equivalent remains available for other metabolism. No additional carbon dioxide is required for the rearrangement itself.

A convenient balanced representation starts with three GAP and two DHAP molecules among the five triose phosphates reserved for regeneration. Aldolase condenses one GAP and one DHAP to form fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, a six-carbon compound. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase removes a phosphate by hydrolysis to give fructose-6-phosphate. This reaction is a hydrolysis, not an ATP-producing reversal of phosphofructokinase. The released phosphate becomes part of the stromal phosphate pool. The other three triose phosphates remain available for subsequent steps.

Transketolase transfers a two-carbon fragment from fructose-6-phosphate to a second GAP molecule. The result is erythrose-4-phosphate and xylulose-5-phosphate. The four-carbon erythrose phosphate then combines with the remaining DHAP through an aldolase reaction to give the seven-carbon sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate. Sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase converts it to sedoheptulose-7-phosphate. At this point, one GAP remains from the initial allocation, alongside the seven-carbon compound and the first xylulose-5-phosphate.

A second transketolase reaction transfers two carbons from sedoheptulose-7-phosphate to that remaining GAP. The products are ribose-5-phosphate and another xylulose-5-phosphate. The five original trioses have thereby yielded one ribose-5-phosphate and two xylulose-5-phosphate molecules. The distinction between transketolase and aldolase is worth noting: transketolase transfers a two-carbon unit between sugar phosphates, whereas aldolase forms a longer carbon skeleton by condensation in the reactions described here. Transketolase requires thiamine pyrophosphate, connecting this mechanism with the coenzyme principles studied earlier.

Ribose-5-phosphate is isomerized to ribulose-5-phosphate, and xylulose-5-phosphate is converted into ribulose-5-phosphate by epimerase. An epimerase changes configuration at a stereogenic center, whereas an isomerase can change functional-group arrangement; neither reaction introduces new carbon. Finally phosphoribulokinase (PRK) transfers a phosphate group from ATP to each ribulose-5-phosphate, generating ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Three ATP are required for three regenerated acceptors. This last reaction is the energy investment specific to completion of the acceptor-regeneration sequence.

The detailed sequence can be checked without memorizing every intermediate: start with fifteen carbon atoms, track each reaction using carbon counts, and verify that the result remains fifteen. If a proposed scheme produces four five-carbon sugars from five triose phosphates without an extra carbon input, it violates conservation of matter. If an inhibitor affects PRK, the rearrangements may still produce pentose phosphates, but RuBP replenishment becomes defective. Such reasoning connects enzyme location to whole-cycle behavior.

Organizing the reactions into carbon-conserving modules

The network can be easier to reconstruct when its reactions are grouped by the type of carbon change. The first condensation produces a six-carbon compound from two trioses. Transfer of a two-carbon fragment from that hexose to another triose creates a four-carbon acceptor fragment and a five-carbon product. The four-carbon fragment is then combined with a further triose to give a seven-carbon compound. Finally, a second two-carbon transfer from the seven-carbon compound to the last triose produces two more five-carbon skeletons. This ordering uses every initial triose exactly once in the carbon allocation and leaves no unused intermediate at the end of the reconstruction.

A carbon ledger is especially effective in verifying this sequence. Begin with five trioses: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3. The first condensation changes the inventory to 6 + 3 + 3 + 3. The first transketolase changes it to 4 + 5 + 3 + 3. The second condensation gives 7 + 5 + 3, and the second transketolase gives 5 + 5 + 5. At no point is a carbon atom released as CO₂ or added from an external source in this ideal reconstruction. This ledger shows why aldolase and transketolase alone can change the distribution of molecule sizes without changing total carbon.

The phosphate ledger also matters. The initial five triose monophosphates contain five phosphate groups. Formation of each bisphosphate by aldolase conserves the phosphate groups of its two substrates. Hydrolysis by fructose bisphosphatase and sedoheptulose bisphosphatase releases one phosphate each. Three pentose monophosphates therefore remain, carrying a total of three phosphate groups. Phosphoribulokinase uses three ATP molecules to add the three missing phosphate groups, resulting in three RuBP molecules each with two phosphates. The combination of hydrolytic steps and ATP-dependent phosphorylation explains why regeneration does not merely rearrange carbon atoms.

Both types of phosphatase are associated with directional control. Their reactions remove phosphate by hydrolysis rather than transferring it to ADP to make ATP. In a highly active photosynthetic chloroplast, the corresponding net reactions favor regeneration of carbon skeletons. A student may recall that glycolysis uses phosphofructokinase to phosphorylate fructose-6-phosphate; the Calvin cycle instead uses a phosphatase in the relevant direction. The existence of distinct enzyme activities permits physiological regulation without assuming every ATP-dependent reaction must run backward under photosynthetic conditions.

Aldolase accepts appropriately oriented carbon skeletons rather than simply combining any two sugar phosphates of suitable size. The difference between GAP and DHAP matters because the chemistry of their carbonyl groups makes them complementary partners in the described condensations. The stoichiometric statement that five trioses supply fifteen carbons is necessary but does not by itself specify a feasible sequence of reactions. The detailed enzymatic pathway shows how the actual available molecular structures permit that conservation law to be satisfied.

A transketolase deficiency could impair both transfer steps described above, because they use the same class of enzymatic activity. The expected steady-state metabolic effects cannot be predicted solely by identifying a single accumulated substrate: altered carbon flux, feedback and alternative reactions may change several pools simultaneously. Nevertheless the direct catalytic lesion gives a strong prediction that the conversion of triose-derived carbon into the correct pentose inventory will be impaired. Rescue by adding a downstream intermediate in a defined extract, provided all other substrates and activities are available, can help test that hypothesis. A whole intact leaf would also require that the supplied compound reach the correct compartment.

7. Carbon and energy accounting for complete turnover

It is helpful to calculate the budget for three CO₂ molecules rather than for one, because a net three-carbon product can then be represented by whole numbers. Three RuBP molecules contain fifteen carbon atoms. Three incoming carbon dioxide molecules bring three more. Six molecules of 3-PGA contain eighteen carbon atoms, and their reduction yields six triose phosphate molecules with the same total. Five trioses, containing fifteen carbons, regenerate the acceptors. One triose, containing three carbons, is the ideal net product.

Six ATP are used during phosphorylation of six 3-PGA molecules, and three more during regeneration of three RuBP molecules. Each of the six activated 3-PGA derivatives requires one NADPH for reduction. The result is nine ATP and six NADPH for three assimilated CO₂ molecules. Dividing by three gives a formal average of three ATP and two NADPH per CO₂. This average does not describe an isolated one-CO₂ reaction that releases a complete triose. It is a normalized rate or stoichiometric cost for the operation of the full cycle.

For a six-carbon sugar equivalent, the ideal demand is eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH, because six CO₂ molecules have to enter. The Calvin cycle itself yields net triose phosphates, not a free glucose molecule in one final enzymatic step. Further transformations are needed to synthesize hexose phosphates, sucrose or starch. Their energetic requirements, as well as photorespiration and additional regulatory ATP usage, are not included in the ideal eighteen/twelve figure. Therefore this figure is a minimum core carbon-reduction budget under the stated assumptions, rather than a complete energy balance for a growing leaf.

Suppose a chloroplast can make 90 nanomoles of ATP and 48 nanomoles of NADPH available for the ideal cycle in a specified interval. The ATP supply could sustain fixation of 90 divided by 3, or 30 nanomoles of CO₂. NADPH could sustain only 48 divided by 2, or 24 nanomoles. The lower value gives the maximum possible fixation in this simple supply model, namely 24 nanomoles of CO₂, equivalent to 8 nanomoles of net triose phosphate. Seventy-two nanomoles of ATP are required at that rate. The residual ATP does not guarantee additional fixation because the reducing equivalents remain limiting.

This calculation assumes the stated carriers are entirely available for the ideal cycle, there is enough active Rubisco and CO₂, and no carbon is lost through oxygenation. These assumptions are useful for examination reasoning but cannot be silently applied to field-grown plants. A measured ATP pool size, for example, is not equivalent to an ATP supply flux. A small pool can support high activity if continuously regenerated, and a large pool need not be accessible to the relevant enzymes. Units and the system boundary must match the question.

Supply rates, product rates and a net budget

A formal carbon-assimilation cost must have units that match the metabolic quantity specified. If gross fixation is measured in micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second, the ideal ATP demand is three times that number in micromoles of ATP per square metre per second. The NADPH demand is twice that number in corresponding NADPH units. Dividing by three gives the net rate of triose equivalents only if the acceptor pools remain steady and there are no additional carbon inputs or losses. The numerical conversion is not valid for a transient pulse during which pool sizes change substantially.

For a leaf assimilating twelve micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second in an idealized no-oxygenation model, the ATP requirement is thirty-six micromoles per square metre per second, of which twenty-four are assigned to reduction and twelve to regeneration. The NADPH requirement is twenty-four in the same area and time units. The net triose phosphate equivalent is four micromoles per square metre per second. If a problem states that ATP delivery is reduced to thirty while NADPH delivery stays at twenty-four, the new theoretical supply ceiling becomes ten micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second. The cycle cannot maintain its original steady-state assimilation at those constrained inputs.

Now suppose two measurements are given as percentages rather than amounts. An inhibitor decreases available ATP supply by twenty percent and NADPH supply by ten percent from exactly balanced starting values. The ATP-limited ceiling becomes eighty percent of the starting gross carboxylation rate, while the NADPH-limited ceiling becomes ninety percent. Under the ideal model the lower ceiling is eighty percent. If the initial ATP supply had instead been much greater than its minimum requirement, the same percentage changes might have a different effect. The starting balance between demand and supply is essential to interpreting percent changes.

The net stoichiometry can also be expressed with carrier recycling rather than focusing only on consumed compounds. Every ATP consumed at these kinase steps gives ADP, and every NADPH used in reduction gives NADP⁺. In ideal accounting, fixing three CO₂ therefore returns nine ADP and six NADP⁺ equivalents to the corresponding metabolic pools. Inorganic phosphate is released in several reactions, and the retained phosphate in the net triose must be recognized if one writes a fully balanced equation with explicit phosphate and proton conventions. Different textbooks may group protons and water differently depending on the ionic forms used. Carbon and carrier balances are the invariant features needed for this examination-level calculation.

The minimum photon requirement of the linear light reactions gives a separate comparison. Four electron equivalents are needed for the formal reduction of a carbon dioxide carbon to the average carbohydrate oxidation state. Oxygenic linear electron transport requires one photochemical excitation in each photosystem per transferred electron. In a perfect idealization this gives eight photochemical excitation events for four electrons, or eight per assimilated CO₂ if the required reducing equivalents come entirely from linear flow. This is not an exact photon cost for a leaf: ATP balancing may require extra cyclic flow, absorbed photons are not all used photochemically, and photorespiration and other processes increase demand. It is also essential to distinguish incident photons from successfully absorbed and productively used photons. These qualifications prevent a stoichiometric lower bound from being mistaken for measured quantum yield.

8. Coordinating light, enzyme activation and metabolic flux

Illumination changes not only the concentrations of ATP and NADPH but also the chemical conditions under which stromal enzymes operate. Proton translocation toward the thylakoid lumen contributes to a more alkaline stroma during illumination. Changes in magnesium distribution accompany this shift. Many enzymes of carbon assimilation operate more effectively under the appropriate illuminated stromal conditions than they do in the dark. It would be misleading to interpret the pH change as an independent supply of carbon or as a replacement for ATP.

An important regulatory link connects photosystem I to ferredoxin and thioredoxin. Reduced ferredoxin can supply electrons through ferredoxin–thioredoxin reductase, allowing thioredoxins to reduce regulatory disulfide bonds in target proteins. This can change the activities of stromal enzymes such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase and phosphoribulokinase. The effect is coordinated with illumination because the electron-transfer system supplies reduced ferredoxin during photosynthesis. The redox control is conceptually distinct from NADPH consumption during the chemical reduction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate.

When the light is removed, a leaf does not instantly lose every ATP molecule or deactivate every enzyme. Metabolic and regulatory changes occur over finite times. Therefore the measured response depends on when a sample is collected. A sample taken a fraction of a second after darkening may show a different distribution of intermediates from one taken after several minutes. One should not infer that light is unnecessary from the detection of brief post-illumination metabolism.

Rubisco activase adds another regulatory layer. It helps maintain active Rubisco sites, but its own ability to function is sensitive to physiological state, including nucleotide balance and temperature. Under heat stress, activation may become limiting even if isolated Rubisco can catalyze carboxylation effectively under standardized assay conditions. Comparing actual and maximal enzyme activity can distinguish changes in activation from changes in enzyme abundance. A decline in assimilation with unchanged protein abundance is consequently not a paradox.

Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I can supplement ATP production relative to NADPH and contributes to maintaining the energy balance under conditions where metabolic demands shift. Its role should be understood as a response to the demand of several chloroplast processes rather than as a mechanism guaranteeing an immutable ATP/NADPH output ratio. The ideal C3 budget requires an ATP-to-NADPH consumption ratio of 3:2; the total requirement of an intact chloroplast can deviate from that ratio because of oxygenation, biosynthesis and regulation.

Why coordinated activation is advantageous

Carbon assimilation requires many reactions to carry a compatible flux. If one enzyme in regeneration is highly active while a downstream enzyme remains inactive, carbon may accumulate in an intermediate rather than completing the cycle. Regulation of several enzymes through light-associated signals helps the chloroplast avoid mismatched activities when illumination changes. This does not mean that all enzymes have identical regulation or respond at identical rates. The relevant advantage is coordination at the level of pathway function.

A redox-regulated enzyme can contain a regulatory disulfide bond formed between cysteine residues. Reduction converts the disulfide into thiol groups, which may favor a conformation with different catalytic activity. Oxidation can restore the disulfide. This kind of reversible modification changes the behavior of a pre-existing protein and does not require a new protein molecule to be synthesized every time light changes. Because the electrons ultimately arise from a light-driven system, the regulatory state communicates the availability of photosynthetic reducing power to enzymes that consume metabolic resources.

The specificity of the redox signal matters. Reduced ferredoxin does not indiscriminately reduce every protein or replace the NADPH used by glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. It can transfer electrons through dedicated regulatory components. The existence of different routes allows a common source of light-derived reducing equivalents to support both chemical carbon reduction and regulation. If a regulatory protein is defective while the ATP and NADPH pools remain adequate, the carbon-assimilation response may still be impaired because certain enzymes fail to reach their normal active state.

When a previously dark-adapted leaf is illuminated, there can be an induction period before full photosynthetic rates are attained. Energy supply begins to respond rapidly, but the activation of enzymes, changes in metabolite pools and stomatal responses need time. A comparison between a light-adapted leaf and a dark-adapted leaf during the first seconds or minutes of illumination can reveal different initial rates despite equal incident light. A prolonged steady-state measurement alone would not reveal all the transient causes. Timing and pretreatment therefore form an essential part of the experimental description.

Conversely, exposing a leaf to fluctuating light can repeatedly shift the balance between ATP/NADPH supply and carbon demand. If activation is slow when light increases, some of the absorbed energy cannot immediately support increased carbon assimilation. If the light then falls, enzyme activation may briefly persist although the supply of newly formed ATP and NADPH declines. This produces a dynamic mismatch, not a permanent contradiction between light and carbon reactions. The previous photoprotection chapter provides a conceptual context for the management of excess excitation during such changes.

9. Net carbon products: starch, sucrose and phosphate exchange

A newly formed triose phosphate may remain in the chloroplast for continued cycling, be used in stromal carbohydrate synthesis or be exchanged across the chloroplast envelope. A common daytime storage destination is transient starch in the chloroplast. Another destination is cytosolic sucrose, which can be transported from photosynthetically active source tissues to growing or storage sinks. These downstream routes allocate assimilated carbon rather than creating new carbon atoms.

The triose phosphate/phosphate translocator in the chloroplast inner envelope participates in exchange of phosphorylated carbon intermediates with inorganic phosphate. This exchange supports export of assimilates while replenishing phosphate required for stromal metabolism and ATP production. Sucrose synthesis in the cytosol includes dephosphorylation reactions that help make phosphate available for return. The coordinated traffic means that an apparently distant bottleneck in cytosolic metabolism can influence photosynthesis inside the chloroplast.

Starch formation helps buffer a mismatch between daytime carbon fixation and the immediate capacity to use or export assimilates. At night, degradation of stored carbohydrate supports metabolism and growth when current photosynthetic carbon assimilation is low or absent. The amount partitioned to starch or sucrose varies with plant species, development, environment and sink demand. The storage product should not be confused with the first stable carboxylation product, 3-PGA, or with the ideal net triose output of the Calvin cycle.

Suppose a leaf receives bright light and adequate CO₂ but its sinks cannot accept additional sucrose. The immediate response is not necessarily a permanent cessation of Rubisco catalysis. Metabolite concentrations, starch synthesis and feedback regulation may temporarily accommodate the altered demand. If utilization of triose phosphate remains limiting, recycling of phosphate and availability of ATP can eventually constrain the rate of assimilation. This is one reason that increasing light or CO₂ alone cannot be expected to increase photosynthesis without bound.

Why export requires phosphate balance rather than carbon transport alone

A phosphorylated triose is charged at physiological pH and does not freely cross a lipid bilayer as though it were an uncharged gas. The chloroplast envelope therefore requires specific transport activities to coordinate movement of phosphorylated metabolites. The triose phosphate/phosphate translocator couples outward transfer of suitable phosphorylated carbon compounds to inward transfer of inorganic phosphate or related counter-substrates. By exchanging phosphorylated carbon for phosphate, the chloroplast can retain phosphorus needed for continued ATP synthesis even while supplying carbon to the cytosol.

The amount of inorganic phosphate available is determined by several interacting pools. Phosphate can be present in nucleotides, sugar phosphates and free inorganic forms. Accumulation of organic phosphorylated intermediates may reduce the free inorganic phosphate pool even when total cellular phosphorus is unchanged. ATP synthase needs inorganic phosphate as a substrate, and inadequate free phosphate can constrain its ability to sustain ATP formation. This explains why phosphate recycling affects energy metabolism rather than merely mineral nutrition.

A source leaf normally coordinates production and export with the needs of sinks such as growing leaves, roots, fruits or seeds. A developing sink can promote sustained demand for exported carbon, whereas a reduction in sink activity can alter the distribution between soluble sugars and starch. However, source–sink feedback is not instantaneous and may involve several physiological processes. If a measurement shows that starch increases while net assimilation initially remains constant, it may indicate that carbon has been diverted toward storage. If assimilation later decreases, the result could reflect altered product utilization or other regulatory limitations. A single starch measurement cannot establish the full causal sequence.

Two trioses can contribute to a six-carbon hexose phosphate through complementary sugar-phosphate chemistry. Yet a real leaf does not necessarily route each newly fixed carbon atom into sucrose in the same interval in which it was fixed. Some carbon remains in intermediate pools, some supplies respiration and biosynthesis, and some enters storage polymers. Thus, the theoretical rate of net triose formation should be distinguished from the measured rate of sucrose export. To relate the two quantitatively, one must specify carbon allocation and the time scale, not merely divide the carbon uptake rate by six.

10. Rubisco oxygenation and the physiological limits of C3 plants

Rubisco can catalyze an alternative reaction in which molecular oxygen reacts with RuBP. Instead of forming two 3-PGA molecules, oxygenation yields one 3-PGA and one two-carbon 2-phosphoglycolate. No carbon dioxide is assimilated in this reaction. The original five carbons are conserved initially in the three-carbon and two-carbon products, but the subsequent recovery of phosphoglycolate carbon through photorespiration leads to energy expenditure and carbon dioxide release. The complete recovery pathway is treated in Subunit 6.B.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide compete as alternative substrates at the same enzyme system. The relative rates depend on concentrations at the enzyme and its discrimination between the two gases. Under a simple competitive model, the ratio of carboxylation rate to oxygenation rate is the specificity factor multiplied by the dissolved CO₂/O₂ concentration ratio. The concentrations must refer to the conditions around the enzyme, not merely the percentages of gases in the external atmosphere. This distinction is important because diffusion, solubility and metabolic activity influence local concentrations.

When stomata close during water deficit, less CO₂ can diffuse inward. If oxygen concentration and enzyme properties remain comparable, the lower CO₂ availability shifts the relative balance toward oxygenation. Higher temperature can also increase the relative importance of oxygenation through changes in dissolved gas ratios and Rubisco specificity. These responses are not universal predictions about the absolute photosynthetic rate at every temperature. At low temperature, warming may accelerate many biochemical reactions, whereas above the physiological optimum heat can reduce activation and damage metabolic capacity.

Consider an illustrative enzyme system with a specificity factor of 80 and a dissolved CO₂/O₂ ratio of 0.025. The ratio of carboxylation to oxygenation rates is 80 multiplied by 0.025, which equals two. Out of three catalytic events in this simplified model, two are carboxylations and one is oxygenation. If the gas ratio halves with no change in specificity, the rates become equal. This is not a measurement of Rubisco in a particular species; it demonstrates the interaction of enzyme specificity and substrate availability. Neither a specificity factor of 80 nor a gas ratio of 0.025 should be memorized as a universal constant.

Net CO₂ assimilation must be distinguished from gross carboxylation. With conventional photorespiratory bookkeeping, two oxygenations give rise to the release of one CO₂ during recovery of phosphoglycolate carbon. A simplified leaf carbon balance may be expressed as A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd, where A is net CO₂ uptake, Vc is Rubisco carboxylation rate, Vo is oxygenation rate, and Rd is the relevant nonphotorespiratory respiratory CO₂ release in the light. All terms must share compatible flux units. The equation assumes standard pathway accounting and does not independently determine stomatal or electron-transport limitation.

For example, with Vc = 20, Vo = 8 and Rd = 1 in micromoles of CO₂ equivalents per square metre per second, the net uptake is 20 − 4 − 1 = 15. Suppressing oxygenation while leaving carboxylation and respiration fixed would raise that calculated rate to 19. A real experimental change in O₂ may also influence other processes and carboxylation itself, so the fixed-rate prediction is conditional. The exercise teaches the difference between counting enzyme reactions and measuring exchange at the whole leaf surface.

Competition between alternative substrates

The specificity factor for Rubisco can be understood by comparing catalytic efficiencies for its two gas substrates. For a simplified model at a given temperature and appropriate RuBP conditions, define S as (kcat,c/Kc)/(kcat,o/Ko), where kcat,c and kcat,o are catalytic turnover constants for carboxylation and oxygenation, and Kc and Ko are their corresponding kinetic constants. The ratio compares two efficiencies and is dimensionless when the concentration units are the same. Multiplication by dissolved CO₂ concentration divided by dissolved O₂ concentration gives the relative rates in the conventional competition expression.

A higher specificity factor does not mean that the enzyme is completely incapable of oxygenation. It means that, for equal dissolved substrate concentrations in the model, the enzyme discriminates more strongly in favor of carbon dioxide. At the same specificity, a fall in dissolved CO₂ concentration decreases the carboxylation-to-oxygenation ratio. A comparison between two enzymes should therefore distinguish intrinsic kinetic properties from the gas environment. An enzyme with high specificity operating in a low-CO₂ environment may still have substantial oxygenation relative to carboxylation.

Temperature can affect both parts of this relationship. Gases have different solubilities in aqueous media and their solubilities change with temperature; enzyme kinetic constants also vary. Rubisco generally discriminates less strongly against oxygenation as temperature rises within physiologically relevant ranges, while the dissolved CO₂/O₂ balance also changes. These effects may favor oxygenation even when external gas fractions have not changed. At the same time the maximal turnover of carboxylation, the activation state of Rubisco and electron-transport capacity may change. Consequently the relative oxygenation fraction and absolute net assimilation rate are not interchangeable measurements.

The stoichiometry of phosphoglycolate recovery explains the factor of one half in the carbon balance. Two oxygenation events form two molecules of 2-phosphoglycolate containing four carbons altogether. In the conventional photorespiratory salvage process, three of those carbons are returned to photosynthetic metabolism as a three-carbon compound and one is released as CO₂. Thus each oxygenation is associated on average with a half molecule of carbon dioxide released through that recovery bookkeeping. The detailed enzymes, organelles and nitrogen recycling are reserved for the photorespiration chapter. Here the essential consequence is that oxygenation lowers carbon recovery and imposes additional metabolic costs.

At the CO₂ compensation point under a specified illuminated condition, net uptake equals zero even though carboxylation and oxygenation may still be occurring. Ignoring ordinary respiratory CO₂ release for a moment, the simplified relation Vc = 0.5Vo gives Vc/Vo = 0.5. Substitution into the competitive expression gives dissolved CO₂/O₂ = 0.5/S. If respiration contributes an additional loss, carboxylation must exceed half the oxygenation rate for zero net exchange. The ordinary measured compensation point therefore depends on more than intrinsic Rubisco specificity alone. This is a useful conceptual distinction without needing to memorize values for particular species.

Photorespiratory salvage should not be called a useless chemical accident. Oxygenation imposes a cost, but once phosphoglycolate is formed, recovery of carbon and removal of potentially disruptive intermediates serve important cellular functions. Suppressing the initial oxygenation and abolishing the recovery pathway are very different interventions. If the latter is impaired while oxygenation continues, the consequences can be severe because both carbon recycling and metabolite homeostasis are disrupted. The C4 and CAM strategies mainly address the first problem by changing CO₂ availability around Rubisco rather than eliminating all need for photorespiratory capacity.

11. Interpreting carbon dioxide, light and temperature responses

A gas-exchange instrument can measure the difference between incoming and outgoing CO₂ in a chamber containing a leaf. Combining this difference with gas flow and leaf area allows a net assimilation rate to be estimated. The measured quantity includes effects of diffusion, carboxylation, oxygenation and respiration. It is not a direct isolated assay of Rubisco catalytic activity. Comparing leaf gas exchange with enzyme assays or fluorescence measurements can therefore provide more informative mechanistic tests than any one measurement alone.

At low CO₂ availability, increasing CO₂ generally favors carboxylation and suppresses the relative contribution of oxygenation in a C3 leaf. As CO₂ increases, however, the supply of RuBP or metabolic energy can become more restrictive. At still higher assimilation demands, use of triose phosphate may limit turnover. A curve of net assimilation against intercellular CO₂ can reveal different limiting regions, but the shape must be interpreted in light of the experimental conditions. Intercellular CO₂ is not identical to chloroplastic CO₂ when mesophyll diffusion resistance is appreciable.

A light response curve likewise has distinct regimes. Under very dim illumination, photon capture and photochemical energy supply constrain the rate, and respiratory carbon release may exceed fixation. As light increases, the rate eventually approaches a range where other factors constrain additional assimilation. Excess illumination can impose stress even when carbon uptake no longer increases. The light compensation point is the irradiance at which net CO₂ exchange is zero under specified conditions, while the CO₂ compensation point is the CO₂ level at which net exchange is zero under specified illumination and other conditions. Confusing these two independent variables leads to incorrect graph interpretation.

To test whether a low net rate is largely caused by oxygenation, a researcher could compare assimilation at normal and reduced oxygen under otherwise matched light, temperature, humidity and CO₂ conditions. An increase in net assimilation at reduced oxygen is consistent with reduced competition and photorespiratory loss. It does not establish that every change is caused solely by one enzyme, because oxygen can affect other cellular processes. Measurements of metabolite pools, chlorophyll fluorescence and enzyme activation may help distinguish mechanisms. Such controlled comparisons underpin the reasoning used in C3/C4 physiology.

The upcoming C4 and CAM chapters will build on this limit. Rather than replacing the Calvin cycle, these mechanisms increase the CO₂ concentration available to Rubisco in different ways. An additional carbon-concentrating pathway can reduce photorespiratory loss but requires metabolic investment and specialized physiological organization. The baseline C3 cycle described here makes that trade-off intelligible.

Separating diffusion, biochemical and environmental constraints

An assimilation-versus-intercellular-CO₂ curve is often constructed by changing the external CO₂ concentration in a controlled gas-exchange chamber while estimating the intercellular value from measured flux and conductance. Under low CO₂, carboxylation substrate availability and Rubisco capacity strongly influence the response. At higher CO₂, the rate may become constrained by the production and regeneration of RuBP through energy metabolism. A further limitation can arise when utilization of triose phosphate cannot sustain the carbon flux. These are conceptual regimes, not sharply separated universal zones in every measured leaf.

If two leaves display similar assimilation at ambient CO₂ but diverge at higher intercellular CO₂, their shared ambient rate does not establish that their biochemical capacities are identical. One leaf might have lower regeneration capacity that is not exposed when both are strongly CO₂-limited. Conversely, if two leaves have comparable rates at matched high intercellular CO₂ but differ under normal air, diffusion or Rubisco-related limitations may deserve investigation. A full interpretation should consider mesophyll conductance, enzyme properties and experimental conditions. The purpose of manipulating the substrate concentration is to expose constraints that may be hidden under one fixed condition.

A light-response experiment also requires control of leaf temperature because changing irradiance can change thermal conditions. Humidity and stomatal aperture affect access to CO₂, while prior light exposure affects induction. If the response of two leaves is measured under different pretreatments or temperatures, a difference in initial slope cannot be attributed confidently to a difference in photosystem efficiency alone. Measuring absorbed light rather than merely incident light is helpful when leaves differ in pigment content or optical properties. These controls illustrate how experimental design follows from the multiple processes involved in the measured rate.

Under drought, closing stomata can reduce water loss while also limiting CO₂ entry. The trade-off is not that the plant chooses water loss or carbon fixation independently; both are influenced by gas exchange through the same pores. A mild change in water availability may alter stomatal conductance before severe biochemical injury occurs. With more intense or prolonged stress, metabolic capacity, enzyme activity and photoprotection can also change. Increasing ambient CO₂ in a controlled experiment may relieve some limitation caused by restricted substrate supply, but it would not necessarily repair damaged metabolic machinery. Comparing rescue and non-rescue responses can help distinguish hypotheses.

C3 photosynthesis is not inherently inferior under all conditions. In cool or moderate conditions with adequate water, CO₂ availability and suitable light, the extra ATP investment required by a carbon-concentrating mechanism may offer less advantage. C3, C4 and CAM physiologies represent different solutions to environmental and metabolic constraints rather than a simple ranking of pathway quality. Wheat and rice provide familiar examples of C3 crops, while maize illustrates a C4 strategy. Actual productivity also depends on development, nutrient supply and growth conditions. The appropriate comparison uses a specific set of environmental conditions and a defined measure such as carbon uptake, water-use efficiency or biomass, rather than an unsupported generalization about all species.

12. Integrating the phases: metabolic pools, fluxes and perturbations

The cycle as a set of coupled balances

A pathway map is often drawn with equally sized arrows, yet the arrows denote chemical transformations rather than measured rates. To reason about the behavior of a living chloroplast, it is useful to attach a rate to each major transformation. Let the rate of Rubisco carboxylation be Vc, the rate of oxygenation be Vo, and the rate of formation of RuBP by phosphoribulokinase be Vr. Since either Rubisco reaction consumes one RuBP, an idealized local RuBP balance can be written as change in RuBP pool per unit time = Vr − Vc − Vo. This equation is simply a statement of conservation of acceptor molecules. It does not require a sophisticated metabolic model, but it already explains why a steady rate cannot persist if regeneration is slower than the combined reactions of Rubisco.

When oxygenation is neglected and the pool is at steady state, Vr equals Vc. This is the kinetic meaning of the carbon ledger for the ideal cycle. Steady state does not require that the three rates be zero. It requires that the entry and exit rates for the measured pool match. If a leaf fixes carbon rapidly and steadily, a given amount of RuBP may be produced and consumed many times during a measurement interval. A snapshot of the amount of RuBP cannot reveal how many times it has been renewed unless a turnover or flux measurement is also available.

A corresponding simple balance for 3-PGA formation includes two units from each carboxylation and one from each oxygenation. Return of three-carbon material from photorespiratory salvage can add to this formation term, while phosphoglycerate kinase removes 3-PGA into the reduction pathway. Other metabolic inputs or withdrawals must be included if they are important under the experimental conditions. Immediately after a change in light or CO₂, these terms need not be balanced. An increase in the 3-PGA pool means that its total formation currently exceeds its total removal, rather than automatically proving that Rubisco carboxylation has accelerated.

A similar principle applies to the triose phosphate pool. In an ideal no-oxygenation model, every carboxylation eventually yields two triose phosphates through reduction. Of the six formed from three CO₂, the regeneration pathway requires five. In a steady-state rate description, the triose-equivalent demand for regeneration is five-thirds of the carboxylation flux, while the net output is one-third of that flux. A value of five-thirds is not a fractional molecule in a single reaction; it expresses an average relation among many reactions in a population of molecules. Using rates allows the stoichiometric relation to be extended naturally to any steady rate of assimilation.

Predicting the response to a specific lesion

Consider a model chloroplast that begins at steady state under conditions of negligible oxygenation. Suppose its carboxylation and RuBP regeneration rates are both ten nanomoles per minute. An intervention reduces regeneration to six nanomoles per minute while the carboxylation rate remains temporarily at ten. The initial net loss of RuBP is four nanomoles per minute. If this difference persisted for thirty seconds, the RuBP pool would decline by two nanomoles. The calculation specifies an initial rate and a short interval; it cannot legitimately be extended indefinitely because falling RuBP eventually changes the rate of carboxylation. The steady state reached later depends on the remaining regeneration capacity and other constraints.

The same perturbation need not cause an immediate reduction in the ATP production rate of the light reactions. Indeed, a kinase defect can reduce ATP consumption before ATP generation has adjusted. A measurement of the ATP pool shortly afterward might even show an increase rather than a decrease. This does not contradict the loss of assimilation. A metabolite can accumulate because its use is impaired. The correct interpretation depends on the difference between supply and demand, and not on the assumption that a high concentration of a useful substance must indicate a high rate of the process that normally uses it.

If phosphoglycerate kinase is specifically inhibited, newly formed 3-PGA cannot enter the reduction phase at its usual rate. Early in the response, carboxylation can still consume RuBP, so 3-PGA tends to accumulate if alternative removal pathways do not compensate. The later decline in the supply of reduced triose phosphates then impairs regeneration. A downstream bottleneck can therefore influence an upstream reaction through a change in acceptor supply. This is a general feature of cyclic pathways and differs from a simple linear pathway with an indefinitely replenished external first substrate.

A lesion in Rubisco carboxylation gives a different initial prediction. If regeneration temporarily continues while consumption of RuBP by the impaired reaction falls, the acceptor pool may increase. Formation of 3-PGA from that route decreases, even if the remaining enzymes have full capacity. One must specify whether the lesion affects carboxylation alone or also oxygenation. Because the same enzyme catalyzes both alternatives, an actual change in Rubisco activity can influence both. The model is most informative when the experimental manipulation is described precisely.

Consider also the distinction between an inhibitor and a mutant. An inhibitor introduced into an already functioning chloroplast can reveal transient responses before substantial physiological adjustment. A genetic change present throughout leaf development can produce compensatory changes in protein abundance, photosystem investment, growth or leaf structure. The steady-state phenotype of a mutant may therefore differ from the immediate response to inhibiting the same enzyme in a wild-type leaf. Neither form of evidence is automatically stronger; they answer different questions. Agreement among acute perturbation, enzyme assay and genetic evidence would strengthen a mechanistic interpretation.

Measuring a bottleneck rather than assuming one

It is common to call Rubisco a rate-limiting enzyme of photosynthesis, but the term should be applied conditionally. When CO₂ availability or active Rubisco capacity restricts the flux, increasing the relevant carboxylation capacity may increase assimilation. When regeneration or product use is more restrictive, adding Rubisco alone may have little effect. A cycle has no unique permanently limiting step independent of the environment. Flux control can be distributed among several reactions, and its distribution can change as conditions change.

An instructive comparison uses two hypothetical leaf extracts with equal maximum Rubisco capacity. One has lower PRK activity. Under saturating ATP and NADPH in an assay of Rubisco alone, the extracts may give equal carboxylation rates if RuBP is supplied externally. In a system that must generate its own RuBP from triose phosphates, the second extract may be unable to sustain the same overall rate. These are not inconsistent observations because the two assays impose different substrate boundaries. The isolated assay tests the capacity of one enzyme; the self-regenerating system tests coordination of several activities.

The most informative rescue experiment addresses the proposed bottleneck directly while controlling the rest of the system. If an isolated preparation accumulates 3-PGA and fails to make GAP, supplying 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate could in principle bypass the PGK step, provided it is stable and accessible in the assay and NADPH and GAPDH remain available. Recovery of GAP formation would be consistent with a defect upstream of the supplied intermediate. Lack of recovery would not by itself establish a second specific lesion: the supplied substrate might be degraded, excluded from the relevant compartment or unavailable at the necessary concentration. Rescue is evidence that must be interpreted with its experimental controls.

This approach gives a general framework for Part C questions. First identify the transformation directly affected. Next identify the immediate substrate and product of that transformation. Then determine which other cycle reaction loses a required input. Finally distinguish initial responses from the later steady state. This is more reliable than treating every inhibitor of photosynthesis as though it directly stopped photosystem II or every accumulation of an intermediate as evidence of increased upstream production.

13. Evidence-based interpretation of C3 assimilation measurements

Gas-exchange rate and the amount of fixed carbon

Suppose a chamber supplies dry air to a leaf at a molar flow of 0.0004 mol per second. The inlet CO₂ mole fraction is 420 micromoles per mole of air and the outlet fraction is 390 micromoles per mole. Ignoring water-vapor dilution and other corrections for this teaching example, the difference is thirty micromoles of CO₂ per mole of air. Multiplying by the flow gives 0.012 micromoles of net CO₂ removed per second. If the enclosed leaf area is 0.002 square metres, the area-normalized uptake is six micromoles per square metre per second.

This worked calculation illustrates why a CO₂ concentration difference alone is not a photosynthetic rate. Doubling the airflow while retaining the same difference doubles the inferred amount removed per unit time. Doubling the leaf area while retaining the same total removal halves the rate per unit leaf area. Experiments comparing leaves need consistent units and a defined area normalization. Real gas-exchange instruments also handle water vapor, pressure, flow corrections and other measurement details, but the elementary balance is sufficient to understand the principle.

An empty-chamber measurement can reveal instrumental offsets or leaks, and stable conditions should be established before interpreting steady assimilation. If the measured outlet concentration is greater than the inlet concentration, the net carbon exchange is in the opposite direction from uptake. This does not require an unusual reverse Calvin cycle. Respiratory carbon release can exceed carbon assimilation when illumination is inadequate. If CO₂ uptake is close to zero, even a small instrumental offset may be large relative to the net signal, which is why measurement quality is particularly important around a compensation point.

A stable measured net uptake does not mean that the rate of gross carboxylation is exactly the reported value. The earlier equation relating net assimilation to carboxylation, oxygenation and respiration describes why. For example, a net rate of six could correspond to Vc of ten, Vo of six and Rd of one in compatible units, or to Vc of eight, Vo of two and Rd of one. Both yield the same net rate but imply different underlying enzyme reactions and photorespiratory costs. A single net measurement cannot uniquely identify all these fluxes. Further constraints are needed to distinguish them.

Interpreting a controlled comparison

The following hypothetical results illustrate how manipulating both CO₂ and O₂ can test a mechanistic hypothesis. Suppose at a moderate fixed irradiance and temperature, net uptake at low internal CO₂ is four units in normal oxygen and eight units in reduced oxygen. At higher internal CO₂, the rates are fourteen and fifteen units respectively. The oxygen effect is larger in the low-CO₂ comparison. That pattern is consistent with competition between oxygen and carbon dioxide, because the relative importance of oxygenation is expected to be greater when CO₂ availability is low.

The difference between the paired rates should not automatically be interpreted as an exact quantitative measure of photorespiratory CO₂ release. Reducing oxygen may alter other processes or change the rate of carboxylation itself. If the experiment is interpreted as though Vc is fixed, the assumption must be stated and defended. The appropriate conclusion from the illustrative pattern is that the oxygen response is consistent with the expected gas competition and that the interaction with CO₂ strengthens that interpretation. It is not a complete independent determination of every flux in the carbon network.

The comparison should maintain similar leaf temperature, irradiance and developmental condition. Humidity and stomatal behavior also need attention because changing internal gas composition is more complicated than changing only the setting of an external gas cylinder. Where possible, an analysis based on estimated internal CO₂ helps reduce a particular confounding influence, though intercellular and chloroplastic concentrations may still differ. A matched or repeated measurement with attention to order effects is often more informative than comparing unrelated leaves under unrelated conditions.

A complementary measurement of chlorophyll fluorescence can provide information about photosystem II photochemistry and excitation use. If net carbon uptake falls while measures of photochemical activity remain relatively high, it may suggest that electrons are being used in processes not captured by net CO₂ assimilation or that carbon-side limitations are developing. A decrease in fluorescence-derived photochemical yield may instead accompany impaired electron transport or protective regulation. Neither fluorescence nor gas exchange alone reveals the complete pathway. Combining them helps distinguish the photochemical supply side from the carbon-utilization side without assuming that every electron transferred must become part of net carbohydrate production.

Connecting assay design to the biological question

A direct assay of Rubisco can test whether a sample has the biochemical capacity for carboxylation when the required substrates and appropriate activation conditions are present. A whole-leaf gas-exchange assay instead asks how much carbon is gained under specified environmental conditions. A tracer experiment asks where newly supplied carbon goes and how quickly labeling is redistributed. These three approaches measure related but different quantities. When results appear contradictory, checking the experimental system boundary often resolves the apparent contradiction.

For instance, an extract may contain abundant active Rubisco, yet an intact leaf from which it was prepared may have low uptake during water stress. Limited CO₂ diffusion in the intact leaf can prevent the enzyme from expressing its full potential. Alternatively, a low whole-leaf rate might persist at experimentally elevated CO₂ because another biochemical capacity is limiting. Comparing responses across conditions, together with enzyme measurements, permits a narrower mechanistic interpretation than simply assigning every decrease in uptake to Rubisco.

An examination problem may provide a table of results without identifying the limiting step explicitly. The task is to identify which interpretation is supported and which would require additional data. A change in the variable manipulated, followed by a predicted change in the measured rate, supports the hypothesis that the manipulated condition matters. It does not necessarily prove that the proposed molecular pathway is the only route connecting those quantities. Alternative mechanisms must be excluded or measured when they make different predictions. The Calvin cycle is a useful model for learning this distinction because multiple experimentally accessible inputs converge on a single measured net assimilation rate.

14. C3 assimilation in the whole plant and comparison with related metabolism

The carbon balance extends beyond the chloroplast

Carbon exported from the Calvin cycle is a precursor pool rather than a single finished plant product. Photosynthetically derived sugar phosphates can be converted to soluble carbohydrates, structural polysaccharides, organic acids and carbon skeletons for other biosynthetic reactions. The relative allocation depends on tissue function and developmental state. A rapidly expanding leaf may use a high proportion of the available carbon for growth, whereas a mature source leaf can export substantial assimilate to other organs. The same biochemical cycle can therefore contribute to different physiological outcomes.

Respiration continues in living plant cells during illumination as well as darkness. Some newly assimilated carbon may consequently be oxidized to support ATP production and biosynthetic needs. In carbon accounting at the whole-plant scale, the amount of fixed carbon retained as biomass depends on the difference between inputs and respiratory or other losses over time. The ideal stoichiometry of the Calvin cycle does not include these later processes. A student comparing a short-term photosynthetic rate with a long-term growth rate must therefore recognize that they are linked by allocation and time integration, not by a single instantaneous conversion factor.

A simplified illustration makes the distinction precise. Suppose a leaf has an average net CO₂ uptake of eight micromoles per square metre per second for a ten-hour light period. Multiplication by 36,000 seconds yields 288,000 micromoles, or 0.288 mol CO₂ per square metre, taken up during the light period. If the average net release in the following fourteen-hour dark period is one micromole per square metre per second, the dark-period release is 0.0504 mol per square metre. The net twenty-four-hour carbon balance in this simplified leaf-scale example is 0.2376 mol carbon per square metre, because each CO₂ molecule carries one carbon atom.

The calculation does not predict an equal increase in leaf carbon mass. Export to other organs may transfer carbon out of the measured leaf, and the net carbon balance of the whole plant includes respiration in nonphotosynthetic tissues. Nor does it predict the amount of glucose synthesized at any instant. The example instead shows how day–night carbon exchange can be integrated in time. It also demonstrates why a plant cannot be evaluated from a single short period of high photosynthetic performance if the biological question concerns accumulated biomass.

Relationship with carbohydrate and pentose phosphate metabolism

Some reactions of the Calvin cycle resemble the nonoxidative pentose phosphate pathway because both systems redistribute carbons among sugar phosphates. The Calvin cycle uses these transformations to reconstruct a five-carbon acceptor from triose phosphates. The nonoxidative branch of the pentose phosphate pathway can connect pentose sugars with hexose and triose phosphates according to cellular demand. Recognition of the shared chemical logic is useful because the same carbon-transfer rules apply. However, an enzyme name shared by two pathways does not imply that the pathways have the same net substrates, products or physiological role.

The oxidative portion of the pentose phosphate pathway can produce NADPH while oxidizing glucose-6-phosphate and releasing CO₂. Carbon assimilation in the Calvin cycle instead consumes reducing power to incorporate CO₂ into organic molecules. These contrasting directions make regulation important. If opposing metabolic processes ran strongly and indiscriminately in the same compartment, they could spend ATP and reducing power without corresponding net carbon gain. The light-associated regulation of stromal metabolism helps maintain appropriate pathway activity. A complete account of the pentose phosphate pathway belongs to carbohydrate metabolism; here the connection clarifies why shared intermediates require coordinated regulation.

In an exam question comparing glycolysis and the Calvin cycle, identify both the reaction and its net direction. The glycolytic sequence generally converts carbohydrate into smaller products while conserving some released energy. The Calvin cycle uses related reversible reactions in the direction of carbohydrate synthesis. Some transformations require different enzymes to establish physiologically appropriate net directions, including phosphatases in regeneration. The distinct cofactor requirements of relevant dehydrogenases also matter. A comparison based only on the number of carbon atoms would miss these energetic and regulatory distinctions.

Why C3 is the baseline for C4 and CAM comparisons

A C3 plant does not require an additional primary CO₂ concentration cycle to deliver the majority of incoming carbon to Rubisco. The direct assimilation route has a smaller ideal ATP requirement than a comparable route with added energy-consuming concentration steps. That energetic economy is advantageous when oxygenation is relatively limited and CO₂ is readily available. It is not a guarantee of greater productivity in every environment because the cost of photorespiration and the limitations of water and gas exchange also contribute to the total carbon balance.

In a typical C4 system, the first stable product of the initial PEPC carboxylation is a four-carbon organic acid, oxaloacetate. The carbon is subsequently released as CO₂ in a location where Rubisco operates at elevated CO₂ availability. Thus the first stable product of the initial C4 fixation and the first stable product of subsequent Rubisco carboxylation answer different questions. In a CAM system, temporal separation allows initial CO₂ fixation and later release to be associated with different periods of the day. In both cases the Calvin cycle remains the biochemical route through which Rubisco-derived carbon is reduced and the RuBP acceptor regenerated.

A rigorous comparative analysis should therefore begin with the core C3 carbon and energy budget and add the costs and benefits of the concentration mechanism, rather than substituting an unrelated budget. If a question states that photorespiration is negligible, the energetic advantage of suppressing it is correspondingly small under those stated assumptions. If a question instead describes a hot, bright environment where stomatal closure lowers internal CO₂, reducing oxygenation can have a substantial physiological benefit. The relative advantages depend on conditions; the next two nodes develop the mechanisms that alter those conditions around Rubisco.

15. Misconceptions and examination traps

The first stable product is glucose.

The first stable carboxylation product is 3-PGA; glucose equivalents require downstream synthesis.

Why the error occurs: Photosynthesis is often summarized using glucose in an overall equation.

Exam reasoning: When asked for the first stable product, trace the direct Rubisco reaction, not the overall carbohydrate equation.

RuBP has six carbons.

RuBP has five; the transient intermediate has six after CO₂ addition.

Why the error occurs: Five-carbon RuBP is easily confused with its transient carboxylated intermediate.

Exam reasoning: Count 5 + 1 = 6 and then split into two three-carbon products.

Rubisco directly consumes ATP.

ATP is spent by phosphoglycerate kinase and phosphoribulokinase in the core cycle.

Why the error occurs: ATP expenditure in the complete pathway is confused with catalysis by the first enzyme.

Exam reasoning: Identify the actual kinase reactions before assigning ATP to an enzyme.

NADPH supplies carbon.

NADPH supplies reducing equivalents; incoming CO₂ supplies new carbon.

Why the error occurs: NADPH is described as a photosynthetic product and then mistaken for the source of organic carbon.

Exam reasoning: Separate carbon atom tracing from transfer of electrons.

The Calvin cycle takes place in the lumen.

The major plant Calvin-cycle enzymes are stromal.

Why the error occurs: Diagrams often draw all chloroplast processes inside a single outline.

Exam reasoning: Label the thylakoid membrane, lumen and stroma separately.

Dark reactions are restricted to the night.

The reactions do not directly absorb photons but depend on light-generated carriers and regulation.

Why the error occurs: The historical term dark reactions suggests a time of day.

Exam reasoning: Ask whether a reaction directly uses photons or indirectly depends on energy and activation.

Six trioses can all be exported after three carboxylations.

Five trioses must replenish three acceptors under ideal steady-state accounting.

Why the error occurs: Six gross reduced trioses are mistaken for six net products.

Exam reasoning: Subtract five trioses for regeneration before counting export.

Nine ATP are used in the reduction phase alone.

Six ATP are used in reduction and three in regeneration.

Why the error occurs: The remembered total of nine ATP is applied to one phase.

Exam reasoning: Check whether the question asks for reduction, regeneration or whole-cycle demand.

Rubisco activase replaces Rubisco.

Activase maintains catalytic competence by relieving inhibitory ligand binding.

Why the error occurs: Similar names obscure the different catalytic and regulatory roles.

Exam reasoning: Separate the enzyme that incorporates CO₂ from the ATP-dependent protein that restores access to its active sites.

Carbamylating CO₂ becomes a second fixed carbon.

Activation and substrate carboxylation are distinct roles.

Why the error occurs: Both events involve CO₂, so activation carbon is mistaken for product carbon.

Exam reasoning: Track substrate carbon into the 3-PGA product and distinguish the regulatory carbamate.

Oxygenation produces two 3-PGA.

Oxygenation produces one 3-PGA and one 2-phosphoglycolate.

Why the error occurs: Students memorize the carboxylation products but use them for both Rubisco reactions.

Exam reasoning: Write the carbon balance for oxygenation independently: 5 = 3 + 2.

C4 plants do not have the Calvin cycle.

Their concentration mechanism supplies CO₂ to the Calvin cycle.

Why the error occurs: C4 is incorrectly learned as a wholly separate replacement pathway.

Exam reasoning: Identify first fixation by PEPC and subsequent fixation by Rubisco as separate events.

Gross carboxylation equals net CO₂ uptake.

Photorespiratory and respiratory release lower net uptake.

Why the error occurs: Gas-exchange data are mistaken for an isolated enzyme assay.

Exam reasoning: Subtract photorespiratory and respiratory carbon losses when predicting net uptake.

The light and CO₂ compensation points are identical.

They are zero net exchange at different manipulated independent variables.

Why the error occurs: Both compensation points refer to zero net exchange.

Exam reasoning: Read the horizontal axis or manipulated variable first: irradiance or CO₂.

Rising CO₂ always increases assimilation indefinitely.

Energy supply, RuBP regeneration and product utilization can become limiting.

Why the error occurs: A positive initial response to a substrate is extrapolated indefinitely.

Exam reasoning: Consider the next limiting process when the response saturates.

17. Revision and active recall

Chapter summary and high-yield mechanisms

In C3 carbon assimilation, stromal Rubisco incorporates CO₂ into RuBP to produce two 3-PGA per carboxylation. Phosphoglycerate kinase and NADP-dependent GAPDH convert 3-PGA into triose phosphate; aldolase, phosphatases, transketolase, isomerase and epimerase reconstruct pentose phosphates. PRK consumes ATP to regenerate RuBP. Five of six trioses from three carboxylations are retained for regeneration. A net triose requires nine ATP and six NADPH under ideal conditions. Light supplies ATP/NADPH and favors activation of selected enzymes. Oxygenation competes with carboxylation; lower CO₂ availability or unfavorable temperature may increase its relative contribution.
