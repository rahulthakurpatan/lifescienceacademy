1. The biological problem: converting inorganic carbon into organic matter

A green leaf can increase its dry mass even when it receives no ready-made organic food. This observation separates photosynthesis from the ordinary digestion of food. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf from the atmosphere, while water and mineral nutrients reach the leaf from the roots. The carbon atoms of newly synthesized sugars, amino acids and many other organic compounds ultimately come from the assimilated carbon dioxide. The conversion is not merely a physical absorption of gas: carbon dioxide contains carbon in a highly oxidized state, and its carbon must be incorporated into bonds in an organic skeleton and then reduced. This chemical challenge explains why the production of ATP and NADPH by illuminated thylakoids is only part of photosynthesis.

The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, often shortened to the Calvin cycle, is a connected series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions that solves this problem. In the chloroplast stroma of a C3 plant, the cycle first attaches CO₂ to an existing five-carbon acceptor, then changes the resulting three-carbon acids into phosphorylated sugars, and finally reconstructs the acceptor. The last step is essential because every new carboxylation needs another acceptor molecule. If an acceptor were consumed without replacement, the rate of CO₂ uptake would collapse as soon as the initial supply was exhausted. The cycle therefore invests most of its immediate product in maintaining the capacity for subsequent carbon assimilation.

Carbon fixation and carbon reduction are related but distinct. Fixation here means incorporation of inorganic carbon into an organic molecule. Rubisco, the fixing enzyme, forms two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate from a single CO₂ and ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Reduction takes place afterward, when another enzyme transfers reducing equivalents from NADPH to an activated derivative of 3-phosphoglycerate. The immediate stable carbon-fixation product is therefore not glucose, and the first fixation reaction itself does not directly consume ATP or NADPH. Both carriers are indispensable for continued overall turnover because the product must be reduced and the acceptor regenerated.

A cyclic pathway is not a system that creates matter from nothing. Its intermediate pools may remain approximately constant even while carbon atoms continuously enter and leave. A useful analogy is a circulating collection of reusable molds: each passage produces some new material, but the molds have to be restored for the next passage. The analogy is incomplete because the actual cycle does not preserve an individual RuBP molecule as an unchanged object. Carbon atoms are redistributed among intermediates, so an incoming carbon atom can remain inside the cycle for several rounds rather than immediately appearing in an exported sugar. What is conserved at metabolic steady state is the size and composition of the cycling pools on average.

The designation C3 refers to the three carbon atoms in the first stable product of the main carboxylation reaction. Wheat, rice and many other plants use this basic mode of assimilation. The same Calvin cycle also operates in C4 and CAM plants, although those plants use additional mechanisms to increase CO₂ availability to Rubisco. Understanding the unmodified C3 pathway gives a reference against which the energetic cost and physiological advantage of those additional mechanisms can be judged.

2. Where carbon fixation occurs and how light supplies its energy

The chloroplast has an envelope surrounding the stroma, and a membrane system of flattened thylakoids enclosing a lumen. The two compartments have different chemical roles. Photosystem II, photosystem I and the intervening electron carriers reside within or beside the thylakoid membrane. ATP synthase releases ATP on the stromal side, and reduction of NADP⁺ by ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase also supplies the stromal pool. The major enzymes of the Calvin cycle operate in this same stromal environment. This proximity couples the reactions without requiring all carbon intermediates to cross the thylakoid membrane.

ATP is used to drive group-transfer reactions, especially phosphorylation. NADPH is a reduced electron carrier; it delivers reducing equivalents in chemical transformations. Neither carrier contributes the carbon atom that is fixed by Rubisco. Consider a chloroplast supplied with abundant ATP but almost no NADPH. Phosphoglycerate kinase can activate 3-phosphoglycerate while the ATP supply lasts, yet the subsequent reduction cannot be sustained. Conversely, NADPH cannot compensate for the absence of ATP needed for the activation step or for phosphorylation of ribulose-5-phosphate. Energy supply and reducing power are consequently separate constraints.

The historical expression dark reactions describes the lack of direct photon absorption by the enzymes of the carbon-fixation pathway. It does not mean that the pathway normally functions only at night. In illuminated chloroplasts, the light reactions continuously replenish ATP and NADPH. Illumination also changes stromal pH, magnesium availability and the redox state of regulatory proteins, favoring activation of several carbon-assimilation enzymes. After darkness begins, existing metabolite pools can support some transient reactions, but a sustained normal daytime rate cannot continue without replenishment and enzyme activation.

The carbon reactions also influence the light reactions. During productive assimilation, oxidation of NADPH returns NADP⁺, and ATP consumption returns ADP. These compounds become available again for photosynthetic electron transport and photophosphorylation. A sudden reduction in carbon demand, such as that produced by stomatal closure, may leave insufficient electron acceptor capacity relative to incoming excitation. Protective dissipation of excess light and adjustments in electron flow become important under such conditions. The cycle is therefore not a downstream machine entirely independent of its energy supplier; the two subsystems communicate through shared metabolic pools.

3. How experiments revealed the path of carbon

A major historical difficulty in studying photosynthesis was that the products appeared inside a complicated mixture of compounds. Measuring the total amount of carbohydrate formed after a prolonged period of illumination could not reveal the order of individual reactions. A label was needed that would follow a particular carbon atom through the network. Radioactive carbon-14 made such experiments possible because a molecule containing radioactive carbon could be detected even among a much larger amount of chemically identical unlabeled material.

The investigations associated with Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson and James Bassham used photosynthetic material, short exposures to labeled carbon dioxide, rapid interruption of metabolism and separation of extracted compounds. Autoradiography could locate radioactive spots after chromatographic separation. Comparing samples collected at different labeling times provided a way to distinguish early incorporation from later redistribution. When exposure was brief, 3-phosphoglycerate was a prominent early stable labeled compound; with longer exposure, radioactivity appeared in other organic intermediates and sugar phosphates.

A time series is much more informative than a single late sample. If labeled carbon first appears in compound X and subsequently in compound Y, X is a plausible precursor of Y. Nevertheless, appearance order alone cannot prove a direct enzyme-catalyzed conversion: different pool sizes and reaction rates alter labeling kinetics. Identification of compounds, carbon positions, changes after experimental perturbations, and biochemical tests of enzymes are necessary to establish a cycle rather than a simple list of radioactive metabolites. The 1954 publication on cyclic regeneration of the carbon dioxide acceptor is an important historical record of this reconstruction.

An unlabeled pre-existing RuBP pool helps explain how the experiment works. A newly supplied labeled CO₂ molecule can enter a carboxylation reaction with RuBP containing unlabeled carbon. One of the immediate products can then carry the newly incorporated label while the other consists of previously present carbon atoms. After recycling and further turnover, the label is redistributed into other positions and compounds. Thus, seeing radioactivity in six metabolites at a later time does not imply that six independent initial carbon-fixation mechanisms are operating.

Controls matter when interpreting this method. Comparable samples should differ primarily in the duration of exposure or in the chosen perturbation, rather than accidentally differing in temperature, illumination or starting physiological state. A reliable identification of a chromatographic spot requires comparison with known compounds or additional chemical evidence. Rapid metabolic interruption is required because the very reactions being studied can continue during sample collection. The experiment measures movement of a tracer, not necessarily an immediate change in the total concentration of each metabolite.

4. Phase I: Rubisco and the chemistry of carboxylation

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) is a five-carbon ketose carrying phosphate groups at carbon positions 1 and 5. Its name distinguishes it from ribulose-5-phosphate, which has only one phosphate group. The enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase is abbreviated Rubisco. In higher plants, its usual form I complex includes eight large subunits and eight small subunits. The catalytic sites are associated with the large-subunit architecture, while the small subunits influence the properties of the assembled enzyme. These structural facts explain why alterations of the enzyme's function may involve nuclear as well as chloroplast gene products.

At an activated catalytic site, magnesium participates in organizing the reactive substrate and its chemical environment. RuBP undergoes enolization, allowing reaction with CO₂. The initially formed six-carbon carboxylation intermediate is not accumulated as a stable six-carbon sugar. Through hydration and cleavage, the enzyme produces two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Each product has three carbons. The arithmetic is direct: five carbons in the acceptor plus one entering carbon equal six carbons in the two products. This bookkeeping is necessary for distinguishing a C3 first product from the fleeting six-carbon intermediate.

There is no ATP term in the carboxylation reaction itself. The energy needed to generate RuBP and transform 3-PGA is supplied in the other phases. Similarly, NADPH is not a direct substrate of Rubisco. A student who assigns NADPH consumption to carboxylation may have remembered that photosynthesis is reductive but placed the reducing event at the wrong step. Locating each energy-consuming transformation prevents this error. The reaction is also distinct from the phosphorylation of ribulose-5-phosphate by phosphoribulokinase, which directly uses ATP.

Rubisco has to be activated before it can efficiently catalyze either reaction. Carbamylation of a specific active-site lysine by a CO₂ molecule, followed by Mg²⁺ binding, helps establish the active configuration. This activating CO₂ molecule is conceptually different from the substrate CO₂ molecule that enters the carbohydrate carbon skeleton. Therefore, counting two newly assimilated carbons for each carboxylation would be incorrect. Activation changes catalytic competence, whereas the carboxylation reaction changes the carbon inventory of organic metabolites.

Sugar phosphates can occupy Rubisco active sites in inhibitory configurations. Rubisco activase is an ATP-dependent regulatory protein that helps remove inhibitory ligands and restore access to catalytically competent sites. It does not catalyze the production of 3-PGA in place of Rubisco. Its ATP consumption is also outside the simplified core cycle budget of three ATP per fixed CO₂. This distinction becomes particularly useful when comparing an enzyme assay performed after maximal activation with actual assimilation in a heat-stressed leaf: the amount of Rubisco protein may be unchanged even though a smaller fraction is active.

5. Phase II: phosphorylation and reduction of 3-phosphoglycerate

Three carboxylations form six 3-PGA molecules. Each is converted by phosphoglycerate kinase into 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, using one ATP and releasing one ADP. This reaction adds a phosphate group to the molecule, creating an activated acyl phosphate derivative. The significance of this activation is that direct reduction of an ordinary carboxylate is chemically difficult. Coupling reduction to prior ATP-dependent activation allows the cell to reach an intermediate whose conversion is more favorable when NADPH is available.

NADP-dependent glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase then converts 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP, also called G3P). The reaction consumes one NADPH per product, oxidizes NADPH to NADP⁺ and releases inorganic phosphate. The acyl carbon is reduced to an aldehyde-level carbon. The other phosphate group remains attached to the triose phosphate. Both 3-PGA and GAP contain three carbon atoms, so the phase changes chemical state and energy content without changing the total number of carbon atoms.

These steps resemble reactions known from glycolysis, but the direction and physiological setting differ. In glycolysis, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate oxidation and subsequent substrate-level phosphorylation participate in the harvesting of energy from sugar. In the Calvin cycle, related transformations run in the direction of sugar phosphate formation with expenditure of ATP and reducing power. Shared chemical intermediates do not mean that the two pathways perform the same metabolic function. The relevant isoenzymes and cofactor preferences are also important in maintaining compartment-specific metabolism.

Triose phosphate isomerase interconverts GAP and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). The two compounds have the same carbon number and molecular formula but differ in the placement of the carbonyl group. They provide complementary substrates for aldolase reactions during regeneration and downstream carbohydrate formation. A count of total triose phosphates therefore includes both forms. One should not treat GAP and DHAP as two additional products created from a single triose without an accompanying change elsewhere in the carbon balance.

For three entering CO₂ molecules, the reduction phase consumes six ATP and six NADPH because six molecules of 3-PGA need to be processed. If a question specifies only the reduction phase, the correct ATP answer is six for that scale, not nine. Nine ATP is the combined requirement after regeneration has been included. This distinction illustrates a general examination technique: identify the system boundary before applying a familiar stoichiometric number.

6. Phase III: rearranging sugar phosphates and regenerating RuBP

The cycle cannot continue if all six triose phosphates leave for carbohydrate synthesis. Five of the six contain fifteen carbon atoms, which exactly match the carbon content of three five-carbon acceptors. Regeneration redistributes these fifteen carbon atoms through enzyme-catalyzed reactions that form and rearrange sugar phosphates. One net three-carbon equivalent remains available for other metabolism. No additional carbon dioxide is required for the rearrangement itself.

A convenient balanced representation starts with three GAP and two DHAP molecules among the five triose phosphates reserved for regeneration. Aldolase condenses one GAP and one DHAP to form fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, a six-carbon compound. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase removes a phosphate by hydrolysis to give fructose-6-phosphate. This reaction is a hydrolysis, not an ATP-producing reversal of phosphofructokinase. The released phosphate becomes part of the stromal phosphate pool. The other three triose phosphates remain available for subsequent steps.

Transketolase transfers a two-carbon fragment from fructose-6-phosphate to a second GAP molecule. The result is erythrose-4-phosphate and xylulose-5-phosphate. The four-carbon erythrose phosphate then combines with the remaining DHAP through an aldolase reaction to give the seven-carbon sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate. Sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase converts it to sedoheptulose-7-phosphate. At this point, one GAP remains from the initial allocation, alongside the seven-carbon compound and the first xylulose-5-phosphate.

A second transketolase reaction transfers two carbons from sedoheptulose-7-phosphate to that remaining GAP. The products are ribose-5-phosphate and another xylulose-5-phosphate. The five original trioses have thereby yielded one ribose-5-phosphate and two xylulose-5-phosphate molecules. The distinction between transketolase and aldolase is worth noting: transketolase transfers a two-carbon unit between sugar phosphates, whereas aldolase forms a longer carbon skeleton by condensation in the reactions described here. Transketolase requires thiamine pyrophosphate, connecting this mechanism with the coenzyme principles studied earlier.

Ribose-5-phosphate is isomerized to ribulose-5-phosphate, and xylulose-5-phosphate is converted into ribulose-5-phosphate by epimerase. An epimerase changes configuration at a stereogenic center, whereas an isomerase can change functional-group arrangement; neither reaction introduces new carbon. Finally phosphoribulokinase (PRK) transfers a phosphate group from ATP to each ribulose-5-phosphate, generating ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Three ATP are required for three regenerated acceptors. This last reaction is the energy investment specific to completion of the acceptor-regeneration sequence.

The detailed sequence can be checked without memorizing every intermediate: start with fifteen carbon atoms, track each reaction using carbon counts, and verify that the result remains fifteen. If a proposed scheme produces four five-carbon sugars from five triose phosphates without an extra carbon input, it violates conservation of matter. If an inhibitor affects PRK, the rearrangements may still produce pentose phosphates, but RuBP replenishment becomes defective. Such reasoning connects enzyme location to whole-cycle behavior.

7. Carbon and energy accounting for complete turnover

It is helpful to calculate the budget for three CO₂ molecules rather than for one, because a net three-carbon product can then be represented by whole numbers. Three RuBP molecules contain fifteen carbon atoms. Three incoming carbon dioxide molecules bring three more. Six molecules of 3-PGA contain eighteen carbon atoms, and their reduction yields six triose phosphate molecules with the same total. Five trioses, containing fifteen carbons, regenerate the acceptors. One triose, containing three carbons, is the ideal net product.

Six ATP are used during phosphorylation of six 3-PGA molecules, and three more during regeneration of three RuBP molecules. Each of the six activated 3-PGA derivatives requires one NADPH for reduction. The result is nine ATP and six NADPH for three assimilated CO₂ molecules. Dividing by three gives a formal average of three ATP and two NADPH per CO₂. This average does not describe an isolated one-CO₂ reaction that releases a complete triose. It is a normalized rate or stoichiometric cost for the operation of the full cycle.

For a six-carbon sugar equivalent, the ideal demand is eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH, because six CO₂ molecules have to enter. The Calvin cycle itself yields net triose phosphates, not a free glucose molecule in one final enzymatic step. Further transformations are needed to synthesize hexose phosphates, sucrose or starch. Their energetic requirements, as well as photorespiration and additional regulatory ATP usage, are not included in the ideal eighteen/twelve figure. Therefore this figure is a minimum core carbon-reduction budget under the stated assumptions, rather than a complete energy balance for a growing leaf.

Suppose a chloroplast can make 90 nanomoles of ATP and 48 nanomoles of NADPH available for the ideal cycle in a specified interval. The ATP supply could sustain fixation of 90 divided by 3, or 30 nanomoles of CO₂. NADPH could sustain only 48 divided by 2, or 24 nanomoles. The lower value gives the maximum possible fixation in this simple supply model, namely 24 nanomoles of CO₂, equivalent to 8 nanomoles of net triose phosphate. Seventy-two nanomoles of ATP are required at that rate. The residual ATP does not guarantee additional fixation because the reducing equivalents remain limiting.

This calculation assumes the stated carriers are entirely available for the ideal cycle, there is enough active Rubisco and CO₂, and no carbon is lost through oxygenation. These assumptions are useful for examination reasoning but cannot be silently applied to field-grown plants. A measured ATP pool size, for example, is not equivalent to an ATP supply flux. A small pool can support high activity if continuously regenerated, and a large pool need not be accessible to the relevant enzymes. Units and the system boundary must match the question.

8. Coordinating light, enzyme activation and metabolic flux

Illumination changes not only the concentrations of ATP and NADPH but also the chemical conditions under which stromal enzymes operate. Proton translocation toward the thylakoid lumen contributes to a more alkaline stroma during illumination. Changes in magnesium distribution accompany this shift. Many enzymes of carbon assimilation operate more effectively under the appropriate illuminated stromal conditions than they do in the dark. It would be misleading to interpret the pH change as an independent supply of carbon or as a replacement for ATP.

An important regulatory link connects photosystem I to ferredoxin and thioredoxin. Reduced ferredoxin can supply electrons through ferredoxin–thioredoxin reductase, allowing thioredoxins to reduce regulatory disulfide bonds in target proteins. This can change the activities of stromal enzymes such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase and phosphoribulokinase. The effect is coordinated with illumination because the electron-transfer system supplies reduced ferredoxin during photosynthesis. The redox control is conceptually distinct from NADPH consumption during the chemical reduction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate.

When the light is removed, a leaf does not instantly lose every ATP molecule or deactivate every enzyme. Metabolic and regulatory changes occur over finite times. Therefore the measured response depends on when a sample is collected. A sample taken a fraction of a second after darkening may show a different distribution of intermediates from one taken after several minutes. One should not infer that light is unnecessary from the detection of brief post-illumination metabolism.

Rubisco activase adds another regulatory layer. It helps maintain active Rubisco sites, but its own ability to function is sensitive to physiological state, including nucleotide balance and temperature. Under heat stress, activation may become limiting even if isolated Rubisco can catalyze carboxylation effectively under standardized assay conditions. Comparing actual and maximal enzyme activity can distinguish changes in activation from changes in enzyme abundance. A decline in assimilation with unchanged protein abundance is consequently not a paradox.

Cyclic electron flow around photosystem I can supplement ATP production relative to NADPH and contributes to maintaining the energy balance under conditions where metabolic demands shift. Its role should be understood as a response to the demand of several chloroplast processes rather than as a mechanism guaranteeing an immutable ATP/NADPH output ratio. The ideal C3 budget requires an ATP-to-NADPH consumption ratio of 3:2; the total requirement of an intact chloroplast can deviate from that ratio because of oxygenation, biosynthesis and regulation.

9. Net carbon products: starch, sucrose and phosphate exchange

A newly formed triose phosphate may remain in the chloroplast for continued cycling, be used in stromal carbohydrate synthesis or be exchanged across the chloroplast envelope. A common daytime storage destination is transient starch in the chloroplast. Another destination is cytosolic sucrose, which can be transported from photosynthetically active source tissues to growing or storage sinks. These downstream routes allocate assimilated carbon rather than creating new carbon atoms.

The triose phosphate/phosphate translocator in the chloroplast inner envelope participates in exchange of phosphorylated carbon intermediates with inorganic phosphate. This exchange supports export of assimilates while replenishing phosphate required for stromal metabolism and ATP production. Sucrose synthesis in the cytosol includes dephosphorylation reactions that help make phosphate available for return. The coordinated traffic means that an apparently distant bottleneck in cytosolic metabolism can influence photosynthesis inside the chloroplast.

Starch formation helps buffer a mismatch between daytime carbon fixation and the immediate capacity to use or export assimilates. At night, degradation of stored carbohydrate supports metabolism and growth when current photosynthetic carbon assimilation is low or absent. The amount partitioned to starch or sucrose varies with plant species, development, environment and sink demand. The storage product should not be confused with the first stable carboxylation product, 3-PGA, or with the ideal net triose output of the Calvin cycle.

Suppose a leaf receives bright light and adequate CO₂ but its sinks cannot accept additional sucrose. The immediate response is not necessarily a permanent cessation of Rubisco catalysis. Metabolite concentrations, starch synthesis and feedback regulation may temporarily accommodate the altered demand. If utilization of triose phosphate remains limiting, recycling of phosphate and availability of ATP can eventually constrain the rate of assimilation. This is one reason that increasing light or CO₂ alone cannot be expected to increase photosynthesis without bound.

10. Rubisco oxygenation and the physiological limits of C3 plants

Rubisco can catalyze an alternative reaction in which molecular oxygen reacts with RuBP. Instead of forming two 3-PGA molecules, oxygenation yields one 3-PGA and one two-carbon 2-phosphoglycolate. No carbon dioxide is assimilated in this reaction. The original five carbons are conserved initially in the three-carbon and two-carbon products, but the subsequent recovery of phosphoglycolate carbon through photorespiration leads to energy expenditure and carbon dioxide release. The complete recovery pathway is treated in Subunit 6.B.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide compete as alternative substrates at the same enzyme system. The relative rates depend on concentrations at the enzyme and its discrimination between the two gases. Under a simple competitive model, the ratio of carboxylation rate to oxygenation rate is the specificity factor multiplied by the dissolved CO₂/O₂ concentration ratio. The concentrations must refer to the conditions around the enzyme, not merely the percentages of gases in the external atmosphere. This distinction is important because diffusion, solubility and metabolic activity influence local concentrations.

When stomata close during water deficit, less CO₂ can diffuse inward. If oxygen concentration and enzyme properties remain comparable, the lower CO₂ availability shifts the relative balance toward oxygenation. Higher temperature can also increase the relative importance of oxygenation through changes in dissolved gas ratios and Rubisco specificity. These responses are not universal predictions about the absolute photosynthetic rate at every temperature. At low temperature, warming may accelerate many biochemical reactions, whereas above the physiological optimum heat can reduce activation and damage metabolic capacity.

Consider an illustrative enzyme system with a specificity factor of 80 and a dissolved CO₂/O₂ ratio of 0.025. The ratio of carboxylation to oxygenation rates is 80 multiplied by 0.025, which equals two. Out of three catalytic events in this simplified model, two are carboxylations and one is oxygenation. If the gas ratio halves with no change in specificity, the rates become equal. This is not a measurement of Rubisco in a particular species; it demonstrates the interaction of enzyme specificity and substrate availability. Neither a specificity factor of 80 nor a gas ratio of 0.025 should be memorized as a universal constant.

Net CO₂ assimilation must be distinguished from gross carboxylation. With conventional photorespiratory bookkeeping, two oxygenations give rise to the release of one CO₂ during recovery of phosphoglycolate carbon. A simplified leaf carbon balance may be expressed as A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd, where A is net CO₂ uptake, Vc is Rubisco carboxylation rate, Vo is oxygenation rate, and Rd is the relevant nonphotorespiratory respiratory CO₂ release in the light. All terms must share compatible flux units. The equation assumes standard pathway accounting and does not independently determine stomatal or electron-transport limitation.

For example, with Vc = 20, Vo = 8 and Rd = 1 in micromoles of CO₂ equivalents per square metre per second, the net uptake is 20 − 4 − 1 = 15. Suppressing oxygenation while leaving carboxylation and respiration fixed would raise that calculated rate to 19. A real experimental change in O₂ may also influence other processes and carboxylation itself, so the fixed-rate prediction is conditional. The exercise teaches the difference between counting enzyme reactions and measuring exchange at the whole leaf surface.

11. Interpreting carbon dioxide, light and temperature responses

A gas-exchange instrument can measure the difference between incoming and outgoing CO₂ in a chamber containing a leaf. Combining this difference with gas flow and leaf area allows a net assimilation rate to be estimated. The measured quantity includes effects of diffusion, carboxylation, oxygenation and respiration. It is not a direct isolated assay of Rubisco catalytic activity. Comparing leaf gas exchange with enzyme assays or fluorescence measurements can therefore provide more informative mechanistic tests than any one measurement alone.

At low CO₂ availability, increasing CO₂ generally favors carboxylation and suppresses the relative contribution of oxygenation in a C3 leaf. As CO₂ increases, however, the supply of RuBP or metabolic energy can become more restrictive. At still higher assimilation demands, use of triose phosphate may limit turnover. A curve of net assimilation against intercellular CO₂ can reveal different limiting regions, but the shape must be interpreted in light of the experimental conditions. Intercellular CO₂ is not identical to chloroplastic CO₂ when mesophyll diffusion resistance is appreciable.

A light response curve likewise has distinct regimes. Under very dim illumination, photon capture and photochemical energy supply constrain the rate, and respiratory carbon release may exceed fixation. As light increases, the rate eventually approaches a range where other factors constrain additional assimilation. Excess illumination can impose stress even when carbon uptake no longer increases. The light compensation point is the irradiance at which net CO₂ exchange is zero under specified conditions, while the CO₂ compensation point is the CO₂ level at which net exchange is zero under specified illumination and other conditions. Confusing these two independent variables leads to incorrect graph interpretation.

To test whether a low net rate is largely caused by oxygenation, a researcher could compare assimilation at normal and reduced oxygen under otherwise matched light, temperature, humidity and CO₂ conditions. An increase in net assimilation at reduced oxygen is consistent with reduced competition and photorespiratory loss. It does not establish that every change is caused solely by one enzyme, because oxygen can affect other cellular processes. Measurements of metabolite pools, chlorophyll fluorescence and enzyme activation may help distinguish mechanisms. Such controlled comparisons underpin the reasoning used in C3/C4 physiology.

The upcoming C4 and CAM chapters will build on this limit. Rather than replacing the Calvin cycle, these mechanisms increase the CO₂ concentration available to Rubisco in different ways. An additional carbon-concentrating pathway can reduce photorespiratory loss but requires metabolic investment and specialized physiological organization. The baseline C3 cycle described here makes that trade-off intelligible.

Nine ATP are used in the reduction phase alone.

Carbamylating CO₂ becomes a second fixed carbon.

13. Original examination practice

These are original practice questions, not previous-year questions. The numerical questions use the stated ideal assumptions.

CSIR-NET Part B

1. Which compound is the primary CO₂ acceptor in C3 assimilation?
  1. Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate
  2. Phosphoenolpyruvate
  3. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
  4. Ribose-5-phosphate

Answer: A. RuBP is the five-carbon substrate carboxylated by Rubisco. PEP participates in initial C4/CAM fixation; the other two are cycle intermediates.

2. What is the first stable product of Rubisco carboxylation?
  1. Glucose
  2. 3-phosphoglycerate
  3. Oxaloacetate
  4. Ribulose-5-phosphate

Answer: B. The transient six-carbon intermediate cleaves into two molecules of 3-PGA. Oxaloacetate is associated with PEPC fixation.

3. Which compartment contains the major Calvin-cycle enzymes in a leaf chloroplast?
  1. Thylakoid lumen
  2. Intermembrane space
  3. Stroma
  4. Vacuole

Answer: C. The stroma receives ATP and NADPH from thylakoid-associated energy metabolism. The lumen is the proton-accumulating compartment.

4. Which enzyme directly phosphorylates ribulose-5-phosphate to RuBP?
  1. Rubisco
  2. Transketolase
  3. Triose phosphate isomerase
  4. Phosphoribulokinase

Answer: D. PRK consumes ATP for acceptor regeneration; the other enzymes catalyze carbon fixation or carbon rearrangements.

5. Which reaction directly consumes NADPH?
  1. Reduction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate
  2. Carboxylation of RuBP
  3. Hydrolysis of fructose bisphosphate
  4. Isomerization of ribose phosphate

Answer: A. NADP-dependent GAPDH reduces the activated carboxyl derivative. Rubisco and hydrolytic/rearrangement reactions do not use NADPH directly.

6. What is the ideal ATP cost of fixing three CO₂ into one net triose phosphate?
  1. 6
  2. 9
  3. 12
  4. 18

Answer: B. Six ATP activate six 3-PGA molecules, and three ATP regenerate three RuBP. Eighteen ATP corresponds to six CO₂.

7. Which product is unique to Rubisco oxygenation relative to carboxylation?
  1. 3-PGA
  2. ATP
  3. 2-phosphoglycolate
  4. NADPH

Answer: C. Oxygenation produces one 3-PGA and one 2-phosphoglycolate; carboxylation yields two 3-PGA.

8. Which enzyme interconverts GAP and DHAP?
  1. Rubisco activase
  2. Phosphoribulokinase
  3. Sedoheptulose bisphosphatase
  4. Triose phosphate isomerase

Answer: D. TPI changes the arrangement of the triose phosphate carbonyl. It does not change carbon count.

9. What distinguishes a carboxylation reaction from a reduction reaction?
  1. Carboxylation adds CO₂ to an organic substrate
  2. Carboxylation necessarily uses NADPH
  3. Reduction always fixes CO₂
  4. Both necessarily produce ATP

Answer: A. CO₂ incorporation defines carboxylation; electron transfer defines reduction. These events occur in separate Calvin-cycle steps.

10. Which regulatory protein uses ATP to relieve inhibition of Rubisco?
  1. Plastocyanin
  2. Rubisco activase
  3. Ferredoxin
  4. PsbS

Answer: B. Rubisco activase helps remove inhibitory sugar phosphates. Plastocyanin and ferredoxin are electron carriers; PsbS participates in photoprotection.

11. Which pathway is a direct downstream destination of chloroplast triose phosphate?
  1. Water oxidation
  2. Oxygen evolution
  3. Starch synthesis
  4. Photolysis

Answer: C. Starch is a carbon storage polymer. The other processes belong to light-reaction chemistry, not carbon-product allocation.

12. How many carbon atoms are present in sedoheptulose-7-phosphate?
  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 5
  4. 7

Answer: D. The heptose prefix denotes seven carbon atoms, irrespective of the position of its phosphate.

13. What is the direct role of transketolase in regeneration?
  1. Transfer of a two-carbon fragment
  2. Addition of CO₂
  3. Reduction of an aldehyde
  4. ATP-driven proton pumping

Answer: A. Transketolase redistributes two-carbon units between sugars and uses a thiamine-derived cofactor; it does not itself add inorganic carbon.

14. What is the ideal ratio of ATP to NADPH consumption per CO₂?
  1. 1:1
  2. 3:2
  3. 2:3
  4. 3:1

Answer: B. The core cycle costs three ATP and two NADPH per assimilated CO₂. This is not a universal ratio of light-reaction output.

15. Which cofactor helps establish the active Rubisco catalytic configuration?
  1. Chlorophyll b
  2. Heme
  3. Mg²⁺
  4. Biotin

Answer: C. Carbamylation and Mg²⁺ binding establish catalytic competence; chlorophyll participates in light harvesting, not Rubisco catalysis.

16. What happens to phosphate during conversion of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to fructose-6-phosphate?
  1. It produces ATP from ADP
  2. It becomes carbon dioxide
  3. It becomes NADPH
  4. It is released by hydrolysis

Answer: D. FBPase hydrolyzes a phosphate ester; it does not generate ATP as a simple reverse kinase reaction.

17. What does the term C3 describe?
  1. Carbon count of the first stable carboxylation product
  2. Number of ATP synthases
  3. Number of photosystems
  4. Carbon count of RuBP

Answer: A. C3 refers to 3-PGA, not the five-carbon acceptor or a number of photosystems.

18. Where is sucrose predominantly synthesized from exported photosynthetic carbon in a typical source leaf?
  1. Thylakoid lumen
  2. Cytosol
  3. Nucleolus
  4. Cell wall

Answer: B. Exported phosphorylated carbon supplies cytosolic sucrose synthesis; transient starch synthesis occurs in the chloroplast.

19. Which electron carrier helps link illumination to thioredoxin-mediated enzyme regulation?
  1. Ubiquinone
  2. Hemoglobin
  3. Ferredoxin
  4. Coenzyme A

Answer: C. Reduced ferredoxin can provide electrons via ferredoxin–thioredoxin reductase. The other molecules do not serve that specific regulatory role.

20. Which statement about C4 plants is correct?
  1. Rubisco is entirely absent
  2. The Calvin cycle is replaced by glycolysis
  3. No ATP is needed for CO₂ assimilation
  4. The Calvin cycle operates with an additional CO₂-concentrating mechanism

Answer: D. C4 plants retain Rubisco and the Calvin cycle. Their extra fixation and decarboxylation reactions concentrate CO₂ around Rubisco.

CSIR-NET Part C

1. A chloroplast has enough active enzymes, 54 nmol ATP and 30 nmol NADPH available for ideal assimilation. What is the maximum CO₂ fixation?
  1. 15 nmol
  2. 18 nmol
  3. 27 nmol
  4. 30 nmol

Answer: A. ATP permits 18 nmol CO₂ but NADPH permits 15 nmol. The smaller capacity controls. The remaining ATP cannot replace NADPH.

2. A selective PRK inhibitor is added to an illuminated chloroplast with a pre-existing RuBP pool. What is the likely later effect?
  1. Permanent rise of RuBP
  2. Decline in RuBP regeneration and sustained fixation
  3. Immediate elimination of every molecule of ATP
  4. Direct conversion of RuBP into glucose

Answer: B. Existing acceptor supports transient fixation, but without ATP-dependent regeneration the RuBP pool declines and fixation slows.

3. If six CO₂ are fixed in the ideal cycle, what is the total number of 3-PGA molecules formed before reduction?
  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 12
  4. 18

Answer: C. Two 3-PGA arise per CO₂ because each carboxylation cleaves a six-carbon intermediate. The ATP budget of eighteen is not a product count.

4. An isolated enzyme has specificity S=100. Dissolved CO₂/O₂ is 0.02. What fraction of its carboxylation plus oxygenation events are oxygenations in the simple competition model?
  1. 1/5
  2. 1/2
  3. 2/3
  4. 1/3

Answer: D. Vc/Vo=100×0.02=2. Total events are three proportional units; one is oxygenation.

5. At steady state, five triose phosphates enter regeneration. A proposed model makes four pentoses without another carbon input. Which observation rejects the model?
  1. It creates five carbon atoms
  2. It consumes too much oxygen
  3. It requires a sixth phosphate group
  4. It produces four ATP

Answer: A. Five trioses contain fifteen carbons and four pentoses contain twenty. The proposed regeneration violates carbon conservation.

6. A brief ¹⁴CO₂ pulse labels 3-PGA before several other metabolites. What can be concluded most directly?
  1. Every molecule of 3-PGA in the cell is newly made
  2. 3-PGA is a plausible early stable fixation product
  3. 3-PGA must be the only product at all times
  4. No unstable intermediate can exist

Answer: B. Early labeling supports a role as an early stable product. Pool sizes and turnover influence tracer kinetics; unstable intermediates may escape detection.

7. A leaf has Vc=24, Vo=10 and Rd=2 in compatible flux units. Calculate net assimilation.
  1. 12
  2. 16
  3. 17
  4. 22

Answer: C. A=24−0.5(10)−2=17. Subtracting the full oxygenation flux rather than half would miscount the conventional photorespiratory loss.

8. A mutant accumulates ribulose-5-phosphate while RuBP decreases despite sufficient ATP. Which defect is most consistent with these observations?
  1. Enhanced Rubisco activation
  2. Increased starch synthesis alone
  3. Loss of photosystem II oxygen release alone
  4. Impaired phosphoribulokinase

Answer: D. The substrate of PRK accumulates and its product declines. Other changes could influence pools indirectly, but PRK impairment best explains this pair.

9. Two matched leaves are measured at identical CO₂ and light. Lowering O₂ raises net uptake in one. What is the most defensible explanation?
  1. Reduced competition with CO₂ and lower photorespiratory loss
  2. Increased ATP directly synthesized by Rubisco
  3. Complete elimination of all respiration
  4. Replacement of RuBP by PEP

Answer: A. Less oxygenation is consistent with the response. The result does not prove that every respiratory process stopped or that a new fixation pathway appeared.

10. A chloroplast has abundant NADPH but ATP regeneration fails. Which pair of Calvin-cycle reactions is directly constrained?
  1. Rubisco and TPI
  2. PGK and PRK
  3. Aldolase and epimerase
  4. Rubisco and transketolase

Answer: B. PGK uses ATP in reduction-phase activation and PRK uses it in RuBP regeneration. NADPH cannot substitute for phosphate transfer.

11. A heat treatment reduces actual Rubisco activity, but activity measured after full in vitro activation is unchanged. Which inference is strongest?
  1. All Rubisco was degraded
  2. The Calvin cycle changed location
  3. A lower activation state is consistent with the data
  4. Rubisco cannot catalyze oxygenation

Answer: C. Unchanged maximal activity argues against major loss of intrinsic enzyme capacity; altered activation is plausible. Additional measurements are required to establish the precise regulatory cause.

12. A leaf chamber measures zero net CO₂ exchange at low irradiance. Which conclusion is valid?
  1. No metabolic reaction occurs
  2. No respiratory CO₂ is released
  3. All Rubisco is inactive
  4. Carbon fixation and carbon release balance at that irradiance

Answer: D. Zero net exchange need not mean zero gross flux. The light compensation point is defined by equal net opposing exchanges under specified conditions.

13. A new experiment halves chloroplastic CO₂/O₂ while keeping specificity constant. How does Vc/Vo change?
  1. It halves
  2. It doubles
  3. It remains unchanged
  4. It becomes zero

Answer: A. Vc/Vo is proportional to dissolved CO₂/O₂ in the simplified model. A finite half-ratio does not eliminate carboxylation.

14. The rate of triose export falls while CO₂ and light remain high. Which downstream constraint may develop?
  1. More water oxidation directly fixes carbon
  2. Restricted phosphate recycling and ATP production
  3. Loss of the carbon atom in every triose
  4. Conversion of the chloroplast into a mitochondrion

Answer: B. Phosphate exchange helps maintain stromal phosphate availability. Restricted utilization can constrain ATP synthesis and turnover; storage may initially buffer the effect.

15. An illuminated leaf is darkened and fixation briefly persists. What does this show?
  1. Light is irrelevant to sustained fixation
  2. Rubisco directly absorbs light
  3. Existing metabolites and enzyme activation can sustain transient reactions
  4. The leaf switches to C4 fixation immediately

Answer: C. ATP/NADPH pools and activation states do not change instantaneously. The transient response does not establish long-term light-independent carbon assimilation.

16. Which hypothetical enzyme lesion would most specifically block the first direct NADPH-dependent carbon-reduction step?
  1. Loss of TPI
  2. Loss of FBPase
  3. Loss of PRK
  4. Loss of stromal NADP-dependent GAPDH

Answer: D. GAPDH reduces 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to GAP. PRK and FBPase affect regeneration; TPI interconverts trioses.

17. The cycle assimilates 12 CO₂ under ideal conditions. How many ATP are used specifically in regeneration?
  1. 12
  2. 24
  3. 36
  4. 48

Answer: A. One ATP per RuBP regenerated and one RuBP per carboxylation give twelve ATP. The full cycle consumes thirty-six.

18. A short pulse followed by unlabeled CO₂ produces declining specific radioactivity in the 3-PGA pool, while pool size stays constant. What does that demonstrate?
  1. 3-PGA is not involved in metabolism
  2. Carbon in the pool turns over despite constant total amount
  3. 3-PGA becomes permanently radioactive
  4. No unlabeled carbon is entering

Answer: B. The label is diluted and redistributed in a steady-size dynamic pool. Constant concentration does not imply no flux.

19. A high-CO₂ leaf shows a plateau of assimilation when further CO₂ is supplied. Which inference is justified without other measurements?
  1. Rubisco is absent
  2. CO₂ is toxic in all plants
  3. An additional limitation is likely; identity needs further testing
  4. The plateau proves the exact rate of ATP synthase

Answer: C. A plateau suggests increasing CO₂ no longer increases the net rate under the measured conditions. It does not identify the unique limiting process.

20. A model predicts two net trioses from six CO₂, but treats all twelve 3-PGA as exported. What is the central contradiction?
  1. There is too much CO₂ in the air
  2. There is no NADPH in the light
  3. Triose has four carbons
  4. The acceptor carbon inventory cannot be maintained

Answer: D. Twelve reduced trioses contain thirty-six carbons, of which thirty are required to regenerate six RuBP. Exporting all products depletes the acceptor pool.

Mixed SET and analytical practice

1. Assertion: Calvin-cycle enzymes do not directly require photons for their catalysis. Reason: light supplies ATP/NADPH and favors activation of several enzymes.
  1. Both true; reason explains why sustained assimilation normally depends on light
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false

Answer: A. The two statements are compatible. Indirect light dependence follows from metabolic supply and regulation, not direct photon absorption by the carbon-fixation enzymes.

2. Assertion: Rubisco oxygenation produces no newly fixed carbon. Reason: O₂, rather than CO₂, reacts with RuBP in oxygenation.
  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion true, reason false

Answer: B. Oxygenation rearranges five pre-existing organic carbons into three- and two-carbon compounds; O₂ adds no carbon.

3. Under ideal conditions, calculate NADPH demand for five net triose phosphate molecules.
  1. 10
  2. 15
  3. 30
  4. 45

Answer: C. Five net trioses correspond to fifteen CO₂ and require two NADPH per CO₂, or thirty. Forty-five is the ATP cost.

4. Which comparison provides the strongest test of an O₂ effect on C3 net assimilation?
  1. Different species under different temperatures
  2. Two leaves in uncontrolled outdoor light
  3. A dark leaf compared with an illuminated one
  4. Matched leaves under fixed CO₂, light and temperature but different O₂

Answer: D. Changing the variable of interest while holding major alternatives constant strengthens causal inference. A species or illumination difference would confound it.

5. Which statement correctly distinguishes RuBP from ribulose-5-phosphate?
  1. Both have five carbons; RuBP bears two phosphates
  2. RuBP has six carbons
  3. Ribulose-5-phosphate has no phosphate
  4. The two compounds are identical

Answer: A. PRK adds a phosphate to ribulose-5-phosphate without changing its carbon count.

6. In a standard C3 leaf, which organelle contains the Calvin-cycle carboxylation reaction?
  1. Mitochondrion
  2. Chloroplast
  3. Golgi apparatus
  4. Peroxisome

Answer: B. Rubisco-mediated carboxylation occurs in the chloroplast stroma; peroxisomes participate in downstream photorespiratory metabolism.

7. An illustrative sample has 60% of measured ¹⁴C in 3-PGA at 1 s and 15% at 30 s. What is a reasonable interpretation?
  1. 3-PGA must disappear completely
  2. The enzyme has stopped working
  3. Label is redistributed into later metabolites
  4. The 30-second sample necessarily contains less total carbon

Answer: C. Relative tracer distribution changes over time. A fall in the fraction of radioactivity does not by itself prove that the metabolite concentration fell.

8. Which pairing of reaction and ATP usage is incorrect?
  1. PGK — ATP use
  2. PRK — ATP use
  3. Rubisco activase — ATP use outside ideal core budget
  4. Rubisco carboxylation — direct ATP use

Answer: D. Rubisco catalysis does not directly hydrolyze ATP. Its activation machinery and other cycle enzymes consume ATP.

9. A C3 leaf becomes warmer and closes its stomata. What conditional prediction follows?
  1. Relative Rubisco oxygenation may increase
  2. Oxygenation must become impossible
  3. All ATP use ceases
  4. C3 instantly changes to C4 anatomy

Answer: A. Lower CO₂ availability and temperature-dependent changes in gas solubility and specificity may favor oxygenation; the absolute net rate still depends on other constraints.

10. Which is a sound interpretation of the ideal 18 ATP and 12 NADPH requirement?
  1. It includes all respiratory costs
  2. It is the core-cycle cost for six fixed CO₂, excluding additional expenses
  3. It is a universal measured yield of light reactions
  4. It is the cost of one 3-PGA molecule

Answer: B. The calculation describes ideal carbon assimilation, not the entire leaf energy economy or a guaranteed photochemical output.
