1. The biological problem: gaining carbon without losing too much water

A terrestrial leaf must obtain carbon dioxide from an atmosphere that contains far more nitrogen and oxygen than carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide enters primarily through stomata, adjustable pores in the epidermis. Opening a pore improves the passage of carbon dioxide into the leaf, but it also exposes the moist internal air spaces to external air. Water evaporates from internal cell surfaces and diffuses outward through the same openings. A leaf cannot simply increase carbon acquisition independently of water loss. This coupling is particularly consequential when soil water is scarce, the air is dry, and leaf temperatures are high. A plant that fixes carbon quickly for a short period may eventually suffer greater dehydration than one that assimilates carbon slowly but sustains tissue function over a longer period.

Crassulacean acid metabolism, abbreviated CAM, changes the time at which a large fraction of atmospheric carbon dioxide enters the leaf. In a strongly expressed CAM cycle, substantial uptake takes place during darkness, when evaporation may be lower than in daylight. The captured carbon is temporarily incorporated into organic acids. During the following light period, the leaf liberates carbon dioxide from these acids inside its photosynthetic cells, where the Calvin–Benson cycle can assimilate it. The mechanism therefore does not eliminate the requirement for sunlight. Instead, it separates external carbon acquisition from much of the light-powered assimilation of that carbon. This distinction resolves the apparent contradiction in the phrase nighttime photosynthesis.

The ecological advantage depends on the environment. Cooler nights commonly have a lower vapor pressure deficit, but a hot, dry night does not necessarily provide favorable conditions for gas exchange. Moreover, a succulent leaf cannot store unlimited organic acid, and the carbon skeletons needed for nighttime fixation must themselves be replenished. CAM is an adaptation with costs rather than a means of circumventing the laws of metabolism. It is found in diverse plants, including pineapple, many agaves and cacti, and numerous epiphytic orchids. These organisms need not have identical leaf architecture or daily gas exchange patterns.

The term CAM derives from studies of plants in the Crassulaceae and their characteristic changes in acidity between night and day. The name refers to a physiological phenomenon, not a taxonomic restriction. A plant belonging to another family can exhibit CAM, and a plant with a fleshy appearance does not necessarily exhibit it. To establish the mechanism, one must examine carbon exchange and biochemical changes rather than infer metabolism solely from external appearance. The following sections develop that evidence and connect it with the enzyme reactions that make the daily cycle possible.

From a change in taste to a testable biochemical problem

The daily alteration in leaf acidity was recognized long before the reactions of carboxylation and decarboxylation could be assigned to individual enzymes. An early account by Benjamin Heyne in 1815 described the strongly acidic taste of a plant now known as Kalanchoë pinnata in the morning and the much less acidic taste later in the day. The significance of the report is not that tasting leaves is a useful modern laboratory practice; it is that a conspicuous reversible physiological change demanded an explanation. A mineral salt accumulated continuously from the soil would not normally account for a rapid repeated rise and decline in leaf acidity under a daily light cycle. An organic acid produced and consumed in metabolism offered a more plausible explanation. Nevertheless, the early observation alone could not identify malate, demonstrate fixation of external carbon dioxide, or establish the enzymatic route.

A useful scientific distinction is between a descriptive name and a causal mechanism. Crassulacean acid metabolism originally points to a recognizable metabolic rhythm. The full modern explanation depends on identifying metabolites and their cellular locations, showing how external inorganic carbon becomes part of organic acids, and determining how the stored carbon is subsequently delivered to the photosynthetic reduction cycle. The four-phase framework developed in the twentieth century, particularly the synthesis by Osmond in 1978, brought together changing gas exchange and metabolic events throughout the day. Its usefulness rests on relating physiological measurements to specific processes. It should not be confused with a classification that assigns a different species to each phase; an individual plant may move between several phases within a single day.

Consider how two alternative explanations of the morning acidity could be tested. In one, acids are synthesized principally from carbon already present in leaf carbohydrates, with little association with the acquisition of new carbon. In another, degradation of carbohydrate provides an acceptor that captures external inorganic carbon into a newly synthesized four-carbon acid. Both explanations could predict a rise in acidity. Only the second necessarily predicts incorporation of an atmospheric carbon tracer during darkness, provided stomata are sufficiently open. The distinguishing measurement is the destination of the supplied label in specific metabolites soon after exposure. If label first appears in a four-carbon acid and is later found in carbohydrate during the light period, the findings support a temporal transfer mechanism. The observation is especially persuasive when combined with independently measured changes in the size of the acid pool.

The recognition that C4 photosynthesis uses a four-carbon intermediate provided another conceptual comparison. Spatially organized delivery of carbon dioxide in C4 plants and temporally organized delivery in CAM plants solve a similar biochemical problem in different ways. Their resemblance does not establish that one universally evolved directly from the other. Both draw on enzymes already present for other purposes in ordinary plant metabolism, and CAM has arisen independently in multiple lineages. For the syllabus, the important lesson is how familiar metabolic reactions acquire a new physiological role through changes in regulation, capacity and integration. An enzyme need not be invented from scratch for a new carbon acquisition system to evolve.

Water loss, boundary conditions and the reason timing matters

Water vapor diffuses outward because its partial pressure is usually higher in the hydrated internal leaf atmosphere than in the surrounding air. A common simplified description expresses transpiration per unit leaf area as conductance to water vapor multiplied by the difference in water vapor mole fraction between the leaf and surrounding atmosphere. If vapor pressure deficit, D, is expressed in kilopascals and total atmospheric pressure, P, in the same units, their ratio is dimensionless and can approximate this difference. With a suitable total conductance g expressed in moles of water per square meter per second, the relation E approximately equals g times D divided by P. The expression assumes that the internal air is close to saturation at the measured leaf temperature and that the conductance represents the relevant diffusion pathway, including the boundary layer where necessary. It is not a model of the entire soil-to-leaf water transport system.

The corresponding carbon dioxide diffusion relation depends on conductance to carbon dioxide and the concentration difference from outside to inside. Conductance to water vapor and to carbon dioxide are not numerically identical because the gases have different diffusivities. Stomatal conductance to water vapor is often treated as roughly 1.6 times the stomatal conductance to carbon dioxide when the same pore geometry applies. This ratio should not automatically be imposed on an arbitrary total conductance that also includes cuticular pathways and boundary layers. The distinction matters when a numerical question supplies one conductance but asks about another gas. Use the conductance in the units and for the gas explicitly specified.

Suppose an illustrative leaf maintains a total water vapor conductance of 0.10 mol per square meter per second during two intervals. At a pressure of 100 kPa, a daytime vapor pressure deficit of 2.0 kPa gives an estimated transpiration of 0.002 mol per square meter per second. At night, a deficit of 0.5 kPa gives 0.0005 mol per square meter per second. The second estimate is one quarter of the first. This calculation holds conductance constant to isolate the effect of the physical driving force; a living CAM leaf may change its conductance considerably between the two intervals. The example therefore demonstrates the potential advantage of changing the time of gas exchange, not a universal prediction of fourfold improvement in daily water-use efficiency.

A related distinction concerns relative humidity. Air at 50 percent relative humidity at a high temperature has a different water vapor deficit from air at 50 percent relative humidity at a lower temperature. Saturation vapor pressure rises nonlinearly with temperature. Consequently a comparison based only on relative humidity may not reflect the evaporative demand at the leaf surface. Leaf temperature can differ from air temperature because radiation, wind and transpiration change its energy balance. This is one reason that both leaf temperature and humidity must be considered when interpreting day-night gas exchange.

A CAM leaf may also avoid extensive daytime water loss even when photosynthetic carbon assimilation is substantial. The mechanism involves retaining and releasing previously acquired carbon inside the leaf, not an ability of stomata to allow carbon dioxide through while perfectly blocking water. Cuticular transpiration and residual stomatal leakage continue to contribute to water loss when the pores are nearly closed. Severe water limitation can still constrain CAM plants. Their advantage is that the time and conditions of gas exchange can be adjusted to reduce the water cost of a given carbon gain. The strength of the advantage must be assessed in the actual environment rather than inferred from the pathway name.

2. Nighttime carbon capture: PEPC and the four-carbon intermediate

The immediate substrate of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, or PEPC, is bicarbonate rather than molecular carbon dioxide. Carbonic anhydrase facilitates the interconversion of dissolved carbon dioxide and bicarbonate. In a simplified representation, a molecule of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), containing three carbon atoms, reacts with bicarbonate to form oxaloacetate (OAA), containing four, and releases inorganic phosphate. The extra carbon is the newly incorporated inorganic carbon. This reaction proceeds in the cytosol of a typical photosynthetic CAM cell. PEPC is distinct from Rubisco in both its substrates and its products.

OAA is not a suitable long-term store in this context. NAD-dependent malate dehydrogenase can reduce its carbonyl group to produce malate, using NADH and releasing NAD+. It is useful to separate the two enzymes in a pathway diagram because PEPC introduces the inorganic carbon whereas malate dehydrogenase changes the oxidation state of the four-carbon product. If the latter reaction is impaired, initial carboxylation and subsequent malate accumulation are not necessarily affected to the same extent. A biochemical question that measures OAA accumulation is therefore not identical to one that measures the quantity of acid retained overnight.

Unlike Rubisco, PEPC has no competing oxygenase reaction analogous to Rubisco oxygenation of RuBP. This does not mean that the CAM leaf has abolished oxygen-dependent metabolism. The leaf continues to respire, and oxygen remains necessary for aerobic mitochondrial ATP generation. The absence of a Rubisco-type oxygenation side reaction at the initial PEPC step is particularly useful when inorganic carbon is acquired under conditions where maintaining a high carbon dioxide supply to Rubisco directly would be difficult.

Where does the three-carbon PEP originate at night? It is supplied from metabolism of previously accumulated carbohydrate. Depending on the plant, the daytime reserve can include starch or soluble carbohydrate. Breakdown of these compounds supplies intermediates that enter glycolytic metabolism and ultimately provide PEP. A simple arrow from carbohydrate to PEP represents a series of reactions, not conversion by a single enzyme. The dark period also requires energy for cellular maintenance and acid transport. Respiration and carbohydrate metabolism provide chemical energy in the absence of ongoing light-driven electron transport. The stored carbon skeleton and the energy reserve are therefore as important for nocturnal fixation as the presence of PEPC.

Imagine that a CAM leaf received unusually little light on the preceding day. Even if its stomata open normally at night, limited carbohydrate availability could restrict PEP supply and acid accumulation. Conversely, an enzyme assay showing high potential PEPC activity does not prove that the intact leaf has a high night-time carbon flux. Substrate availability, cellular pH, regulatory state and storage capacity all affect the realized rate. In an experiment, it is necessary to distinguish the capacity of an isolated enzyme from its actual operation in a living tissue.

Some of the bicarbonate fixed at night may originate from mitochondrial respiration rather than from external air. This matters for carbon accounting. Refixed respiratory carbon reduces the carbon that would otherwise escape the tissue, but it does not constitute an additional acquisition of atmospheric carbon. A leaf may accumulate some acid without showing a corresponding positive net carbon dioxide uptake from the chamber surrounding it. The difference between internal fixation and external net exchange becomes especially important when distinguishing weak CAM, CAM cycling and CAM idling.

Carbonic anhydrase and the energetic direction of the carboxylation reaction

In an aqueous environment, dissolved carbon dioxide participates in reversible hydration and dissociation reactions that produce bicarbonate and protons. Carbonic anhydrase accelerates approach to equilibrium. It does not change the equilibrium constant and does not pump carbon dioxide across a membrane merely by catalyzing interconversion. This is an example of a principle that applies to every enzyme: catalysis changes the rate of reaction without supplying an independent source of free energy. The local concentrations of carbon dioxide, bicarbonate and protons depend on the physicochemical conditions and the metabolic reactions consuming or generating them. At the near-neutral pH typical of cytosol, bicarbonate is a significant form of dissolved inorganic carbon. Its availability is relevant to PEPC.

PEP has a high phosphoryl-transfer potential, and the PEPC reaction releases inorganic phosphate as it produces oxaloacetate. The mechanism differs fundamentally from simply joining a carbon dioxide molecule to an ordinary three-carbon sugar. The phosphorylated acceptor and the chemistry of phosphate release help make formation of the new carbon-carbon bond favorable. In a biochemical pathway chart, the reaction is normally written with the physiological direction from PEP toward OAA. It should not be reversed merely because the daytime carbon dioxide-releasing step appears to undo nighttime fixation. Decarboxylating enzymes are different catalysts with different cofactor and energy requirements.

The stoichiometry also clarifies how a reserve supplies the initial carbon acceptor. Two molecules of PEP can in principle be derived from a six-carbon carbohydrate through the appropriate glycolytic sequence. If each accepts one new inorganic carbon, two four-carbon molecules can be produced. This is carbon bookkeeping, not a complete estimate of ATP or NADH yield from starch breakdown. The source of carbohydrate, reactions used to mobilize it and the downstream conversion of glycolytic intermediates influence energetic accounting. For example, phosphorolysis and hydrolysis of carbohydrate stores do not necessarily impose identical requirements at the first phosphorylation step. A student should distinguish a reliable count of conserved carbon atoms from an energetic calculation that needs more detailed assumptions.

PEPC is widely distributed beyond CAM leaves. It can help replenish intermediates of organic acid metabolism in plants that otherwise perform C3 photosynthesis. Such an anaplerotic reaction restores carbon skeletons that have been withdrawn for biosynthesis. Detecting PEPC protein in a leaf therefore cannot prove operation of CAM. A comparison of absolute enzyme abundance between CAM and C3 species can be informative, but activity needs to be linked to the temporal pattern of substrate supply and the fate of products. A high proportion of PEPC activity during darkness together with a large repeated accumulation of malate, followed by daytime decarboxylation and reassimilation, offers stronger evidence for its CAM role.

The same logic applies when identifying the immediate product of inorganic carbon fixation. A very short pulse of labeled carbon may allow identification of the first stable organic compound containing the label. A longer pulse can show label in many downstream intermediates, and it becomes increasingly difficult to infer the order of reactions from the final distribution. If label is found in malate shortly after exposure, rapid reduction of OAA may explain why the OAA pool itself is not heavily labeled at sampling. A small intermediate pool can turn over rapidly. The size of that pool is not necessarily a measure of its flux, because synthesis and consumption may both be rapid. This principle will recur in analyzing the daily malate reserve.

Reducing equivalents during malate synthesis

The conversion of OAA to malate illustrates why carbon and electron bookkeeping must be carried out together. Oxaloacetate contains a keto group that accepts reducing equivalents when it is transformed to the hydroxyl group of malate. In the commonly described cytosolic NAD-dependent reaction, NADH is oxidized back to NAD+. The recycled NAD+ can support other reactions that require oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. During glycolytic carbohydrate metabolism, the oxidation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate is one route that generates NADH. Thus nocturnal carbohydrate degradation can provide both a carbon acceptor for PEPC and reducing equivalents for subsequent malate synthesis.

The reaction must be distinguished from reduction of 3-phosphoglycerate in the Calvin cycle. The latter uses ATP for phosphorylation and NADPH for reduction, whereas the nocturnal OAA-to-malate reaction can use NADH. Although NADH and NADPH both carry reducing equivalents, their metabolic roles, enzyme specificities and compartmental pools are not interchangeable by default. A question that asks whether nighttime malate synthesis requires electrons supplied directly by photosystem I is therefore misleading. Nighttime metabolism can provide its own reducing equivalents from carbohydrate oxidation. Light-driven NADPH production remains necessary for sustained daytime photosynthetic assimilation.

If an enzyme with a preference for NADH is placed in a mixture containing only NADPH, the fact that NADPH is a reducing agent does not guarantee substantial catalytic activity. Substrate and cofactor specificity are molecular properties of the particular enzyme. Plants contain several malate dehydrogenases, including isoforms in different compartments and with different cofactor preferences. The reaction named on a syllabus diagram describes its physiological role; it does not imply that all cellular malate dehydrogenases have the same properties. In the CAM night pathway, it is the coordinated cytosolic supply of OAA, reducing equivalents and downstream transport that is central.

In an illustrative experiment, one might compare initial PEPC product formation and malate production in an extract prepared from control leaves and leaves affected in cytosolic NAD-dependent malate dehydrogenase activity. If PEPC activity is measured using an assay that indirectly detects consumption of NADH through a coupled malate dehydrogenase reaction, the interpretation must account for the coupling enzyme. A reduction in assay signal may indicate insufficient coupling capacity rather than a change in PEPC itself. Providing the coupling enzyme in excess and checking a known standard helps distinguish assay limitation from the behavior of the enzyme of interest. This is an important general lesson in data interpretation: a coupled assay reports a chain of events, not always an isolated molecular step.

At the intact leaf level, nighttime respiration continues alongside CAM fixation. The two processes can compete for some substrates while also supporting each other. Respiration generates energy for maintenance and transport; glycolytic intermediates are needed for PEP production. Complete oxidation of every available carbon skeleton would leave fewer acceptors for nocturnal assimilation, whereas directing every intermediate toward storage without sufficient maintenance energy would also be unsustainable. The plant must allocate its resources among competing functions. The essential biological point is not that respiration is absent in CAM, but that its carbon dioxide can be partly recovered and that its energy can support processes occurring during darkness.

3. Vacuolar storage, acid accumulation and the tonoplast

The central vacuole occupies a large fraction of the volume of many mature plant cells. It is bounded by a membrane known as the tonoplast, which separates the vacuolar lumen from the cytosol. CAM exploits this compartment to accommodate a substantial amount of malate and associated acid equivalents during the night without requiring the entire cytosol to undergo a comparable decrease in pH. This spatial arrangement is necessary for the temporal strategy: metabolism in one period creates a reserve that must remain available until another period.

A proton electrochemical gradient across the tonoplast contributes to the movement and retention of solutes. Vacuolar H+-ATPases use ATP hydrolysis to transport protons into the vacuole; vacuolar H+-pyrophosphatases use the energy available from hydrolysis of pyrophosphate. Malate transport involves membrane transport proteins and depends on the physicochemical conditions on both sides of the membrane. In the acidic vacuolar environment, a greater fraction of malate exists in protonated forms than in the relatively neutral cytosol. It is therefore appropriate at the physiological level to speak of an accumulation of malic acid. It is not appropriate to assume that every malate molecule is present as fully protonated neutral acid or that a single universal transporter explains every CAM species.

Organic acid accumulation changes the proton buffering demand of the vacuole. A buffer resists changes in pH by exchanging protons with its surroundings. Consequently pH and total acid content are related but are not interchangeable quantities. Two tissues with the same pH may require very different amounts of base to titrate their extracts to the same endpoint because they have different buffering capacities. In CAM research, titratable acidity is commonly compared at the end of the dark period and the end of the light period. The difference measures a change in acid equivalents on the chosen tissue-mass or area basis; it should not be directly equated with moles of newly absorbed atmospheric carbon without information about acid identity, proton stoichiometry and recycling.

A large vacuolar volume can increase the quantity of organic acid accommodated per cell, but total volume alone does not establish storage capacity. A vacuole must tolerate the concentration, osmotic effects and acid-base consequences of accumulation, and solute transport must be coordinated with proton transport. Succulence provides space and water reserves in many CAM plants, but it is neither a necessary diagnostic in isolation nor sufficient evidence of the entire metabolic cycle.

A thought experiment clarifies why the compartment matters. Suppose nocturnal PEPC activity remains normal but malate retention in the vacuole falls substantially. In the early part of darkness, OAA and malate synthesis may still take place. As the period proceeds, accumulation outside the storage compartment, altered pH homeostasis and feedback on metabolism can restrict continued carbon uptake. During the following day the usable acid reserve will be smaller. The exact gas exchange response cannot be predicted from the storage defect alone because respiratory carbon loss and stomatal behavior may also change. Measuring night-time conductance, acid accumulation and daytime assimilation together provides a stronger diagnosis than measuring one variable.

The tonoplast and thylakoid membrane should not be confused. Both can sustain proton gradients, but their roles here differ. The tonoplast participates in storage and cellular homeostasis; the thylakoid membrane couples light-driven electron transport to ATP synthesis. The two gradients belong to different compartments and do not directly substitute for one another. CAM links the resulting energy and solute fluxes through metabolism over the full day-night cycle.

Concentration, volume and capacity of an acid store

An acid storage compartment can be compared quantitatively without assuming that its pH directly reports the amount of malate. If a vacuole has an effective aqueous volume V and a mean malate concentration C, the amount of malate in that compartment is C multiplied by V. An illustrative volume of 0.8 milliliters of vacuolar water per gram of fresh tissue and a concentration of 100 millimoles per liter correspond to 0.0008 liters multiplied by 100 millimoles per liter, or 0.08 millimoles of malate per gram of tissue. Doubling the volume at the same concentration doubles the stored amount. Doubling the concentration at the same volume also doubles the amount, but may impose greater demands on pH regulation, solute transport and osmotic balance.

The calculation emphasizes that a concentration measured in an extract and a concentration within the intact vacuole are different quantities. An extract mixes solutes from cytosol, chloroplasts, mitochondria, vacuoles and extracellular spaces, and its volume depends on the extraction procedure. Converting an extract measurement to a compartment concentration requires information about how much tissue was used, extraction efficiency and the relevant compartment volume. In examinations, such values are usually supplied as explicit simplifying assumptions. In an actual experiment, reporting a concentration without its sample basis makes a comparison difficult to interpret.

The amount of acid that can be retained overnight is limited not only by the capacity of the storage pool but by the flux required to fill it in the available time. A large vacuole with inefficient transport may accumulate less malate by dawn than a smaller vacuole with a high transport rate. Conversely, increasing transporter capacity may have little effect if PEPC or PEP supply is already limiting. These are examples of serial metabolic processes with distributed control. An intact leaf does not automatically operate at the maximum activity measured for each isolated component. If a problem supplies a maximum storage capacity and a constant acid input rate, the smaller of potential input and available storage limits accumulation in the simplest model. This calculation is revisited in the experimental reasoning section.

Storage also has implications for leaf architecture. Thick leaves can have substantial water-storing tissue and large photosynthetic mesophyll cells. Their compact arrangement may reduce the effective internal airspace relative to some thin C3 leaves. These features can help conserve water and accommodate acid reserves, but may create a longer or more resistant diffusion path for external carbon dioxide. A specialized daytime internal carbon dioxide source partly changes the relevance of this path. The situation is not a universal law relating thickness to metabolic type. Anatomical studies find diverse arrangements among CAM species, and thick leaves can also occur in plants that rely on C3 carbon assimilation. It is better to ask what the particular feature contributes to storage, water balance or diffusion rather than label every thick leaf as CAM.

Leaf fresh mass may change during water deficit, complicating comparisons of acid accumulation. If two samples have the same amount of malate but one loses water, its concentration per gram of fresh tissue may rise even without net malate synthesis. Measurements on an area or dry-mass basis, accompanied by water content, can help distinguish concentration caused by water loss from genuine accumulation of acid. Dry mass is itself not a perfect normalization for every purpose, because structural and photosynthetic tissue proportions vary. The appropriate normalization should match the biological question. A calculation of atmospheric flux per leaf area and acid per fresh mass cannot be compared directly until they are converted to a common basis.

Proton pumps, solute transport and the consequences of uncoupling

The electrical potential and proton concentration difference across a membrane jointly determine the energy associated with proton movement. When a proton moves down its electrochemical gradient, its energy can be coupled to other transport processes. When a pump moves a proton against that gradient, an energy source is required. The vacuolar proton ATPase and proton pyrophosphatase contribute to generating and maintaining the gradient. Their presence does not imply that all malate transport is accomplished by a fixed proton-to-malate exchange stoichiometry. Different channels, carriers and gradients may be involved. A useful elementary diagram therefore marks the direction of proton pumping and the direction of net malate storage while leaving molecular details that vary among species qualified.

Imagine reducing proton-pumping capacity while leaving nocturnal carboxylation enzymes intact. The first consequence is not necessarily a complete cessation of PEPC. The storage environment may become less favorable, malate retention can decrease, and the cytosolic metabolic state can change. Continued carbon fixation can then be limited by the inability to accommodate the product and maintain acid-base balance. Conversely, a defect in the initial carboxylation pathway can leave the proton pumps structurally intact but deprive them of the normal solute flux associated with the CAM cycle. The two defects can therefore yield a similarly small dawn-dusk acid difference for different reasons. Measuring vacuolar pH and membrane transport alongside enzyme activity would make the alternatives more distinguishable.

A membrane perturbation that increases proton permeability may dissipate the gradient even if the pumps consume ATP at their normal rate. Energy use can continue without producing effective acid accumulation. This illustrates a general distinction between enzyme activity and coupling efficiency. An ATPase assay on an isolated preparation might show considerable hydrolytic activity, yet the membrane system might be incapable of maintaining a gradient. Functional coupling needs an intact membrane with suitable permeability and appropriate transport machinery. Similar reasoning is used to understand uncoupling in the thylakoid or mitochondrial inner membrane, though the physiological purpose and orientation of the gradients in those organelles differ from the tonoplast.

A further limitation concerns leakage of stored solute. A storage compartment may initially accumulate malate rapidly but retain it poorly through the latter part of the night. If an experiment measures only the final dawn concentration, the result could be interpreted as low uptake into the compartment even though earlier influx was substantial. A time series across the dark period, together with an independent measure of cytosolic production, helps discriminate slow accumulation from excessive loss. This is the same reason that a full gas exchange trace is more informative than a single measurement at midnight. Biological fluxes are best understood when the time dependence of both input and output is considered.

The integrity of the vacuole matters beyond CAM carbon transport. The compartment contributes to turgor and ionic homeostasis, and disrupting it can influence leaf water status and stomatal regulation. An intervention intended to test acid transport may therefore have secondary effects on gas exchange. A careful interpretation separates the immediate biochemical defect from downstream physiological consequences. A suitable control includes monitoring general viability and water status rather than attributing every change in assimilation to one specific transporter.

4. Daytime decarboxylation and the Calvin–Benson cycle

At the beginning of the light period, stored malate becomes a potential internal carbon dioxide supply. It is mobilized from the vacuole and processed through decarboxylating reactions. CAM species differ in their use of NADP-malic enzyme, NAD-malic enzyme and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase-associated pathways. A malic enzyme converts malate into pyruvate and carbon dioxide with reduction of its appropriate pyridine nucleotide cofactor. In a PEP carboxykinase route, malate can first be converted to OAA and OAA can then undergo nucleotide-dependent decarboxylation to produce PEP and carbon dioxide. These routes are related in their function, but differ in their intermediates, cofactor use, compartmentation and energetic consequences.

Carbon dioxide released internally diffuses toward Rubisco in the chloroplast stroma. When the rate of carbon dioxide supply is adequate and diffusive loss is restricted, the local availability of carbon dioxide relative to oxygen increases. Rubisco carboxylation becomes more favorable relative to oxygenation. Its normal carboxylation reaction adds carbon dioxide to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), a five-carbon sugar bisphosphate, forming a short-lived six-carbon intermediate that yields two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate. Thus the downstream pathway is the same Calvin cycle used by C3 plants. The name CAM does not designate a replacement for the Calvin cycle.

Reduction of 3-phosphoglycerate to triose phosphate requires ATP and NADPH. Further ATP is used to regenerate RuBP. Under the ideal carboxylation-only accounting, three carbon dioxide molecules yield one net triose phosphate at a cost of nine ATP and six NADPH. This represents Calvin-cycle requirements alone. The cost of generating and replenishing PEP, transporting acid, running the cell and offsetting losses must be considered separately. A universal total ATP cost for every CAM species cannot be inferred merely by adding the conventional C4 cost to the Calvin-cycle total.

The three-carbon remainder after decarboxylation is not simply waste. The metabolism of pyruvate, PEP and related intermediates can contribute to restoring carbohydrates, which provide the next night's carbon acceptor supply. Carbon conservation requires keeping track of both the four-carbon acid pool and the carbohydrate reserve. If a schematic represents only one carbon dioxide entering and one leaving malate, it must also show where the remaining three carbons go. Otherwise the drawing incorrectly suggests that the plant needs a fresh supply of three organic carbon atoms from the environment for every turn.

During the day, many strongly CAM-expressing leaves keep their stomatal conductance low while internal decarboxylation supplies Rubisco. This can produce an apparently puzzling result: the leaf may show little atmospheric carbon dioxide uptake in a gas exchange measurement while its chloroplasts are actively fixing carbon dioxide. Gas exchange instruments measure net transfer across a tissue boundary, whereas Rubisco activity concerns a biochemical reaction inside that boundary. No contradiction exists when the substrate is being supplied by an internal reserve.

If light is abruptly removed during the decarboxylation period, the Calvin cycle can become limited by ATP, NADPH and light-regulated enzyme activity. Released carbon dioxide may then accumulate, be reprocessed or escape according to the plant's physiological state. Nighttime fixation cannot compensate indefinitely for a failure of light reactions because the complete carbon economy depends on replenishing organic reserves and maintaining cellular energy supply. This is why CAM is not an example of a plant performing oxygenic photosynthesis without light.

Carbon bookkeeping and carbohydrate regeneration

A complete description of CAM must follow the three carbon atoms of the initial PEP molecule as carefully as the newly acquired fourth carbon. After nocturnal carboxylation, the four carbon atoms reside in oxaloacetate and then malate. During the day, a decarboxylation reaction separates one carbon atom as carbon dioxide and leaves a three-carbon compound. In a closed idealized carbon shuttle, the three-carbon remainder participates in regenerating the carbohydrate reserve from which the following night's PEP is produced. Carbon dioxide released by the decarboxylation is assimilated into new carbohydrate through the Calvin cycle. The reserve is therefore not equivalent to the net new carbohydrate gained by the leaf. Much of the reserve carbon cycles repeatedly, whereas net biomass production depends on the incorporation and retention of carbon acquired from outside.

A simplified example can make the distinction precise. Consider three molecules of PEP, containing nine carbon atoms in total. They capture three additional inorganic carbon atoms to form three malate molecules, which contain twelve carbon atoms. Decarboxylation produces three three-carbon residues and three carbon dioxide molecules. If all released carbon dioxide is assimilated in the ideal Calvin cycle, it contributes enough carbon for one net triose phosphate. The three-carbon residues, containing nine carbon atoms altogether, are available for regeneration of the reserve. It would be incorrect to count the original nine carbon atoms as new atmospheric carbon gain each time the cycle turns. This model ignores respiratory losses, metabolite exchanges and alternative sources of carbon, but the bookkeeping demonstrates why gross turnover can substantially exceed net gain.

Rebuilding the reserve from the three-carbon residues involves reactions of gluconeogenic metabolism. Gluconeogenesis is not simply glycolysis written in reverse: some glycolytic reactions are strongly favorable in their normal physiological direction and must be bypassed by other reactions when carbohydrate is synthesized. In malic enzyme-dependent schemes the initial residue is pyruvate, whereas phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase-associated decarboxylation can return a phosphorylated three-carbon compound directly. The appropriate steps, energy requirements and compartments consequently differ. The reserve need not take the same chemical form in every species; starch and soluble sugars may contribute in different proportions.

It is tempting to assume that every molecule of carbohydrate present at dusk will become a carbon acceptor during darkness. The leaf, however, requires carbohydrate for maintenance, transport to other organs, growth and respiratory metabolism. Some newly formed sugars may be exported through the phloem and never return to the photosynthetic cell. A plant must balance its investment in the next cycle with current demands. This gives an explanation for why changes in source-sink relations can influence the capacity for nocturnal carbon assimilation without a mutation in PEPC. For example, a prolonged decrease in daily light supply may reduce both net carbon production and carbohydrate available for the next night. The associated decrease in acid gain could reflect reserve limitation rather than failure of the acid storage compartment.

One way to analyze such a situation is to determine the changes in reserve carbohydrate from dusk to dawn and the amount of malate accumulated over the same interval. If the carbohydrate reserve declines substantially while malate gain remains modest, several alternatives deserve attention, including high respiratory demand, reduced PEPC fixation or loss of stored acid. If the reserve remains unusually high at dawn despite a low acid gain, impaired mobilization or restricted downstream use becomes plausible. Neither pattern alone demonstrates a unique enzyme defect. Measurements of the relevant intermediates and rates, interpreted with a control, are needed to refine the explanation.

Decarboxylation mechanisms and metabolic compartmentation

A malic enzyme can catalyze oxidative decarboxylation of malate. The substrate loses carbon dioxide, a three-carbon product pyruvate is formed, and a pyridine nucleotide is reduced. NAD-malic enzyme and NADP-malic enzyme are distinguished by the cofactors associated with their reactions. In the more detailed schemes the localization of the enzyme matters because NADH and NADPH are generated and used in different subcellular contexts. A mitochondrial enzyme can contribute to respiratory metabolism, whereas a chloroplast-associated reaction may affect local reducing power. The syllabus does not require a universal map of the organelle localization of every isoform in every CAM lineage. Rather, it requires recognition that the basic carbon transfer can be accomplished by related but nonidentical biochemical routes.

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase offers another way to liberate carbon dioxide. In a simplified scheme malate is first oxidized back to OAA. The OAA can then undergo a nucleotide-dependent reaction yielding PEP and carbon dioxide. The ATP- or GTP-related details depend on the enzyme system in question; the plant PEP carboxykinase commonly encountered in plant physiology uses ATP. This pathway differs from direct oxidative decarboxylation of malate because the oxidation and decarboxylation are performed in distinct reactions. Its product is already phosphorylated. Energetic comparisons must therefore account for all steps required to restore carbohydrate from the three-carbon residue, rather than compare only the moment of carbon dioxide release.

Compartmentation poses a diffusion problem. Carbon dioxide released outside the chloroplast must reach Rubisco in the stroma. At the same time, it can move toward intercellular air spaces and potentially escape through stomata or other diffusive routes. Reduced daytime conductance helps retain the internally released gas, but the effectiveness of the concentrating mechanism also depends on where decarboxylation occurs and how quickly Rubisco consumes carbon dioxide. The local carbon dioxide concentration around Rubisco is not necessarily equal to an estimate calculated from external gas exchange using assumptions appropriate to a standard C3 leaf. Internal generation of carbon dioxide violates the simplest assumption that all carbon dioxide supporting Rubisco arrives through the stomata.

The distinction can be illustrated by an ideal chamber measurement. Suppose net atmospheric exchange is zero at midday but an independent estimate indicates active acid breakdown. The absence of net exchange might reflect close balance of inward and outward flux, or simply very low permeability combined with internal carbon transfer. It does not establish the precise internal carbon dioxide concentration. If respiration adds carbon dioxide as well, the internal source is a combination of multiple processes. Comparing the daytime acid decline with the formation of carbohydrate, oxygen evolution or direct estimates of photosynthetic activity provides complementary evidence.

It is possible to observe some simultaneous synthesis and consumption of malate without the whole CAM cycle being inefficient. Cells contain multiple metabolic functions that use organic acids, and no real plant is described exactly by a diagram with only one reaction operating at a given instant. However, a large futile cycle in which the newly fixed carbon dioxide is immediately released and lost at night would reduce the benefit of investing in storage. Temporal regulation of carboxylation, decarboxylation and transport helps bias flux in the appropriate direction. The central physiological question concerns net flux through the full cycle, not whether an enzyme can be detected at a time when its dominant role is reduced.

Coordinating light reactions and internal carbon release

During a typical illuminated Phase III, the energy absorbed by photosystems is transformed into chemical energy through thylakoid electron transport. Water oxidation supplies electrons and releases oxygen; linear electron flow contributes to ATP formation and NADPH production. The cyclic electron flow associated with photosystem I can help adjust the balance of energy supply. These processes are the same general machinery studied earlier in this subunit. CAM is an additional layer of carbon delivery rather than an alternative photochemical system. Consequently a CAM plant is still an oxygenic photosynthetic organism, even though much of its external carbon acquisition can occur at night.

The rate at which carbon dioxide is released from storage and the capacity of the Calvin cycle to consume it must be coordinated. In bright light, a sufficiently large malate reserve can support substantial daytime carbon assimilation. When the reserve becomes exhausted, the leaf may experience an internal carbon limitation if stomata remain closed. If environmental conditions permit stomatal reopening, direct daytime assimilation can take over. If the plant instead maintains low conductance because of severe water stress, photochemical energy absorption may exceed the capacity for carbon assimilation. Mechanisms of photoprotection discussed in the preceding node become important in this situation. The physiological benefit of CAM does not remove the need for protection against excessive excitation.

The relationship between ATP and NADPH requirements is not captured fully by the Calvin cycle alone. In the simplified carboxylation-only cycle, fixation of one carbon dioxide requires an average of three ATP and two NADPH. Carbon concentration, acid storage and reserve regeneration can introduce additional demands for ATP or reducing power, while oxidative decarboxylation may supply reducing equivalents in particular compartments. The cofactors cannot be pooled across the entire cell as if every organelle shared one freely mixing solution. A correct qualitative answer recognizes that energy costs are distributed in time and space. A quantitative total should be calculated only when the question specifies the pathway, the relevant reactions, and whether it is counting nucleotide molecules or high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents.

Consider an idealized problem in which three internally released carbon dioxide molecules are assimilated with no oxygenation or leakage. The Calvin-cycle expenditure is nine ATP and six NADPH. If a separate given model stipulates an extra six ATP equivalents for restoring the acceptor and maintaining the store, the total specified ATP-equivalent demand becomes fifteen. This is an arithmetic consequence of the assumptions and not a general CAM stoichiometry. In a real plant, the energetic contribution of reserve metabolism and transport can differ with species, decarboxylation route and physiological state. A common examination error is to apply a textbook C4 ATP estimate to CAM without examining the assumptions.

Light-dependent regulation of Calvin-cycle enzymes adds a further connection between the two halves of the cycle. Supplying carbon dioxide to a chloroplast is not sufficient for sustained Calvin-cycle assimilation if ATP, NADPH and the relevant activated enzymes are unavailable. Similarly, supplying abundant light does not guarantee net carbon gain if carbon dioxide availability is restricted. The two requirements are complementary. When an experiment compares photosynthetic rates under different light intensities, the available acid store and external conductance should be considered alongside the capacity of electron transport. A decline in rate at a given time of day may reflect changing substrate supply rather than permanent damage to the photosystems.

5. Four phases across a diel cycle

A diel cycle is a recurring approximately twenty-four-hour light-dark sequence. Strong CAM can be described using four phases, which express changes in dominant metabolism rather than rigid transitions at prescribed clock times. Phase I is the dark period in which stomatal conductance can increase, PEPC fixes inorganic carbon, and malic acid accumulates. A leaf that experiences water deficit may reduce even its nocturnal opening. Uptake can therefore vary within the dark period rather than remain constant from sunset to sunrise.

Phase II occurs around the transition to light. In some plants, stomata remain open briefly and both PEPC and Rubisco can contribute to carbon fixation as the photosynthetic machinery becomes active. The transition matters because PEPC activity does not necessarily drop to zero at the instant of dawn. Phase II can be brief, extended or poorly developed, depending on environmental conditions and the species studied. It is misleading to identify it solely by an exact hour on a graph without examining the light schedule and gas exchange response.

Phase III occupies much of the illuminated period in typical strong CAM. Vacuolar malate declines as carbon dioxide is released internally. Rubisco assimilates this carbon using the products of the light reactions while stomatal conductance is commonly low. Internal carbon dioxide can become elevated despite little or no uptake from external air. Acid depletion is not proof that all released carbon dioxide is assimilated: respiratory flux, diffusion and other metabolic routes can complicate the balance.

Phase IV may occur later in the afternoon when the acid reserve has been drawn down and external carbon dioxide again becomes useful to Rubisco. Provided the water status and environment permit it, stomata can reopen, allowing direct daytime assimilation. If water is scarce or atmospheric evaporative demand remains high, this phase may be suppressed. The idealized four-phase diagram should therefore be understood as a physiological reference model, not a rule that all CAM plants must follow exactly.

Consider the relationship between conductance and acidity on a graph. Stomatal conductance concerns the ability of gases to move through pores; titratable acidity concerns the stored acid pool. Acid content increases when the rate of acid synthesis and retention exceeds the rate of acid removal. Its maximum commonly occurs near the end of darkness, whereas conductance can peak several hours earlier. The timing of the maximum of a pool need not coincide with the maximum of its input flux. This general principle applies to many biological systems: concentration is a stock, metabolic rate is a flow, and a stock increases whenever its net rate of change is positive.

Daily regulation of PEPC helps coordinate the cycle. In many CAM systems, nocturnal phosphorylation by PEPC kinase reduces the sensitivity of PEPC to feedback inhibition by malate. Day-night changes in kinase expression or activity interact with metabolic and environmental signals. The regulatory adjustment is important because malate accumulates precisely while the enzyme must continue supplying new four-carbon acid. Phosphorylation should not be interpreted as permanently switching the enzyme on or as a replacement for substrate supply. Its significance lies in changing enzyme properties in a particular metabolic context.

The internal biological clock helps organize these responses, while light, water status, temperature and carbon availability can modify their expression. An experiment using constant conditions after entrainment can help separate persistence of endogenous rhythmic behavior from a response driven directly by alternating light and darkness. However, absence of a clear rhythm in one readout under constant conditions is not sufficient to conclude that the organism has no biological clock; that output may depend strongly on environmental or metabolic inputs.

Reading the four-phase record as a mass balance

A plot of nocturnal acid accumulation and daytime acid depletion is easiest to interpret by assigning each measured variable a physical meaning. The vertical axis of a malate content curve represents an amount or concentration. Its slope represents the net rate at which the pool changes. A gas exchange curve instead often reports a rate directly. Integrating the latter over time estimates the net carbon exchanged with the atmosphere, whereas the difference between two pool measurements estimates the change in storage. A positive gas exchange signal and a rising malate curve need not have identical shapes because carbon can simultaneously enter several processes and because some malate carbon originates from internal recycling.

For a simple illustrative four-phase record, suppose malate content starts at 10 mmol per square meter at dusk and reaches 70 mmol per square meter at dawn. The net gain is 60 mmol per square meter. If the dark interval lasted twelve hours, the mean net rate of storage gain is 5 mmol per square meter per hour. Now suppose the leaf's net uptake of carbon dioxide from external air during that period was 50 mmol per square meter. The difference of ten is not automatically measurement error, nor does it imply that ten more carbon atoms were created. Some respiratory carbon may have been refixed, the measured malate pool may exchange with other pools, and not all newly acquired carbon is necessarily retained in malate. A complete carbon balance would require independent measurements of the other fluxes. The discrepancy motivates an experiment instead of providing a definitive explanation by itself.

The slope of the malate curve may change during the night as the storage compartment fills. Early in darkness, substrates may be abundant and storage capacity largely available, leading to rapid accumulation. Later, reserve depletion or storage-related feedback can reduce the net rate. The malate content can continue to increase even while its rate of increase declines. A curve that approaches a plateau therefore does not indicate that malate is necessarily being consumed at an increasing rate; it indicates that net accumulation approaches zero. Production and removal may still both be occurring. This distinction prevents the common error of interpreting a high pool size as a high instantaneous metabolic flux.

The same reasoning applies to the light interval. A steep decline in acid content suggests a high net rate of depletion, but it is not itself a direct measure of Rubisco assimilation. Released carbon dioxide may be partly lost or enter other processes. If acid depletion continues in dim light while Calvin-cycle assimilation is restricted, the coupling between acid use and assimilation may become less efficient. Direct measurement of internal carbon processing or photosynthetic activity would help determine whether decarboxylation and reassimilation remain coordinated. The simplified phase model is valuable because it identifies a dominant sequence, while mass balance explains how to interpret deviations from that sequence.

The transition at dawn also illustrates the difficulty of assigning phase boundaries from a single variable. During Phase II, incoming light may activate the Calvin cycle while PEPC remains active for a period. A gas exchange record could show an increase in uptake, but without enzyme activity or labeling information one cannot specify how much is contributed by each carboxylase. Likewise, Phase IV may begin once the reserve is sufficiently depleted and stomata reopen, but the exact timing varies with environmental conditions. Phase labels are shorthand for mechanistically defined patterns and should not be assigned by arbitrary clock times.

Regulation of PEPC and the logic of feedback

Feedback inhibition occurs when a downstream product influences an upstream enzyme. In the case of CAM, accumulation of malate creates an apparent regulatory difficulty because the product associated with the storage process can inhibit the enzyme that supports continued fixation. The response of PEPC to malate is affected by its regulatory state. In many CAM systems, phosphorylation at a regulatory serine residue reduces sensitivity to malate. The phosphorylation is mediated by PEPC kinase, and the amount or activity of that kinase follows a daily regulatory pattern. The key point is that the chemical modification changes the enzyme's response to its metabolic environment rather than simply increasing the amount of enzyme protein.

An experiment can distinguish changes in enzyme abundance from changes in enzyme properties. Extracts obtained at different times of day can be compared at the same substrate concentration and under defined assay conditions. If two extracts contain similar amounts of PEPC but their activities respond differently to increasing malate concentration, a regulatory difference is plausible. To investigate phosphorylation specifically, one could compare the phosphorylation state independently and assess the effect of removing or preventing the modification. Changes in other factors, such as isoform composition or interacting metabolites, would need consideration before concluding that phosphorylation alone accounts for the response.

The malate inhibition curve is a particularly useful examination example. At a fixed PEP concentration, imagine a night-state enzyme preparation retaining 70 percent of its activity at a specified malate concentration, while a day-state preparation retains only 30 percent. If their activities in the absence of added malate are similar, the result supports different sensitivities to inhibition rather than a simple difference in maximum activity. The values are illustrative rather than measurements from a particular species. A student should recognize that percentage inhibition equals the activity lost relative to the appropriate reference; it is not the same as residual activity. The preparation retaining 70 percent has undergone 30 percent inhibition, whereas the one retaining 30 percent has undergone 70 percent inhibition.

Circadian regulation provides a means to anticipate regular environmental change, but the observed rhythm is also modified by immediate environmental conditions. A plant experiencing drought may alter the timing and magnitude of stomatal opening even if its internal oscillator continues to cycle. Similarly, a large change in available carbohydrate may alter metabolic flux without eliminating the daily pattern of gene regulation. The relationship between clock-controlled expression and final physiological behavior is therefore not necessarily one-to-one. Experimental designs that compare enzyme regulation, stomatal conductance and metabolic pools are more informative than those that assume a clock gene expression pattern is itself proof of carbon acquisition.

When evaluating a proposed regulatory mutant, distinguish direct consequences from secondary responses. For instance, reduced PEPC phosphorylation could increase sensitivity to malate, leading to reduced sustained fixation later in the night. The smaller acid reserve may then reduce internal daytime carbon dioxide supply, potentially changing stomatal behavior or photoprotection. Those daytime effects are downstream of the initial defect and may vary with environmental conditions. A correct mechanistic prediction includes this causal chain while avoiding the claim that every phenotype is an immediate action of the kinase.

6. CAM expression is a continuum

Obligate or constitutive CAM describes plants for which CAM is a characteristic part of mature photosynthetic metabolism. It does not imply that every molecule of atmospheric carbon dioxide enters at night. Young tissues, developmental changes and favorable environmental conditions can alter the relative contributions of night-time PEPC fixation and daytime Rubisco fixation. Even a strongly CAM-expressing individual can assimilate external carbon directly during transitional phases of the day.

Facultative CAM describes the ability of a plant to increase CAM expression in response to a change in conditions, often drought or salinity, with a return toward C3-dominated behavior when favorable conditions are restored in reversible systems. Mesembryanthemum crystallinum is a classical example used to study an environmentally induced shift. The term does not mean that the plant creates chloroplasts only after stress or replaces every component of C3 photosynthesis. Instead, changes in gene expression, enzyme regulation, carbohydrate turnover, transport and stomatal timing allow an alternative carbon acquisition schedule to operate alongside the shared photosynthetic machinery.

CAM cycling refers to a pattern in which daytime atmospheric assimilation can predominate while some respiratory carbon dioxide is refixed at night into organic acid, with little or no net nocturnal atmospheric uptake. The diagnostic distinction is between internal carbon recycling and external carbon acquisition. A small nocturnal increase in acidity is compatible with such recycling. A gas exchange trace with no positive night-time uptake cannot by itself establish that there is no CAM-related metabolism.

Under severe water limitation, some CAM plants can show CAM idling, with stomata largely closed throughout both night and day. Carbon dioxide generated internally by respiration can be captured and subsequently used again in the daily acid cycle. This minimizes losses of carbon and water but does not imply indefinite growth without atmospheric carbon acquisition. Respiration, cellular maintenance and any residual losses consume resources. The strategy is best understood as a persistence mechanism during extreme conditions, not a productive alternative to access to external carbon dioxide.

These distinctions also explain why different techniques may give different classifications. A measurement of carbon isotope composition reflects the integrated history of carbon assimilation, while a twenty-four-hour gas exchange record samples one period and a dawn-dusk acidity difference samples acid metabolism during that interval. A facultative plant examined under favorable conditions might show C3-like behavior even though it can express CAM after stress. Repeated measurements during a controlled change in water availability are therefore more informative than a single observation.

An appropriate comparison uses matched leaf age, light exposure, temperature and water status. A treatment should be interpreted relative to a control of the same species rather than solely relative to a different species that may have different anatomy, growth rate or stomatal density. When evaluating reversibility, observing the same plants after restoration of water is especially valuable. It separates an environmentally induced change in metabolism from permanent damage or differences among individuals.

How environmental induction can be demonstrated

A useful investigation of facultative CAM begins with the distinction between a plant's capacity to express a metabolic pathway and its expression under a particular condition. Suppose a group of plants is grown under well-watered conditions, with a controlled light-dark cycle and adequate mineral nutrition. At this stage the leaves may acquire most atmospheric carbon during the day. The investigator then imposes water limitation on part of the group, leaving matched plants well watered. Gas exchange and dawn-dusk acid differences are measured in both groups over several days. A gradual emergence of nocturnal acid gain, accompanied by a change in the daily distribution of carbon exchange, provides evidence of altered carbon metabolism in the stressed plants.

The direction of change matters. A stressed C3 leaf may close its stomata during the day simply because of water shortage. That closure does not by itself demonstrate CAM. The stressed leaf must show biochemical and physiological features of nocturnal acid metabolism rather than just a reduction in daytime uptake. A nocturnal acid increase without net positive atmospheric uptake may indicate a weak recycling mode; a larger acid increase together with substantial nocturnal uptake suggests a more pronounced shift. Comparing these responses allows the investigator to characterize the degree of expression instead of forcing every observation into a binary classification.

Rewatering the stressed plants provides an additional test. If normal hydration is restored and nocturnal acid accumulation declines as daytime assimilation recovers, the observations support physiological reversibility. If the stressed plants fail to recover, several possibilities remain, including persistent metabolic regulation, developmental changes, injury or incomplete restoration of water status. Reversibility should not be asserted merely because the species is known to exhibit facultative CAM. It must be demonstrated under the conditions investigated. Similarly, an experiment showing induction under salinity does not automatically establish that every other kind of stress will induce a quantitatively identical response.

Development introduces another source of variation. A young expanding leaf may allocate carbohydrate and cellular space differently from a mature photosynthetic leaf. As tissue matures, the effective storage capacity, enzyme complement and daily carbon economy can change. When comparing ages, one should not attribute all metabolic differences to a treatment if the sampled leaves are at different developmental stages. An apparent stress effect could otherwise reflect the fact that one group contains newly formed leaves and the other contains mature leaves. Matched sampling and repeated measurements help separate these influences.

An examination question might provide data from three conditions: well watered, water limited and rewatered. The appropriate response is to trace the changes in both daily carbon exchange and acid storage. If daytime uptake declines under stress but nocturnal uptake and acid gain increase and then decline after recovery, a facultative shift is a plausible explanation. If both daytime and nighttime uptake decline with no corresponding rise in acid gain, general suppression of metabolism may fit the data better. If a modest acid rhythm persists even when external exchange is very low, recycling may explain part of the response. This pattern-based reasoning is stronger than relying on any single physiological marker.

Survival during carbon limitation and the return to growth

When a plant closes stomata during most of the day and night, external carbon acquisition becomes very small. The available carbon in its tissues can still circulate through respiration, acid fixation and subsequent release. Such circulation may conserve resources that would otherwise be lost to the atmosphere. The important distinction is that reusing carbon does not create additional carbon atoms. A plant that survives an interval of CAM idling must rely largely on previously acquired resources, and it cannot maintain indefinite increases in dry mass without acquiring new material.

During an extreme dry period, allocation to growth may be reduced and maintenance becomes comparatively important. Cells must preserve membrane integrity, protein function and ion homeostasis. The metabolic benefit of refixing internally generated carbon dioxide is greatest when it prevents a loss that would otherwise occur. If a plant retains most respiratory carbon, its measured net carbon loss can be small even though gross respiration and gross carboxylation continue. A gas exchange reading near zero should therefore not be equated with metabolic inactivity. It describes the balance at the boundary of the organ.

The recovery period illustrates the importance of the stored resource. Following an improvement in water availability, stomatal opening can increase and external carbon acquisition can resume. Depending on the species and conditions, daytime assimilation, nocturnal assimilation or both may increase. A plant that has maintained viable photosynthetic tissue during drought can take advantage of the improved conditions without first replacing all of that tissue. However, recovery also depends on the extent of previous damage, reserve availability and the ability of the root system to supply water. CAM is one component of the overall drought response, not a guarantee of survival regardless of the intensity or duration of stress.

Plants with comparatively weak CAM can benefit from recycling carbon even when the total contribution of nocturnal atmospheric uptake is small. This explains why a metabolic phenotype need not dominate the lifetime carbon budget in order to be physiologically relevant. An ecological comparison should ask whether a particular metabolic adjustment improves survival, reproductive success or carbon retention under the circumstances in which it operates. These outcomes cannot be inferred solely from a laboratory rate measured during one favorable interval. The relative contribution of each carbon acquisition mode may change throughout development and across seasons.

7. C3, C4 and CAM: three solutions to carbon acquisition

The names C3 and C4 refer to the number of carbon atoms in characteristic early stable products of carbon fixation. Direct Rubisco carboxylation in C3 metabolism produces 3-phosphoglycerate; initial PEPC carboxylation in C4 metabolism produces OAA. CAM shares the latter preliminary four-carbon chemistry but is named for its daily acid metabolism. Consequently CAM should not be treated as a pathway whose final carbohydrate contains a different number of carbon atoms from carbohydrates made by C3 plants.

In a conventional C3 leaf, carbon dioxide diffuses into the photosynthetic tissue and Rubisco assimilates it directly during the light period. In a conventional two-cell C4 leaf, PEPC in mesophyll cells initially fixes bicarbonate. Four-carbon acids transfer carbon to bundle sheath cells, where decarboxylation raises carbon dioxide availability near Rubisco. CAM typically uses time instead of this intercellular division of labor: nighttime preliminary fixation, temporary storage and daytime release in photosynthetic tissue. Exceptions to canonical anatomy and behavior exist, so the functional distinction is more reliable than demanding that every representative match a single diagram.

C4 and CAM are both carbon-concentrating strategies. Their advantage is not that PEPC directly makes the final carbohydrate but that it helps supply inorganic carbon to the Calvin cycle while restricting a competing reaction at Rubisco. In C3 plants, low internal carbon dioxide and high temperature can favor oxygenation relative to carboxylation. The resulting photorespiratory pathway recovers part of the carbon and consumes energy. By elevating carbon dioxide near Rubisco, C4 and CAM can reduce the relative cost of oxygenation, although neither should be described as eliminating photorespiration under every condition.

Conventional C4 metabolism typically requires additional ATP equivalents to regenerate PEP. In a simplified PPDK-based model, ATP conversion to AMP and pyrophosphate represents expenditure of two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents per regenerated PEP. CAM also requires coordinated carbon acceptor regeneration and acid transport, but its daily reserve metabolism and diverse decarboxylation routes complicate a fixed total energy cost. The energetic advantage of direct C3 assimilation can matter under conditions where photorespiratory losses are small and water is plentiful. In contrast, a carbon-concentrating strategy can be advantageous when the reduction in photorespiratory or water-loss costs offsets the extra metabolic expenditure.

Water-use efficiency and absolute productivity are different outcomes. A slow-growing plant can acquire more carbon per unit water lost while gaining less total carbon per day than a rapidly growing irrigated crop. CAM often improves the ratio of carbon acquisition to transpiration by changing the timing of gas exchange, but acid storage and nocturnal uptake capacity can constrain the rate at which new carbon is supplied to daytime metabolism. C4 plants can achieve high productivity under bright and warm growing conditions; C3 plants can be highly productive when water, temperature and carbon dioxide supply are favorable. These are conditional ecological tendencies rather than a universal ranking.

The examples of rice, maize and pineapple provide useful anchors for C3, C4 and CAM respectively. They do not constitute an experimentally controlled comparison. Each has been selected and cultivated in different environments and differs in anatomy and development. To compare the influence of carbon acquisition mechanism itself, one must distinguish pathway traits from unrelated species traits. In examination questions, look for the specified limiting factor before predicting which strategy has an advantage.

Rubisco competition and environmental tradeoffs

Rubisco's two competing reactions provide the biochemical background for comparing all three photosynthetic strategies. In its carboxylation reaction, carbon dioxide is added to RuBP and two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate are produced. In its oxygenation reaction, oxygen competes with carbon dioxide, producing one molecule of 3-phosphoglycerate and one molecule of 2-phosphoglycolate. The latter requires processing through photorespiratory metabolism. The complete recycling pathway is treated in the following subunit. For the present comparison, it is sufficient to recognize that oxygenation imposes a cost through additional metabolic processing and partial carbon loss.

The relative rates of carboxylation and oxygenation depend on the concentrations of competing gases at the enzyme, the properties of Rubisco and temperature. At fixed enzyme properties and temperature, increasing carbon dioxide relative to oxygen tends to favor carboxylation. C4 and CAM increase the availability of carbon dioxide near Rubisco through different mechanisms. Neither changes the basic fact that Rubisco can catalyze both reactions. If carbon dioxide supply falls or a concentrating mechanism is compromised, oxygenation can become more important. The relevant variable is the environment at the enzyme, not simply the amount of carbon dioxide measured in the air outside the leaf.

In a C3 leaf, closure of stomata under water stress may lower internal carbon dioxide while light absorption continues. This can favor oxygenation and reduce the effectiveness of net carbon assimilation. Conventional C4 plants can maintain an elevated carbon dioxide supply to bundle-sheath Rubisco through continued delivery of four-carbon acids, provided mesophyll fixation and transport remain functional. A CAM leaf can maintain internal supply from a pre-existing acid reserve during the day, even if daytime entry through the stomata is restricted. However, the finite reserve will eventually be drawn down. The concentrating mechanisms thus have different temporal relationships with external diffusion.

The advantage of C4 over C3 is often especially apparent under high light and warm conditions when the reduction of photorespiratory costs offsets the extra energy expenditure of the C4 cycle. In cool environments or under high external carbon dioxide, the advantage can be reduced because direct Rubisco fixation becomes relatively less constrained by oxygenation. CAM offers a different advantage when the conditions at night allow carbon acquisition at a lower water cost than during the day. It may be particularly useful when water availability is intermittent and persistent leaf tissue is valuable. These differences show why the question of which pathway is best is incomplete unless an environmental constraint and a measure of performance are specified.

One can separate a physiological advantage from an ecological outcome by considering a hypothetical comparison under high carbon dioxide and abundant water. Under these conditions, a C3 plant may experience relatively little carbon limitation at Rubisco, and the additional machinery for concentration may be less advantageous from an energy-budget perspective. That does not prove every C3 species will grow faster than every C4 or CAM species. Growth depends on nutrient availability, developmental traits, leaf area, source-sink relations and other factors. The proper examination inference concerns the specified pathway cost and benefit under the stated conditions, not an unqualified ranking of entire organisms.

Spatial and temporal concentration as contrasting engineering constraints

In a conventional C4 leaf, the distance between mesophyll fixation and bundle-sheath assimilation is small compared with the time separating the dominant reactions in CAM. Four-carbon acids move between adjacent cell types and can be continuously synthesized and consumed during the light period. The rate of carbon delivery must match the rate of assimilation in the receiving tissue. A disturbance of intercellular transfer can disrupt the system even if both types of carboxylase remain active. Kranz anatomy is a common structural arrangement supporting this coordination, although not all C4 lineages have the canonical arrangement. The defining functional issue is how carbon dioxide concentration near Rubisco is achieved and sustained.

A typical CAM cell must instead hold enough carbon in an intermediate pool for a later period. Its maximum daily capacity depends in part on the quantity of acid accumulated and how completely it can be mobilized. Its carbon acceptor must also be regenerated across the day-night sequence. The storage compartment is therefore not merely an incidental location for an intermediate; it connects physiologically separated intervals. A defect that shortens storage lifetime can directly reduce the amount of carbon available during Phase III, even when nocturnal initial fixation occurred normally. This constraint is distinct from the need for continuous movement of acids between conventional C4 cell types.

Both mechanisms must restrict loss of the carbon dioxide they release. In C4 leaves the bundle-sheath environment and diffusion properties influence the extent of leakage. In CAM leaves low daytime stomatal conductance and internal anatomy contribute to retention. Carbon that escapes after a plant has spent energy concentrating it represents a reduction in the efficiency of the process. The energy cost per net carbon assimilated increases when more concentration cycles are required for a given net gain. A numerical problem can express this distinction using a specified retention fraction; the fraction must not be assumed to be identical in the two types of leaf.

For example, if an illustrative concentrating system releases 100 units of carbon dioxide and retains 80 units for the specified assimilation process, its retention fraction is 0.8. To deliver 100 retained units at the same fraction requires 125 released units. This does not mean the plant necessarily absorbs 125 units from the atmosphere because internal recycling may recover some of the carbon; the calculation explicitly refers to the modeled delivery step. If a separate energy cost is given per release cycle, the extra turnover can be included in an energetic estimate. This calculation teaches the general distinction between gross cycling and useful output without assigning a fixed leakage rate to real plants.

There are also differences in the consequences of environmental timing. If a hot afternoon is followed by a cool, humid night, the schedule of CAM gas exchange can reduce water loss while retaining daytime assimilation from storage. If nights are persistently hot and dry, the difference in evaporative demand may be smaller, and the potential benefit of nocturnal uptake may decrease. If day length changes, the relative durations of storage accumulation and consumption also change. A plant's physiological adjustments to these circumstances determine its realized carbon gain. The underlying biochemical route remains recognizable even as the relative sizes of its phases vary.

8. Evidence, measurements and quantitative interpretation

To study the daily metabolism of a leaf, a gas exchange system can measure the net rate at which carbon dioxide enters or leaves a chamber. A second measurement can compare titratable acidity of tissue sampled near dawn and dusk. When substantial positive night-time carbon uptake coincides with acid accumulation and daytime acid decline, the two observations together support operation of a CAM cycle. They do not identify each enzyme in the pathway. Enzyme assays, tracer experiments and appropriate controls provide additional information about the mechanism.

Carbon isotope tracers can help distinguish the order of metabolic incorporation. If labeled inorganic carbon is supplied during darkness, early label appearing in four-carbon organic acids is consistent with PEPC-dependent fixation. During a subsequent unlabeled light period, label can move into products of Calvin-cycle metabolism. Because isotope can move through multiple pathways and pools, a final labeled sugar sample alone does not establish the order of reactions. A series of sampling times, identification of metabolites, and comparison with the labeling of tissues exposed to tracer during the light period are essential for a causal interpretation.

Natural carbon isotope composition gives a different type of evidence. The ratio of carbon-13 to carbon-12 in tissue reflects source carbon and discrimination during assimilation. Rubisco and PEPC-related carbon acquisition have different fractionation characteristics, but the resulting tissue isotope signature also depends on diffusion, the extent of direct daytime uptake and the proportions of carbon acquired by each route. Strong CAM may show isotope signatures closer to those of C4 plants than typical C3 plants. Weak or facultative CAM may overlap with C3 values. Thus isotope composition is useful for estimating integrated contributions but cannot uniquely identify every CAM phenotype.

Consider a hypothetical constant night-time net carbon uptake rate of 3 micromoles per square meter per second over an eight-hour period. Multiplying by 8 × 3600 seconds gives 86,400 micromoles per square meter, or 86.4 millimoles per square meter. If measurements are variable, the relevant quantity is the time integral of the net flux, estimated by summing intervals or using a trapezoidal approximation. If the gas exchange values are net, respiratory losses are already represented in the measured boundary flux. Subtracting those same losses a second time would double-count them.

A second hypothetical comparison concerns water use. A leaf that gains 90 millimoles of carbon dioxide per square meter while losing 6 moles of water has a ratio of 15 millimoles of carbon dioxide per mole of water. Another leaf gains 150 millimoles while losing 20 moles, giving 7.5 millimoles per mole. The first leaf has higher carbon gain per unit water lost, while the second has greater total carbon gain. This numerical example deliberately separates efficiency from productivity. It does not describe published data or assert that real CAM or C4 leaves have fixed ratios.

Finally, a reduction in overnight acid gain after a treatment can be caused by several distinct processes: reduced stomatal opening, decreased supply of PEP, impaired PEPC activity, altered acid transport, increased acid consumption or changes in respiratory refixation. A strong experiment measures at least one variable upstream and one downstream of the proposed defect. If conductance and PEPC capacity remain similar to the control but vacuolar acid retention is decreased, the storage hypothesis becomes more plausible, though additional evidence is needed to distinguish transport from metabolic consumption. The examination skill is to identify which measurements discriminate competing mechanisms rather than simply to match a phenotype to a familiar enzyme name.

Titration as a quantitative indicator of daily acid metabolism

The historical observation of a daily change in acidity can be converted into a reproducible measurement by titrating extracts from tissue sampled at defined times. A standardized quantity of base is added until a specified endpoint is reached. The quantity of base required reports titratable acid equivalents under the measurement conditions. Comparing dawn and dusk values provides an estimate of the nocturnal acid increment. Because pH is a logarithmic measure of proton activity while titratable acidity reflects neutralization capacity over a specified pH interval, the two measurements answer different questions.

Suppose a hypothetical extract represents 2 grams of fresh tissue. A dawn sample requires 8 milliliters of 0.02 molar sodium hydroxide to reach a defined endpoint, whereas a matched dusk sample requires 3 milliliters. Each milliliter of the base contains 0.02 millimoles of hydroxide, so the dawn and dusk extracts require 0.16 and 0.06 millimoles, respectively. The difference is 0.10 millimoles of base equivalents for 2 grams, or 0.05 millimoles per gram fresh mass. Expressed in micromoles, the difference is 50 micromoles of acid equivalents per gram. The calculation depends on using the same tissue basis, endpoint and extraction conditions for the two samples.

A conversion of acid equivalents to moles of malate requires additional assumptions. Malic acid is diprotic, and the titration range and chemical environment determine how many proton equivalents are represented by a particular measured quantity of acid. Other organic acids and buffering substances can also contribute to the measured base requirement. Unless a problem states that the change is entirely due to malic acid and specifies the relevant equivalence, it is safer to report the directly measured acid equivalents. This avoids converting a well-defined analytical measurement into an unsupported carbon-fixation estimate.

An important control is sampling of comparable tissues at the same defined points in the light-dark cycle. If one sample consists of older leaves and another of younger leaves, differences in baseline acid content, water content or storage capacity can confound the result. Destructive sampling may require matched leaves or matched individuals rather than repeatedly extracting the same intact tissue. Biological replication allows variability among plants to be distinguished from a systematic dawn-dusk difference. The purpose of the control is to isolate the diel effect from differences in the material sampled.

The strength of titration as evidence increases when it is used alongside a specific metabolite measurement. A rise in titratable acidity accompanied by a rise in measured malate supports the identification of the responsible acid. Coupling this with external carbon exchange identifies whether the increase is associated with net uptake of atmospheric carbon. If the three observations diverge, the discrepancy is scientifically useful: it suggests additional acid species, internal recycling, altered normalization or limitations of measurement. It should prompt investigation rather than automatic rejection of the results.

Stable isotope composition and its limits

Stable isotopes of carbon differ in mass but share the same number of protons. Photosynthetic carbon acquisition can discriminate between carbon isotopes because diffusion and enzyme-catalyzed reactions have slightly different rates for molecules containing the two isotopes. The ratio of carbon-13 to carbon-12 in a tissue therefore reflects the history of carbon entering that tissue as well as the isotope composition of its sources. This is distinct from a deliberate tracer pulse: natural abundance measurements integrate ordinary carbon acquisition over the formation of tissue.

A commonly used notation expresses the isotope ratio of the sample relative to a standard. If R is the carbon-13 to carbon-12 ratio, delta-13-C equals the quantity R sample divided by R standard minus one, multiplied by one thousand. Values are reported in parts per thousand, also called per mil. An illustrative sample with a ratio 0.98 times that of the standard has a delta value of minus 20 per mil. A negative value indicates that the ratio is lower than that of the reference; it does not mean the sample contains a negative amount of carbon-13. The notation allows small relative differences to be expressed conveniently.

C3 carbon acquisition often yields a more negative tissue isotope signature than strong CAM or conventional C4 acquisition. Yet the final value is influenced by the relative contribution of different routes, the discrimination of the overall system, environmental conditions and the isotope composition of incoming carbon. The method therefore has important limitations for identifying weak or facultative CAM. A plant may have acquired most tissue carbon by daytime direct fixation even if it exhibits measurable CAM during a particular stress period. Its tissue signature can remain close to a C3 value. Strong CAM and C4 can also overlap because both initially employ PEPC for much of their external carbon acquisition.

For a conceptual mixing calculation, suppose two sources of tissue carbon have illustrative isotope signatures of minus 28 and minus 12 per mil, and a simplified question explicitly assumes linear mixing of their contributions with no additional discrimination. An equal contribution of the two sources gives an approximate signature of minus 20 per mil. A three-to-one contribution of the first source gives a value of minus 24 per mil. The example is a stipulated model, not a universal calibration of the contribution of CAM. Actual isotope mixing is based on isotope ratios and depends on source composition and biochemical discrimination; a single tissue value cannot be used to assign an exact nighttime fraction without suitable assumptions.

Combining isotope analysis with short-term physiology is particularly valuable in the study of facultative plants. If current gas exchange shows strong nocturnal uptake but old tissue has a C3-like isotope signature, there need be no contradiction. The old tissue can retain the integrated signal of earlier growth, whereas gas exchange reports the current state. Analysis of newly formed tissue after a sustained treatment, with an appropriate comparison, may reveal a shift. In an examination, first identify the time window represented by each measurement before deciding whether two observations are compatible.

Examination traps

1. CAM makes carbohydrate without light

Correction: Nocturnal PEPC captures inorganic carbon into organic acids; light-powered Calvin-cycle assimilation largely follows in daylight. Reasoning: Distinguish initial fixation from reduction of carbon to triose phosphate.

2. PEPC consumes CO₂ directly

Correction: Bicarbonate is the immediate inorganic substrate of PEPC. Reasoning: Distinguish the external gas from the actual enzyme substrate.

3. Malate is the first product of PEPC

Correction: OAA is the first product; malate is formed by subsequent reduction. Reasoning: A PEPC defect and a malate dehydrogenase defect need not give identical profiles.

4. All CAM plants open stomata every night

Correction: Nocturnal conductance varies with water status, species and phase. Reasoning: Do not extrapolate the idealized cycle to every condition.

5. Closed stomata imply zero Rubisco activity

Correction: Internally released CO₂ can supply Rubisco during Phase III. Reasoning: Boundary gas exchange is not the same as enzyme turnover.

6. Acidity peaks when malate breakdown peaks

Correction: Acidity measures a stored pool; it falls when removal exceeds formation. Reasoning: Distinguish a pool from a flux.

7. CAM and C4 are identical

Correction: Typical C4 uses spatial separation; CAM uses temporal separation. Reasoning: Identify the organizing dimension, not simply a shared enzyme.

8. CAM plants never fix CO₂ directly in daylight

Correction: Phase II and IV can involve direct Rubisco carboxylation. Reasoning: CAM retains the Calvin cycle.

9. Acid gain equals external CO₂ uptake exactly

Correction: Respiratory CO₂ can be refixed; titratable acidity measures acid equivalents. Reasoning: A night-time acid increase without positive net uptake may indicate recycling.

10. All succulent plants perform CAM

Correction: Succulence is not a sufficient diagnostic of CAM. Reasoning: Require physiological evidence rather than appearance.

11. CAM always eliminates photorespiration

Correction: Internal CO₂ concentration can suppress relative oxygenation, not guarantee its absence. Reasoning: Consider supply and leakage.

12. PEPC phosphorylation supplies ATP

Correction: Phosphorylation changes enzyme regulation including malate sensitivity. Reasoning: Separate regulatory effects from energy supply.

13. CAM idling permits unlimited growth

Correction: Recycling conserves carbon but cannot supply unlimited new biomass without external carbon. Reasoning: Distinguish conservation from gain.

14. Higher water-use efficiency means higher productivity

Correction: Carbon gain per water lost is a ratio; absolute carbon gain is separate. Reasoning: Calculate both quantities.

15. Carbon isotope measurement identifies every CAM plant

Correction: Weak CAM can overlap C3 and strong CAM can overlap C4 isotope signatures. Reasoning: Integrate acidity, isotope and gas exchange evidence.

Examination practice

1. Part B — The immediate inorganic substrate of PEPC is:

  1. O₂
  2. HCO₃⁻
  3. RuBP
  4. NADPH
Answer and explanation

B. HCO₃⁻ PEPC adds bicarbonate to PEP; RuBP is the organic substrate of Rubisco.

2. Part B — The immediate organic product of PEPC is:

  1. Malate
  2. Pyruvate
  3. Oxaloacetate
  4. 3-PGA
Answer and explanation

C. Oxaloacetate PEPC produces OAA, subsequently reduced to malate; Rubisco carboxylation produces 3-PGA.

3. Part B — Which membrane encloses the principal overnight organic acid store?

  1. Outer chloroplast membrane
  2. Tonoplast
  3. Thylakoid
  4. Plasma membrane
Answer and explanation

B. Tonoplast The tonoplast encloses the vacuole. Thylakoids instead support light-driven energy conversion.

4. Part B — In typical strong CAM, peak acidity is expected near:

  1. Dawn
  2. Midday
  3. Late afternoon
  4. Immediately after dusk
Answer and explanation

A. Dawn Acid accumulates in darkness and is mobilized in daylight; exact timing varies.

5. Part B — Which enzyme reduces OAA to malate?

  1. Rubisco
  2. PEPC kinase
  3. Malate dehydrogenase
  4. Carbonic anhydrase
Answer and explanation

C. Malate dehydrogenase Malate dehydrogenase catalyzes reduction, whereas PEPC kinase regulates carboxylase activity.

6. Part B — What distinguishes CAM from conventional C4 assimilation?

  1. Rubisco only in CAM
  2. Temporal versus spatial separation
  3. Absence of PEPC in CAM
  4. Different final sugar carbon number
Answer and explanation

B. Temporal versus spatial separation Both use PEPC and the Calvin cycle; the principal separation differs.

7. Part B — Which phase commonly involves daytime decarboxylation and low stomatal conductance?

  1. I
  2. II
  3. III
  4. IV
Answer and explanation

C. III Phase III is the principal internal CO₂ supply phase.

8. Part B — Which is a classic facultative CAM example?

  1. Mesembryanthemum crystallinum
  2. Rice
  3. Maize
  4. All algae
Answer and explanation

A. Mesembryanthemum crystallinum The ice plant can shift toward CAM under environmental stress.

9. Part B — Ideal Calvin-cycle demand per three CO₂ is:

  1. 3 ATP and 3 NADPH
  2. 9 ATP and 6 NADPH
  3. 6 ATP and 9 NADPH
  4. No ATP
Answer and explanation

B. 9 ATP and 6 NADPH Three CO₂ yield one net triose phosphate at the specified cost, excluding extra concentrating expenses.

10. Part B — What commonly supplies nocturnal PEP?

  1. Photolysis of water
  2. Reserve carbohydrate breakdown
  3. Rubisco oxygenation only
  4. Atmospheric PEP
Answer and explanation

B. Reserve carbohydrate breakdown Carbohydrate metabolism generates glycolytic intermediates and PEP.

11. Part C — A leaf accumulates acid at night but external net CO₂ exchange is zero. What is a defensible inference?

  1. CAM impossible
  2. All acid carbon came from atmosphere
  3. Respiratory carbon may be refixed
  4. Rubisco inactive all day
Answer and explanation

C. Respiratory carbon may be refixed Internal recycling can produce acid without net external uptake. Additional metabolic measurements are needed.

12. Part C — Normal night conductance and PEPC capacity coexist with low retained vacuolar acid. Which process should be investigated first?

  1. PSI antenna size alone
  2. Tonoplast acid transport or retention
  3. Rubisco binding alone
  4. Root gravity sensing
Answer and explanation

B. Tonoplast acid transport or retention The likely deficit is downstream of initial fixation; increased acid consumption is another possibility.

13. Part C — A night flux of 4 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ continues for six hours. Integrated net uptake is:

  1. 24 µmol m⁻²
  2. 86.4 mmol m⁻²
  3. 8.64 mmol m⁻²
  4. 144 mmol m⁻²
Answer and explanation

B. 86.4 mmol m⁻² 4 × 6 × 3600 = 86,400 µmol m⁻², or 86.4 mmol m⁻².

14. Part C — X gains 120 mmol CO₂ and loses 8 mol H₂O; Y gains 160 mmol and loses 20 mol. Which is correct?

  1. X greater carbon gain
  2. Y greater efficiency
  3. X greater efficiency, Y greater carbon gain
  4. Identical ratios
Answer and explanation

C. X greater efficiency, Y greater carbon gain X gains 15 mmol per mol water; Y gains 8. Y has greater absolute gain.

15. Part C — A dark pulse produces labeled malate before labeled sugars appear next day. This supports:

  1. Malate as final carbohydrate
  2. Temporal intermediate supplying Calvin cycle
  3. Absence of photosystems
  4. Direct production of RuBP by PEPC
Answer and explanation

B. Temporal intermediate supplying Calvin cycle A time series of labeled metabolites supports preliminary organic acid storage followed by redistribution.

16. Part C — Severe drought suppresses external exchange day and night but a small acid rhythm persists. Which interpretation is plausible?

  1. Increased Phase IV
  2. CAM idling
  3. Obligate C4 transition
  4. Respiration ceased
Answer and explanation

B. CAM idling Internal carbon recycling with minimal exchange is compatible with idling.

17. Part C — After a cloudy day, acid accumulation falls despite unchanged atmospheric CO₂. What additional variable matters?

  1. Carbohydrate reserve
  2. Chromosome number only
  3. Flower color
  4. Atmospheric nitrogen alone
Answer and explanation

A. Carbohydrate reserve Low light can limit carbohydrate and thus PEP supply. Conductance also deserves measurement.

18. Part C — Malate declines during Phase III while external uptake is near zero. Which conclusion is valid?

  1. Assimilation impossible
  2. Internal CO₂ can support Rubisco
  3. ATP synthase absent
  4. All released carbon retained
Answer and explanation

B. Internal CO₂ can support Rubisco Boundary flux need not reflect biochemical assimilation. The data cannot establish complete retention.

19. Part C — C3-like isotope signature occurs alongside stress-induced nocturnal acid gain. What follows?

  1. CAM excluded
  2. Weak or facultative CAM possible
  3. C4 anatomy proven
  4. Isotope data necessarily wrong
Answer and explanation

B. Weak or facultative CAM possible An integrated C3-like isotope signal can coexist with low or intermittent CAM contribution.

20. Part C — A treatment blocks nocturnal PEPC phosphorylation without lowering enzyme abundance. Predict:

  1. PEPC disappears
  2. Malate sensitivity may restrict sustained fixation
  3. Rubisco becomes PEPC
  4. Vacuole disappears
Answer and explanation

B. Malate sensitivity may restrict sustained fixation Phosphorylation in many CAM plants reduces malate inhibition. Inhibiting it may restrict sustained flux.

Revision and active recall

CAM connects preliminary PEPC fixation, vacuolar acid storage, daytime CO₂ release and the Calvin cycle. Its advantages depend on gas exchange, acid capacity and energy balance.

  1. Explain the CO₂–water tradeoff at stomata.
  2. Trace carbon from PEP to vacuolar malate.
  3. Distinguish CO₂ and bicarbonate in PEPC fixation.
  4. Compare tonoplast and thylakoid gradients.
  5. Explain low external uptake with active daytime Rubisco.
  6. Draw four phases and explain loss of Phase IV under drought.
  7. Distinguish facultative and constitutive CAM.
  8. Distinguish CAM cycling and idling.
  9. Compare C3, conventional C4 and CAM.
  10. Integrate time-resolved carbon flux.
  11. Explain limitations of carbon isotope signatures.
  12. Distinguish water-use efficiency from total productivity.

Mastery checklist

  • Trace carbon through both periods.
  • Explain four CAM phases and variable expression.
  • Interpret gas exchange, acidity and isotope evidence.
  • Calculate integrated flux and water-use efficiency.
  • Relate CO₂ concentration to oxygenation and energy demand.
