1. Why the chemistry of sugars matters

Imagine three containers containing glucose, cotton fibre and glycogen extracted from liver. Glucose dissolves rapidly; cotton remains a tough fibre; glycogen forms a hydrated reserve that cells can mobilise rapidly. All three are built from the same basic chemical vocabulary, yet their behaviour is strikingly different. The explanation is not the presence of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen alone. It is the arrangement of those atoms in three dimensions, the way sugars connect, and the way the resulting chains interact with water, enzymes and each other.

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that yield such units upon hydrolysis, including biologically important derivatives. Many simple neutral sugars have the empirical composition (CH₂O)n, but this is a useful historical pattern, not a definition. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen than ribose; amino sugars contain nitrogen; oxidised sugars contain carboxyl groups; some glycans carry sulfate or phosphate. Conversely, formaldehyde and acetic acid fit the empirical ratio without being carbohydrates. The decisive feature is chemical structure, not a ratio of atoms.

A carbohydrate can function as a fuel, an activated biosynthetic precursor, an osmotic solute, a structural material, a molecular recognition signal or a hydrated extracellular scaffold. These roles frequently coexist: a ribose residue is part of ATP and RNA; a glucose residue can become cellulose, glycogen or a glycoprotein. In this chapter we follow a logical path from the individual carbonyl and hydroxyl groups to stereochemistry, rings, glycosidic bonds, large polymers and cell-surface information.

Learning outcomes

Predict stereoisomers and ring configurations; explain mutarotation and reducing behaviour mechanistically; decode carbohydrate names and linkages; compare storage and structural polymers; explain glycan recognition and glycoconjugates; interpret quantitative and experimental questions at CSIR-NET Part B and Part C level. Detailed glycolysis and carbohydrate metabolism belong to later metabolic nodes; this lesson establishes their chemical foundation.

2. The molecular vocabulary: classification and functional groups

In an open-chain monosaccharide, one carbon is a carbonyl carbon and most other carbons carry hydroxyl groups. If the carbonyl lies at the end of the chain, the sugar is an aldose; if it lies within the chain, typically at C2, it is a ketose. Carbon atoms are numbered from the end nearest the carbonyl. Aldoses consequently have C1 as the aldehyde carbon, whereas common 2-ketoses have C2 as the ketone carbon. This numbering persists when the chain becomes cyclic.

Glucose and fructose are constitutional isomers: both contain six carbon atoms, but differ in carbonyl position and atom connectivity. Glucose and galactose, in contrast, have the same connectivity but differ in three-dimensional configuration. A disaccharide consists of two monosaccharide residues joined covalently; an oligosaccharide is a short chain, often described as roughly 3–10 or up to about 20 residues depending on convention. A polysaccharide has many residues. These boundaries are descriptive rather than universal molecular thresholds.

Homopolysaccharides contain one principal monosaccharide type; heteropolysaccharides contain two or more. This distinction is independent of branching: a homopolymer may be linear (cellulose) or branched (glycogen). A polysaccharide can be chemically modified after assembly, for example by acetylation or sulfation. The word glycan broadly describes a carbohydrate chain, whether free or attached to another molecule. A glycoconjugate contains a glycan covalently attached to a noncarbohydrate moiety such as protein or lipid.

3. Stereochemistry: why the direction of one OH group changes biology

A tetrahedral carbon bonded to four distinguishable substituents can be a stereogenic centre. In glyceraldehyde, C2 is attached to H, OH, CHO and CH₂OH. Its two configurations are non-superimposable mirror images, called enantiomers. The Fischer projection is a convention for displaying the three-dimensional configuration on paper: horizontal bonds project towards the observer; vertical bonds project away. Rotating a Fischer projection 180° in its plane preserves configuration, whereas rotating it 90° and interpreting it as a new Fischer projection does not.

D/L nomenclature compares a sugar's highest-numbered stereogenic carbon with the reference configuration of glyceraldehyde. In a conventional Fischer projection with the most oxidised carbon at the top, OH on the right at this reference carbon indicates D; OH on the left indicates L. For an aldohexose the reference is C5, not C1 or C2. D and L do not describe whether a compound rotates plane-polarised light clockwise or counterclockwise. That experimentally measured property is denoted (+) or (−). D-fructose is a familiar counterexample to equating D with dextrorotation.

The R/S system assigns priorities to substituents using the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules. D/L is a relative reference convention for a family of molecules, while R/S specifies the absolute configuration of an individual centre. Do not assume every stereocentre in a D-sugar is R. Indeed, a D-sugar can contain both R and S centres. Changing one OH at a centre that is not the D/L reference may produce another D-sugar.

For an ordinary acyclic aldose with n carbons, the terminal CHO and CH₂OH are not stereogenic, leaving n−2 potential stereocentres. If they are independent and no symmetry reduces the total, the maximum number of stereoisomers is 2n−2. An acyclic 2-ketose has n−3 such centres: the carbonyl carbon and both terminal CH₂OH carbons are excluded. Thus aldohexoses have 16 configurations, eight D and eight L; ketohexoses have eight, four D and four L. Dihydroxyacetone has no stereocentre and is achiral; it is not assigned D or L.

Worked example

An aldopentose has stereocentres at C2, C3 and C4. It has 2³ = 8 acyclic configurations, four D and four L. Cyclisation introduces an additional anomeric centre, but counting ring forms requires specifying ring size and configuration rather than simply doubling the number of all molecular species.

In an achiral solvent, enantiomers have identical ordinary equilibrium properties except for opposite optical rotations and interactions with chiral environments. Diastereomers differ in properties even in achiral surroundings. Enzymes, transporters and lectins are chiral molecular surfaces: a changed OH orientation can lose a hydrogen bond, create a steric clash or reposition the substrate for catalysis. Stereochemical specificity is therefore not memorisation trivia; it governs biological selection.

4. The aldose family: enantiomers, epimers and isomer comparisons

Two stereoisomers that are not mirror images are diastereomers. Epimers are a special class of diastereomers that differ at precisely one stereogenic centre. For example, D-glucose and D-mannose differ at C2. D-glucose and D-galactose differ at C4. D-mannose and D-galactose differ at both C2 and C4, so are not epimers. The distinction must be made between corresponding molecular forms: compare open chains to open chains, or cyclic compounds with corresponding ring size and anomeric configuration.

In metabolism, epimerases catalyse inversion at a specified stereocentre. UDP-galactose 4-epimerase interconverts UDP-glucose and UDP-galactose through reversible oxidation and reduction of C4 with a tightly bound NAD cofactor. The transient carbonyl is planar, so reduction from the opposite face changes configuration. The net reaction exchanges stereochemistry without net consumption of NAD. This is a useful chemical model of how enzymes can rearrange a molecule without changing its molecular formula.

Do not confuse

Glucose versus fructose: constitutional isomers. D-glucose versus L-glucose: enantiomers. D-glucose versus D-mannose: C2 epimers. α-D-glucopyranose versus β-D-glucopyranose: anomers, a special type of epimer formed at the anomeric centre.

5. From an open chain to a ring: hemiacetals and hemiketals

The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic because the oxygen withdraws electron density. An alcohol oxygen can donate a lone pair to this carbon, while the carbonyl π electrons move towards oxygen. After proton transfers, an aldehyde plus one alcohol yields a hemiacetal; a ketone plus one alcohol yields a hemiketal. The former carbonyl carbon now bears an OH and an OR group. If the alcohol and carbonyl are parts of the same molecule, the reaction is intramolecular and a ring forms without net loss of water.

In D-glucose, the C5 hydroxyl oxygen commonly attacks C1. The resulting ring contains C1–C5 plus the oxygen of the former C5 hydroxyl, so it has six ring atoms and is called glucopyranose. The C6 group remains outside the ring. If the C4 hydroxyl attacks C1, the product has five ring atoms and is glucofuranose. The former carbonyl carbon C1 becomes the anomeric centre; since the carbonyl was planar, attack can lead to either of two configurations. For fructose, C2 is the carbonyl and anomeric centre; C5 OH attack gives fructofuranose, C6 OH attack fructopyranose. D-fructose in water is not exclusively furanose; fructofuranose is especially important in sucrose.

The names pyranose and furanose identify ring size, not total carbon number. A pentose such as ribose can form a six-membered pyranose as well as a five-membered furanose. A six-carbon ketose can form a five-membered ring. The preferred population reflects steric interactions, solvent effects and conformational free energy; the dominant free solution ring is not necessarily the ring selected by a particular enzyme or found in a glycosidic linkage.

A full acetal forms when the hemiacetal anomeric OH is replaced by an OR group. Unlike a free hemiacetal, an acetal does not readily reopen in neutral water to regenerate the carbonyl. This chemical distinction explains why glycosidic bond formation can eliminate reducing behaviour. It is also important to distinguish cyclisation, which involves no net water loss, from the formal condensation equation used for glycoside formation.

6. Reading and drawing Haworth projections

Haworth projections show a ring in simplified perspective, often with the ring oxygen at the upper right and the anomeric carbon at the right. For the conventional D-glucopyranose drawing, C1 is on the right, followed clockwise by C2, C3, C4 and C5; the ring oxygen connects C5 to C1. C6 is the CH₂OH group attached to C5 and points up. The OH groups that appear on the right of the standard Fischer projection appear down at C2–C4; groups on the left appear up. This gives glucose the C2 down, C3 up, C4 down sequence.

Anomers differ at the new anomeric stereocentre. In conventional Haworth drawings of D-glucopyranose, α has anomeric OH down, opposite the C5-linked CH₂OH group; β has it up, on the same side. The α/β convention relates the anomeric configuration to the anomeric reference atom, not to an arbitrary vertical direction. For the corresponding L-glucopyranose drawings the reference CH₂OH is down, α-OH up and β-OH down. Therefore “alpha always down” is false. The same-side/opposite-side shortcut works for these standard drawings but must be applied carefully to unusual ring structures and orientations.

To draw β-D-galactopyranose, retain the glucose drawing but turn the C4 OH up. To draw β-D-mannopyranose, turn C2 OH up. To draw an α-anomer from the β-anomer, change only the anomeric OH. If multiple nonanomeric substituents change, one has changed the underlying sugar as well. When a figure is rotated, read the actual relative positions and carbon numbering rather than blindly applying up/down rules.

7. Beyond flat rings: chair, boat and furanose puckering

A six-membered sugar ring is not planar. A hypothetical flat hexagon would bring bonds into eclipsed arrangements and strain bond angles. Pyranoses predominantly adopt chair conformations, where substituents are axial (roughly parallel to a vertical axis) or equatorial (directed outward around the ring). Chair inversion exchanges axial and equatorial positions while preserving whether a substituent lies on the upper or lower face of the ring. It therefore does not turn α into β. Boat and skew-boat conformations are accessible but often higher in energy, and can be important in enzyme-bound states.

In the usual 4C1 chair of β-D-glucopyranose, the OH groups at C1–C4 and the CH₂OH at C5 are all equatorial. This limits 1,3-diaxial interactions and helps explain its stability. α-D-glucopyranose in the same chair has an axial anomeric OH, whereas D-mannose has an axial C2 OH and D-galactose an axial C4 OH in their corresponding standard chairs. Each axial group can interact sterically with axial groups two carbons away. Solvent and electronic effects modify these energetic costs, so counting axial groups is a starting heuristic, not a universal quantitative law.

The anomeric effect is a stereoelectronic tendency in many heterocyclic acetals for an electronegative substituent at the anomeric carbon to adopt an orientation that may be axial despite steric cost. A common orbital description is donation from a lone pair on the ring oxygen into the antibonding orbital of the exocyclic C1–X bond when geometry is favourable. Electrostatic interactions and solvent also contribute. The effect is not a rule that all α sugars must dominate: aqueous glucose favours β because the total free energy, including steric and hydration effects, favours the equatorial configuration. Glycosidic torsion preferences also reflect related exo-anomeric effects.

Five-membered furanose rings adopt envelope or twist conformations and interconvert by pseudorotation. In nucleic acids, ribose ring puckering helps control backbone geometry: C3′-endo is typical of A-form RNA and A-DNA, while C2′-endo is typical of B-DNA. This is a direct bridge to later nodes on nucleotide and nucleic acid structure. It also demonstrates that a Haworth drawing captures configuration but cannot convey all conformational dynamics.

8. Mutarotation: an experiment that reveals ring opening

Dissolve pure crystalline α-D-glucose in water and measure its optical rotation. Initially it is about +112°. Over time it approaches about +52.7° under customary measurement conditions. Starting with β-D-glucose gives about +18.7° and the same final value. The changing rotation is mutarotation. It is not a conversion between D and L glucose: the configurations at the original stereocentres remain unchanged. The ring temporarily opens, restores the planar aldehyde, then recloses to either anomer.

In the simple two-anomer approximation, if fα is the mole fraction of α and fβ = 1−fα, the observed specific rotation is the weighted sum: [α]mix = fα[α]α + fβ[α]β. Using +112.2°, +18.7° and +52.7° gives fα = (52.7−18.7)/(112.2−18.7) = 0.364, and fβ ≈ 0.636. Real aqueous glucose includes small amounts of open-chain and minor cyclic forms, so these are approximate populations. Temperature, solvent and measurement wavelength affect the values.

For an equilibrium β/α ratio of 0.636/0.364 ≈ 1.75, the standard free-energy difference for α → β is ΔG° = −RT ln K. At 298 K, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, giving about −1.39 kJ mol⁻¹. A modest energy difference can significantly bias an equilibrium without eliminating the less favoured state. This connects directly to node 1.B.01. An enzyme called aldose 1-epimerase (mutarotase) speeds interconversion without changing its equilibrium position.

If a polarimeter uses a path length l in decimetres and concentration c in g mL⁻¹, the conventional specific rotation is [α] = αobs/(lc), with temperature and wavelength specified. A changing signal is not by itself proof of mutarotation; hydrolysis, decomposition or contamination could also change optical rotation. Compare purified starting anomers, mass balance and complementary structural measurements to support the interpretation.

Key prediction

A free anomeric hemiacetal can mutarotate if ring opening is accessible. A methyl glucoside, whose anomeric centre is an acetal, does not mutarotate under ordinary nonhydrolysing conditions. Maltose and lactose can mutarotate at their free reducing end; intact sucrose cannot.

9. Reducing sugars: chemistry rather than a memorised list

A reducing sugar can reduce certain mild oxidising agents. For an aldose, the open-chain aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylate (or aldonic acid after acidification), while the oxidant is reduced. A sugar in water may be overwhelmingly cyclic, yet still reacts because ring opening continually replenishes a small aldehyde population as it is consumed. In a glycoside, the donor anomeric centre is locked as an acetal or ketal, so that particular end cannot regenerate a carbonyl without cleavage.

Benedict's and Fehling's reagents contain alkaline complexed Cu²⁺. Reducing sugars generate Cu⁺, which can precipitate as Cu₂O. Benedict's uses citrate to help keep copper soluble; Fehling's uses tartrate. Tollens' reagent contains a silver(I) ammine complex that can be reduced to metallic silver. These tests detect reducing capacity, not a unique glucose molecule. Colour and precipitate depend on conditions and are not a precise concentration measurement without calibration. Other reducing compounds can interfere.

Why does fructose give a positive result although it is a ketose? In alkaline solution it undergoes reversible enediol/enediolate formation and aldose–ketose isomerisation, producing species including glucose and mannose. The aldehyde-containing products can react with the oxidising reagent. This is the Lobry de Bruyn–Alberda van Ekenstein transformation. Under milder acidic conditions, bromine water can oxidise aldoses preferentially compared with ordinary ketoses; reagent conditions therefore matter.

In a disaccharide, inspect both anomeric centres. If one is free, the molecule is reducing even though the other is engaged in a glycosidic bond. In an ordinary connected, acyclic polysaccharide there is at most one reducing end, although branching produces multiple nonreducing ends. Some biological polymers have their nominal reducing end capped or attached to a protein, as in glycogen associated with glycogenin. A reducing-end count is not equal to the number of residues.

Sucrose contains glucose C1 linked to fructose C2, so neither can open independently. Hydrolysis releases glucose and fructose, both of which reduce Benedict's reagent. Trehalose similarly connects both glucose anomeric carbons and is nonreducing. These examples show that the same monomers can yield opposite assay results depending on the linkage. A glycan can also have reducing groups capped by chemical derivatisation, which changes assay interpretation.

10. Sugar derivatives and chemical transformations

The hydroxyl and carbonyl groups provide chemical handles that cells modify in different ways. Reduction of the carbonyl produces a sugar alcohol (alditol). Oxidation at different ends produces chemically distinct sugar acids. Replacement of a hydroxyl group by hydrogen or an amino group creates deoxy or amino sugars. Covalent phosphate or sulfate groups add negative charge, affecting transport, binding and hydration. These are not peripheral exceptions; modified sugars build membranes, walls, nucleic acids and extracellular matrices.

Sorbitol illustrates the link between chemistry and physiology. Aldose reductase uses NADPH to reduce glucose to sorbitol, and sorbitol dehydrogenase can oxidise sorbitol to fructose using NAD⁺. If sorbitol accumulates faster than it is cleared, its osmotic effects may be significant in tissues. This does not mean every sugar alcohol causes the same cellular effects: permeability, enzyme expression and concentration determine the outcome.

Oxidising glucose C1 gives gluconate; oxidising C6 while retaining the anomeric chemistry gives glucuronate. Do not confuse glucuronic acid with gluconic acid simply because both are derived from glucose. Glucuronic acid residues contribute negative charge to glycans, and activated UDP-glucuronate donates glucuronyl groups in conjugation reactions. Many uronic acids exist in ring forms and retain an anomeric centre.

Phosphorylation helps retain sugars inside cells because charged sugar phosphates do not simply diffuse across lipid membranes. It also prepares them for subsequent chemistry. A phosphate on the anomeric carbon, as in glucose 1-phosphate, has different chemical connectivity from one on C6. A nucleotide sugar is an activated donor, not the same as a nucleotide containing ribose as its backbone. CMP-Neu5Ac uses a different nucleotide linkage from UDP-hexose donors, reflecting the unusual anomeric C2 of sialic acid.

Classical diagnostic reactions include osazone formation with phenylhydrazine: in common aldoses and 2-ketoses the reaction modifies the first two carbons. Glucose, fructose and mannose form the same osazone because the distinguishing configurations at those positions are erased and the remaining chain is shared. Seliwanoff's test exploits the faster acid dehydration of ketoses to hydroxymethylfurfural, followed by colour formation with resorcinol; prolonged heating reduces selectivity. These historical tests are conceptually useful but modern identification relies on complementary analytical methods.

11. Glycosidic bonds: a chemical language for molecular assembly

A glycosidic bond involves the anomeric carbon of a sugar and a nucleophile such as oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur or carbon. Most common carbohydrate polymers are O-glycosides. A methyl glucoside contains an anomeric C–O–CH₃ bond, while a nucleoside has an N-glycosidic bond between a sugar and a nitrogenous base. Some natural products have C-glycosidic linkages, with a direct carbon–carbon bond, which can have greater resistance to hydrolysis. The non-sugar portion of a glycoside is called the aglycone.

Write α-D-Glcp-(1→4)-D-Glcp to identify a donor α-D-glucopyranose whose C1 is joined through oxygen to C4 of the second glucose. The p denotes pyranose and f denotes furanose. A β-(1→4) bond has the same positions but different donor configuration. In sucrose, α-D-Glcp-(1↔2)-β-D-Fruf, the double arrow emphasises that both anomeric centres participate. The donor anomeric configuration is fixed in the intact glycoside; the free reducing residue may undergo mutarotation and need not be assigned a single α/β state in an abbreviated structure.

In textbooks, formation of a glycosidic bond is often written sugar–OH + HO–sugar → sugar–O–sugar + H₂O. That is useful net stoichiometry for unmodified monosaccharides, but cells generally do not directly condense two unactivated sugars in water. Glycosyltransferases transfer a sugar from an activated donor (frequently a nucleotide sugar) to an acceptor, releasing a leaving group. For example, UDP-glucose can donate glucosyl units to an elongating carbohydrate. Enzymes select the donor, acceptor, linkage position and anomeric outcome.

Glycosyltransferases can be inverting or retaining relative to the donor's anomeric configuration. Inverting enzymes commonly support a displacement mechanism in which attack occurs from the opposite face to the leaving group. Retaining enzymes use diverse mechanisms; a single mechanism must not be imposed on all of them. Glycoside hydrolases likewise include retaining and inverting classes, often using acid/base catalysis. Hydrolysis returns a hydroxyl at the linkage position and can restore a reducing end. Glycosidic linkages are stable enough for structural storage but can be selectively cleaved by the right catalyst.

Glycosidic bonds have conformational freedom around their component single bonds, commonly described by torsion angles φ and ψ (and additional ω for many 1→6 bonds). Steric contacts, hydrogen bonding and stereoelectronic effects favour some torsion combinations. A linkage change from α to β changes the local geometry, and repeated linkage changes produce entirely different polymer shapes. A change from 1→4 to 1→6 introduces another flexible bond and creates branching possibilities.

Residue mass calculation

An unmodified free hexose has molar mass about 180.16 g mol⁻¹. A covalently incorporated hexosyl residue contributes about 162.14 g mol⁻¹ (loss of H₂O in net condensation). For a connected, acyclic, unmodified chain of n hexoses, M = 180.16n − 18.015(n−1) ≈ 162.14n + 18.02 g mol⁻¹. A cyclic chain with n glycosidic bonds instead has approximately 162.14n g mol⁻¹.

All common unmodified hexose disaccharides have the same nominal formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, even when their linkages differ. Molecular formula alone cannot tell maltose from cellobiose or sucrose. Maltose and cellobiose are especially instructive: both contain two glucoses linked at 1→4, but the donor configuration differs. An α-glucosidase that readily cleaves maltose need not hydrolyse cellobiose. Recognition depends on the spatial orientation of the bond and substituents.

Sucrose hydrolysis is called inversion of cane sugar because the mixture's optical rotation changes from positive towards negative owing to the strong negative rotation of fructose. That reaction is hydrolysis, not mutarotation of intact sucrose. The resulting glucose and fructose themselves can interconvert among their solution forms. In plants, a nonreducing transport sugar is chemically useful because its anomeric centres are both engaged; its actual mobility depends on membrane transporters and source–sink gradients.

Trehalose contains two anomeric centres linked symmetrically. It is compatible with low-water and stress-protective environments in numerous organisms; hydrogen bonding and glass formation can help stabilise biological structures under some conditions. Raffinose-family oligosaccharides are built by adding galactosyl residues to sucrose. Humans generally lack digestive α-galactosidase activity sufficient to digest these in the small intestine; intestinal microbes can ferment them. These examples establish a general principle: chemical structure predicts potential cleavage, but biological outcome also depends on which enzymes are present.

13. Storage polysaccharides: starch and glycogen

Keeping thousands of separate glucose molecules in a cell would raise osmotic pressure. Joining them into large polymers greatly reduces the number of independently dissolved particles and provides a compact reserve. The ideal dilute approximation π = iCRT depends on particle concentration C, not total glucose-equivalent mass. Polymerisation does not eliminate hydration, crowding or nonideal behaviour, but it greatly changes osmotic accounting. Cells can mobilise the reserve by selectively cleaving terminal or internal linkages.

Starch: amylose and amylopectin

Starch is a plant storage material composed mainly of amylose and amylopectin, with proportions depending on species and tissue. Amylose is mostly linear α(1→4)-glucan, although slight branching can occur. Its α linkages favour curved helical conformations; iodine and iodide form polyiodide complexes within suitable helices, producing characteristic blue coloration. The intensity and wavelength depend on chain length, conformation, iodine conditions and composition. Amylopectin has α(1→4) chains connected by α(1→6) branch points, typically separated by tens of residues rather than one fixed interval. Its organisation into chain clusters contributes to semicrystalline starch granules.

Heating starch in water can disrupt ordered granules and cause gelatinisation, increasing hydration and changing accessibility to enzymes. Cooling may lead to retrogradation as chains reassociate. These physical changes illustrate that carbohydrate digestibility depends not only on the covalent linkage but also on supramolecular organisation, hydration and processing. α-amylase cleaves internal α(1→4) bonds, whereas β-amylase in plants and other organisms releases maltose from nonreducing ends and stops near α(1→6) branches. Debranching activities are needed to fully dismantle branched glucans.

Glycogen: rapid access to a reserve

Glycogen is an α(1→4)-linked glucan with α(1→6) branches, commonly described as branching about every 8–12 residues on average. This is more frequent than in typical amylopectin, but neither polymer has perfectly regular spacing. Glycogen is highly branched, compact and hydrated. Many chain ends provide parallel sites for glycogen synthase during elongation and glycogen phosphorylase during mobilisation. Phosphorylase releases glucose 1-phosphate from nonreducing ends by phosphorolysis of α(1→4) bonds. It stalls near branches; debranching enzyme transfers a short block of residues and hydrolyses an α(1→6) bond, yielding one free glucose at the branch.

Glycogenin can initiate a glycogen particle through covalent attachment of a glucosyl chain to a tyrosine residue. The nominal reducing end is thus often protein-associated. Glycogen synthase elongates existing nonreducing ends using UDP-glucose, while branching enzyme transfers a segment to form an α(1→6) linkage. Branching provides both increased accessibility and a compact architecture. Liver glycogen participates in systemic glucose homeostasis; skeletal muscle glycogen largely supports its own energy demands, a distinction developed in metabolism.

For a connected tree of glucose residues with each branch event generating exactly one additional chain end, the number of nonreducing ends equals one plus the number of branch points. This is a topology relation, not a precise estimate of the shape of a real crowded glycogen particle. For example, a simple branched glucan containing 49 α(1→6) branch bonds and one reducing-end direction has 50 nonreducing ends. The total number of glycosidic bonds remains n−1 if there are n residues and no cycles.

14. Structural polysaccharides and microbial diversity

Cellulose contains D-glucose linked through β(1→4) bonds. The geometry produces extended chains with successive residues approximately rotated 180° relative to each other; cellobiose is a convenient repeat unit. Intrachain and interchain hydrogen bonding, together with dispersion interactions and packing, stabilise cellulose microfibrils. Crystalline regions are particularly inaccessible to water and enzymes. Cellulose chains are not held together exclusively by hydrogen bonds, nor are the chains covalently cross-linked in ordinary cellulose itself.

Humans lack endogenous cellulases that hydrolyse cellulose efficiently, although microbial communities can degrade it to varying degrees. Cellulolytic systems often combine endoglucanases, processive cellobiohydrolases and β-glucosidases; oxidative accessory enzymes can help expose crystalline material. Starch and cellulose have the same nominal repeat formula, (C₆H₁₀O₅)n, but different stereochemical links, conformation and enzyme accessibility. This is one of the clearest illustrations of the structure–function rule.

Chitin is a β(1→4) polymer of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc). Its acetamido groups modify hydrogen-bonding and packing, helping form strong structures in arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. Chitosan is produced by partial or extensive deacetylation of chitin; its free amino groups can be protonated at acidic pH, giving a cationic polymer with changed solubility and binding properties. The degree of deacetylation and pH influence its behaviour.

Pectin and alginate demonstrate how charge creates material properties. Carboxylate groups coordinate cations such as calcium, forming junction zones between chains. Degree of esterification, sequence pattern and ionic conditions govern gel strength. Agarose is widely used in electrophoresis because its hydrated network provides pores for molecular sieving; changing polymer concentration changes typical pore size. These are practical laboratory consequences of glycan architecture rather than unrelated applications.

Peptidoglycan in bacterial walls is a composite: repeating β(1→4)-linked GlcNAc and N-acetylmuramic acid (MurNAc) sugars form chains, and peptide stems attached to MurNAc create cross-links between chains. Lysozyme hydrolyses glycosidic bonds in the glycan backbone; β-lactam antibiotics interfere with susceptible penicillin-binding proteins involved in peptide cross-linking, not by directly hydrolysing the sugar backbone. The two targets illustrate how a composite structure has distinct chemical vulnerabilities.

15. Glycosaminoglycans: hydration, charge and extracellular mechanics

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are long, generally unbranched carbohydrate chains with repeating disaccharide units. Most contain an amino sugar and a uronic acid; keratan sulfate is a key exception because its repeating unit includes galactose rather than a uronic acid. Many GAGs carry sulfate and carboxylate groups that create strongly negative charge. Counterions and water accumulate in their vicinity; together with a constrained extracellular network, this contributes to swelling pressure and resistance to compression.

Hyaluronan is unusually large and non-sulfated; its negative charge comes from glucuronate. It is synthesised at the plasma membrane by hyaluronan synthases and extruded into extracellular space. Many other GAGs are built on core proteins and modified within the secretory pathway. The polysaccharides should not be treated as perfectly regular chemical repeats, because sulfation, epimerisation and acetylation create sequence heterogeneity along their lengths.

A proteoglycan contains one or more GAG chains attached to a core protein. Aggrecan, for example, carries many chondroitin sulfate and keratan sulfate chains and forms large noncovalent assemblies on hyaluronan with link proteins. Water retention and electrostatic/osmotic effects resist compression, while the collagen network provides tensile constraint. Cartilage function therefore emerges from cooperation between proteins and carbohydrates, not from GAGs alone.

For chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate, heparan sulfate and heparin, attachment to suitable protein serine residues commonly uses a linkage region Xyl–Gal–Gal–GlcA (written from the protein outward). Keratan sulfate follows other linkage routes. A heparin chain is not uniformly anticoagulant merely because it is negatively charged: specific sulfation patterns generate an antithrombin-binding pentasaccharide, and chain length influences effects on different coagulation proteases. Thus exact carbohydrate sequence and modification matter as much as net charge.

16. Glycoproteins, glycolipids and the glycan information layer

Proteins and lipids can carry short or long glycans whose structures affect folding, trafficking, stability and recognition. Unlike DNA and proteins, glycans are typically not copied directly from a linear template. Their structures emerge from enzyme specificities, substrate availability, accessibility, compartmentalisation and processing time. A single protein site may consequently carry a distribution of glycan structures (microheterogeneity), and a site may be occupied or unoccupied (macroheterogeneity).

N-linked and O-linked protein glycans

In canonical eukaryotic N-glycosylation, a glycan is attached through GlcNAc to the amide nitrogen of an asparagine residue, commonly in an Asn–X–Ser/Thr motif, where X is not proline. The motif is necessary for most canonical sites but not sufficient: protein folding, accessibility and enzyme context matter, and rare noncanonical sites exist. A precursor oligosaccharide is assembled on dolichol-linked intermediates in the ER membrane, translocated to the luminal side during assembly and transferred by oligosaccharyltransferase. The commonly taught precursor in many eukaryotes is Glc₃Man₉GlcNAc₂; subsequent glucose and mannose trimming participates in folding quality control.

In the Golgi, mannosidases and glycosyltransferases remodel N-glycans. High-mannose, complex and hybrid forms share a conserved Man₃GlcNAc₂ core but differ in outer branches. Terminal fucose, galactose, GlcNAc and sialic acid modify recognition. N-linked glycans are not all identical, and an N-linked glycan does not mean that every linkage within the sugar chain is a nitrogen bond; most intersugar linkages are O-glycosidic.

Common mucin-type O-glycosylation begins with GalNAc attached to the hydroxyl oxygen of serine or threonine, frequently in the Golgi. Further sugars produce diverse core structures, branches and terminal modifications. O-glycosylation is broader than this: cytosolic and nuclear O-GlcNAc is a dynamic single GlcNAc modification on many proteins, often participating in signalling regulation; O-mannose, O-fucose and other classes also exist. Unlike many N-glycans, mucin-type O-glycans do not have a universal short sequon equivalent to Asn–X–Ser/Thr.

Glycolipids contain carbohydrate covalently linked to lipid. Glycosphingolipids are built on ceramide: cerebrosides contain a single neutral sugar, globosides contain neutral oligosaccharides, and gangliosides contain one or more sialic acid residues. Sulfatides carry sulfate. The glycan headgroups are typically exposed on the noncytosolic side of membranes and participate in molecular recognition. Full lipid chemistry and membrane organisation are covered in 1.B.02; here the emphasis is the sugar headgroup and how its structure is read by other molecules.

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors connect selected proteins to membranes through a conserved glycan-containing lipid anchor. This provides another example of a sugar-containing molecule linking a protein to membrane organisation. A protein may also carry both N- and O-glycans or multiple different glycan modifications. The term glycocalyx describes the carbohydrate-rich surface layer formed collectively by glycoproteins, glycolipids and proteoglycans.

17. Glycan recognition, biological specificity and glycation

Lectins are proteins that bind carbohydrate structures noncovalently with defined specificity. A single sugar–lectin interaction may be relatively weak, but multivalent presentation allows many contacts to cooperate, producing high functional avidity. This is different from high microscopic affinity at a single binding site. Glycan orientation, density, flexibility and spacing determine whether multivalent binding is geometrically possible. Enzymes that bind sugars are not automatically lectins in the usual functional sense.

ABO blood-group determinants are a classic example of the effect of a single terminal sugar. The H antigen contains a fucose-linked precursor structure. A-transferase adds terminal GalNAc, B-transferase adds terminal galactose, and common O alleles encode inactive transferase, leaving H antigen largely unchanged. The Bombay phenotype illustrates the need for precursor synthesis: lack of functional FUT1 in relevant erythroid cells prevents normal H antigen formation on red cells, so typical A/B structures cannot be formed there even when an A or B allele is present. These glycans occur on proteins and lipids; the blood-group phenotype is not simply a difference in the primary sequence of one red-cell surface protein.

Selectins mediate aspects of leukocyte rolling through recognition of specific glycan motifs such as sialyl Lewis X in suitable contexts. Terminal sialic acid can alter receptor binding and glycoprotein clearance; loss of sialic acid from some circulating glycoproteins exposes galactose and can increase recognition by hepatic asialoglycoprotein receptors. Lysosomal enzyme targeting frequently uses mannose 6-phosphate on N-glycans; defective tagging can misroute many hydrolases. Each example demonstrates an information-bearing carbohydrate modification interpreted by a binding protein.

Glycosylation is enzyme-mediated transfer of sugars to defined acceptors, whereas glycation commonly begins nonenzymatically when a reducing sugar carbonyl reacts with an amino group on a protein or other molecule. A reversible Schiff base can rearrange to a more stable Amadori product for glucose-derived aldose glycation; ketoses can form related Heyns products. Further reactions may generate advanced glycation end products. HbA1c measures a stable glucose-derived modification, principally at the N-terminal valine of haemoglobin β chains, and reflects glycaemic exposure over the life of circulating red cells, subject to factors affecting red-cell turnover. It is not the product of a glycosyltransferase adding a standard glycan.

The enormous diversity of glycans can be understood combinatorially. A protein chain has one conventional backbone linkage direction; a sugar can offer multiple acceptor hydroxyls, two anomeric configurations, several ring sizes and multiple branch positions. Even two identical glucose units can form multiple distinct disaccharides. Sequence alone does not specify a glycan without linkage and configuration. This is the chemical basis of glycobiology and why glycan analysis needs more than a list of monosaccharides.

18. How carbohydrate structures are measured and inferred

No single method establishes every feature of a glycan. A good experimental strategy separates questions of composition, linkage, configuration, sequence, branching and three-dimensional conformation. Hydrolysis followed by chromatography or mass spectrometry identifies constituent residues but destroys much of the linkage information. Acid hydrolysis may destroy some labile sugars or alter modifications, so conditions and recovery standards are important. A total hexose peak does not distinguish glucose from galactose without an appropriate separation or derivatisation.

In methylation analysis, initially free hydroxyl groups are methylated. After hydrolysis, previously linkage-substituted hydroxyls become exposed. Subsequent reduction, acetylation and GC-MS of partially methylated alditol acetates can reveal which carbon positions were involved in linkages. A terminal glucosyl residue, for example, typically yields a derivative corresponding to methylation at O2, O3, O4 and O6; a 4-linked internal glucose lacks methylation at O4; a 4,6-linked branch residue lacks methylation at O4 and O6. The interpretation assumes complete methylation and appropriate derivatisation. It does not by itself tell whether the original linkage was α or β, nor the entire order of residues.

In the usual 4C1 chair of glucopyranose, β has axial H1 and axial H2; their approximately antiperiplanar relationship produces a relatively large vicinal J1,2 coupling, often around 7–9 Hz. α has equatorial H1 and axial H2, giving a smaller coupling, often around 3–4 Hz. This is an application of the Karplus relationship between vicinal coupling and dihedral angle. The same simple numerical rule cannot be applied blindly to mannose, other chairs or furanoses because their H1/H2 geometry differs.

Suppose two glucose polymers yield only glucose after acid hydrolysis. Both show predominantly 4-linked glucose in linkage analysis. One is cleaved by α-amylase, the other by cellulase. The combination of assays supports an α(1→4)-glucan versus a β(1→4)-glucan. If the first polymer also yields 4,6-linked residues, it is branched. A careful experiment includes substrate controls, enzyme-free controls and suitable time courses; absence of hydrolysis alone may result from insolubility or enzyme inactivation rather than linkage configuration.

In mass spectrometry, an addition of a neutral hexosyl residue commonly changes mass by about 162.0528 Da (monoisotopic), a HexNAc residue by 203.0794 Da, a deoxyhexose residue by 146.0579 Da, and Neu5Ac residue by 291.0954 Da. These are residue mass increments, not average molar masses or definitive structural assignments. Adducts, charge states, derivatisation and losses must be accounted for. Glucose, galactose and mannose all give the same unmodified hexose residue mass; isomer identification needs linkage, separation or spectroscopic evidence.

19. Quantitative reasoning and integrated worked examples

Example A: branching and bonds

An idealised acyclic glucan contains 1,001 glucose residues, 60 of its links being α(1→6) branch bonds. There are 1,000 total glycosidic bonds. The remaining 940 are α(1→4). If every branch produces one additional terminal chain end, there are 61 nonreducing ends. The mass is approximately 162.14 × 1,001 + 18.02 = 162,320 g mol⁻¹. A linear chain of the same residue count has the same nominal mass but only one nonreducing end. Mass alone cannot distinguish branching.

Example B: osmotic consequences of storage

Consider 0.100 mol L⁻¹ glucose equivalents either as free glucose or as ideal chains of exactly 1,000 residues. The free glucose particle concentration is 0.100 M; polymer particle concentration is 0.000100 M. At 310 K, using R = 0.08206 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and i = 1, the ideal osmotic pressures are about 2.54 atm and 0.00254 atm respectively. The thousandfold difference explains the physical logic of polymer storage; real macromolecular solutions deviate from the ideal model, particularly at high concentrations.

Example C: solving a polarimetry mixture

A mixture contains only α and β D-glucopyranose at the instant of measurement. Suppose its specific rotation is +65.45°, with reference rotations +112.2° and +18.7°. The α fraction is (65.45−18.7)/93.5 = 0.50. The β fraction is 0.50. If the solution is allowed to equilibrate at customary conditions, its rotation will approach +52.7°, indicating an increase in β fraction. The instantaneous measurement matters because mutarotation continues during observation.

Example D: hydrolysis reveals reducing ends

A connected linear maltooligosaccharide has 12 glucose residues and one reducing end. An endohydrolase cuts three distinct internal bonds, creating four chains, each with one free reducing end if no end is blocked. The residue total remains 12, but reducing-end count rises from one to four. Complete hydrolysis produces 12 free glucose molecules. This is why an assay of reducing ends can estimate bond cleavage even when total carbohydrate mass barely changes apart from added water.

Example E: infer a branch frequency

Linkage analysis of an ideal glucan reports 100 terminal, 801 exclusively 4-linked and 99 4,6-linked residues. The 1,000 residues include 99 branch residues, consistent with 100 nonreducing ends for an acyclic singly branching structure. The reducing-end residue may be counted in the 4-linked class depending on sample treatment; check how the analytical protocol treats it. The branch fraction is 99/1,000 = 9.9%; the average number of residues per terminal chain is roughly ten, not proof of equal branch spacing.

Example F: glycan precursor dependence

A cell expresses normal B-transferase but fails to make its H precursor at the erythrocyte surface. Adding B-transferase alone cannot restore B antigen because the acceptor is missing. Providing or restoring the correct precursor synthesis should enable B-transferase activity if donor availability and localisation are normal. This is a general biochemical lesson: a downstream enzyme may be catalytically normal but produce no product when an upstream substrate is absent.

A rapid strategy for unfamiliar structures is to number the carbon atoms, identify the carbonyl-derived anomeric centre, establish D/L using the reference stereocentre, determine ring size, then identify α/β and the acceptor carbon. Ask which anomeric centres remain free. For polymers, count chain ends and branch points before predicting enzyme susceptibility. Finally, distinguish chemical potential from biological accessibility: a bond can be thermodynamically hydrolysable yet persist because the activation barrier is high or the required enzyme is absent.

Carbohydrates connect every chemical foundation already studied in Unit 1. Atomic bonding explains carbonyl electrophilicity and stereochemical geometry. Water and hydrogen bonding explain solvation and hydrated matrices. Kinetics explains mutarotation rates and enzyme specificity. Thermodynamics explains equilibrium anomer populations and polymer conformations. The next node transfers these principles to fatty acids, amphipathic lipids and membranes, while later nucleic-acid nodes use sugar conformation to explain backbone organisation.

  1. Many nonreducing ends associated with branching
  2. Each branch creates two reducing ends
  3. All bonds are β(1→4)
  4. The exact sequence is fully known
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. In a large singly branching tree terminal and branch counts differ by only one; linkage analysis alone does not assign α/β.

28. Part C · A mutation eliminates FUT1-dependent erythrocyte H antigen synthesis, but A-transferase is normal. Expected result?

  1. Normal A antigen
  2. Increased B antigen
  3. Failure of normal A antigen formation on those cells
  4. A-transferase becomes a hydrolase
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The required H acceptor is missing; enzyme activity alone cannot bypass the upstream step.

29. Part C · A patient protein shows a glucose-derived Amadori modification at an amino group. What process is indicated?

  1. N-linked glycosylation
  2. O-linked glycosylation
  3. Nonenzymatic glycation
  4. GAG polymerisation
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Reducing-carbonyl chemistry with amino groups produces glycation products.

30. Part C · A cartilage matrix loses much of its sulfated GAG content while collagen remains. Which property is most directly affected?

  1. Hydrated swelling and compressive resistance
  2. DNA base pairing
  3. Membrane phospholipid synthesis
  4. All peptide bond formation
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. GAG charge and hydration support swelling pressure and resistance to compression.

31. Part C · A 0.2 M glucose-equivalent solution is polymerised into ideal 200-residue chains without changing volume. Approximate osmotic particle concentration?

  1. 0.2 M
  2. 0.001 M
  3. 40 M
  4. 0.1 M
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 0.2/200 = 0.001 M. This is an ideal approximation.

32. Part C · A glycosidase treatment removes terminal sialic acid from a glycoprotein and exposes galactose. What can follow in a suitable mammalian system?

  1. Altered receptor recognition and potentially faster hepatic clearance
  2. The peptide sequence changes
  3. All N-linked glycans detach
  4. Galactose becomes fructose
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Terminal sugar exposure can alter lectin/receptor binding, including hepatic asialoglycoprotein receptor recognition.

33. Part C · A sugar derivative no longer mutarotates but gives a free reducing sugar after mild acid hydrolysis. What modification best fits?

  1. C1 methyl glycoside
  2. C6 oxidation alone
  3. C2 epimerisation alone
  4. Chair flip
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Anomeric acetal formation blocks ring opening; acid hydrolysis can regenerate the hemiacetal.
