1. Carbon dioxide as a product of living metabolism

An animal can survive only while its cells continually exchange matter with their surroundings. Nutrients provide carbon-containing molecules from which cells extract usable energy. In aerobic metabolism, much of the carbon eventually leaves the cell as carbon dioxide. An adult mammal at rest produces carbon dioxide every minute, including between breaths. Yet the concentration of carbon dioxide in arterial blood remains within a relatively narrow range. This apparent contradiction is resolved by treating the body as an open system: continuous production is normally balanced by continuous removal. The purpose of respiratory carbon dioxide transport is therefore not merely to move a gas. It is to connect metabolic activity, tissue perfusion, chemical buffering and gas exchange in a way that prevents progressive accumulation of acid.

Carbon dioxide is produced when carbon atoms are removed from metabolic intermediates in decarboxylation reactions. Pyruvate dehydrogenase converts pyruvate into an acetyl group attached to coenzyme A and releases carbon dioxide. Within the citric acid cycle, oxidative decarboxylations at isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase provide additional carbon dioxide. Molecular oxygen is consumed mainly at the end of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, where it is reduced to water; it is not simply combined directly with all the carbon atoms of glucose to form carbon dioxide in one reaction. Distinguishing these biochemical processes prevents the common error of assuming that oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output must always be numerically identical.

For complete oxidation of glucose the overall equation is C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O. The ratio of carbon dioxide production to oxygen consumption under these conditions is one. Complete oxidation of palmitic acid gives 16 molecules of carbon dioxide for every 23 molecules of oxygen consumed, yielding approximately 0.70. This difference occurs because the fatty acid is more reduced than carbohydrate. The respiratory quotient, RQ, is a ratio of metabolic gas fluxes under defined conditions. A respiratory exchange ratio measured at the mouth is equal to the metabolic quotient only when relevant body gas stores are stable. During transient changes in ventilation or buffering, carbon dioxide output at the mouth can differ from current metabolic carbon dioxide generation. This distinction is useful for interpreting exercise experiments without assuming that every transient rise in expired gas represents a change in substrate oxidation.

Carbon dioxide is sometimes described solely as a waste product. The word waste refers to the need to remove the net metabolic excess, not to the biological uselessness of the molecule. Bicarbonate derived from carbon dioxide participates in buffering, epithelial transport and metabolic reactions. Carbon dioxide itself contributes to control of breathing through changes in hydrogen ion concentration. A physiological system must therefore maintain an appropriate amount rather than eliminate every molecule. The normal arterial partial pressure is a regulated outcome of the balance between metabolism and alveolar ventilation. In a steady state, a higher rate of production can coexist with an unchanged arterial partial pressure if ventilation rises proportionally.

Carbon dioxide leaving a cell enters interstitial fluid and then a nearby capillary. It is an uncharged molecule and can diffuse across lipid membranes; the direction of net diffusion follows its chemical potential gradient, conveniently represented by partial pressure for dissolved gas under comparable conditions. Carbon dioxide may also be converted into bicarbonate inside tissues. This chapter follows the dominant blood transport mechanisms in mammals; a cell need not wait for carbon dioxide to be carried away in its original molecular form. By coupling diffusion to chemical conversion, blood can receive far more carbon dioxide than would be accommodated as dissolved molecular gas at the same pressure.

A useful conceptual distinction is between production, transport and elimination. Production is an intracellular chemical flux. Transport is a circulatory flux between organs, with chemical transformations along the path. Elimination is a gas flux across the respiratory surface and eventually to the environment. At steady state, whole-body production and elimination are equal even though the quantities stored in each intermediate compartment can differ greatly. A brief obstruction to removal initially increases stores rather than instantly preventing production. When ventilation resumes, elimination can temporarily exceed production as the excess stored carbon dioxide is cleared. Thus a gas measurement at one moment cannot reveal the entire metabolic history without information about storage and flow.

2. Partial pressure, solubility and content

Three quantities must be kept separate in any discussion of gas carriage. Partial pressure expresses the tendency of a gas to move between phases at equilibrium. Concentration of dissolved molecular carbon dioxide describes the quantity of unreacted gas per unit volume of fluid. Total carbon dioxide content includes carbon present in chemically transformed forms as well as molecular gas. Unlike partial pressure, content depends strongly on the chemical composition of the blood. Two blood samples at identical carbon dioxide partial pressure can therefore contain unequal amounts of total carbon dioxide. The difference is fundamental to understanding the Haldane effect.

At a fixed temperature, Henry's law relates the dissolved concentration of molecular carbon dioxide to its partial pressure: C = αP. For plasma near 37 °C, a commonly used approximation is α = 0.030 mmol L⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹. If PCO₂ is 40 mmHg, the dissolved concentration is approximately 1.20 mmol L⁻¹. At 46 mmHg it is approximately 1.38 mmol L⁻¹. The six millimetre increase in pressure therefore adds only 0.18 mmol of dissolved molecular gas per litre under the same conditions. This calculation applies to the dissolved component, not to the total carbon dioxide transported in plasma and erythrocytes. Temperature, solvent and conventions for defining dissolved carbon dioxide influence the coefficient.

The carbon dioxide gradient from mixed venous blood to alveolar gas is small in pressure units compared with the gradient that oxygen initially experiences in the opposite direction. Nevertheless, healthy lungs remove carbon dioxide effectively. Carbon dioxide is appreciably more soluble than oxygen in biological fluids; its permeability through the respiratory membrane is consequently high. Fick's diffusion principle relates transport rate to exchange area, barrier thickness, diffusivity and the appropriate gradient. A small pressure difference can produce adequate flux when the effective diffusibility is large. This explains why diffusion limitation of carbon dioxide is usually less prominent than oxygen diffusion limitation across the same lung barrier, although severe abnormalities can compromise both gases.

Partial pressure is an intensive variable, whereas total quantity depends on volume. If two equal-pressure samples at the same temperature differ only in volume, their dissolved concentration is the same but their dissolved amounts differ. If the samples differ in haemoglobin or bicarbonate concentration, their total carbon dioxide concentration can also differ. Consider a thought experiment in which a solution containing no buffer and a blood sample are equilibrated with gas at PCO₂ = 40 mmHg. Both have a molecular gas concentration determined approximately by their solubility coefficients. Blood additionally binds carbon dioxide to protein groups and converts carbon dioxide to bicarbonate. Gas continues entering until chemical and physical equilibrium is reached. The gas source supplies the extra carbon; the conversions do not create carbon out of nothing.

A resting human's arterial PCO₂ is often represented as 40 mmHg and mixed venous PCO₂ as about 46 mmHg. These are illustrative values, not constants for every person or tissue. The blood leaving a highly active muscle can have a different pressure from the mixed venous blood entering the lungs, because venous samples from different organs combine. Local tissue gradients are determined by metabolic production, capillary supply and interstitial diffusion. An organ with high carbon dioxide generation relative to blood flow can require a larger increase in blood carbon dioxide content per unit volume. If perfusion rises at the same time as metabolic activity, the local gradient need not increase in the same proportion.

Gas volume units require explicit conventions. A volume of carbon dioxide at standard temperature and pressure is not numerically identical to the volume occupied by the same molecules in warm humid gas. For chemical concentrations, millimoles express the number of molecules without requiring a gas reference temperature or pressure. Classical blood gas content is often expressed as millilitres of gas per decilitre of blood under specified reference conditions. At standard dry conditions, one millimole of ideal gas occupies about 22.4 mL; hence 1 mmol L⁻¹ corresponds to approximately 2.24 mL dL⁻¹. One should not insert mmol L⁻¹ values into an equation expecting mL dL⁻¹ without conversion. The same caution applies to the alveolar ventilation relationship developed later.

3. Three routes of carbon dioxide carriage

In blood, carbon dioxide is distributed among three major forms: physically dissolved molecular CO₂, bicarbonate ion and carbamino compounds. These pools exchange carbon with one another and are not independent transport circuits. A carbon atom that enters an erythrocyte as molecular carbon dioxide may spend most of its circulatory journey in plasma as bicarbonate and become molecular carbon dioxide again in the pulmonary capillaries. The name of the transport form describes the chemical state at a particular point, not an irreversible destination.

The dissolved fraction is small relative to total content but is indispensable. Molecular carbon dioxide is the form that establishes the partial pressure gradient across the exchange surface. Bicarbonate is charged and cannot leave the blood by simple diffusion as a gas. Carbamino groups are attached to protein and likewise must release molecular carbon dioxide before that carbon can be exhaled. Chemical conversion in tissue and reversal in the lungs allow a relatively small molecular gas pool to communicate with much larger reservoirs.

Bicarbonate is the major carbon dioxide carrying form in ordinary mammalian blood. Erythrocytes contain carbonic anhydrase, which makes interconversion of carbon dioxide and bicarbonate fast enough for capillary passage. Much of the bicarbonate subsequently enters plasma. This division of labour is important: the red cell is a reaction compartment rich in enzyme and haemoglobin, while the extracellular fluid offers additional volume for carrying the reaction product. A plasma bicarbonate measurement thus reflects a pool generated and regulated by several organs and compartments, not an indication that all bicarbonate was originally made in plasma.

Carbamino compounds are formed when carbon dioxide reacts reversibly with suitable amino groups on proteins. Haemoglobin contributes prominently because it is abundant within red cells, and its ability to form carbamino groups is modified by oxygenation. Some carbon dioxide also reacts with plasma proteins. The carbamino linkage is not an attachment to the ferrous iron of the haem group. Oxygen binds at haem iron, whereas carbamino formation uses amino groups, particularly globin amino termini. Carbon monoxide, an entirely different molecule, can compete at haem iron with oxygen; it must not be confused with carbon dioxide or with carbaminohaemoglobin.

Introductory descriptions often allocate approximately 70% of transported carbon dioxide to bicarbonate and the rest to dissolved gas and carbamino compounds, commonly with values around 7–10% and 20–23% respectively. Such partitions are useful orientation but are not universal stoichiometric rules. A percentage can describe the distribution of the additional carbon dioxide acquired during a systemic circulation or the distribution of the total carbon dioxide pool at a particular sampling site. Those denominators are different. Changes in oxygen saturation, haemoglobin concentration, pH and the sampled pressure modify the proportions. The secure examination conclusion is that bicarbonate provides the predominant transport pathway in normal mammalian physiology, with molecular gas and carbamino forms contributing smaller but functionally essential shares.

To understand why bicarbonate predominates, consider an unbuffered aqueous solution receiving carbon dioxide. Hydration and dissociation generate hydrogen ions together with bicarbonate. As hydrogen ions accumulate, their increased activity favours the reverse reaction, limiting further conversion. Blood differs because its proteins and other buffers take up a portion of the newly generated hydrogen ions. Removal of bicarbonate from the red cell into plasma also changes the local concentration of a reaction product. Neither process violates chemical equilibrium; both change the conditions under which a new equilibrium is approached. The combination of enzyme-catalysed equilibration, proton buffering and ion transport gives whole blood an impressive capacity for carrying carbon dioxide at modest partial pressures.

4. Carbonic anhydrase and red-cell reaction kinetics

The principal chemical transformations can be written in two stages. CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ describes reversible hydration, followed by H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Carbonic acid is a weak acid and can dissociate into its conjugate base, bicarbonate. For most physiological calculations, dissolved carbon dioxide and the small associated carbonic acid pool are treated together. The net reaction CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ is especially useful for tracking carbon and acid equivalents. It preserves atoms and charge. The hydrogen ion on the right is not itself carbon dioxide; it arises because hydration and dissociation couple carbon transport to acid–base chemistry.

An enzyme accelerates a chemical reaction by providing a lower activation-energy pathway. It does not change the equilibrium constant of the overall reaction. Carbonic anhydrase is a zinc-containing enzyme; in the common red-cell isoenzyme CA II the metal supports formation of a reactive hydroxide that attacks carbon dioxide. Bicarbonate is produced and released, water replenishes the metal-bound ligand, and proton transfer allows the catalytic state to be regenerated. The detailed geometry of the active site is less important for physiology than the consequence: reversible hydration and dehydration approach equilibrium very rapidly. CA I also contributes to erythrocyte carbonic anhydrase activity, but CA II has high catalytic activity.

The same enzyme supports carbon dioxide uptake in systemic capillaries and release in pulmonary capillaries. It does not require a separate tissue-specific and lung-specific catalytic reaction. A catalyst accelerates both directions; the net direction is determined by concentrations, hydrogen ion activity, gas partial pressure and the continual removal or addition of products. At the tissue, carbon dioxide entry raises the available substrate and haemoglobin buffers protons. At the lung, molecular carbon dioxide leaves for alveolar gas, and oxygenation favours proton release. These changing conditions make the reverse reaction useful. Thinking of the enzyme as a one-way bicarbonate pump would give an incorrect prediction in the pulmonary capillary.

The importance of reaction speed can be investigated without changing the final equilibrium. Imagine equal samples of a bicarbonate-containing solution, one with active carbonic anhydrase and one without it, initially equilibrated with gas at the same PCO₂. Suddenly change the gas pressure while maintaining temperature. Measure pH continuously. The solution containing enzyme approaches the new pH more rapidly. Given sufficient time and identical final conditions, both should approach the same equilibrium pH. If final pH values differ, one must examine other changes introduced by the experiment, such as protein buffering or ionic composition. A kinetic experiment distinguishes time required to reach equilibrium from the equilibrium position itself.

Blood spends a finite interval in systemic and pulmonary capillaries. A reaction that occurs eventually in a test tube may be too slow to support rapid physiological exchange. Carbonic anhydrase solves this timing problem. Inhibition of erythrocyte carbonic anhydrase would be predicted to delay bicarbonate formation during tissue passage and reconversion during lung passage. The size of the observed effect depends on transit time, enzyme reserve, circulation and external conditions. Systemic administration of a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor has additional effects, notably in renal bicarbonate handling. A whole-animal pH change therefore cannot be attributed to red-cell enzyme inhibition alone unless the experimental design separates those mechanisms.

Enzyme activity and diffusion also interact. If hydration in a red cell consumes freely dissolved molecular carbon dioxide, the intracellular partial pressure rises more slowly than it would without conversion. A gradient from the surrounding plasma is then maintained for longer. The enzyme does not directly transport carbon dioxide through a membrane; it couples membrane diffusion to a chemical sink. In the lungs, rapid conversion in the opposite direction replenishes molecular gas as it diffuses out. The same general strategy appears elsewhere in physiology: binding or metabolism in one compartment sustains a concentration gradient and thereby indirectly promotes exchange across a membrane.

5. Haemoglobin buffering and the chloride shift

Conversion of carbon dioxide into bicarbonate generates one hydrogen ion for each bicarbonate ion formed in the net reaction. If both products accumulated freely inside an erythrocyte, the rise in hydrogen ion activity and bicarbonate concentration would oppose continued hydration. In a functioning erythrocyte, much of the hydrogen ion is accepted by titratable groups on haemoglobin. Deoxygenated haemoglobin binds protons more readily than oxygenated haemoglobin under comparable conditions. Protein buffering therefore limits the increase in free hydrogen ion activity as carbon dioxide is taken up. The protons are not destroyed; they become reversibly associated with buffer groups.

Bicarbonate generated in the erythrocyte can exchange with chloride across its plasma membrane. The major protein responsible is anion exchanger 1, abbreviated AE1, also called band 3 and encoded by SLC4A1. During systemic passage the usual net direction is bicarbonate out of the red cell and chloride into it. Both ions carry one negative charge, so exchange of one for one is electroneutral. No net charge is transferred in one exchange cycle, and the mechanism does not hydrolyse ATP directly. The net direction depends on the relative activities of exchangeable anions on the two sides of the membrane. Calling this process an ATP-dependent chloride pump obscures the thermodynamic basis of the transport.

The name chloride shift, or Hamburger shift, refers to this redistribution during changes in gas carriage. Chloride entering the cell replaces much of the negative charge associated with bicarbonate leaving. In the absence of a counterion movement, sustained uncompensated movement of negatively charged bicarbonate across the membrane would rapidly establish an electrical opposition to further movement. AE1 permits bicarbonate transport without such net electrogenic separation. The term shift does not mean that blood chloride is generated or consumed. Chloride moves between plasma and red cells; its total amount in a closed blood sample is conserved. Water redistribution can accompany altered intracellular solute and protein charge conditions, so red-cell volume may also change modestly with oxygenation and carbon dioxide loading.

This system illustrates compartmental cooperation. Enzyme activity in the red cell rapidly makes bicarbonate; haemoglobin binds a fraction of the associated protons; AE1 redistributes bicarbonate into plasma; circulation carries plasma and red cells together. Removing bicarbonate from the reaction compartment makes extracellular volume available to the carbon dioxide reservoir. The two products of the reaction are thus handled by different physical processes. One is buffered by proteins and the other is transferred by an electroneutral exchanger. Neither the protein buffer nor the transporter is a substitute for the carbonic anhydrase reaction.

Consider two otherwise comparable suspensions of intact erythrocytes in a chloride-containing medium. In one, AE1 is functional; in the other, an experimental intervention specifically reduces anion exchange. Expose both to the same increase in PCO₂ for a short interval. Carbon dioxide can still enter both cell populations, and intracellular carbonic anhydrase can still catalyse hydration. In the impaired suspension, newly formed bicarbonate cannot leave as rapidly through AE1. Its retention, in conjunction with buffer conditions, alters the response and reduces the rate at which plasma receives bicarbonate from the red cells. A useful measurement is the time course of extracellular bicarbonate or chloride redistribution. In interpreting the result, one must control cell volume, haemoglobin concentration, pH and any off-target influence of the intervention. It would be incorrect to predict that blocking AE1 abolishes all molecular carbon dioxide diffusion or all carbamino formation.

AE1 is also an important structural membrane protein and has a related form in renal acid-secreting epithelial cells. Those roles explain why genetic disturbances of SLC4A1 can produce different combinations of red-cell and renal phenotypes. They do not make the normal erythrocyte chloride shift a mechanism of net urinary acid excretion. Red cells transiently exchange anions within the circulating blood. Renal epithelial transport, covered separately, transfers acid–base equivalents between body fluid and tubular fluid. A physiological explanation should always identify both compartments before interpreting the consequence of a transporter.

6. Pulmonary reversal and exhalation

When venous blood arrives at the pulmonary capillary, its molecular carbon dioxide partial pressure ordinarily exceeds that in ventilated alveolar gas. Carbon dioxide diffuses from blood toward the alveolar space. This loss lowers the local dissolved gas pool. Reactions that had supported uptake in peripheral tissues now replenish the departing molecular gas. At the same time, oxygen diffuses in the opposite direction and binds haemoglobin. Oxygenation modifies haemoglobin's ability to bind hydrogen ions and carbamino groups. The chemistry of oxygen uptake thus facilitates the elimination of carbon dioxide.

During pulmonary passage bicarbonate in plasma enters red cells through AE1 in exchange for chloride leaving the cells. This is the reverse chloride shift. As haemoglobin becomes oxygenated, hydrogen ions are released from some of its buffer groups. The hydrogen ions combine with bicarbonate, and carbonic anhydrase accelerates conversion toward molecular carbon dioxide and water. Newly produced molecular gas can leave the red cell, enter plasma and cross the thin alveolar barrier. Carbamino groups also dissociate, adding carbon dioxide to the pool available for exhalation. The net events conserve carbon, hydrogen and electrical charge even though the chemical forms differ from those in incoming mixed venous blood.

It is helpful to trace a single carbon atom rather than imagine the lung eliminating bicarbonate directly. Suppose that carbon entered blood in exercising muscle as CO₂, was converted in an erythrocyte and crossed into plasma as bicarbonate. In a pulmonary capillary it can return to an erythrocyte through AE1. Following protonation and dehydration, it becomes molecular carbon dioxide. It diffuses into alveolar gas and is carried out during expiration. Other carbon atoms remain in the blood as part of the normal bicarbonate and dissolved pools. Only the net difference between venous and arterial content must be removed on each circulation. The lung does not strip the blood of its buffering capacity with every breath.

Ventilation is necessary to maintain the receiving compartment. If an alveolus were perfused but not ventilated, gas exchange would initially bring alveolar gas toward equilibrium with incoming venous blood. Subsequent removal would become limited because the external sink is not refreshed. Chemical conversion in blood alone cannot maintain a sustained gradient under such circumstances. Similarly, an alveolus that receives fresh air but no blood cannot receive carbon dioxide generated in distant tissues. Efficient removal requires both convective flows and a functioning exchange barrier. This connection revisits ventilation–perfusion matching without repeating its entire treatment.

Exhaled air contains carbon dioxide and water vapour. Air passing through moist respiratory surfaces approaches body temperature and high humidity; water molecules evaporate into the gas. Some heat and moisture are recovered in the upper airway during expiration, depending on conditions. The net water loss through breathing depends on inspired humidity, ventilation and temperature. Unlike carbon dioxide, much of this water is not specifically generated in the moment by the carbonic anhydrase reaction. It comes from the water lining the respiratory tract and ultimately from body fluid. At a respiratory rate and depth sufficient to increase gas throughput, respiratory water loss generally rises if other conditions are unchanged. Aquatic respiratory exchange and water conservation in other species follow different physical circumstances.

Nitrogenous wastes require other elimination pathways. The lung's ordinary role in carbon dioxide elimination must not be confused with renal clearance of urea or ammonia excretion in fish. Carbon dioxide is a volatile acid precursor because molecular gas can be transported into the atmosphere. Fixed acid loads arising from metabolism cannot generally be removed simply by exhaling their anions. Respiratory compensation can modify dissolved carbon dioxide and thereby change pH, but sustained correction of a fixed acid load requires its metabolism or excretion and restoration of buffering capacity. This division of labour explains why the lungs and kidneys are complementary rather than interchangeable regulators.

7. The Haldane effect and its experimental meaning

The dependence of carbon dioxide carrying capacity on haemoglobin oxygenation is called the Haldane effect. At a given carbon dioxide partial pressure, deoxygenated blood can generally carry more total carbon dioxide than oxygenated blood under otherwise comparable conditions. This property favours uptake in tissues, where oxygen is unloaded, and release in lungs, where oxygen is loaded. The definition must explicitly state the quantity being compared and what is held constant. A description that merely says oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange in opposite directions is insufficient, because independent diffusion gradients could produce opposite fluxes even without allosteric coupling in haemoglobin.

The molecular explanation has two closely related components. First, oxygenation alters the conformational and electrostatic properties of haemoglobin, affecting the ability of amino groups to form carbamino compounds. Deoxygenated haemoglobin generally forms such compounds more readily. Second, deoxygenated haemoglobin is a stronger proton buffer than oxygenated haemoglobin. It can accept protons generated during bicarbonate formation, thereby favouring additional bicarbonate carriage. Oxygenation releases protons, which can participate in the conversion of bicarbonate back toward molecular carbon dioxide. Both direct carbamino binding and the indirect effect through bicarbonate contribute to the total change in content. Treating the Haldane effect as exclusively the displacement of carbon dioxide from a carbamino site omits the important buffering contribution.

The classical work of Christiansen, Douglas and Haldane, published in the Journal of Physiology in 1914, examined carbon dioxide uptake and dissociation in human blood. The relevant scientific question was whether blood carbon dioxide capacity could be described solely by carbon dioxide pressure or whether oxygenation modified the relationship. A controlled comparison of equilibrated blood at differing oxygenation states and specified carbon dioxide pressures allows the effect to be distinguished from differences in exposure to carbon dioxide. Total carbon dioxide content, rather than pressure alone, is the dependent variable of interest. Temperature and blood composition must be controlled because both influence gas carriage. The observed oxygenation dependence established a physiological coupling of the two respiratory gases. Modern mechanistic interpretation includes haemoglobin proton buffering and carbamino chemistry; it should not be assumed that all modern molecular details were directly demonstrated in the original experiments.

A carbon dioxide dissociation graph places PCO₂ on the horizontal axis and total carbon dioxide content on the vertical axis. Curves are drawn for different oxygenation conditions. Over a limited physiological range the curves are often approximately linear, unlike the strongly sigmoid oxygen saturation curve. At the same horizontal coordinate, the deoxygenated-blood curve lies above the oxygenated-blood curve. At the same vertical coordinate, oxygenated blood has a higher PCO₂ than deoxygenated blood, provided the curves rise monotonically and are being compared within their common range. These two statements are equivalent readings of the same graphical relationship. In the lungs, the rise in oxygenation can raise the tendency of a fixed total carbon dioxide load to escape as molecular gas, complementing the pre-existing gradient into the alveolus.

The Bohr effect, covered with oxygen transport, is a different observation. A rise in carbon dioxide or hydrogen ion activity decreases haemoglobin oxygen affinity, so at a given PO₂ haemoglobin is less oxygen saturated. On a graph of oxygen saturation versus PO₂ this is represented by a rightward shift under the usual conditions. For the Haldane effect, oxygenation is changed and carbon dioxide content is measured at a given PCO₂. These two effects are linked through haemoglobin chemistry but answer distinct experimental questions. In metabolically active tissue, carbon dioxide loading assists oxygen unloading through the Bohr effect, while oxygen unloading assists carbon dioxide carriage through the Haldane effect. In lungs, the reverse changes favour both oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide release.

If two samples have identical carbon dioxide pressure but different haemoglobin concentrations, their content may differ independently of oxygenation. A valid demonstration of the Haldane effect therefore compares oxygenation at matched haemoglobin concentration and other relevant conditions. Similarly, changes in pH may be a consequence of the oxygenation-linked buffer reaction rather than a variable that should always be clamped in the same way. The experimental interpretation must specify whether pH or acid–base composition is independently controlled. Simplified examination curves usually depict whole blood equilibrated under comparable conditions; they should not be applied indiscriminately to a purified haemoglobin solution or a saline sample containing no haemoglobin.

8. The circulatory mass balance for carbon dioxide

The respiratory system cannot remove gas that has not reached its exchange surface. To connect tissue production to pulmonary removal we therefore need a quantitative account of blood flow. The central idea is conservation of mass: if an organ receives blood carrying a particular amount of carbon dioxide per unit volume and releases blood carrying more, the difference multiplied by flow is the net carbon dioxide added to blood per unit time. This is the carbon dioxide form of the Fick principle. It is distinct from the diffusion law that also bears Fick's name. The diffusion law describes molecular transfer across a barrier; the Fick principle describes a material balance around an organ or the entire body.

Let Q represent whole-body cardiac output in litres of blood per minute, CvCO₂ mixed venous carbon dioxide content in millilitres of gas per decilitre of blood, and CaCO₂ arterial carbon dioxide content in the same units. At steady state, the rate of carbon dioxide production in millilitres of gas per minute is V̇CO₂ = 10Q(CvCO₂ − CaCO₂). The factor ten converts litres to decilitres. It is essential that both contents and gas production use consistent reference conditions for gas volume. When contents are expressed in millimoles per litre instead, the factor ten is unnecessary and the calculated flux is in millimoles per minute. The positive venous-minus-arterial difference reflects the addition of carbon dioxide to systemic blood, opposite to the arterial-minus-venous convention commonly used for oxygen consumption.

For an illustrative resting circulation, assume cardiac output is 5 L min⁻¹, arterial carbon dioxide content is 48 mL dL⁻¹ and mixed venous content is 52 mL dL⁻¹, with the same reference conditions for gas volumes. The difference is 4 mL dL⁻¹ and 5 L min⁻¹ equals 50 dL min⁻¹. The calculated production is 50 × 4 = 200 mL min⁻¹. These are constructed teaching values rather than observations from a named experimental subject. The arterial content is much greater than the quantity added on a single passage. That fact is physiologically meaningful: the circulation recycles a large bicarbonate reservoir while delivering only the net metabolic excess to the lungs for removal.

Suppose the same organism's carbon dioxide production doubles to 400 mL min⁻¹ while cardiac output stays at 5 L min⁻¹. If a new steady state is possible, the venous-minus-arterial difference must double to 8 mL dL⁻¹. If cardiac output instead rises to 10 L min⁻¹, the difference can remain 4 mL dL⁻¹. Actual changes in pressure cannot be inferred merely by dividing the content difference by an invariant coefficient; the carbon dioxide dissociation relation depends on oxygenation and blood chemistry. This is one reason a calculation using pressure instead of content would be incorrect. The example also shows why exercise-related changes in blood flow and respiration must be understood together.

The same approach can be applied locally. Suppose two organs each produce 20 mL min⁻¹ of carbon dioxide. Organ A receives 1 L min⁻¹ of blood; organ B receives 0.25 L min⁻¹. Their content differences are respectively 20/10 = 2 and 20/2.5 = 8 mL dL⁻¹. The smaller-flow organ has to add four times as much carbon dioxide to each decilitre of blood. This does not mean that its total production is greater. The result concerns the quantity of carrier delivered per unit time. When venous blood from both organs mixes, content is weighted by the blood flow from each organ, not by taking the simple arithmetic mean of two concentrations unless their flow rates are equal.

For the two organs above, the combined effluent receives 40 mL min⁻¹ of carbon dioxide and has a flow of 1.25 L min⁻¹, equivalent to 12.5 dL min⁻¹. The mixed venous-minus-arterial content difference is therefore 3.2 mL dL⁻¹. The average of 2 and 8, which is 5, would be incorrect because the larger-flow organ contributes more blood. Moreover, mixed venous PCO₂ is not generally obtained by flow-weighting the partial pressures without considering dissociation chemistry. Content is the conserved quantity under mixing, while pressure follows from the new chemical state. This distinction recurs whenever measurements of blood from different vascular beds are combined.

The whole-body Fick balance assumes that net tissue carbon dioxide storage is negligible during the measurement interval. If production increases abruptly, some carbon dioxide can enter blood and tissue stores before a corresponding increase appears in expired gas. If blood flow changes rapidly, redistribution between organs can also influence measured venous content. In such transient settings, conservation still holds, but an accumulation term must be included. A flux equation that assumes steady state cannot by itself determine the time needed to achieve that state. This limitation is especially relevant when evaluating brief changes in breathing, exercise or experimentally altered gas mixtures.

9. Effective ventilation and the removal of metabolic carbon dioxide

Minute ventilation is the total volume of air moved into or out of the respiratory tract per minute. It equals tidal volume multiplied by breathing frequency. Not all of that volume reaches gas-exchanging alveoli. Air in the conducting airways does not participate directly in exchange during its passage, and ventilated alveoli with little or no blood supply may also contribute to wasted ventilation. Physiological dead space describes ventilation that does not effectively exchange gas with perfused blood, including anatomical and alveolar components. In the idealised calculation where a representative dead-space volume is assigned to each breath, alveolar ventilation is V̇A = f(VT − VD). Here f is breaths per minute, VT is tidal volume per breath and VD is dead-space volume per breath. When volumes are in litres, alveolar ventilation is expressed in litres per minute.

The subtraction of dead space before multiplication by frequency is important because every breath incurs a dead-space cost. Consider a constructed comparison of two patterns, both with a minute ventilation of 6 L min⁻¹ and a fixed dead-space volume of 0.15 L. Pattern A uses a tidal volume of 0.50 L at 12 breaths per minute. Its effective ventilation is 12 × (0.50 − 0.15) = 4.2 L min⁻¹. Pattern B uses a tidal volume of 0.25 L at 24 breaths per minute. Its effective ventilation is 24 × (0.25 − 0.15) = 2.4 L min⁻¹. The two patterns move the same total amount of air through the mouth, but pattern B allocates a much larger share of it to dead space.

The greater carbon dioxide retention predicted for pattern B is not a consequence of breathing frequency acting directly on carbonic anhydrase or haemoglobin. It follows from the lower effective flow of fresh gas through perfused alveoli. A question stating only that frequency has increased is incomplete if it asks whether PaCO₂ will decrease. The change in tidal volume, dead-space fraction and metabolic production must also be known. In physiological language, hyperventilation refers to ventilation excessive relative to carbon dioxide production, sufficient to lower arterial PCO₂, rather than simply a fast respiratory rate. Hypoventilation refers to inadequate effective ventilation relative to production. Hyperpnoea refers to increased breathing, often appropriate to increased metabolic demand, and need not cause hypocapnia.

A simple steady-state material balance gives the alveolar ventilation equation. Suppose the inspired carbon dioxide fraction is negligibly small, the average alveolar gas fraction of carbon dioxide is FACO₂, and net carbon dioxide production equals its removal. Removal is proportional to alveolar gas flow multiplied by that gas fraction. If both production and ventilation were expressed at identical temperature, pressure and humidity conditions, then their ratio would give the appropriate carbon dioxide fraction directly. In conventional respiratory physiology, however, production is usually expressed at standard temperature and pressure, dry (STPD), while alveolar ventilation is expressed at body temperature and pressure, saturated with water vapour (BTPS). This distinction leads to a conversion factor when relating the ratio to partial pressure.

For normal body temperature the familiar form is PACO₂ ≈ 0.863 V̇CO₂/V̇A, when production is in millilitres STPD per minute, ventilation is in litres BTPS per minute and pressure is in mmHg. Equivalently, the coefficient is 863 when both fluxes are expressed in litres per minute with their respective reference conditions. The coefficient can be understood from the ideal-gas relationship: the reference pressure at STPD is 760 mmHg and the temperature conversion from approximately 273 K to 310 K gives 760 × 310/273 ≈ 863 mmHg. The factor 1000 distinguishing millilitres from litres then yields the smaller coefficient. Appropriate corrections for humid gas and local pressure are implicit in the conventions used for BTPS ventilation and gas partial pressure.

In healthy lungs, arterial PCO₂ is usually close enough to representative alveolar PCO₂ that the relation can be written PaCO₂ ≈ 0.863 V̇CO₂/V̇A for teaching calculations. This approximation is not a claim that every alveolus has the same partial pressure or that a single expired measurement is always identical to arterial blood. Regional ventilation–perfusion inequalities, substantial shunting, dead space and differences between end-expiratory and average alveolar gas can complicate interpretation. The equation is most transparent for a lung represented by a single well-mixed exchanging compartment at steady state and negligible inspired carbon dioxide.

Using the earlier pattern A and an illustrative metabolic production of 200 mL min⁻¹, PaCO₂ ≈ 0.863 × 200/4.2 = 41.1 mmHg. For pattern B the predicted value is 0.863 × 200/2.4 = 71.9 mmHg. These predictions assume metabolic production and dead space stay fixed and that the breathing pattern is maintained long enough to reach a new steady state. In an intact organism feedback would ordinarily change breathing in response to carbon dioxide and pH; the calculated values represent imposed conditions, not necessarily the stable spontaneous response of a healthy individual.

At constant carbon dioxide production, the relationship between PaCO₂ and alveolar ventilation is inverse rather than linear. If ventilation doubles, PaCO₂ halves; if it falls to two-thirds of its original value, PaCO₂ rises to one and a half times the original value. A graph of pressure against ventilation is a descending rectangular hyperbola in the ideal model. The curve is steeper at low ventilation, meaning that a small further absolute reduction in ventilation can have a large predicted pressure effect there. The mathematical expression diverges as ventilation approaches zero, but this extrapolation has no realistic steady-state physiological meaning. Without sustained elimination, carbon dioxide accumulates over time; the assumptions of the steady-state equation fail rather than producing an instantaneous infinite pressure.

When production varies, the entire curve changes. At a fixed ventilation, doubling metabolic production doubles predicted pressure, assuming the same conversion factor and steady conditions. To maintain a given pressure when production doubles, alveolar ventilation must double. If production increases by 50% but ventilation increases by only 20%, the ratio increases by 1.50/1.20 = 1.25. Starting at 40 mmHg, the new predicted pressure is 50 mmHg. If ventilation instead increases by 80%, the new pressure is 40 × 1.50/1.80 ≈ 33.3 mmHg. These calculations establish why absolute ventilation cannot alone define hypo- or hyperventilation during exercise or altered metabolism.

10. Carbon dioxide storage, expired gas and respiratory water

A physiological body contains substantial stores of carbon dioxide in dissolved, bicarbonate and protein-bound forms. These stores are distributed unevenly among blood, interstitial fluid, intracellular fluid and other buffering compartments. The instantaneous relationship between carbon dioxide production and elimination is therefore described by a more general balance: rate of change of total body carbon dioxide stores = metabolic production minus pulmonary elimination, when other relevant carbon dioxide fluxes are negligible. If the two rates are equal, total stores do not change. If elimination is less than production, stores rise. This conservation statement remains valid during transitions when the steady-state ventilation equation alone is inadequate.

A thought experiment illustrates the difference. A resting subject produces carbon dioxide at 200 mL min⁻¹. For a brief period net pulmonary elimination falls to 120 mL min⁻¹ while production stays constant. In an idealised one-minute interval the net storage increase is 80 mL of gas equivalent at the stated reference conditions. The calculation says nothing directly about how many millimetres of mercury PaCO₂ will rise. The mapping from extra carbon dioxide to partial pressure depends on buffer capacities, circulation and compartment distribution. Later, if elimination becomes 280 mL min⁻¹ at unchanged production, the net stores decrease at 80 mL min⁻¹. A temporarily high exhaled carbon dioxide output can thus reflect washout of previously stored gas rather than a sudden increase in cellular oxidation.

Measurements of carbon dioxide in exhaled air require a precise description of which gas is sampled. At the beginning of expiration, gas from the conducting airways contributes much of the expired volume and has relatively little carbon dioxide after a normal inspiration. As expiration proceeds, gas increasingly originates in the exchanging lung regions. The end-expiratory carbon dioxide partial pressure, often denoted PETCO₂, can approximate alveolar gas pressure under favourable conditions. It is not identical by definition to arterial PaCO₂. The mixed expired gas, obtained by averaging all expired gas including dead-space gas, typically has a lower carbon dioxide fraction than gas from perfused alveoli. Comparing these measurements reveals information about how ventilation and perfusion are distributed.

Consider a simplified two-compartment teaching model. One half of the expired volume comes from effective exchange units with PCO₂ of 40 mmHg and the other half from ventilated dead-space units containing no carbon dioxide. The volume-weighted mixed expired pressure is 20 mmHg when gas conditions are comparable. This average does not mean that arterial blood has equilibrated at 20 mmHg, since the nonperfused compartment contributes air without contributing blood. In a real lung, different exchange units may have different gas compositions and perfusion. A mixed expired carbon dioxide measurement and an arterial blood gas answer different questions. Failure to distinguish them can lead to the mistaken interpretation that low expired carbon dioxide always proves low arterial carbon dioxide.

Carbon dioxide elimination depends on the difference between inspired and expired carbon dioxide fluxes, not simply the expired concentration. With negligible inspired carbon dioxide, expired flux dominates the balance. If inspired gas contains appreciable carbon dioxide, the incoming flux must be subtracted, and the simple alveolar ventilation equation must be adjusted. At the same time, an increase in inspired carbon dioxide may stimulate respiratory control, further changing ventilation. A theoretical closed chamber with recirculated gas and no carbon dioxide removal is different from an open atmosphere: its carbon dioxide content can rise as an animal continues metabolism, even though breathing continues.

Water vapour loss is another component of respiratory exchange. Saturated water vapour pressure at 37 °C is about 47 mmHg. At sea level, where total barometric pressure is represented as 760 mmHg, this leaves about 713 mmHg for the dry gas components of fully humidified air in the airways. Water vapour contributes to total pressure and must be accounted for when translating between dry gas fractions and partial pressures. Under a simple ideal-gas estimate, the water vapour concentration of saturated air at 37 °C is P/(RT), about 0.00243 mol per litre, corresponding to approximately 44 mg L⁻¹ of water. This value describes saturated warm air, not the net water loss per litre of actual breathing.

If incoming air already contains water vapour, only the additional humidity supplied by the body contributes to net loss, and upper-airway heat and moisture exchange can recover some water during expiration. As a deliberately simplified upper-bound example, fully dry incoming air converted to saturated 37 °C gas at an outgoing flow of 6 L min⁻¹ would carry about 264 mg min⁻¹ of water, or 0.38 kg per day if maintained continuously with no recovery. Real net loss is modified by ambient humidity, temperature, actual expired gas temperature, airflow and recovery. This calculation illustrates why ventilation is also relevant to fluid and thermal balance, while avoiding the mistaken interpretation of every water molecule in exhaled air as a direct product of mitochondrial oxidation.

In mammals, nasal passage architecture can reduce water loss by exchanging heat and moisture between the airway surface and incoming and outgoing air. In hot environments some animals use enhanced respiratory evaporation for heat dissipation. Because extra ventilation through dead space can contribute to evaporation without proportionally increasing alveolar ventilation, respiratory cooling and carbon dioxide balance need not change in identical proportions. Such comparative examples clarify the relationship between respiratory waste removal and other homeostatic functions; the regulation of body temperature itself belongs to 7.G.

11. Hydrogen ions, buffers and the special status of bicarbonate

The internal environment of an animal must support thousands of enzyme-catalysed reactions, membrane proteins and electrical gradients. Many of these systems depend on the protonation state of chemical groups. Hydrogen ion activity is conventionally expressed as pH, defined as the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity relative to the standard state. Because the concentration is small in dilute biological solutions, pH is often discussed using hydrogen ion concentration as an approximation. A pH decrease indicates increased hydrogen ion activity. One pH unit represents a tenfold difference, not a change of one unit in molar concentration.

At pH 7.40 the approximate hydrogen ion concentration is 10⁻⁷·⁴ mol L⁻¹, about 40 nmol L⁻¹. At pH 7.10 it is approximately 79 nmol L⁻¹. A change of only 0.30 pH units therefore nearly doubles hydrogen ion concentration. The narrow usual arterial pH range of roughly 7.35 to 7.45 in humans reflects the sensitivity of internal processes, but different cellular compartments normally maintain different pH values. A lysosome is intentionally acidic and its internal pH should not be compared directly with arterial blood pH as though every biological compartment had one universal optimum.

A buffer consists of species capable of accepting and donating hydrogen ions reversibly. When acid is added to a buffered solution, part of the added proton load is taken up by buffer bases. When base is added, buffer acids can release protons. This process limits the change in free hydrogen ion activity. Buffering does not destroy added acid equivalents. In a closed container with finite buffer capacity, sufficiently large acid or base loads eventually cause marked pH changes. The capacity depends on buffer concentration and the relative abundance of protonated and deprotonated forms. Proteins, phosphate and bicarbonate all contribute to physiological buffering, although their distribution and importance differ between compartments.

Bicarbonate has an especially important property in extracellular fluid: its acid partner can communicate with an external gas phase through the lungs. If a fixed acid adds protons to extracellular fluid, some hydrogen ions react with bicarbonate, leading toward carbon dioxide and water. The resulting carbon dioxide can be exhaled. The fixed acid anion remains unless subsequently removed or metabolised, and the bicarbonate pool may be depleted. Thus respiratory elimination can prevent accumulation of part of the resulting volatile carbon dioxide but cannot automatically restore all bicarbonate consumed by a fixed acid load. This is why describing the lungs as excreting every form of acid is misleading.

Haemoglobin provides a complementary buffer system within erythrocytes. Its large concentration and oxygenation-dependent proton affinity connect blood buffering to oxygen transport. Plasma proteins provide additional buffering. Phosphate is especially relevant within cells and in renal tubular fluid, but extracellular phosphate concentration is much smaller than extracellular bicarbonate concentration. The complete blood buffering system cannot be represented exactly by bicarbonate alone in every transient situation. Nevertheless, the bicarbonate-to-dissolved-carbon-dioxide ratio describes arterial pH very effectively under ordinary equilibrium conditions and provides an important starting point for acid–base reasoning.

The unusual strength of the physiological bicarbonate system does not mean that its apparent pKa lies close to arterial pH. For a conventional closed buffer of a fixed total concentration, buffering against small perturbations is strongest near its pKa. The apparent pKa for the dissolved carbon dioxide–bicarbonate pair is near 6.1 under physiological conventions, whereas arterial pH is around 7.4. The body's bicarbonate system remains effective because the concentrations of its components are not constrained to a sealed fixed pool: ventilation can adjust dissolved carbon dioxide, and the kidneys regulate bicarbonate availability over longer intervals. It is the coupling of chemical buffering to physiological control that distinguishes the living system from a simple flask.

The distinction between an acidifying process and the measured pH is important. Acidosis refers to a process tending to decrease pH; alkalosis refers to a process tending to increase pH. Acidaemia refers to measured arterial pH below the normal reference range, and alkalaemia to a value above it. An organism can have an acidifying process together with a compensatory alkalinising process, yielding a pH within or near the reference interval. In examination questions, classification should begin with the direction and origin of the processes rather than infer that every normal pH excludes a disturbance. Conversely, the mere presence of bicarbonate below a typical reference value does not by itself determine whether the primary process is metabolic acidosis or compensation for respiratory alkalosis.

12. Deriving and applying the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship

The equation used to relate plasma bicarbonate, arterial carbon dioxide and pH follows from the equilibrium of a weak acid and its conjugate base. For a generic weak acid HA, the dissociation reaction is HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. The acid dissociation constant Ka equals the activity product of hydrogen ion and conjugate base divided by the activity of undissociated acid. Rearranging gives hydrogen ion activity as Ka times the ratio of acid to conjugate base. Taking the negative base-ten logarithm of both sides yields pH = pKa + log₁₀(aA⁻/aHA). This algebra explains the sign of the logarithmic term. Increasing the conjugate base relative to acid increases pH; increasing acid relative to conjugate base decreases pH.

For blood, the actual hydrated carbonic acid concentration is small relative to dissolved molecular carbon dioxide. Physiological convention uses an apparent dissociation constant for the combined hydration and dissociation process, treating the acid component through the dissolved carbon dioxide concentration. At about 37 °C, the commonly used values are apparent pKa ≈ 6.1 and dissolved carbon dioxide concentration ≈ 0.030 × PaCO₂ mmol L⁻¹, with PaCO₂ in mmHg. The physiological form is therefore pH = 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻]/(0.030 PaCO₂)). Bicarbonate is entered in mmol L⁻¹, equivalent numerically to mEq L⁻¹ for this monovalent ion. The denominator has the same units as the numerator, so their ratio is dimensionless.

For representative normal arterial values, bicarbonate is 24 mmol L⁻¹ and PaCO₂ is 40 mmHg. Dissolved carbon dioxide is 1.2 mmol L⁻¹ and the ratio is 24/1.2 = 20. The base-ten logarithm of 20 is 1.301, giving pH 7.401. The often-quoted twenty-to-one ratio is not the ratio of all chemically carried carbon dioxide to oxygen, nor is it the ratio of bicarbonate to total carbon dioxide content. It compares bicarbonate concentration with the dissolved carbon dioxide term in this specific buffer equation. Confusing the denominator with total blood carbon dioxide content produces a meaningless pH calculation.

If PaCO₂ rises to 60 mmHg while bicarbonate is held at 24 mmol L⁻¹, the denominator becomes 1.8 mmol L⁻¹. The ratio is approximately 13.33, whose logarithm is 1.125. The predicted pH is 7.225. This is an artificial fixed-bicarbonate comparison designed to isolate the effect of the denominator. In a living animal, rapid non-bicarbonate buffering normally produces some change in measured bicarbonate during an acute increase in PaCO₂. A prediction with the numerator fixed must not be presented as an exact observed acute response. The equation itself remains valid for the actual measured pair of values.

Now consider PaCO₂ = 60 mmHg and bicarbonate = 26 mmol L⁻¹. The pH is 6.1 + log₁₀(26/1.8) ≈ 7.260. If over time bicarbonate becomes 32 mmol L⁻¹ at the same PaCO₂, the pH becomes 6.1 + log₁₀(32/1.8) ≈ 7.350. In both examples carbon dioxide pressure remains elevated, but the higher bicarbonate concentration in the latter partly restores the ratio. The equation thus explains why the pH change associated with a sustained respiratory disturbance can be smaller after renal compensation than immediately after its onset. It does not prove that a particular bicarbonate rise is renal compensation without temporal and physiological context.

A reverse calculation is also possible. If PaCO₂ = 30 mmHg and arterial pH is 7.48, the bicarbonate concentration implied by the equation is 0.030 × 30 × 10^(7.48 − 6.1). The product 0.9 × 10^1.38 is approximately 21.6 mmol L⁻¹. This calculation is useful for checking internally consistent values. In a typical arterial blood gas analyser, pH and PaCO₂ are measured and bicarbonate may be calculated from those two quantities rather than measured independently. Agreement of such calculated bicarbonate with the same equation is not an independent validation of all three measurements; a separately measured total carbon dioxide concentration provides different information.

A further property of the logarithmic expression is that doubling both numerator and denominator leaves pH unchanged. For example, 48 mmol L⁻¹ bicarbonate with PaCO₂ = 80 mmHg produces the same ratio of 20 as 24 mmol L⁻¹ with PaCO₂ = 40 mmHg. Both would predict pH near 7.40, yet the first combination represents markedly abnormal absolute values and would prompt consideration of more than one acid–base process. An apparently normal ratio does not establish that metabolism, ventilation and renal function are all normal. Examination datasets may deliberately exploit this distinction by presenting normal pH alongside abnormal bicarbonate and carbon dioxide values.

Because the coefficients are convention-dependent, a pressure in kilopascals must be converted before using a solubility coefficient specified per mmHg. One kPa is approximately 7.50 mmHg. A PaCO₂ of 5.33 kPa is therefore about 40 mmHg. Alternatively, the solubility coefficient can be expressed as approximately 0.225 mmol L⁻¹ kPa⁻¹. Inserting 5.33 into the original mmHg form with coefficient 0.030 would produce a denominator about seven and a half times too small, falsely predicting a large increase in pH. Checking dimensions before logarithms is one of the simplest ways to prevent such errors.

The physiological Henderson–Hasselbalch equation describes a chemical equilibrium under specified conditions. It does not itself provide an equation for breathing rate, kidney transport, haemoglobin proton buffering or the rate of approach to equilibrium. Its coefficients vary somewhat with temperature and chemical environment. It is most appropriate for interpretation of equilibrated plasma acid–base variables near the usual physiological range. In rapidly changing systems or strongly compartmentalised solutions, additional information may be needed. The relationship is nevertheless a powerful bridge between the molecular chemistry of carbon dioxide transport and whole-animal regulation because it expresses exactly how the two controllable components combine to determine pH.

13. Primary respiratory changes and compensation

A respiratory acid–base disturbance originates in a change in carbon dioxide pressure caused by an imbalance between effective ventilation and carbon dioxide load. In respiratory acidosis, PaCO₂ increases and tends to decrease pH. In respiratory alkalosis, PaCO₂ decreases and tends to increase pH. The terms refer to primary processes; a higher carbon dioxide pressure occurring as compensation for a metabolic alkalosis is not automatically evidence of a separate primary respiratory acidosis. Identifying the origin requires the direction of pH change, the bicarbonate response, the context and the degree of expected compensation.

When alveolar ventilation decreases relative to carbon dioxide production, less carbon dioxide is eliminated than generated at first. Blood and tissue stores rise and the dissolved carbon dioxide pressure increases. Hydration and dissociation of the added carbon dioxide increase hydrogen ion activity. Non-bicarbonate buffers, notably haemoglobin and proteins, bind some of the generated protons. Because removal of free hydrogen ions favours the formation of additional bicarbonate, plasma bicarbonate rises modestly in an acute respiratory acidosis even before substantial renal adaptation occurs. The change in bicarbonate is therefore not necessarily evidence that the kidney has already responded.

If elevated PaCO₂ persists, renal adjustments can increase bicarbonate conservation and net acid excretion. The extracellular bicarbonate concentration may increase further, bringing the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved carbon dioxide closer to its previous value. These processes usually require hours to begin and days to approach a sustained response. Compensation ameliorates the pH change but does not remove the primary ventilatory cause of carbon dioxide retention. Detailed transport processes in the nephron belong to 7.F; for the respiratory chapter the important prediction is that chronic respiratory acidosis generally has a larger bicarbonate elevation for a given PaCO₂ increase than the acute form.

The reverse logic applies to primary respiratory alkalosis. Excess alveolar ventilation relative to metabolic production reduces dissolved carbon dioxide pressure. The bicarbonate equilibrium shifts in a direction that lowers hydrogen ion activity. Non-bicarbonate buffers release some protons, limiting the initial pH increase, and bicarbonate concentration declines modestly. If the low PaCO₂ persists, renal bicarbonate handling can reduce the extracellular bicarbonate pool further. The result partly counters the pH elevation. An organism adapting to lower oxygen availability at altitude may illustrate this sequence: increased ventilation initially lowers PaCO₂, and longer-term adjustment reduces the associated alkalinising effect. The triggers and adaptive physiology are addressed in 7.C.08 and 7.H rather than developed in detail here.

Useful empirical teaching approximations distinguish acute and chronic respiratory responses. For each 10 mmHg increase in PaCO₂ above a baseline near 40 mmHg, acute respiratory acidosis often increases bicarbonate by roughly 1 mmol L⁻¹, whereas chronic respiratory acidosis can increase it by roughly 3–4 mmol L⁻¹. For each 10 mmHg decrease in PaCO₂, acute respiratory alkalosis often decreases bicarbonate by roughly 2 mmol L⁻¹; chronic respiratory alkalosis often decreases it by approximately 4–5 mmol L⁻¹. These are approximate empirical expectations for otherwise simple disturbances, not exact biochemical identities. Baseline status, duration, renal function and mixed processes affect observations. Different reference tables use slightly different ranges; the physiological distinction is more important than memorising one boundary.

A constructed acute respiratory acidosis example has PaCO₂ = 70 mmHg and bicarbonate = 27 mmol L⁻¹. The rise in pressure above 40 is 30 mmHg, and the bicarbonate increase above 24 is 3 mmol L⁻¹, consistent with the simple acute approximation. The calculated pH is 6.1 + log₁₀(27/2.1) ≈ 7.21. A sustained case at the same pressure with bicarbonate = 35 mmol L⁻¹ gives pH ≈ 7.32. The second response lies near the chronic prediction using an increase of about 3.7 mmol L⁻¹ per 10 mmHg. Both pH values remain on the acidic side of the reference midpoint; compensation has not corrected the original carbon dioxide excess.

For a respiratory alkalosis comparison, assume PaCO₂ falls from 40 to 20 mmHg. An acute bicarbonate prediction is approximately 24 − 4 = 20 mmol L⁻¹. The calculated pH is 6.1 + log₁₀(20/0.6) ≈ 7.62. A chronic prediction using a decrease of 4.5 mmol L⁻¹ per 10 mmHg is 15 mmol L⁻¹, giving pH ≈ 7.50. The values are deliberately illustrative, not clinical treatment targets. The more substantial bicarbonate decrease in the chronic example limits the alkalinaemia despite the same carbon dioxide pressure. In real physiological interpretation, unusually high or low bicarbonate relative to the expected range can indicate an additional metabolic process.

An acidifying primary metabolic disturbance differs because the bicarbonate component initially decreases relative to carbon dioxide. For example, addition of a fixed acid consumes buffering base and can lower bicarbonate. Increased ventilation then lowers PaCO₂ as a compensatory response, increasing the bicarbonate-to-dissolved-carbon-dioxide ratio relative to what it would have been without compensation. This does not replenish the bicarbonate consumed by the original acid. A primary metabolic alkalosis raises bicarbonate relative to carbon dioxide, and a compensatory rise in PaCO₂ can limit the pH increase. Ventilatory compensation has physiological limits, including the need to maintain oxygen exchange. The key examination pattern is that the compensatory change tends to oppose the pH effect of the primary disturbance.

When interpreting values, start by checking that the pH, bicarbonate and PaCO₂ are chemically consistent. Then compare pH with the reference range and identify which component changes in the direction that would explain it. A low pH with high PaCO₂ indicates an acidifying respiratory component; a low pH with low bicarbonate indicates an acidifying metabolic component. If both components act in the same direction, both may contribute to the disturbance. If they act in opposite directions, one may be compensating or there may be independent mixed processes. Examine the magnitude and temporal context before claiming a simple disorder. Compensation usually does not overshoot the original pH in a simple disturbance; an unexpected direction or excessive magnitude calls for further explanation. This approach emphasises causal reasoning rather than a memorised classification table.

14. Experimental evidence: separating transport, reaction and measurement

A physiological mechanism is especially convincing when different methods measure different parts of the same process and their conclusions agree. The study of carbon dioxide carriage has progressed from measuring whole-blood gas contents to examining enzyme catalysis and membrane transport separately. A student should understand why these experiments are complementary. Gas equilibration shows how much carbon dioxide blood can hold under specified conditions; it does not by itself identify the transport protein responsible for redistribution of bicarbonate. An experiment with isolated membrane transport identifies an exchange process but cannot by itself establish the magnitude of whole-body carbon dioxide elimination. Moving between these scales requires both experimental evidence and mass balance.

The classic carbon dioxide absorption studies compared blood equilibrated with different gas mixtures. A common principle behind such measurements is to expose a blood sample to a known gas phase until the dissolved partial pressure and gas partial pressure have equilibrated. Gas content can then be determined by releasing chemically combined carbon dioxide and measuring the recovered carbon. An appropriate comparison uses samples with matched haemoglobin content and controlled temperature. By varying oxygenation and carbon dioxide exposure independently, one can ask whether the carbon dioxide content depends on oxygenation as well as PCO₂. A difference observed at matched PCO₂ cannot be explained solely by Henry's law for molecular carbon dioxide; the chemically carried components must contribute. This is the logic that gives the Haldane effect its explanatory force.

Whole-blood gas content measurements need care because acidifying a sample converts bicarbonate into carbon dioxide and facilitates release of other labile forms. An assay that liberates these pools measures substantially more than the amount initially present as dissolved molecular gas. If a student compares an acid-extraction measurement with a direct pressure measurement, they are comparing distinct physical quantities. For example, 40 mmHg corresponds to roughly 1.2 mmol L⁻¹ of dissolved carbon dioxide in plasma at the specified temperature. Plasma total carbon dioxide around 25 mmol L⁻¹ under normal conditions largely reflects bicarbonate, together with the dissolved fraction and minor contributions. Neither number is inconsistent with the other. The first describes an equilibrium pressure-derived molecular pool and the second a chemical inventory. Whole-blood total content additionally incorporates the erythrocyte compartment and haemoglobin-bound forms.

The discovery and study of erythrocyte anion exchange required measurements with shorter time resolution than a final equilibrium gas content. A 1975 paper in the Journal of Physiology entitled Chloride/bicarbonate exchange in human erythrocytes examined transport by following extracellular pH changes when chloride-loaded erythrocytes were introduced into appropriate media containing bicarbonate and carbonic anhydrase. The experiment exploits the coupling between bicarbonate concentration and the carbon dioxide–bicarbonate equilibrium. As extracellular composition changes, pH provides an indirect signal of anion redistribution. The paper also examined anion competition, a clue that transfer involved a selective transport process. Modern reviews identify AE1 as the dominant erythrocyte chloride/bicarbonate exchanger and distinguish its anion exchange function from its structural role in linking the membrane skeleton.

The important experimental question is whether a pH transient reflects a membrane exchange step or another process that changes the acid–base state. An extracellular indicator can respond to bicarbonate redistribution, but it can also respond to molecular carbon dioxide diffusion or to reactions in the medium. Introducing carbonic anhydrase into a controlled external medium helps accelerate the equilibration that connects bicarbonate concentration to measurable pH. Appropriate controls include matched cell volume fraction, temperature, initial ion gradients, buffer composition and cell integrity. A comparison lacking cells reveals purely chemical changes in the bathing solution. A comparison altering the chloride gradient tests whether the direction or rate of bicarbonate redistribution depends on the counterion. An intervention known to affect anion exchange can provide additional support, although specificity and off-target effects must be evaluated.

A mechanistic prediction can be constructed from the stoichiometry of AE1. For a simplified model with chloride inside and bicarbonate outside, movement of bicarbonate into a cell should be accompanied by chloride out of the cell, provided the relative gradients favour that direction. Measuring both ion changes would be stronger evidence for exchange than measuring one ion alone. An approximately matched molar movement supports one-for-one exchange; it does not require the two extracellular concentration changes to be identical if experimental volumes differ. One must convert concentration changes to amounts using the appropriate compartment volumes. If the external bath is very large relative to cell volume, a substantial change inside the cell may appear as only a small concentration change outside. This is a practical example of why amount, concentration and flux cannot be treated as synonyms.

A transport mechanism can also be distinguished from a simple pore by looking at dependence on the counterion and the behaviour of competing substrates. A coupled exchanger has a cycle in which ion binding and release from opposite sides are linked; simple electrodiffusive leakage need not depend on the availability of a particular counterion in the same way. Anion exchange is often described as facilitated transport because the protein provides a pathway for a process driven by chemical gradients. Under physiological conditions, the ratio of bicarbonate and chloride activities on each side helps determine the net exchange direction. For a reversible one-to-one process, an approximate equilibrium condition is that the product of activities of reactants equals the corresponding product for products, with the same membrane-potential contribution cancelling for the exchanged ions. This is why reversal can be induced by changing the gradients even though the protein remains the same.

Consider an illustrative short-time experiment in which intact red cells receive a pulse of carbon dioxide. External bicarbonate increases by 3 arbitrary amount units and chloride falls by 3 units under the assumed closed-bath conditions. A second preparation with a selectively impaired exchanger shows increases and decreases of only 1 unit at the same time point. The dataset is hypothetical, not a reproduction of a published experiment. The paired changes are consistent with slower exchange in the second preparation. However, the result would not establish that transport is permanently abolished; at a later time, other routes or residual exchange might bring the system closer to its equilibrium distribution. A comparison at one time point establishes a difference at that time. A sequence of time points is necessary to distinguish slower kinetics from a different final state.

One can design a complementary enzyme experiment in a solution containing bicarbonate but no cell membranes. A rapid change in gas PCO₂ creates a departure from equilibrium, and pH is recorded over time. The active-enzyme condition should shorten the time required to reach the new equilibrium compared with a matched condition lacking active enzyme. If the study instead changes temperature while changing enzyme concentration, differences in final pH may arise from temperature-dependent equilibrium or solubility. A clean comparison isolates one variable. A cell-free enzyme experiment and an intact-cell ion exchange experiment answer different mechanistic questions. Taken together, they explain how catalysis and membrane transport coordinate during capillary transit. Neither experiment requires invoking a mysterious active process that pumps carbon dioxide from tissues into blood.

Measurement at the whole-animal level brings these molecular findings into relation with metabolism. Indirect calorimetry estimates oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide output from respiratory gas flows and concentrations. Arterial and mixed venous gas measurements, combined with cardiac output, can provide an independent estimate of steady-state metabolic flux using the Fick principle. Agreement between these methods under stable conditions strengthens confidence in the mass balance. Disagreement in a changing system may indicate storage, sampling error or unmatched reference conditions rather than failure of the underlying conservation law. A useful examination question therefore asks not just which technique is correct but whether the techniques measure the same quantity over the same interval under compatible assumptions.

15. Perturbations of the integrated transport system

The preceding mechanisms describe a red cell completing a normal circuit. A stronger test of understanding is to alter one part of the circuit and predict the consequences at several levels. To avoid ambiguity, begin by stating which other variables are experimentally fixed and whether the observation is made immediately or after a new steady state. An isolated change in blood chemistry has a different interpretation from the response of an intact animal, because the latter may alter ventilation, circulation or kidney function. A student who recognises this distinction can interpret unfamiliar scenarios without memorising a separate outcome for every possible condition.

First consider a reduction in haemoglobin concentration, with arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures initially unchanged. The oxygen content falls because fewer haem groups are available to carry oxygen, even if oxygen saturation remains high. Carbon dioxide carriage differs because bicarbonate, not carbaminohaemoglobin, is the predominant form. Nevertheless, fewer haemoglobin molecules reduce both the number of carbamino binding sites and the contribution of red-cell protein to buffering hydrogen ions. The total carbon dioxide dissociation relation can therefore change. It would be wrong to conclude that carbon dioxide transport remains completely unaffected merely because most of its carbon is carried as bicarbonate, but it would also be wrong to assume the entire carbon dioxide capacity decreases in exact proportion to haemoglobin concentration. Changes in blood flow, pH and ventilation can compensate in an intact animal.

At a fixed PCO₂, a reduction in haemoglobin concentration can reduce the capacity for oxygenation-linked carbon dioxide carriage. However, the net amount that must be excreted is determined by metabolic production, not by a single binding capacity. If production remains unchanged and blood flow rises, the required venous-minus-arterial content difference can decrease, as shown by the Fick equation. These adaptations explain why a person or animal with a reduced haemoglobin concentration need not necessarily have an increased arterial PCO₂. The experimentally secure prediction concerns blood carrying capacity at controlled conditions; the intact-body pressure response depends on the complete system.

Next consider impaired carbonic anhydrase activity. During a short capillary transit, delayed hydration could reduce the rate at which carbon dioxide is converted into bicarbonate, and delayed dehydration could limit the rate at which bicarbonate-derived carbon dioxide becomes available for exhalation. If the same preparations are held at fixed gas pressure for a sufficiently long time, their equilibrium composition need not differ simply because enzyme activity differed. The predicted effect is primarily kinetic under otherwise identical chemical conditions. This distinction is experimentally valuable: changing enzyme activity should be detected most clearly under conditions where reaction time competes with the duration of exposure. When interpreting a systemic intervention, renal effects and feedback responses must be kept separate from erythrocyte kinetics.

A selective reduction of AE1 activity affects a different step. Carbon dioxide can still cross the membrane and carbonic anhydrase can still generate bicarbonate. The impairment is in rapid access to the large extracellular bicarbonate compartment and the reciprocal movement of chloride. If the transporter is defective, changing red-cell bicarbonate concentration may oppose further hydration during tissue passage. The resulting changes in plasma and intracellular bicarbonate depend on the extent of exchange inhibition and transit duration. In the reverse pulmonary situation, bicarbonate returning to the cell may also be delayed. The prediction therefore involves both the loading and unloading portions of the circulation. A mechanism that facilitates reversible exchange cannot be assigned a fixed direction independent of the environment.

Now change haemoglobin oxygenation without first changing the total carbon dioxide content of a sealed blood sample. If the sample is oxygenated under conditions in which carbon dioxide cannot escape, its carbon dioxide partial pressure tends to rise as the blood shifts to the lower-capacity oxygenated dissociation relation. Carbon dioxide molecules have not been added from outside. They have been redistributed between the bound, bicarbonate and dissolved states. If the same oxygenation takes place in contact with a large external gas reservoir held at fixed PCO₂, the pressure may instead stay near the reservoir value while total carbon dioxide content decreases as gas leaves the sample. The two apparently different observations are both manifestations of the Haldane effect, but the experimental boundary conditions differ.

In a pulmonary capillary supplied with low-oxygen gas, less haemoglobin oxygenation may occur than would occur in a well-ventilated region. The oxygenation-dependent contribution to carbon dioxide unloading may consequently be reduced, even if a gradient for dissolved carbon dioxide still exists. This does not imply that carbon dioxide cannot leave blood unless oxygen enters; molecular gas diffusion and chemical equilibration still operate. The Haldane effect enhances the coordination of the two gas exchanges rather than constituting an absolute prerequisite for every molecule of carbon dioxide to leave. When describing feedback systems, distinguishing enhancement from necessity is important. A factor can increase efficiency without being the sole causal mechanism.

A change in the distribution of blood flow can also alter carbon dioxide removal. Suppose total alveolar ventilation increases but much of the increment reaches lung units receiving little blood. Effective exchange may increase far less than total ventilation. The alveolar ventilation model assumes that effective flow rather than total air movement is being used. At the same time, well-ventilated regions can eliminate a larger share of carbon dioxide and partially compensate for poorly ventilated regions, because the carbon dioxide content–pressure relation is comparatively favourable for redistribution over the physiological range. Oxygen loading behaves differently near the upper plateau of its saturation curve: increasing PO₂ in already nearly saturated blood adds relatively little haemoglobin-bound oxygen. This difference helps explain why some ventilation–perfusion disturbances cause appreciable hypoxaemia without equivalent carbon dioxide retention.

The pressure and content relationships also explain why a change in arterial pH does not uniquely identify the site of disturbance. A fall in pH can follow carbon dioxide retention, addition of fixed acid or a combination of both. Carbon dioxide retention increases the denominator of the Henderson–Hasselbalch ratio, whereas a fixed acid load often decreases the numerator. A measurement of pH alone gives the direction of the net effect but not the cause. A second measurement, either PaCO₂ or bicarbonate in conjunction with the equilibrium relationship, is necessary to distinguish the primary components. A careful experimental interpretation requires understanding how variables were measured and whether an apparently independent value was calculated from the others.

Finally, changes in temperature affect several linked quantities. Solubility of carbon dioxide, haemoglobin affinity, enzyme reaction rates and the apparent buffer equilibrium all vary with temperature. A gas analyser reporting values at a standard temperature may not give the same numerical pressures as measurements at the animal's actual body temperature. In a comparative physiology problem, this matters especially for ectotherms whose body temperatures vary widely. The human-oriented constants of 0.030 mmol L⁻¹ mmHg⁻¹ and pKa near 6.1 are appropriate approximations near 37 °C, not universal constants for all species and temperatures. The conceptual framework of chemical equilibrium, mass balance and controlled gas exchange remains applicable, but coefficients and normal ranges must be adapted to the organism studied.

16. From carbon dioxide elimination to physiological homeostasis

During moderate sustained exercise, metabolic carbon dioxide production increases because muscle energy turnover rises. Oxygen consumption generally increases as well. Blood flow through active tissue helps carry the additional carbon dioxide away, and alveolar ventilation increases so the pulmonary elimination rate can match production. If both effective ventilation and carbon dioxide production rise proportionately, the arterial carbon dioxide pressure can remain close to its resting value despite much greater output at the mouth. This is the central idea of exercise hyperpnoea. A larger amount of exhaled carbon dioxide per minute does not necessarily indicate increased arterial carbon dioxide pressure. Flux and pressure describe different aspects of the process, and steady pressure may reflect successful regulatory adjustment rather than unchanged metabolism.

In more intense exercise, additional acid–base changes can contribute to expired carbon dioxide. When a nonvolatile acid load is buffered by bicarbonate, molecular carbon dioxide is generated from the bicarbonate reservoir. Some of that additional carbon dioxide is exhaled. The resulting gas output can temporarily exceed what would be predicted solely from mitochondrial carbon dioxide production. This is one reason the respiratory exchange ratio measured from expired gases can differ from the metabolic respiratory quotient, particularly during non-steady exercise. The biochemical origin of exercise-associated acid–base changes is more complex than simply declaring lactate production to be equivalent to proton production; the physiological analysis here requires only the distinction between net acid equivalents, bicarbonate consumption and measured carbon dioxide flux.

At the cellular level, failure to remove carbon dioxide can influence intracellular pH because molecular gas crosses membranes relatively readily. The blood buffer equilibrium is only one part of the response; each tissue has its own buffer composition and membrane transport systems. Intracellular pH may not change by exactly the same amount as arterial pH. Nevertheless, a sustained disturbance of circulating carbon dioxide can impose a challenge on many organs simultaneously. Proteins involved in catalysis, ion binding and excitability may respond to altered protonation states. Thus respiratory homeostasis supports functions that are not usually classified as respiratory, including neural signalling, muscle contraction and metabolic enzyme activity.

Negative feedback helps limit ordinary deviations. If effective ventilation falls at unchanged carbon dioxide production, the rise in carbon dioxide and associated acidification provides signals that tend to increase ventilatory drive in an intact regulatory system. Increasing effective ventilation then increases gas removal, opposing the original change. The controller does not directly remove bicarbonate from plasma; it acts through muscles and airflow, modifying the dissolved gas component of the equilibrium. The next node describes the sensory receptors, brainstem networks and motor pathways that make this feedback possible. Here the important principle is that carbon dioxide can function simultaneously as a transported metabolic product and as part of the signal controlling its own removal.

The respiratory component of acid–base regulation is comparatively rapid because altered gas exchange can change dissolved carbon dioxide over breaths and circulatory passages. In contrast, renal adjustment of extracellular bicarbonate availability takes longer to develop. Chemical buffering begins even before either organ has completed an adjustment. These time scales must not be regarded as sharply separated switches: buffers, ventilation and renal function operate simultaneously, but their relative effects differ with duration. An immediate pH change after a step in carbon dioxide reflects a combination of gas exchange and chemical buffering; a later change at sustained pressure may additionally reflect renal adaptation. The observation time is consequently part of the experimental definition of an acute or chronic response.

A useful integrated numerical scenario begins with an animal producing 200 mL of carbon dioxide per minute, cardiac output 5 L min⁻¹, arterial PCO₂ 40 mmHg and plasma bicarbonate 24 mmol L⁻¹. The corresponding effective ventilation for the simplified equation is about 4.32 L min⁻¹ and the carbon dioxide content difference is 4 mL dL⁻¹. Suppose metabolic production rises by 50%, cardiac output rises to 7.5 L min⁻¹, and effective ventilation rises to 6.48 L min⁻¹. The calculated content difference and arterial PCO₂ are both essentially unchanged. Under the assumption that bicarbonate remains near 24 mmol L⁻¹, calculated pH also remains near 7.40. The animal has increased production, transport and elimination without a large change in the selected state variables.

Change just one condition in the same example: effective ventilation increases to only 5.0 L min⁻¹ despite production rising to 300 mL min⁻¹. Predicted PaCO₂ becomes 0.863 × 300/5.0 = 51.8 mmHg. Holding bicarbonate artificially at 24 mmol L⁻¹ gives pH approximately 7.29. If non-bicarbonate buffering raises bicarbonate modestly, actual pH would be somewhat higher than this fixed-numerator estimate. The cardiac output increase still allows the same content difference to carry the additional metabolic flux, but it cannot on its own maintain normal PaCO₂ when effective pulmonary ventilation is insufficient. Improving the blood transport step and improving the final gas removal step are related but not interchangeable.

If the same animal instead has a primary metabolic acid load and ventilation rises sufficiently to bring PaCO₂ to 30 mmHg while bicarbonate falls to 18 mmol L⁻¹, the ratio is 18/0.9 = 20 and predicted pH is about 7.40. The normal ratio in this constructed example does not mean every component of homeostasis is unchanged. The bicarbonate pool is reduced and ventilation is excessive relative to carbon dioxide production compared with the original state. For an ordinary simple metabolic acidosis this degree of apparent normalisation would warrant checking expected compensation and considering mixed processes; the example is deliberately an algebraic demonstration rather than a claim about the typical magnitude of compensation. The lesson is to distinguish the measured pH from the separate underlying acid–base processes.

The whole chapter can now be understood as a linked sequence of constraints. Mitochondria and other metabolic reactions produce carbon dioxide. Diffusion transfers molecular gas toward capillaries. Carbonic anhydrase, haemoglobin and AE1 expand blood's capacity to carry the carbon without requiring a proportionally large pressure increase. Circulatory flow determines how much new carbon is carried per unit time. Oxygenation in the lungs and reverse chemical reactions replenish the molecular form that crosses the respiratory barrier. Effective ventilation refreshes the alveolar sink. The bicarbonate-to-dissolved-carbon-dioxide ratio determines an important component of the internal acid–base environment. Feedback and slower renal responses help preserve this ratio when metabolic or respiratory conditions change. Each step follows ordinary chemical and physical principles; the physiological sophistication lies in their coordination.
