1. A beating heart is a repeating hydraulic system

Blood must reach tissues continuously, but the mammalian heart does not generate a continuous muscular contraction. Its ventricles repeatedly collect blood, develop pressure, deliver a portion of their contents and relax. The arterial system helps maintain flow between beats by temporarily storing elastic energy. The question of this chapter is how a repeating sequence of electrical and mechanical events maintains directional flow through the heart even while the pressure in its chambers changes considerably.

A cardiac cycle is the sequence of events that repeats between equivalent points of successive heartbeats. One may begin the description at atrial contraction, the start of ventricular filling or the instant at which the mitral valve closes. Each convention describes the same periodic process. Choosing mitral closure makes pressure–volume analysis convenient, whereas choosing the beginning of atrial contraction makes the connection to the electrocardiogram convenient. A complete sequence must return to the chosen starting state, apart from fluctuations from one beat to the next.

Systole means a period of contraction and diastole means a period of relaxation in the usual description of the chambers. These terms need a chamber name because atrial and ventricular activity is not synchronous throughout the beat. The atria contract late in ventricular diastole; they relax while the ventricles develop pressure and eject. There is also a period during which both pairs of chambers are relaxed. Thus the phrase 'the heart is in systole' generally means ventricular systole, not simultaneous contraction of every cardiac muscle cell.

Consider the left ventricle as a chamber connected by two one-way valves to the left atrium and the aorta. It has no requirement to empty completely each time it contracts. Its function depends on the difference between the volume before and after ejection. The residual blood at the end of ejection does not make the heart inefficient by itself. Residual volume reflects the mechanical interaction between the myocardium and the arterial circulation. A stronger contraction can eject more from a given starting volume, but there are physiological limits on how far the chamber can shrink.

The right and left ventricles are arranged in series. Over many beats in a stable circulation, the right ventricle sends essentially the same amount of blood to the pulmonary circulation as the left ventricle sends to the systemic circulation, disregarding small physiological shunts. If one side delivered persistently more than the other, blood would accumulate between them. Their pressures, however, are very different because the two downstream vascular networks impose different loads. Equality of flow does not demand equality of pressure or external mechanical work.

An ideal one-way pump analogy is useful only up to a point. A hand-operated pump has a rigid chamber and externally driven piston. The heart is a living deformable chamber, whose wall generates tension, stores and releases elastic energy and changes shape during contraction. The atrial reservoir, moving valve apparatus, coronary blood supply and electrical timing all interact with chamber mechanics. We will therefore use hydraulic reasoning while retaining the biological distinctions that make cardiac pumping possible.

2. Pressure differences and the four valves

Pressure is force distributed over area. The force that a fluid exerts on a small surface is equal to its local pressure multiplied by the area of that surface. Cardiac pressure is commonly measured in millimetres of mercury, abbreviated mmHg. A pressure reading is normally expressed relative to atmospheric pressure. What matters for directional flow between two connected regions is the difference between their pressures, not whether either reading is high in isolation.

For the left heart we distinguish left atrial pressure, left ventricular pressure and aortic pressure. The mitral valve lies between the atrium and the ventricle, and the aortic valve lies between the ventricle and the aorta. In an ideal quasi-static description, the mitral valve opens when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure and closes when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure. The aortic valve opens when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure and closes as the gradient reverses. The right heart contains corresponding tricuspid and pulmonary valves with the same functional rules.

These rules are approximations to the behaviour of moving tissues and moving blood. Flow has inertia, valve leaflets have mass, and pressure may vary across a chamber. A small reversal of the pressure difference can precede the actual end of forward flow. The simplified model is appropriate for identifying phases on an examination diagram, but an experimentally recorded valve motion need not occur at exactly the instant at which two sampled pressure curves cross.

The atrioventricular valves are supported by chordae tendineae and papillary muscles. When ventricular muscle contracts, papillary muscle tension limits excessive leaflet motion toward the atrium. Papillary muscles do not pull the mitral or tricuspid valves open to admit blood. Opening is principally determined by the pressure difference across the valve. Loss of supporting tension can impair closure even if ventricular muscle generates substantial pressure.

The semilunar valves have pocket-shaped cusps. During forward ejection the blood displaces the cusps toward the arterial wall. As flow slows and tends to reverse, the geometry of the arterial root and cusps promotes closure. These valves do not have chordae or papillary muscles. The distinct supporting anatomy is appropriate to the geometry and loading of the two kinds of valve.

A helpful thought experiment is to replace a valve with a fixed open tube. When ventricular pressure rises, blood would then travel back toward the low-pressure atrium as well as toward the arterial outlet. The ventricle would lose its capacity to build pressure in a sealed cavity, and a decrease in ventricular volume would no longer be a reliable measure of forward delivery. Valves therefore separate filling from ejection both temporally and mechanically.

The pressure gradient drives flow only when an available path exists. An extremely high ventricular pressure cannot produce substantial forward aortic flow if the outlet is mechanically obstructed. Conversely, a small gradient through an open, low-resistance mitral valve may produce a considerable early filling rate. A rough linear relationship is flow = pressure difference divided by resistance. This is a useful directional model; real valve flow has nonlinear losses, changing opening area and acceleration. Do not use the simple equation as a precise law for an unsteady jet.

3. Late diastole and atrial systole

Begin with the left ventricle in diastole, after its mitral valve has opened. Blood returns from the lungs into the left atrium. The atrium supplies the ventricle through the open valve, and for much of this time the two chambers are hydraulically connected. The atrium nevertheless has its own pressure and volume waveform because blood is arriving from the pulmonary veins, leaving through the mitral opening and interacting with the moving ventricular base.

During early ventricular filling the pressure gradient from atrium to ventricle is often sufficient for a rapid transfer of blood. As the chambers approach pressure equilibration, the filling rate declines. A relatively slow mid-diastolic period is called diastasis. Its duration is variable and can be very short or absent at a rapid heart rate. The slow rate of volume increase on a tracing does not mean that venous return has stopped; it means that net transfer to the ventricular cavity has diminished.

Atrial depolarisation precedes atrial mechanical contraction. The P wave of a surface electrocardiogram is an electrical event, not a direct measure of the force of atrial contraction. After a short excitation–contraction delay, atrial muscle develops tension and raises atrial pressure. This generates a second late-diastolic filling component. The late transmitral flow peak is often called the A wave, while the early peak is called the E wave. Neither label is the same as the atrial pressure a wave, despite the related physiology.

At a resting heart rate in a healthy compliant ventricle, much of the blood enters without active atrial contraction. The fractional contribution of atrial systole depends on age, heart rate, ventricular compliance, the preceding relaxation process and filling pressure. A fixed numerical percentage should not be treated as a universal law. A stiff ventricle may rely more heavily on the final atrial contribution, and tachycardia can alter the relative contribution by shortening the available filling interval.

The mechanical effect of an atrial beat depends on its timing. If atrial contraction happens after the mitral valve closes, the pressure generated by the atrium cannot normally add volume to the ventricle. Instead, atrial pressure may become unusually high and the blood can be displaced toward upstream veins. Coordinated atrioventricular activation is valuable not merely because it maintains a particular sequence of electrical waves but because it permits atrial force to act when the ventricular inlet is available.

At the end of filling, the ventricular cavity reaches its end-diastolic volume, abbreviated EDV. This is the largest volume of the ordinary idealised cardiac cycle. It is not a fixed anatomical capacity; changes in venous return, rate and ventricular mechanics can change it. The pressure at this instant is end-diastolic pressure. Two ventricles can have the same EDV but quite different end-diastolic pressures if their passive mechanical properties differ. One must never substitute a volume number for a pressure number without considering chamber mechanics.

4. Isovolumetric contraction: pressure rises without emptying

Ventricular activation spreads through the conducting system and working myocardium. In a normal electrocardiogram the QRS complex marks the rapid spread of ventricular depolarisation. Calcium enters activated ventricular muscle cells through voltage-sensitive channels and stimulates additional calcium release from internal stores. Calcium binding to troponin permits force generation by the contractile filaments. The coordinated activation of many ventricular cells converts microscopic sarcomere tension into macroscopic pressure development.

Initially the ventricular cavity remains at a low pressure relative to the aorta. As contraction proceeds, ventricular pressure exceeds left atrial pressure, and the mitral valve closes. Yet the aortic valve cannot open until ventricular pressure is sufficient to overcome the arterial pressure at its root. During the interval between inlet closure and outlet opening both valves are closed. In the normal idealised heart, no blood enters or leaves the ventricular cavity, so its volume remains approximately constant.

This interval is called isovolumetric contraction. The prefix iso means equal or unchanged and refers here specifically to cavity volume. It does not mean constant pressure, muscle fibre length, ventricular diameter or wall thickness. The ventricle may change shape and twist while its enclosed blood volume remains nearly fixed. Some local myocardial segments may shorten while others stretch. Treating the intact heart as a single isometric skeletal muscle experiment obscures these three-dimensional interactions.

Blood is nearly incompressible at physiological pressures. Consequently, even a modest attempt to reduce the enclosed volume, constrained by closed valves, increases pressure. Chamber pressure also depends on geometry, wall thickness and the force developed by its wall. An approximate thin-walled spherical model gives wall tension per unit length proportional to pressure times radius, but the thick-walled ellipsoidal heart does not obey this simplification exactly. The conceptual result remains useful: a larger cavity must generally develop greater wall tension to generate a given pressure if other geometric factors are comparable.

The first heart sound, S1, occurs near the beginning of ventricular systole. It arises from vibrations associated with abrupt deceleration of blood and tensioning of ventricular, valvular and supporting structures around atrioventricular valve closure. It is not simply the sound of two dry valve leaflets striking each other. It often follows the onset of QRS because electrical activation takes time to generate enough pressure to close the inlet.

Suppose arterial diastolic pressure is initially 80 mmHg. If the ventricle has reached only 60 mmHg while atrial pressure is 8 mmHg, both valves remain closed in the ideal model. A rise to slightly above 80 mmHg permits opening of the arterial valve. If the arterial pressure were increased to 100 mmHg, a greater ventricular pressure would be needed before opening. At an unchanged rate of pressure development, this would tend to prolong the isovolumetric interval. In a real circulation the rate of pressure development itself may also change.

A graph of ventricular pressure against time has a steep ascending segment during isovolumetric contraction, while a simultaneous volume tracing has an upper plateau. On a pressure–volume graph, the same interval is a nearly vertical ascending line. Recognising equivalent representations is essential because an examiner can remove the labels and ask for the phase based on the shape of either graph.

5. Ventricular ejection: energy is transferred to arterial blood

When left ventricular pressure becomes sufficiently greater than aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens. Forward flow begins. This is the transition from a nearly constant-volume phase to a phase in which the ventricular cavity loses blood. The mitral valve remains closed because ventricular pressure is still much higher than atrial pressure. The ventricle now delivers mechanical energy to the arterial blood while contraction continues.

Ejection can be described as an early rapid phase followed by a later reduced phase. Initially the pressure-generating capability of the muscle is rising and the open valve offers a pathway into the aorta. The rate of ejection becomes high. Ventricular volume declines rapidly; on a volume-versus-time graph this appears as a steep negative slope. A large negative slope indicates rapid net loss of volume, not necessarily the greatest absolute ventricular pressure.

The aortic pressure also rises as blood enters the elastic aorta. Part of the entering volume flows onward through the systemic circulation and part stretches the arterial walls. The aorta therefore behaves as an elastic reservoir rather than a passive pipe. Its pressure does not become zero when ventricular ejection ends. Elastic recoil during the succeeding diastole supports forward peripheral flow. In this chapter the arterial reservoir is treated as the load facing the heart; its detailed haemodynamics belongs to the later vascular physiology node.

Ventricular pressure may begin to decline before ejection is over. This happens because the active tension-generating capability of the myocardium changes over time, the ventricle is smaller and blood continues to move through the outlet. A student who defines the beginning of relaxation as the first downward slope in ventricular pressure may incorrectly conclude that ejection has already ceased. The classification of a valve-defined phase depends on whether the outlet remains open and blood continues leaving the ventricle.

In late systole ventricular repolarisation occurs, giving rise to the T wave of the ECG. The temporal association with declining force is important, but it is not exact equality of electrical and mechanical relaxation. Cytoplasmic calcium concentration and cross-bridge activity change over finite times. A normal T wave alone cannot tell us the volume remaining in the ventricle or the precise instant of aortic closure.

Ejection ends when forward flow ceases and the outlet valve closes. The cavity contains its end-systolic volume, abbreviated ESV. If EDV was 125 mL and ESV is 55 mL, the cavity has lost 70 mL. In a competent-valve heart without shunt this difference is the forward stroke volume. In mitral regurgitation the volume can leave through both the aortic and the mitral paths; the difference still represents total ventricular volume loss, but it no longer equals effective aortic delivery.

The left atrium continues to receive pulmonary venous return during ventricular systole. As the mitral valve is closed, the atrium temporarily stores much of the incoming blood. Its pressure tends to rise toward the end of systole, producing the atrial v wave. The timing of this waveform gives information about reservoir filling; it is not generated by another atrial action potential. Atrial and ventricular volumes therefore have distinct trajectories throughout the cycle.

Ejection does not end because the ventricle is empty. The remaining blood reflects the balance between muscle force, arterial load, geometry and the available duration of contraction. A higher arterial load, under otherwise similar conditions, tends to reduce volume ejected and leave a larger ESV. Greater contractility at a matched starting volume and arterial load generally allows more complete emptying. These controlled comparisons provide the foundation for the later study of pump regulation.

6. Isovolumetric relaxation and early ventricular filling

At the end of ejection the pressure gradient across the aortic valve changes, forward flow ceases and a small tendency toward backflow assists closure of the semilunar cusps. The resulting vibrations contribute to the aortic component of S2. The closure event is associated with a small feature in the central aortic pressure tracing called the incisura or dicrotic notch. Its exact shape depends on measurement location and the interaction between valve closure, arterial elasticity and waves travelling along the arterial system.

The ventricular pressure now falls rapidly, but initially remains greater than atrial pressure. Both the mitral and aortic valves are closed. The ventricle has reached its ESV, which remains approximately constant during this interval. The phase is called isovolumetric relaxation. It appears as a lower plateau of the ventricular volume tracing and a nearly vertical descending limb on the pressure–volume loop.

Relaxation is not simply passive cessation of contraction. Cytoplasmic calcium must fall, allowing calcium to dissociate from troponin and reducing cross-bridge activation. The sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase transports calcium into internal stores. Sodium–calcium exchange contributes to calcium extrusion across the cell membrane, supported indirectly by the sodium gradient maintained by the sodium–potassium ATPase. ATP is also required for normal actin–myosin detachment. Thus a heart that is unable to maintain energy metabolism can suffer impaired relaxation as well as impaired force generation.

Once ventricular pressure becomes lower than atrial pressure, the mitral valve opens. Blood can now enter. Rapid early filling depends on the available atrial pressure, the fall in ventricular pressure produced by relaxation, recoil of the ventricular wall and the mechanical properties of the filling chamber. The ventricle is not simply an inert bag into which the atrium pushes blood. Under appropriate circumstances elastic restoring forces help lower ventricular pressure and assist early inflow.

When relaxation is delayed, the ventricle may remain at a pressure too high for the mitral valve to open at the usual time. The early filling interval may then be altered even if contraction and ejection appear relatively well preserved. A sufficiently high atrial pressure can compensate partly by restoring the pressure gradient for inflow, so an apparently large early filling peak does not necessarily establish normal myocardial relaxation. The measured waveform is a product of both the driving pressure and the chamber response.

The ventricle becomes progressively fuller, and passive resistance to further distension usually increases. Filling can therefore slow even while blood continues to return to the atrium. In late filling, the next atrial contraction adds a final component and the repeating sequence returns to its starting state. Notice the cycle's logic: active contraction raises pressure to establish an outlet gradient, and active relaxation lowers pressure to establish an inlet gradient. Both are essential for sustained flow.

7. Atrial pressure waves and the moving ventricular base

Atrial pressure changes are particularly useful because they show that an atrium is more than a chamber that contracts once per beat. The right atrial pressure waveform can be observed indirectly in the jugular venous pulse, whereas left atrial pressure can be recorded with suitable invasive techniques. The standard waveform contains three positive deflections, called a, c and v, separated by pressure descents. These letters label mechanical events and must be interpreted relative to an independent timing signal.

The a wave corresponds to active atrial contraction. In the right heart it increases pressure in the right atrium and may transiently raise pressure in the great veins. In the left heart it promotes late ventricular filling. If organised atrial contraction is absent, the repeated normal a wave is lost. This prediction follows from the mechanism rather than from recognition of a particular ECG pattern. A coordinated atrial pressure rise requires coordinated atrial force generation.

The c wave appears near the beginning of ventricular contraction. In a direct atrial recording a contribution comes from movement and bulging of the closed atrioventricular valve toward the atrial cavity as ventricular pressure rises. When observing the jugular pulse, transmitted arterial motion may also contribute to the apparent c wave. This difference in measurement matters. A recorded external pulse should not be treated as an unmodified direct measurement of atrial cavity pressure.

After atrial systole the atrial myocardium relaxes, contributing to a fall in pressure. As the ventricle contracts longitudinally, the atrioventricular junction moves toward the ventricular apex. This motion can expand the atrial cavity, encouraging venous entry while the atrioventricular valve is closed. Together, atrial relaxation and descent of the ventricular base contribute to the x descent. The atrial reservoir can thus fill during ventricular systole even though atrial pressure does not rise monotonically.

During later ventricular systole, venous blood continues entering the atrium. As the chamber fills, pressure tends to increase. The v wave develops toward the end of ventricular systole and peaks around the time of atrioventricular opening. Once the ventricle relaxes sufficiently to allow the inlet to open, blood leaves the atrium rapidly and atrial pressure falls. This falling portion is the y descent. Both the size and shape of the descents depend on atrial compliance, venous inflow and ventricular behaviour.

A useful experimental comparison is to introduce an extra atrial contraction at two different phases while recording atrial and ventricular pressure. When the mitral valve is open, the atrial pressure increase can promote ventricular filling. If the valve is already closed, the same contraction cannot send blood normally into the ventricle, and a much greater pressure deflection can result. An unusually large a wave can therefore arise from adverse timing rather than increased atrial muscle strength. In the intact circulation, venous pathways and atrial wall distension determine the precise response.

The atrium performs three related roles over the cycle. It serves as a reservoir when the ventricular inlet is closed, as a conduit during passive filling and as an active booster late in diastole. These roles overlap in time, and their relative contributions change with rate and ventricular properties. A decrease in active atrial transport does not eliminate reservoir or conduit function. Conversely, an atrium that receives blood normally may fail to contribute its normal booster component if its mechanical timing is disturbed.

In a hypothetical recording, ventricular pressure rises sharply while atrial pressure shows a brief small deflection, then falls despite continued venous return. The first small atrial rise is compatible with the c wave; the subsequent fall is compatible with the x descent. When the examiner instead describes a late atrial pressure peak followed by a rapid decrease during ventricular filling, the v wave and y descent are the relevant events. Associating each feature with valve state is more reliable than memorising letter order alone.

8. Heart sounds: mechanical vibrations and physiological timing

The pressure changes of a beat are accompanied by short vibrations transmitted through cardiac structures, blood and surrounding tissue. A stethoscope detects some of these vibrations at the body surface. A phonocardiogram converts acoustic activity into a time-dependent signal that can be compared with an ECG and pressure recordings. The acoustic signal is a separate measurement from electrical voltage and intracardiac pressure.

S1 marks events associated with atrioventricular closure at the beginning of ventricular systole. It has mitral and tricuspid components that are often heard together. The mitral component normally precedes the tricuspid component. The intensity depends partly on the positions and mobility of the valve leaflets immediately before closure and the rate of pressure development. Consequently, a loud first sound does not necessarily mean the ventricular contraction produces more useful forward work.

S2 marks events associated with semilunar closure near the transition into ventricular diastole. The aortic component, A2, and pulmonary component, P2, may be distinguishable. Normally A2 occurs before P2. During spontaneous inspiration, increased systemic venous return to the right heart tends to increase right ventricular filling and prolong right ventricular ejection. Changes in pulmonary vascular loading also influence timing. The pulmonary component consequently occurs later, widening the physiological split. The left heart may receive transiently less filling during inspiration, contributing to the separation.

The important point is that the two ventricles are not exact mechanical duplicates. Electrical activation occurs in close succession, but the durations of pressure development and ejection depend on separate loading conditions. Physiological splitting of S2 demonstrates that a surface sound can contain two closely spaced mechanical events even when the heart is operating normally. This observation is not a reason to infer two separate ventricular beats.

The third sound, S3, is a low-frequency vibration occurring during early diastolic filling, after the second sound. It is associated with the dynamics of rapid filling and deceleration of incoming blood and ventricular structures. It is not the ordinary sound of the mitral valve opening. It can occur physiologically in children, young adults and some other high-flow or compliant states, while in other contexts it may accompany abnormal filling or volume loading. Its interpretation depends on the age and physiological condition of the individual.

The fourth sound, S4, occurs late in diastole during atrial contraction against a ventricle that resists filling. It is associated with atrial-driven motion of blood and the ventricular wall, often when ventricular compliance is reduced. A coordinated atrial contraction is normally required for this mechanism, so an S4 attributable to an atrial kick is not expected during atrial fibrillation. Its presence does not establish a particular disease mechanism by itself.

The distinction between compliance and filling flow clarifies why S3 and S4 are not interchangeable. Early rapid filling can cause a vibration even without active atrial force. Late filling against substantial passive resistance can produce a different vibration through a different force history. Both are diastolic in the ventricular sense, but their positions in the cycle differ. A question giving a low-frequency sound immediately before S1 should direct attention to atrial systole and late filling rather than early rapid filling.

Between the normal major heart sounds, blood moves through open valves. Normal flow is not necessarily audible at the body surface. Abnormal jets or flow through narrowed openings can produce prolonged acoustic vibrations called murmurs, which differ in duration from the discrete closure-associated sounds. A narrowed aortic outlet, for example, affects systolic ejection; reverse flow through an incompetent mitral valve occurs when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure during systole. The timing identifies the pressure gradient capable of driving the abnormal flow, though it does not by itself establish a clinical diagnosis.

Phonocardiography is most instructive when signals are aligned on a common time axis. A sound recorded just after the start of QRS and the ventricular pressure rise is consistent with S1. A sound recorded near the end of ejection with an arterial pressure notch is consistent with S2. A sound between S2 and the next P wave, during rapid volume increase, supports early diastolic origin. A sound after P and just before S1 supports late diastolic origin. This reasoning uses independent evidence to classify events rather than relying on an isolated sound recording.

9. Building an integrated Wiggers diagram

A single pressure waveform cannot reveal all events of the cardiac cycle. A pressure rise could represent a closed chamber developing force, or an open chamber ejecting blood. A volume decline could represent forward flow or a leak through an incompetent inlet. An electrocardiogram identifies electrical activity but does not directly measure flow. Physiological understanding therefore requires simultaneous comparison of several signals.

The integrated time diagram commonly associated with Carl J. Wiggers places ventricular, atrial and arterial pressures on a common time axis together with ventricular volume, ECG and heart sounds. This representation makes it possible to examine the causal order of events without assuming that peaks in different variables are interchangeable. The modern teaching diagram is a schematic synthesis; the individual traces vary from person to person and across experimental conditions. A textbook illustration is not necessarily a recording from an identifiable experimental subject.

To construct the diagram, start with three pressure curves. Ventricular pressure is low during much of diastole, rises steeply at the onset of contraction, reaches a maximum during ejection and then falls rapidly. Aortic pressure remains relatively high even when the ventricular pressure is low. It increases during ejection and declines between ejection periods. The atrial curve stays at much lower pressures than the ventricular systolic and aortic curves, but shows smaller features related to its mechanical activity and filling.

The first major valve event in a ventricular-centred cycle is mitral closure. It can be inferred from ventricular pressure rising above atrial pressure. Draw a vertical guide through this crossing. The ventricular volume is then at its upper plateau. The first sound appears near this guide. If an ECG is superimposed, ventricular depolarisation has already begun.

Find the second event by following the ventricular pressure curve upward until it crosses the aortic pressure curve. This is the approximate opening time of the aortic valve. The upper plateau on the volume trace ends and the volume begins to decline. The interval between the first and second guide lines is isovolumetric contraction. The QRS complex is often substantially complete by the early part of mechanical systole; it is much shorter than the whole ejection period.

During ejection, the ventricular and aortic pressure curves are close but not identical. A small forward gradient through the outlet, together with acceleration and inertia, governs flow. The ventricular volume declines steeply at first and then more slowly. The T wave is typically located toward the later part of mechanical systole, when repolarisation is occurring. It is incorrect to draw ejection ending simply at the peak of ventricular pressure or at the first deflection of T.

The third event is aortic closure. On a central aortic pressure record it is near the dicrotic notch. The volume stops declining and remains at ESV for a short interval. S2 is placed near this event. The ventricular pressure can then decline rapidly without net transfer of blood through either valve. Although arterial pressure is falling during the same interval, it remains much higher than ventricular pressure and keeps the aortic valve closed.

The fourth event occurs when ventricular pressure falls sufficiently below atrial pressure. The mitral valve opens, the ventricular volume increases, and the atrial pressure falls as blood transfers into the ventricle. The earliest part of filling is generally rapid. As the driving gradient and ventricular relaxation change, the slope becomes less steep. The P wave of the succeeding beat appears late in diastole, and atrial contraction follows it.

Notice that the four guides divide a ventricular cycle into filling, isovolumetric contraction, ejection and isovolumetric relaxation. The filling phase can be subdivided into rapid filling, diastasis and atrial systole; the ejection phase can be subdivided into rapid and reduced ejection. This hierarchy reconciles apparently different textbook counts of phases. They describe the same physiological sequence with different levels of detail.

A common diagram-reading task asks which phase is indicated by a flat volume line. There are two major possibilities. A high-volume plateau and rising pressure indicate isovolumetric contraction, while a low-volume plateau and falling pressure indicate isovolumetric relaxation. The word flat alone is insufficient. Similarly, a falling pressure curve may belong to reduced ejection or relaxation. Check the volume slope and valve events to resolve the ambiguity.

The vertical scale of the ECG has electrical units, normally millivolts, while chamber pressure uses mmHg and chamber volume uses mL. A Wiggers diagram may align these traces in separate panels or place several pressure curves on the same scale. Their horizontal alignment represents time, not equality of their vertical values. A P-wave amplitude of one millimetre on the printed diagram has no direct physical relation to a one-millimetre change in a pressure tracing.

A practical strategy is to reconstruct the diagram from memory with only four rules: the ventricular volume rises with net inflow, falls with net outflow, remains approximately constant with both valves closed, and the direction of valve flow follows the appropriate pressure gradient in the simplified model. Add electrical events before their main mechanical consequences, and add sounds around valve closure. This approach generates the main relationships even when unfamiliar axis scales or different starting points are used in an examination.

10. Converting time recordings into a pressure–volume loop

A Wiggers diagram shows what happens at each time. A pressure–volume loop instead pairs ventricular pressure with ventricular volume at every instant of the same cycle. Pressure is conventionally placed on the vertical axis and volume on the horizontal axis. Time is not plotted on a separate axis; it is implicit in the order of successive points. A pressure–volume loop can therefore look different from either individual time trace even though it contains the same paired pressure and volume measurements.

Imagine measuring ventricular pressure and volume simultaneously at a series of instants. At the end of filling, volume is high and pressure relatively low. Mark this point on the lower right of a graph. The inlet closes and ventricular pressure rises at almost constant volume, so subsequent points lie above the starting point. Once the outlet opens, volume decreases as ejection proceeds. The trace moves leftward through a high-pressure region. At outlet closure the volume is at its minimum. Pressure now decreases at almost constant volume, producing a descending limb on the left. Inlet opening allows the volume to increase along the lower part of the loop toward the right.

With the conventional axes the normal ventricular loop runs counterclockwise. The lower limb represents filling, the right limb isovolumetric contraction, the upper limb ejection and the left limb isovolumetric relaxation. If a figure reverses an axis, the apparent direction can reverse; always read the labels. Do not infer that time runs uniformly along the geometrical path. A long segment may occupy a short or long duration depending on the rate at which the points were traversed.

The four corners represent valve events, not peaks of pressure. In the idealised loop, the lower right corner is mitral closure, the upper right is aortic opening, the upper left is aortic closure, and the lower left is mitral opening. Actual loops have curved transitions rather than perfect rectangular corners. Aortic pressure changes during ejection, ventricular pressure depends on activation and geometry, and valve opening and closure are not instantaneous. A perfectly rectangular teaching loop represents a deliberate simplification.

The difference between the extreme horizontal coordinates gives the ventricular volume change over ejection, EDV minus ESV. It is the width of the loop. The vertical height relates to the pressure range developed over the cycle, but the maximum pressure may occur well after ejection begins. Pressure at aortic opening is closely related to arterial pressure at that instant; pressure at the end of ejection is generally different from the maximum pressure of the beat.

The filling curve is not usually horizontal. Although relaxation may briefly lower pressure during early filling, further addition of volume eventually stretches myocardial and extracellular structures. Pressure tends to rise increasingly as the chamber fills. The passive end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship, or EDPVR, describes this increasing resistance when compared under suitable conditions. It is different from a single filling trajectory, which can include ongoing active relaxation, recoil and atrial contraction.

Compliance describes the change in volume for a small change in distending pressure. It has units of mL per mmHg when using these conventional units. Its reciprocal is a measure of stiffness. Compliance depends on the point of the pressure–volume curve at which it is measured. A chamber may be relatively compliant at moderate filling but considerably less compliant when already distended. A single ratio of EDV to end-diastolic pressure is not a valid estimate of differential compliance unless an appropriate model and reference state are specified.

A second relationship, the end-systolic pressure–volume relationship or ESPVR, is constructed by comparing end-systolic points from multiple beats under different loading conditions while attempting to hold the contractile state stable. A simplified representation treats this relationship as approximately linear over a useful range. A change in slope can indicate a change in ventricular contractile performance under controlled assumptions. A line drawn through one end-systolic point is not experimentally sufficient to determine the relationship; multiple conditions or an explicitly justified single-beat estimation method are needed.

The normal loop lies between the passive mechanical constraints during filling and the active pressure-generating capability during systole. The distinction is important: an upward shift of end-diastolic pressure at a given volume suggests altered filling mechanics or loading context, whereas a steeper end-systolic relationship suggests altered systolic pressure-generating capacity. The two changes can coexist, but one does not logically imply the other.

The loop is a useful compact representation of pumping mechanics but has information loss. Two loops with very similar shapes could be traversed at different heart rates or have different early and late ejection profiles. They could therefore have different cardiac output per minute or different timing of sounds. A pressure–volume loop should be combined with time recordings when the scientific question involves relaxation duration, ejection rate or electrical–mechanical coupling.

11. Stroke volume, stroke work and the units of mechanical energy

Quantitative reasoning begins with conservation of volume. Let ventricular volume be V(t). In an incompressible fluid approximation, its rate of change equals the inflow rate minus the outflow rate. During filling with a competent closed outlet, the volume increase reflects net inlet flow. During ejection with a competent closed inlet, the negative volume derivative reflects net outlet flow. Integrating the outflow over the ejection interval gives the volume delivered per beat. If no leakage or alternative route exists, it must equal EDV minus ESV.

For an illustrative cycle with EDV 130 mL and ESV 60 mL, subtract the final volume from the initial volume to obtain a stroke volume of 70 mL. The ratio of this difference to EDV is the ejection fraction, which is dimensionless. Multiplying by 100 gives approximately 53.8 percent. The distinction matters because two ventricles can have equal ejection fraction but different absolute delivery. A ventricle with EDV 100 mL and ESV 40 mL ejects 60 mL, while one with EDV 150 mL and ESV 60 mL ejects 90 mL. Both have an ejection fraction of 60 percent.

Pressure–volume analysis also gives an energy measure. Pressure is force per unit area. If a piston of area A moves a small distance dx against pressure P, the work magnitude is force multiplied by displacement, or P A dx. The corresponding change in fluid volume has magnitude A dx. Thus pressure multiplied by volume change has the dimensions of work. The same physical principle applies to a deforming cardiac cavity even though its geometry is more complicated than that of a piston.

For the sign convention in which dV represents the change in ventricular cavity volume, ejection has negative dV. Work done by the ventricle on its contents during ejection can be written as the negative integral of P dV along the ejection path. Filling has positive dV and represents work in the opposite direction in the net accounting. For a closed counterclockwise loop with pressure on the vertical axis and volume on the horizontal axis, the net external stroke work is the negative closed-path integral of P dV. Its positive magnitude is the area enclosed by the loop.

This area is not simply peak pressure multiplied by EDV. The cavity does not eject all its starting volume, pressure is not constant during ejection, and filling occurs at a nonzero pressure. A rectangular approximation uses a representative ejection pressure minus a representative filling pressure, multiplied by the stroke volume. It is useful for examining units and approximate comparisons, but a measured curved loop should be integrated from the pressure and volume data instead.

Consider an illustrative rectangular loop with mean ejection pressure 100 mmHg, mean filling pressure 10 mmHg and width 70 mL. The approximate net external work is (100 − 10) × 70 = 6300 mmHg mL. One mmHg is approximately 133.322 Pa, and one mL is 0.000001 cubic metres. A pascal multiplied by a cubic metre is a joule. Therefore 1 mmHg mL is approximately 0.000133322 J. Multiplication gives approximately 0.840 J of net external work for this simplified left ventricular beat.

To estimate average external mechanical power, multiply the work per beat by the number of beats per second. At 75 beats per minute, the frequency is 1.25 beats per second. A work of 0.840 J per beat gives about 1.05 W. This is an illustrative net hydraulic power, not the total metabolic power consumed by the myocardium. Some metabolic energy is used in force development without external cavity-volume work, excitation, ion transport, calcium handling and heat production.

Isovolumetric contraction illustrates the difference between mechanical energy measures. The ventricle expends metabolic energy and develops wall stress, yet its cavity volume changes very little. The external pressure–volume work during that idealised segment is consequently near zero. It would be incorrect to conclude that no biochemical energy is used. The external loop area describes only net work associated with changing cavity volume against pressure, not the complete energy budget of living cardiac muscle.

For a nonrectangular loop, one can approximate the net area by calculating the area between the ejection and filling curves across the common volume range. At each volume, subtract filling pressure from ejection pressure. Integrate this difference over volume. With discrete points, approximate each small interval as a trapezoid: multiply the interval width by the average of the pressure differences at its two ends. The approximation improves when the recording has sufficient resolution and the measurements are accurate.

Suppose a teaching dataset gives pressure differences of 70, 100, 105 and 75 mmHg at volumes 50, 70, 90 and 110 mL. The first interval contributes 20 × (70 + 100)/2 = 1700 mmHg mL. The second contributes 20 × (100 + 105)/2 = 2050 mmHg mL. The third contributes 20 × (105 + 75)/2 = 1800 mmHg mL. The total estimate is 5550 mmHg mL or approximately 0.740 J. Here the loop is simplified by assuming both connecting limbs occur at the stated extreme volumes.

These numbers are hypothetical and are not claims about measurements in a particular subject. Their purpose is to show that a larger pressure range does not necessarily imply greater work if the volume range is simultaneously reduced. Compare two simplified loops: the first has a width of 80 mL and average pressure difference of 80 mmHg, while the second has a width of 50 mL and average difference of 110 mmHg. Their areas are 6400 and 5500 mmHg mL respectively. The second develops the larger mean pressure difference but transfers less net external work per beat.

The work performed on each circuit also depends on its pressure. If the right and left ventricles deliver the same stroke volume but the right ventricle develops a much lower ejection pressure, the right ventricular external stroke work is normally lower. This does not imply that right ventricular pumping is biologically less important. Failure of either pump eventually limits the serial circulation. The pressure–volume representation distinguishes flow equality from energy equality.

12. Left and right ventricular cycles compared

The right heart receives systemic venous blood and delivers it into the pulmonary circulation. The left heart receives pulmonary venous blood and delivers it into the systemic circulation. Both pumps follow the same broad sequence of inlet filling, contraction, outlet ejection and relaxation. Their valves are arranged in the same functional order, but they operate against quite different downstream pressures.

Typical illustrative resting adult pressures are approximately 120 mmHg at peak left ventricular systole and 25 mmHg at peak right ventricular systole. Aortic diastolic pressure may be around 80 mmHg, whereas pulmonary arterial diastolic pressure may be around 8–12 mmHg. Values vary with physiological state and method of measurement; the important examination concept is the lower pressure of the pulmonary circuit, not a rigid single normal number.

The right ventricle must develop sufficient pressure to open the pulmonary valve, but this required pressure is usually much lower than the pressure needed to open the aortic valve. Its wall is therefore adapted to a lower normal pressure load. The ventricular wall thickness and cavity geometry differ, and the interventricular septum is shared. The left ventricular pressure rise is not independent of right ventricular shape or volume because the two cavities occupy a mechanically connected structure.

A right ventricular pressure–volume loop is often less rectangular than a left ventricular loop. In particular, continued ejection while pressure changes and the lower pulmonary arterial load can make the constant-volume phases less conspicuous. The idealised four-phase description remains useful, but it would be misleading to copy the left ventricular waveform onto the right heart and simply change the pressure scale. The shape of the loop reflects the interaction between ventricular activation and vascular impedance.

In a stable closed circulation the outputs of the two ventricles must closely match over sufficient time. They need not be precisely equal on every individual beat. Breathing can alter venous inflow to the right heart and redistribute blood within the pulmonary circulation. A temporary difference between right and left output can be accommodated as a transient change in stored blood volume. Persistent unequal output, by contrast, would lead to progressive redistribution until new mechanical conditions or compensations changed the flows.

The lower pulmonary arterial pressure is advantageous because pulmonary exchange surfaces are thin and close to a large capillary network. A low-resistance circulation allows substantial flow without requiring systemic levels of pressure generation. This physiological arrangement does not eliminate the right ventricle's sensitivity to increased pulmonary vascular load. If that load rises substantially, the right ventricle must develop higher pressures and may eject less from the same starting volume.

An experiment comparing ventricular loops must therefore measure both actual chamber pressures and volumes, rather than infer one from the other. In a simplified example, both ventricles eject 70 mL. If the effective mean pressure differences for external work are 90 mmHg on the left and 15 mmHg on the right, the respective approximate external works are 6300 and 1050 mmHg mL. Their ratio is six to one. The output volumes are equal, but the mechanical energy delivered to the two circulations differs.

Respiratory variation provides an accessible example of interaction between the two pumps. In spontaneous inspiration, systemic venous return to the right heart tends to increase. The right ventricular ejection interval may become longer. Simultaneously, the left ventricular filling state can change because pulmonary blood storage and the shared mechanical environment vary. The resulting shifts in timing help explain normal respiratory variation in the separation of semilunar closure sounds. A simple fixed-delay model of the conduction system cannot explain these load-dependent timing changes on its own.

13. Changing heart rate, filling and arterial pressure

Heart rate determines how much time is available for the entire cycle. If the frequency is H beats per minute, the average duration of a beat is 60/H seconds. At 75 beats per minute, the duration is 0.80 seconds; at 120 beats per minute, it is 0.50 seconds. These calculations are exact for a regular rhythm with the specified rate. The durations of individual mechanical phases, however, do not all scale in direct proportion to the total cycle length.

At moderate increases in rate, the diastolic interval commonly shortens proportionally more than the systolic interval. Diastasis may be the first part of filling to become very short because it occurs after the rapid early influx and before atrial contraction. At still higher rates early and late filling waves may approach one another or fuse. If the ventricular muscle relaxes insufficiently between beats, the available filling pressure gradient may be reduced despite increased demand for blood delivery.

It is tempting to predict that a faster rate always produces a smaller EDV. That prediction is reasonable only under a specified set of constraints. During exercise, for example, changes in venous return, ventricular relaxation and contractility can support filling and delivery despite a shorter cycle. A controlled pacing experiment at fixed venous pressure is not equivalent to the integrated physiological response of a person exercising. State the variables held constant before interpreting the result.

Filling changes can be isolated conceptually by comparing two beats with the same downstream pressure and contractile state but different starting volume. A greater initial filling volume stretches the ventricular wall and increases the starting length of many sarcomeres. Length-dependent activation tends to support increased force development and ejection. The pressure–volume loop moves toward a larger EDV and often becomes wider under these conditions. The detailed Frank–Starling mechanism and its relationship to venous return belong to the next node.

Now imagine changing only the pressure in the arterial outlet immediately before a beat. A higher arterial pressure makes the opening threshold higher. The ventricle initially generates pressure at nearly constant volume until that threshold is reached. The ejection phase then occurs against a greater pressure load. With unchanged EDV and contractile state, the ventricle generally ejects less and finishes with a larger ESV. Its loop tends to become taller and narrower. This is a first-beat comparison, not a prediction of a fully compensated long-term circulation.

Increasing contractility at matched loading conditions produces a different pattern. The ventricle can generate greater active pressure at a given volume and tends to achieve a smaller ESV. The loop may expand leftward, increasing stroke volume. If EDV and arterial pressure are not controlled, however, the change in ESV cannot by itself identify the cause. A fall in arterial load could also improve emptying. Mechanistic inference requires distinguishing changed loading from changed intrinsic force generation.

Filling and ejection changes affect loop area in ways that cannot always be predicted from the width alone. A wider loop at lower pressure could have a smaller area than a narrow loop at high pressure. An examination may specify only the changes in EDV and ESV and then ask about work. Unless the pressure trajectory is also known or constrained, a unique conclusion about the change in external work is not justified.

Another useful perturbation is slowed relaxation at an unchanged peak pressure-generating capability. The ventricular pressure declines more slowly after ejection. If atrial pressure remains the same, inlet opening may be delayed. The early filling period is then shortened, and EDV can be reduced unless subsequent filling compensates. If atrial pressure rises, the inlet might open at a similar time despite impaired relaxation. The time of a single valve event is thus not a load-independent measure of relaxation.

These comparisons explain why a set of pressure–volume loops obtained under controlled changes can be more informative than a single resting measurement. The direction of change in loop shape identifies a plausible mechanical effect, while measurements of rate, loading pressures and relaxation provide constraints needed for causal interpretation. For the next chapter, preserve the distinction between pressure, volume, flow and mechanical work: each is necessary, but none substitutes for all the others.

14. Measuring a cardiac cycle: what each instrument can establish

Physiological diagrams become meaningful when we understand how the underlying variables are measured. The investigator cannot directly observe pressure, chamber volume and electrical activity simply by looking at a beating heart. Each variable requires a measurement method with its own assumptions. A coherent model of the cardiac cycle must be compatible with several independent kinds of measurement rather than being inferred from a single instrument.

A pressure sensor placed in the ventricular cavity measures local fluid pressure. When a fluid-filled catheter is connected to an external transducer, pressure is transmitted through the fluid column to the sensing element. When a sensor is located near the catheter tip, pressure can be converted into an electrical signal close to the measurement site. Both methods produce a numerical waveform only after calibration. The recorded signal should identify the pressure corresponding to a known reference and the change in voltage associated with a known change in pressure.

The reference level is important because a column of fluid has a hydrostatic pressure difference between heights. If the external transducer is raised relative to the intended reference, the measured pressure of a static fluid column is reduced. If the transducer is lowered, it is increased. A disagreement of a few mmHg matters especially when comparing atrial and ventricular pressures during filling. It matters less proportionally for a large ventricular systolic pressure, but it can still affect interpretation. Comparing two calibrated sensors requires a consistent reference.

An instrument may also distort rapid pressure changes. A poorly responding fluid-filled system can damp sharp features, whereas an oscillatory response can introduce exaggerated peaks. A slow rise recorded by the instrument is not automatically evidence that the ventricle develops force slowly. One should distinguish the dynamic behaviour of the sensor from the biological variable. This is why independent recordings, calibration checks and appropriate temporal sampling matter when measuring valve timing or the maximal slope of pressure rise.

The derivative of pressure with respect to time, dP/dt, quantifies how rapidly ventricular pressure changes. The units are mmHg per second when pressure is expressed in mmHg and time in seconds. A positive derivative indicates increasing pressure and a negative derivative indicates decreasing pressure. During isovolumetric contraction the positive derivative can be large. During relaxation the derivative can be substantially negative. These observations describe the waveform, but dP/dt is influenced by loading and measurement conditions as well as intrinsic myocardial performance.

For a simple illustrative interval, suppose ventricular pressure changes from 20 to 70 mmHg over 0.025 s. The average derivative is (70 − 20)/0.025 = 2000 mmHg/s. A second interval in which pressure falls from 50 to 10 mmHg over 0.040 s has an average derivative of −1000 mmHg/s. These are interval averages, not instantaneous maximal rates. If a student has only these two end-point measurements, it is impossible to determine the maximum instantaneous derivative uniquely because the slope may vary within each interval.

An ECG electrode detects electrical potential differences at the surface of the body or at another recording location. Its signal provides a convenient time reference for a mechanical measurement, especially the onset of ventricular depolarisation. However, the electrical recording does not calibrate the pressure sensor. An ECG can remain interpretable even when the pressure waveform is distorted by instrumentation. Conversely, the pressure trace may clearly demonstrate ventricular contraction despite difficulty identifying a particular ECG deflection.

Echocardiography uses reflected ultrasound to construct images of cardiac structures. Serial images can show ventricular boundaries moving through the beat and the opening or closure of valves. A chamber volume can be estimated by tracing its internal outline and applying a geometric reconstruction. In an ideal method, a set of slices represents the cavity as a stack of small discs whose volumes are summed. The method is conceptually related to integrating cross-sectional area along an axis.

A single diameter does not uniquely determine ventricular volume unless a particular geometric shape is assumed. A ventricle that changes shape during contraction can violate a simple fixed-shape model. The apparent cavity boundary also depends on image orientation and on identifying the inner myocardial surface. Thus a volume estimate is not a direct count of molecules of blood. The reliability of EDV and ESV depends on acquiring and interpreting images consistently.

Doppler ultrasound provides information about the component of flow velocity along the ultrasound beam. The frequency shift of reflected ultrasound depends on the motion of scatterers relative to the beam. When the beam is aligned with the direction of blood flow, the measured velocity component is largest. An oblique beam measures a smaller component unless the angle is accounted for. This distinction is important when comparing peak flow velocities with pressure gradients or estimating the volume passing through a valve.

The transmitral E and A waves discussed earlier are velocity features, not direct ventricular volume measurements. The instantaneous volume flow depends on the velocity distribution and effective area of the opening. If the effective area is larger, the same mean velocity can correspond to a greater flow rate. A larger peak velocity need not imply a larger total filling volume because the peak may be brief or the area may be different. To infer an integrated volume, additional geometric and temporal information is required.

An idealised outflow measurement can combine area with a velocity-time integral. Suppose the mean through-plane velocity at the outlet has been estimated over an ejection interval. Integrating velocity over time gives a distance, sometimes called a velocity-time integral. Multiplying by the effective cross-sectional area gives a volume under the assumption that the measured velocity represents an appropriate spatial average and the area is effectively constant. The calculation is dimensionally useful: square centimetres times centimetres equals cubic centimetres, and one cubic centimetre equals one millilitre.

For an illustrative area of 3.0 square centimetres and a representative velocity-time integral of 22 cm, the estimated ejected volume is 66 cubic centimetres, or 66 mL. If simultaneous cavity measurements give EDV 126 mL and ESV 60 mL, the two estimates agree in this hypothetical competent-valve experiment. Disagreement in a real experiment could reflect leakage, inaccurate geometry, angle error, nonuniform velocity or different beats being compared. Agreement supports the model but does not eliminate every possible systematic error.

Another method of recording ventricular volume uses electrical conductance measurements within a cavity. The conductivity of blood permits a sensor arrangement to obtain a signal related to the dimensions of the blood-filled region. Calibration must distinguish contributions from blood and surrounding tissue and account for geometry. Simultaneous pressure and calibrated volume signals permit pressure–volume loops to be constructed directly. A detailed specialist protocol is unnecessary for the examination; the important principle is that a loop requires paired, time-aligned pressure and volume observations.

The earliest mechanical physiology experiments used instruments that translated pressure or movement into visible records. Modern electronic sensors and imaging offer greater resolution, but the logic of evidence has not changed. A pressure crossing, a valve movement and a change in chamber volume are different observables. If a theory predicts that inlet opening follows a reversed atrioventricular pressure gradient and is followed by increasing ventricular volume, the prediction can be checked against three kinds of evidence. Convergence of measurements gives stronger support than merely recognising the expected pattern in a textbook figure.

A fair comparison of two cycles requires that the recordings describe equivalent physiological conditions. Suppose a pressure trace is recorded during a quiet breath and an ultrasound volume trace is obtained several seconds later during a deep inspiration. Combining the two as if they came from a single beat may create a pressure–volume path that never occurred. Synchronised acquisition, or a carefully justified ensemble average over comparable cycles, is preferable. Averaging can conceal beat-to-beat variation, so the scientific question determines whether an average or individual beats are more informative.

The same caution applies to experimental models. Isolated hearts permit controlled changes in perfusion and load, but neural and hormonal influences may be absent. Intact animals preserve the integrated circulation but make it harder to change one variable without affecting another. Human noninvasive measurements can reveal timing and flow without catheterisation, but they may not directly provide every chamber pressure. No single method answers every question. Understanding what a measurement establishes, and what it does not establish, is a central part of Part C reasoning.

15. Electrical activation, mechanical latency and refractoriness

The previous electrophysiology and ECG nodes establish how an action potential propagates through the cardiac conduction system. Here the essential connection is that electrical excitation initiates a sequence of events leading to force, rather than constituting force itself. A student should be able to align a voltage waveform with a pressure waveform without confusing their physical meanings.

During atrial depolarisation, membrane channels alter the movement of ions across atrial muscle cell membranes. Calcium-dependent activation subsequently develops atrial force. The resulting pressure increase depends not only on activation but on initial atrial volume, its mechanical environment and whether the atrioventricular valve is open. Two similar P waves can therefore be accompanied by different atrial contributions to ventricular filling. This is a general example of the distinction between the input signal and the mechanically loaded response.

The atrioventricular conduction delay allows atrial activation to precede the major development of ventricular pressure. A useful sequence is atrial depolarisation, atrial mechanical contraction, completion of ventricular filling, ventricular depolarisation and then ventricular pressure development. The events overlap rather than occurring as perfectly separated blocks. The conduction delay is particularly valuable when a final atrial contribution is needed to complete filling.

A ventricular action potential has an extended plateau, during which calcium entry supports contraction. The long refractory interval limits the ability of closely repeated stimuli to generate independent contractions throughout systole. Unlike many skeletal muscles, the normal ventricular myocardium cannot sustain a fused tetanic contraction as its usual pumping mechanism. A sustained contraction would prevent effective ventricular relaxation and refilling. The electrical and mechanical timing is therefore compatible with the functional requirement for alternating pressure production and filling.

The electrocardiogram is a summation of extracellular potential differences produced by the distributed electrical activity of the heart. It is not a direct intracellular action-potential recording. The QRS duration therefore reflects the spread of ventricular activation, not the duration of the plateau in every individual cell. Ventricular force remains elevated long after the rapid spread of depolarisation has produced the QRS complex. A student who equates QRS duration with the duration of ventricular systole would underestimate mechanical systole substantially.

In a simplified illustrative beat, ventricular depolarisation begins at time zero. Mitral closure is recorded 30 ms later, and aortic opening occurs at 85 ms. Aortic closure occurs at 340 ms, followed by mitral opening at 400 ms. The electrical-to-mitral-closure interval in this example is 30 ms, the isovolumetric contraction interval is 55 ms, the ejection interval is 255 ms and the isovolumetric relaxation interval is 60 ms. These differences describe the specified hypothetical measurements, not universal timing constants.

The intervals in such a dataset can answer mechanistic questions. If a perturbation leaves the QRS onset unchanged but delays aortic opening, several explanations are possible. Ventricular pressure might rise more slowly, the arterial opening threshold might be higher, or mitral closure might occur later. Recording the pressure at the time of opening and the pressure slope during the preceding interval helps discriminate these alternatives. An unchanged electrical reference alone cannot select a unique mechanical cause.

Similarly, an abnormal delay between ventricular activation and the development of sufficient pressure can reflect altered electromechanical coordination rather than a complete lack of electrical activity. The QRS complex indicates that electrical activation has occurred; it does not guarantee an adequate stroke volume. Even a strongly contracting ventricle may fail to deliver its usual output if the arterial outlet cannot open or if blood is diverted through an incompetent valve.

When ventricular repolarisation is represented by the T wave, different regions are recovering their resting electrical states over time. The decline of mechanical force depends on calcium removal and cross-bridge detachment. A change in the timing of T therefore cannot be translated directly into an equal change in the timing of mitral opening. Arterial pressure, atrial pressure and the rate of ventricular pressure decline also determine the mechanical intervals.

At a high pacing rate, the time between successive electrical activations is shorter. The tissue's electrical recovery and mechanical recovery both matter. Incomplete relaxation can influence the pressure from which the next beat begins, and less filling time can influence its starting volume. Comparing beats with different preceding intervals illustrates that the mechanical effect of an excitation depends on the state of the tissue at the instant of activation. The ventricular response has a history; it does not begin from an identical mechanical state on every beat.

An examination may provide an ECG and a pressure trace from which the heart sounds have been removed. The order of inference should be to locate electrical activation, identify the ventricular pressure rise, determine when the atrioventricular gradient reverses, and then locate the arterial crossing. The first closure-associated sound belongs near the first crossing rather than necessarily at the maximum of QRS. The second belongs near the end of ejection rather than necessarily at the end of the T wave. This strategy remains valid when exact intervals differ from a familiar diagram.

The principal conceptual outcome is that cardiac conduction establishes the sequence of excitation, calcium-dependent activation creates the mechanical response, and pressure gradients determine the phases of flow. The three levels form a causal chain. Altering one level can change the next, but measuring a variable at one level does not always identify the quantitative state of another. This distinction is useful well beyond the present chapter, particularly in interpreting cardiac output and autonomic regulation.

16. Passive filling mechanics and the distinction between pressure and stretch

Why does a ventricle not fill indefinitely once its inlet has opened? Blood enters because a pressure difference and an available pathway permit flow. But the chamber is bounded by biological materials that resist deformation. As the cavity expands, myocardial fibres, connective tissue and the surrounding mechanical environment limit further expansion. The pressure required to add another small volume generally increases as the ventricle becomes more distended.

A passive pressure–volume relationship is usually nonlinear. At relatively low filling volumes the pressure can change little over a considerable volume interval. At high volumes the same additional volume can require a much larger pressure increase. This can be visualised by imagining a curve that becomes progressively steeper toward its right-hand end. The steepness is stiffness, while the inverse of steepness is differential compliance.

Consider a hypothetical passive dataset in which ventricular pressure is 4 mmHg at 80 mL, 6 mmHg at 100 mL and 14 mmHg at 120 mL. Between 80 and 100 mL, the average incremental compliance is 20/2 = 10 mL/mmHg. Between 100 and 120 mL it is 20/8 = 2.5 mL/mmHg. The same chamber appears more compliant in the first volume interval. These values are interval averages, not constants that can be applied to the whole curve.

Now compare two chambers at the same volume of 110 mL. If the first has passive pressure 8 mmHg and the second has pressure 16 mmHg, the second requires a greater distending pressure to maintain that volume, provided the external pressure and other conditions are comparable. It does not follow from these two readings alone that the second chamber has exactly half the differential compliance of the first. Compliance is a slope and requires a measured change in volume for a known change in pressure.

The pressure difference across a chamber wall is called transmural pressure. It is internal pressure minus external pressure. This distinction explains why an increased intracavitary pressure does not always mean increased stretch of myocardial fibres. If pressure outside the ventricle rises at the same time as the measured pressure inside it, the net distending pressure may change little. For example, if internal pressure rises from 8 to 12 mmHg and external pressure rises from 1 to 5 mmHg, the transmural pressure remains 7 mmHg in this hypothetical comparison.

The pericardium and neighbouring ventricle contribute to the mechanical environment surrounding the heart. A change in the volume of one chamber can alter the space available to another. At low total cardiac volume, the surrounding structures may impose relatively little constraint; at larger volumes, their influence may become more important. Thus ventricular end-diastolic pressure reflects multiple mechanical factors rather than only the passive stiffness of the isolated ventricular wall.

At the cellular level, sarcomeric proteins and extracellular connective tissue contribute to passive resistance. Titin spans part of the sarcomere and provides an elastic contribution as the sarcomere is stretched. Collagen in the extracellular matrix limits and distributes deformation at larger scales. Their relative importance varies with the degree of stretch and the condition of the myocardium. These structures are not responsible for generating the active pressure of systole in the same way that calcium-activated actin–myosin interactions are.

The distinction between active and passive pressure is essential during early diastole. If residual active force remains, the pressure measured at a given volume can exceed the pressure expected from passive tissue alone. Conversely, recoil can contribute to a rapid decline in cavity pressure during the transition into filling. A single observed filling trace contains contributions from changing active tension, passive elasticity, external forces and blood motion. It cannot automatically be identified with a pure passive material curve.

To investigate passive mechanical properties more directly, one can compare end-diastolic pressure and volume under a set of carefully varied filling conditions while allowing sufficient time for relaxation. The design aims to change volume without changing unrelated properties. In an intact circulation, the loading intervention may trigger reflexes or alter external pressures, so these need consideration. If pressure–volume observations shift upward, the investigator must ask whether the cause is intrinsic passive stiffness, residual activation, altered external constraint or a combination.

The mechanical consequences of increased filling resistance are clearest when the driving pressure is controlled. At the same atrial pressure, a less compliant ventricle may accept less volume before its pressure approaches atrial pressure. If the circulation raises atrial pressure, the original volume may be partly restored at the expense of a higher upstream pressure. Thus a preserved EDV is not evidence that filling mechanics are normal. A comparison of pressure, volume and flow is more informative than any one of them.

Consider a conceptual circulation with two ventricular filling states. In state X, EDV is 120 mL at an end-diastolic pressure of 8 mmHg. In state Y, EDV is also 120 mL but pressure is 20 mmHg. If both eject 70 mL, their volume performance for that beat is identical. Yet the second state may impose a higher pressure on the upstream atrium and pulmonary veins if the elevated pressure persists during filling. Equal stroke volumes do not prove equal physiological cost.

The term preload, used in the next chapter, refers to the mechanical loading or stretch of cardiac muscle before contraction. EDV and end-diastolic pressure are common practical indicators, but neither is identical to myocardial fibre stretch in every circumstance. Chamber geometry, wall thickness and external pressure matter. For this node, the important foundation is to understand why a pressure coordinate and a volume coordinate are independent information. The pressure–volume graph deliberately retains both rather than collapsing them into a single filling index.

17. Testing competing explanations with controlled pressure–volume experiments

Pressure–volume relationships are particularly useful for designing experiments because different interventions make different first-order predictions. A rigorous experiment states which variable is manipulated, which variables are measured and which background conditions are maintained. It does not merely compare two loops and assign each to a memorised label.

Consider the question whether an observed fall in stroke volume was caused by an increase in arterial load or a reduction in intrinsic force generation. Both changes can increase ESV. To discriminate them, a controlled preparation could record pressure and volume before and after a change in arterial pressure while maintaining the initial filling volume and the activation conditions as closely as possible. An independent estimate of the end-systolic relationship under different loads would add evidence about the intrinsic pressure-generating state.

If the outlet pressure increases and the end-systolic points remain consistent with the original end-systolic relationship, the observation supports a load-related explanation. If the end-systolic relationship shifts such that less pressure is generated at comparable end-systolic volumes, altered contractility becomes a plausible additional explanation. In practice, the relationship is an approximation and the experimental intervention may influence multiple variables. The appropriate conclusion should state those assumptions.

A useful simplified mathematical model expresses end-systolic pressure as Ees times (Ves − V0). Here Ees is the slope of an approximate end-systolic pressure–volume relation, Ves is the end-systolic volume and V0 is the extrapolated zero-pressure volume intercept. Ees has units of pressure per volume, for example mmHg/mL. V0 is an extrapolated model parameter, not necessarily an anatomically accessible volume in the beating ventricle.

Suppose hypothetical end-systolic points from two controlled beats are (60 mL, 90 mmHg) and (80 mL, 130 mmHg). The slope is (130 − 90)/(80 − 60) = 2 mmHg/mL. Substituting into the first point gives 90 = 2(60 − V0), so V0 = 15 mL. Under this model, an end-systolic pressure of 110 mmHg corresponds to 70 mL. The calculation demonstrates why the same intrinsic slope permits different ESV values when pressure load changes.

This relationship is not a rule that end-systolic pressure is always identical to aortic systolic pressure. End-systolic pressure is defined by the chosen mechanical end-systolic point, while arterial peak pressure may occur earlier. Nor is the slope calculated from a single point by dividing pressure by volume unless the intercept is known to be zero. Many errors in graph questions arise from omitting the intercept or mixing different definitions of pressure.

The classic experimental approach associated with ventricular elastance involves recording pressure–volume relations at different filling conditions and times during the cycle. Such work, including studies by Suga and Sagawa, contributed to the distinction between changes caused by loading and changes in contractile state. The time-varying elastance idea represents the ventricle as a chamber whose relationship between pressure and volume changes over the course of a beat. A detailed research-level derivation is not necessary here. The basic evidence is that pressure at a particular volume differs markedly between relaxed and actively contracting states.

A suitable teaching experiment might collect several beats during a brief change in venous filling while recording ECG, ventricular pressure and cavity volume. The comparison should control the timing and contractile state as far as possible and avoid assuming that reflex changes are absent. End-systolic points can be plotted together to estimate a relation; end-diastolic points can be examined for the filling relation. If the interventions are rapid, some slow compensatory changes may be reduced, but this is not a guarantee that every variable remains constant.

Suppose two end-systolic lines are drawn with the same intercept but one has slope 2 and the other 3 mmHg/mL. At an end-systolic volume 65 mL and an intercept 15 mL, the corresponding predicted pressures are 100 and 150 mmHg. The steeper relationship represents a greater pressure at the specified volume in this simplified model. If the end-systolic pressure instead is held near 100 mmHg, the two predicted volumes are 65 and approximately 48.3 mL. The two ways of reading the same lines illustrate how a stronger pressure-generating state can result in a smaller residual volume under matched pressure load.

A second controlled question concerns relaxation. An investigator observes that the early transmitral velocity peak has fallen. This finding could result from slower ventricular relaxation, lower atrial pressure, a change in valve geometry or a combination. Simultaneous recording of atrial pressure and ventricular pressure reveals whether the early driving gradient changed. Imaging can indicate whether the inlet opening area changed. An isolated velocity measurement cannot discriminate all these causes.

One could compare ventricular pressure at two equal time points after aortic closure. If the pressure remains higher in one condition despite similar starting pressure and similar external loading, the finding is consistent with slower pressure decline. Yet even the decay of ventricular pressure depends on loading and mechanical state. A careful experiment does not equate a single time interval or pressure slope with a completely load-independent intrinsic relaxation constant.

A third question asks whether the net work per beat increased when a heart began ejecting against higher pressure. The necessary observations are the full pressure–volume path or sufficient points to estimate its area. If EDV is unchanged but ESV rises, the width decreases. If ejection pressure increases at the same time, the change in area can be positive, negative or small. A hypothesis about work based only on higher arterial pressure cannot be tested without volume data.

For an examination dataset with a fixed approximate end-diastolic pressure of 8 mmHg, compare a baseline ejection pressure of 88 mmHg and stroke volume of 75 mL with a second state having ejection pressure 108 mmHg and stroke volume 55 mL. The rectangular loop approximations are 80 × 75 = 6000 and 100 × 55 = 5500 mmHg mL. External work per beat has decreased in the second simplified state despite its higher pressure. If heart rate also changes, external work per minute must be evaluated separately.

Controlled comparisons illustrate a general principle of experimental physiology: the same final observation can have several causes. A single stroke volume, pressure maximum or ejection fraction constrains the mechanism but rarely identifies it uniquely. Multiple independent measurements and a carefully stated intervention make the inference stronger. This is why pressure–volume diagrams are valuable tools for physiological reasoning rather than merely illustrations of valve timing.

18. When the ideal four-phase loop is modified

The normal pressure–volume loop is constructed under the assumption that the ventricular inlet and outlet are competent one-way valves. Two nearly constant-volume phases then follow directly from the absence of flow paths. This elegant structure should be treated as a prediction of a particular biological arrangement. If the arrangement changes, the prediction can fail in ways that reveal the underlying mechanism.

An inlet that does not close competently permits reverse flow from ventricle to atrium when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure. In the left heart this can occur during systole through an incompetent mitral valve. The interval normally classified as isovolumetric contraction may now involve loss of ventricular volume before the aortic valve opens. During subsequent ejection the ventricle can deliver blood to both the arterial outlet and the atrium. The usual identity between cavity volume loss and forward arterial stroke volume is no longer valid.

Suppose a hypothetical ventricle begins systole at 150 mL and ends at 60 mL. Its total systolic volume loss is 90 mL. If a simultaneous flow estimate shows only 55 mL passing forward through the aorta and no other abnormal pathway is present, the remaining 35 mL is consistent with systolic reverse flow through the inlet. The effective forward stroke volume is 55 mL, not 90 mL. The cavity ejection fraction is 60 percent, but this apparently substantial volume fraction does not guarantee normal systemic delivery.

The atrial pressure waveform can provide additional evidence in this example. Reverse systolic flow into the atrium adds volume at a time when the atrium would normally be receiving venous return behind a closed inlet. It can exaggerate the late systolic pressure rise. However, the size of the atrial pressure change also depends on atrial compliance. A highly compliant atrium can accommodate a larger volume change with a smaller pressure rise. Therefore the magnitude of a v wave alone is not an exact measure of the regurgitant volume.

An incompetent outlet creates a different pattern. If blood flows back from the aorta into the left ventricle during diastole, ventricular volume can increase before the normal mitral opening event. The interval normally classified as isovolumetric relaxation then ceases to be truly constant-volume. During filling the ventricle receives blood through the normal inlet as well as through the abnormal reverse pathway. The resulting EDV depends on both sources and on the filling mechanics of the chamber.

For an illustrative outlet-leak cycle, suppose the systolic forward aortic volume is 100 mL but 30 mL returns through the aortic valve in diastole. The net aortic delivery per complete cycle is 70 mL. This distinction between gross forward flow and net flow is important. A large systolic ejection does not necessarily imply a large effective output over the full beat if a portion returns. Volume conservation must be applied over the appropriate time interval.

A narrowed but competent outlet produces yet another mechanism. To maintain flow through a smaller effective opening, velocity may increase and an additional pressure difference may be required. The ventricle can develop a much higher systolic pressure than the arterial pressure immediately downstream. In this case the pressure curves during ejection are no longer close together. A student reading simultaneous aortic and ventricular pressure traces should distinguish an increased pressure drop across the outlet from a uniform increase in arterial pressure.

The energetic consequences differ. A ventricle that generates high pressure upstream of a narrowed valve performs external work on its cavity contents, but some of that energy is dissipated across the obstruction rather than being transmitted as useful downstream arterial pressure energy. The ventricular pressure–volume loop measures the external cavity work; it does not separately quantify all losses in a valve jet. Two loops with the same area can therefore differ in effective energy delivered to the systemic circulation.

A narrowed inlet limits filling rather than directly obstructing ventricular ejection. It can maintain an atrioventricular pressure difference throughout much of diastole despite an open valve, because a larger pressure gradient is needed to drive a given flow through the narrowed pathway. A shorter diastolic interval may worsen the limitation on filling. The immediate mechanical reasoning is distinct from any clinical interpretation or treatment of valve disease.

The aim of these examples is to test the logic of the ideal model, not to provide diagnostic instruction. When an examination shows a loop without a vertical limb, ask whether cavity volume is changing during what would otherwise be a closed-valve phase. When a question compares cavity stroke volume with forward flow, check whether it explicitly states valve competence. When ventricular and arterial pressure differ greatly during ejection, consider the pressure loss across the outlet instead of assuming that the arterial trace has been mislabelled.

Other deviations arise even in healthy conditions. Small changes in valve leaflet configuration, motion of the annulus, measurement noise and the definition of the cavity boundary can cause a recorded volume to vary slightly during a nominally isovolumetric interval. The term isovolumetric represents a useful macroscopic approximation. It is not a demand that every real measurement contain an exactly vertical line. A meaningful deviation must be distinguished from the resolution and assumptions of the measurement.

These exceptions reinforce the most general rule in the chapter: cavity volume changes according to net inflow and outflow, and valve state constrains the available pathways. From that rule the student can reconstruct the normal cycle and reason about unfamiliar modifications without memorising every possible pressure–volume loop shape.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All chambers enter systole together

Correction and source of confusion: Atrial contraction occurs late in ventricular diastole. The unspecific use of systole encourages the confusion.

Examination reasoning: Name the chamber and align P and QRS with mechanical events.

2. Valves have a separate electrical opening command

Correction and source of confusion: Valves primarily follow pressure gradients and leaflet mechanics. Electrical activation acts through myocardium.

Examination reasoning: Compare pressures on both sides of the valve.

3. Papillary muscles pull valves open

Correction and source of confusion: Papillary muscles stabilise closed AV valves against prolapse. Chordae can resemble pull cords.

Examination reasoning: Identify the pressure gradient as the opening mechanism.

4. Isovolumetric means isobaric

Correction and source of confusion: Volume is constant but pressure changes; the similar prefixes obscure different variables.

Examination reasoning: Read the axes of a vertical PV limb.

5. Isovolumetric contraction consumes no ATP

Correction and source of confusion: Active tension and calcium cycling use ATP. Zero external volume work is not zero metabolism.

Examination reasoning: Separate the external work integral from energy use.

6. Aortic opening occurs at peak LV pressure

Correction and source of confusion: Opening is near the arterial pressure crossing, which precedes peak pressure. A single pressure curve can hide the threshold.

Examination reasoning: Locate the crossing on two pressure traces.

7. Falling LV pressure always means diastole

Correction and source of confusion: Pressure may fall during late ejection while the outlet remains open. Slopes are mistaken for phase boundaries.

Examination reasoning: Check volume slope and aortic closure.

8. T wave marks the exact instant of aortic closure

Correction and source of confusion: T is distributed repolarisation, not valve motion. Their proximity on graphs encourages conflation.

Examination reasoning: Use flow, sound and pressure evidence for closure.

9. S1 and S2 are valve-opening sounds

Correction and source of confusion: They are vibrations associated with AV and semilunar closure respectively. Sound timing near subsequent flow can mislead.

Examination reasoning: Link sounds to the onset of the closed-volume phases.

10. S3 and S4 describe the same event

Correction and source of confusion: S3 is early filling, S4 late atrial-driven filling. Both are diastolic sounds.

Examination reasoning: Locate the extra sound relative to organised atrial contraction.

11. Stroke volume always equals forward flow

Correction and source of confusion: Cavity loss may include regurgitant flow. The usual equation is applied without its assumptions.

Examination reasoning: Balance volumes through all paths.

12. EF and SV are identical

Correction and source of confusion: EF is a fraction; SV is a volume. Both describe emptying but have different units.

Examination reasoning: Calculate both from EDV and ESV.

13. The tallest PV loop necessarily has most work

Correction and source of confusion: Work depends on area, not maximum pressure alone. Vertical scale can dominate visual judgement.

Examination reasoning: Estimate the enclosed area.

14. Compliance equals EDV divided by pressure

Correction and source of confusion: Incremental compliance is ΔV/ΔP, not the ratio to zero. Passive mechanics are nonlinear.

Examination reasoning: Use the slope over the stated interval.

15. Equal right and left output means equal pressures

Correction and source of confusion: Serial flow equality does not imply equal vascular load. Flow and pressure are distinct.

Examination reasoning: Compare circuit resistance and mechanical work separately.

16. Faster rate always reduces EDV

Correction and source of confusion: Rate shortens available filling time, but venous return and relaxation can change. Controlled pacing and exercise are confused.

Examination reasoning: State which conditions are fixed.

17. Identical PV loops prove identical timing

Correction and source of confusion: The loop has no independent time axis. Students interpret path length as time.

Examination reasoning: Use simultaneous time recordings and rate.

18. Larger peak E velocity proves larger filling volume

Correction and source of confusion: Peak velocity is not integrated flow; valve area and duration matter.

Examination reasoning: Integrate representative flow over time.

19. Higher ESV proves reduced contractility

Correction and source of confusion: Loading and initial volume also affect ESV. Several mechanisms yield the same endpoint.

Examination reasoning: Compare controlled loads and end-systolic relations.

20. End-diastolic pressure universally equals fibre stretch

Correction and source of confusion: External pressure, geometry and passive mechanics alter the relation. A chamber pressure is not a length measurement.

Examination reasoning: Calculate transmural pressure and consider the mechanical context.

Original examination practice

These are original NET/SET-style questions, not previous-year questions. All numerical datasets are hypothetical. For Part C, justify the mechanism and identify assumptions before opening the explanation.

Part B — foundational and mechanistic questions

B1. Which pressure relation closes the mitral valve?

  1. LV exceeds LA
  2. LA exceeds LV
  3. Aorta exceeds LV
  4. LA equals atmospheric pressure
Answer and explanation

A. Rising ventricular pressure reverses the inlet gradient. LA above LV favours opening, whereas aortic pressure determines the outlet gradient.

B2. Which valves are closed in LV isovolumetric contraction?

  1. Mitral and aortic
  2. Aortic only
  3. Mitral only
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

A. Both pathways must close for substantial pressure development without volume loss; either open valve could allow flow.

B3. Which valve is open during ordinary LV filling?

  1. Aortic
  2. Mitral
  3. Pulmonary
  4. Neither left-heart valve
Answer and explanation

B. The open mitral valve permits net inflow from the atrium; high aortic pressure keeps the outlet closed.

B4. S1 occurs near which event?

  1. Atrial depolarisation
  2. Mitral opening
  3. Atrioventricular valve closure
  4. Semilunar closure
Answer and explanation

C. S1 is associated with vibrations around AV closure. Semilunar closure instead produces S2.

B5. The central aortic dicrotic notch is associated with:

  1. Aortic closure
  2. Mitral opening
  3. Atrial contraction
  4. QRS onset
Answer and explanation

A. Closure at the end of aortic ejection produces a small arterial pressure transient; the other events are not the end of ejection.

B6. Which ECG event normally precedes the major ventricular pressure rise?

  1. P alone
  2. QRS onset
  3. T end
  4. ST end
Answer and explanation

B. Ventricular depolarisation initiates excitation–contraction coupling. T reflects repolarisation and P represents atrial activation.

B7. The late transmitral A velocity wave is produced primarily by:

  1. Atrial contraction
  2. Aortic recoil
  3. Pulmonary closure
  4. Ventricular depolarisation
Answer and explanation

A. Atrial force raises the late filling gradient. E, in contrast, is the early diastolic velocity peak.

B8. Which atrial pressure wave reflects reservoir filling while the inlet is closed?

  1. a
  2. c
  3. v
  4. ECG T
Answer and explanation

C. Venous accumulation during ventricular systole produces v; a represents atrial contraction and c early systolic valve-related motion.

B9. The lower right corner of the normal LV PV loop represents:

  1. Aortic closure
  2. Mitral closure
  3. Mitral opening
  4. Peak pressure
Answer and explanation

B. At EDV the inlet closes and pressure rises. Aortic closure and mitral opening lie near the left boundary at ESV.

B10. With volume on the horizontal axis, the usual LV PV loop is traversed:

  1. Clockwise
  2. Counterclockwise
  3. Upward only
  4. Rightward only
Answer and explanation

B. Filling goes right, contraction up, ejection left and relaxation down; the direction follows the phase sequence.

B11. Stroke volume in a competent-valve heart without shunt is:

  1. EDV + ESV
  2. ESV − EDV
  3. EDV − ESV
  4. Peak pressure × EDV
Answer and explanation

C. Net systolic cavity loss equals forward delivery under these assumptions; adding or reversing the volume difference is incorrect.

B12. The enclosed PV-loop area represents:

  1. ECG energy
  2. Net external stroke work
  3. Maximum compliance
  4. Ejection time
Answer and explanation

B. Pressure multiplied by volume has energy dimensions, and the closed integral gives net external cavity work.

B13. Which phase has a nearly constant volume at ESV?

  1. Isovolumetric relaxation
  2. Rapid filling
  3. Atrial systole
  4. Isovolumetric contraction
Answer and explanation

A. Both valves are closed after ejection while pressure falls; contraction instead occurs at EDV.

B14. Physiological S2 splitting usually widens during inspiration because:

  1. Aortic valve stays open indefinitely
  2. RV ejection is prolonged relative to LV
  3. Atria depolarise twice
  4. S2 becomes S1
Answer and explanation

B. Respiratory loading changes delay pulmonary closure relative to aortic closure; the split does not represent a second beat.

B15. S4 is most closely associated with:

  1. Rapid early filling
  2. Atrial contraction against a relatively resistant ventricle
  3. Aortic opening
  4. Ventricular ejection
Answer and explanation

B. S4 is late diastolic and requires organised atrial contraction. S3 is associated with early filling.

B16. What are suitable units of ventricular compliance?

  1. mmHg/mL
  2. mL/mmHg
  3. J/s
  4. mmHg mL
Answer and explanation

B. Compliance is incremental volume change per pressure change; its reciprocal is stiffness.

B17. QRS primarily represents:

  1. Chamber pressure
  2. Entire mechanical systole
  3. Ventricular depolarisation
  4. Stroke work
Answer and explanation

C. QRS represents rapid distributed electrical activation; ventricular mechanical force persists longer and needs independent measurement.

B18. What ends normal LV isovolumetric relaxation?

  1. Aortic opening
  2. Mitral opening
  3. Atrial repolarisation alone
  4. S1
Answer and explanation

B. Once ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the inlet opens and volume can rise.

B19. Why is the normal RV loop lower in pressure than the LV loop?

  1. The RV does not contract
  2. The pulmonary circuit imposes lower pressure load
  3. RV blood has no mass
  4. RV output must always be smaller
Answer and explanation

B. Both pumps contract and their long-term outputs match; pulmonary vascular load is lower.

B20. During isovolumetric contraction myocardial ATP use is:

  1. Zero
  2. Present because active tension develops
  3. Impossible until aortic opening
  4. Identical to external work
Answer and explanation

B. Cross-bridge cycling and calcium handling consume energy even when external cavity-volume work is negligible.

B21. Pressure at aortic opening most closely reflects:

  1. Aortic pressure at that instant
  2. Later peak LV pressure
  3. Atrial a-wave maximum
  4. ESV
Answer and explanation

A. The arterial valve opens near the relevant pressure crossing. The pressure peak normally follows, and ESV is a volume.

B22. Diastasis means:

  1. Rapid closed-volume pressure rise
  2. Relatively slow mid-diastolic filling
  3. Both semilunar valves open
  4. All ventricular systole
Answer and explanation

B. After rapid early filling, the driving gradient declines; this interval may be shortened at high rates.

Part C — analytical and experimental reasoning

C1. LV, LA and aortic pressures are 45, 9 and 82 mmHg. LV volume is fixed at 128 mL while pressure rises. Identify the phase and valve states.

Answer and explanation

Isovolumetric contraction. LV exceeds LA but is below aortic pressure, closing both valves. The high volume and positive pressure slope distinguish it from relaxation.

C2. LV pressure is 35 mmHg and falling, LA 8, aorta 90; LV volume is fixed at 52 mL. Identify the phase and its endpoint.

Answer and explanation

Isovolumetric relaxation. Both valves remain closed; when LV falls below LA, mitral opening permits filling.

C3. EDV 142 mL and ESV 62 mL: calculate SV and EF, then compare with EDV 100 and ESV 40.

Answer and explanation

First SV = 80 mL, EF = 80/142 × 100 ≈ 56.3%. Second SV = 60 mL, EF = 60%. Greater absolute output need not mean greater fraction.

C4. A rectangular PV loop has mean ejection pressure 105 mmHg, filling pressure 5 mmHg and width 65 mL. Calculate work in J.

Answer and explanation

(105 − 5) × 65 = 6500 mmHg mL. Multiply by 0.000133322 J per unit to obtain 0.867 J approximately. This is external work, not total metabolic energy.

C5. Two loops have identical width. Mean ejection and filling pressures are 95 and 5 in A, 115 and 15 in B. Compare approximate net work.

Answer and explanation

Mean net pressure differences are 90 and 100 mmHg. B has 100/90 ≈ 1.11 times the work at matched width, assuming rectangular approximations.

C6. With QRS onset at zero, mitral closure at 35 ms, aortic opening at 90 ms, aortic closure at 320 ms and mitral opening at 380 ms, calculate the three intervals.

Answer and explanation

Isovolumetric contraction = 55 ms; ejection = 230 ms; isovolumetric relaxation = 60 ms. The initial 35 ms is electrical-to-closure latency.

C7. At fixed EDV and early pressure slope, aortic opening pressure rises. Predict the contraction interval.

Answer and explanation

It tends to lengthen because more pressure must be developed at the same rate. A change in starting pressure or slope would alter the prediction.

C8. With fixed EDV, ESV rises from 50 to 75 mL after outlet load increases. Predict loop width and whether work must rise.

Answer and explanation

The left boundary shifts right, reducing width by 25 mL. Work is not determined without pressure data because higher pressures compete with the smaller volume change.

C9. Cavity systolic loss is 95 mL but forward aortic flow is 60 mL; the only abnormal route is mitral regurgitation. Find reverse flow.

Answer and explanation

95 − 60 = 35 mL. Cavity loss includes both routes, so 95 mL overestimates forward delivery.

C10. An outlet leak returns 28 mL during diastole after 98 mL is ejected. Find net arterial delivery and describe the nominal relaxation limb.

Answer and explanation

70 mL. Reverse flow can increase cavity volume before the mitral valve opens, so the nominal isovolumetric relaxation limb is not vertical.

C11. Calculate beat duration at 72 and 120 beats/min and explain why diastole need not occupy half each beat.

Answer and explanation

60/72 = 0.833 s; 60/120 = 0.500 s. Mechanical phases change unequally with rate, especially the disproportionately shortened diastolic interval.

C12. A transmitral early velocity peak is larger in B than A, but effective area and duration are unknown. Does B necessarily receive more early filling volume?

Answer and explanation

No. Volume is the integral of flow, approximately area times representative velocity integrated over time. Peak velocity alone does not determine it.

C13. Pressure falls from 75 to 15 mmHg over 0.05 s in A and 0.10 s in B. Find average slopes and a limitation.

Answer and explanation

−1200 and −600 mmHg/s. B has slower average decline over the stated interval; loading, sensor dynamics and slope variability prevent equating the average with intrinsic relaxation alone.

C14. Passive pressure at 85, 105 and 125 mL is 5, 9 and 19 mmHg. Compare incremental compliance.

Answer and explanation

First 20/4 = 5 mL/mmHg; second 20/10 = 2 mL/mmHg. Distension reduces compliance in this illustrative dataset.

C15. Internal pressure rises from 10 to 16 mmHg while external pressure rises from 3 to 9 mmHg. Find transmural pressures.

Answer and explanation

Both equal 7 mmHg. Intracavitary pressure increases but net distending pressure is unchanged; stretch cannot be inferred from internal pressure alone.

C16. End-systolic points are (50 mL, 80 mmHg) and (70 mL, 120 mmHg). Estimate linear ESPVR slope and intercept, and predict volume at 100 mmHg.

Answer and explanation

Slope = 40/20 = 2 mmHg/mL. 80 = 2(50 − V0), so V0 = 10 mL. Predicted volume = 100/2 + 10 = 60 mL under the same linear relation.

C17. Ees increases at the same V0 and end-systolic pressure. Predict ESV.

Answer and explanation

ESV decreases because V = V0 + P/Ees. This prediction requires matched pressure and intercept; actual loading may vary.

C18. Mitral opening is delayed despite unchanged atrial and end-systolic pressures. What additional signals distinguish slower relaxation from delayed activation?

Answer and explanation

A synchronised LV pressure trace and ECG, ideally with aortic closure timing. The pressure decay relative to closure and the electrical reference distinguishes the possible mechanisms.

C19. Atrial contraction is triggered during ventricular systole against a closed tricuspid valve. Predict atrial pressure and the effect on normal filling.

Answer and explanation

A prominent atrial pressure rise is possible because outflow is blocked. It cannot supply the normal late-diastolic volume increment; amplitude depends on atrial and venous compliance.

C20. A low-frequency sound follows mitral opening but precedes the next P wave. Is S3 or S4 more consistent?

Answer and explanation

S3, because this is early filling rather than atrial-driven late filling. The timing supports a mechanism but does not establish a diagnosis.

C21. Net mean ejection pressure difference and width are 80 mmHg and 75 mL in A, 100 mmHg and 55 mL in B. Compare approximate external work.

Answer and explanation

A = 6000, B = 5500 mmHg mL. B has lower work despite higher pressure. To compare power, rate is also necessary.

C22. Three intervals of 20 mL each have endpoint pressure differences 60, 90, 100 and 70 mmHg. Approximate area and work in J.

Answer and explanation

Trapezoids give 1500 + 1900 + 1700 = 5100 mmHg mL ≈ 0.680 J. The approximation assumes linear interpolation between samples.

SET, assertion–reason and application

SET1. Assertion–reason: isovolumetric contraction has near-zero external cavity-volume work because ventricular pressure is constant.

Answer and explanation

Assertion true, reason false. Work is near zero because volume is nearly constant while pressure rises.

SET2. Assertion–reason: long-term ventricular outputs are approximately equal because the vascular circuits are connected in series.

Answer and explanation

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. Persistent inequality would redistribute stored blood.

SET3. Assertion–reason: S4 usually disappears without organised atrial contraction because it depends on late atrial-driven filling.

Answer and explanation

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. Passive early filling can continue without the active booster.

SET4. Order mitral opening, aortic closure, aortic opening and mitral closure from end-diastole.

Answer and explanation

Mitral closure → aortic opening → aortic closure → mitral opening. The intervening phases are isovolumetric contraction, ejection and isovolumetric relaxation.

SET5. An outlet area is 2.5 cm² and the velocity-time integral is 24 cm. Estimate ejected volume.

Answer and explanation

2.5 × 24 = 60 cm³ = 60 mL. The estimate requires a representative velocity and appropriate area; angle and nonuniform flow can introduce errors.

SET6. External work is 0.80 J/beat at 90 beats/min. Find external power.

Answer and explanation

90/60 = 1.5 beats/s; power = 0.80 × 1.5 = 1.20 W. Total metabolic power exceeds this external component.

SET7. Volume falls before aortic opening while pressure rises. What assumption of the ideal cycle is violated if the measurement is accurate?

Answer and explanation

A significant additional route, such as mitral reverse flow, is present. Substantial cavity volume cannot be lost when all routes are competent and closed.

SET8. What measurement helps distinguish elevated arterial load from a narrowed aortic outlet?

Answer and explanation

Simultaneous LV and proximal aortic pressures during ejection. An unusually large gradient across the valve suggests outlet pressure loss rather than merely a uniformly elevated arterial pressure.

SET9. An ECG and ultrasound volume record were made in different respiratory phases. Why are they insufficient for a valid paired PV loop?

Answer and explanation

ECG is not pressure and the recordings may reflect different loading conditions. A PV loop requires synchronised ventricular pressure and volume.

SET10. Identical loops repeat at 60 and 100 beats/min. Why is external power different?

Answer and explanation

The area is the same work per beat; the faster cycle repeats more times per second, increasing average external power.

Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

The cardiac cycle repeatedly converts myocardial activation into pressure gradients and directed flow. Electrical activation precedes contraction, and active relaxation restores a low-pressure filling state. Valve events separate filling, pressure development, ejection and pressure decline. The Wiggers diagram establishes temporal relationships; the PV loop quantifies mechanical work. Valve competence, loading and measurement quality constrain interpretation.
