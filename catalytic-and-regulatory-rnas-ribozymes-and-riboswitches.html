1. RNA as a working molecule

A newly transcribed RNA does not necessarily have to visit a ribosome and supply instructions for making a protein. Many RNAs reach their functional state when they have acquired the correct molecular shape. The mature transfer RNA is a familiar example. Its anticodon identifies part of a messenger RNA, but its opposite end carries an amino acid. Neither property would be adequately explained by treating the molecule as a simple string of letters. The arrangement of its nucleotides makes the two functions spatially distinct. This chapter examines two further possibilities that arise from the same physical principle. An RNA can arrange atoms so that a chemical reaction occurs more readily, or it can recognize a metabolite and alter the way the genetic message is expressed. These are the defining activities of ribozymes and riboswitches respectively.

A ribozyme is an RNA molecule or an RNA catalytic component that accelerates a chemical transformation. The name is formed by combining ribonucleic acid and enzyme. A riboswitch is a regulatory RNA element whose response to a molecular signal changes gene expression. For a conventional metabolite-responsive riboswitch, the RNA itself provides the recognition site for the metabolite. The distinction concerns the event that the molecule performs, not simply the sequence from which it is produced. A ribozyme might remove part of its own RNA chain, process another RNA, or participate in making a peptide bond. A riboswitch typically changes the accessibility of a functional RNA region or the likelihood that transcription will continue. One should not assume that every riboswitch is a catalyst or that a ribozyme must regulate gene expression. The categories overlap in certain unusual natural systems, which will become easier to understand after the ordinary cases have been established.

The observation that RNA can be functional before translation changes our interpretation of the flow of biological information. DNA supplies a sequence template for transcription. The resulting RNA can either be translated or operate as an RNA. It can also carry a protein-coding region and a functional noncoding regulatory region within the same molecule. A bacterial messenger RNA with a metabolite-sensing leader is not converted into a noncoding RNA simply because its leader has a regulatory function. Conversely, the fact that ribosomal RNA participates in protein synthesis does not mean that ribosomal RNA is translated. To predict the outcome of an experimental perturbation, it is important to identify which molecular region is serving which function.

There are three questions worth separating at the outset. What structure does the RNA form? What does that structure recognize? What subsequent physical or chemical event follows recognition? For RNase P, the structure recognizes a precursor transfer RNA and promotes a specific cleavage reaction. For a thiamine pyrophosphate riboswitch, the structure recognizes a metabolite and couples this binding event to a change in RNA configuration. For an ordinary transfer RNA, the structure is recognized by several proteins and another RNA during its function. The identity of a particular RNA cannot be determined merely by knowing that it binds another molecule; the experimental outcome establishes whether that binding supports catalysis, regulation, transport or some other role.

2. Chemical and structural foundations

Each RNA nucleotide consists of a nitrogenous base, a ribose and phosphate. The nucleotides in a chain are generally connected by phosphodiester linkages in which the 3′ oxygen of one sugar and the 5′ oxygen of the next sugar are attached to a common phosphorus. The chain consequently has chemically distinct ends. The 2′ hydroxyl group, present on RNA ribose but absent from ordinary DNA deoxyribose, is particularly important. It can participate in hydrogen bonding, organize hydration, contact metal ions indirectly through the surrounding structure, and in suitable circumstances serve as the attacking nucleophile in cleavage or splicing chemistry. The presence of this group does not make every RNA catalytic. Its reactivity must be controlled by the spatial and chemical environment.

The phosphate backbone carries negative charge under ordinary cellular conditions. If the RNA folds so that distant stretches of this backbone approach one another, the resulting electrostatic repulsion must be accommodated. Positively charged ions contribute to this process. Sodium and potassium can screen electrostatic interactions, whereas divalent ions such as magnesium can provide stronger local charge compensation and in some contexts participate more directly in catalysis. Water molecules associated with these ions and with RNA also contribute to stabilization. A representation in which only the bases are drawn misses an important part of the environment required for a functional RNA structure.

The bases produce specificity in a different way. Adenine and uracil can form conventional Watson–Crick pairs, as can guanine and cytosine. A guanine–uracil wobble pair is also common in folded RNAs. Stacking interactions between neighboring bases stabilize many local structures. Because a long RNA chain can bring complementary stretches together, a single strand can form double-helical segments interrupted by loops, bulges and junctions. These irregular regions are not necessarily defects. They can allow one segment to turn, bring distant helices together or expose the chemical groups needed to bind a particular ligand. A ligand-binding pocket often depends on base arrangements that are not obvious from a simple two-dimensional hairpin drawing.

Primary structure means the nucleotide sequence. Secondary structure describes which portions of that sequence pair with one another. Tertiary structure refers to the three-dimensional placement of those elements, including interactions between regions that may be distant along the chain. These are useful levels of description rather than three separate objects. An RNA with two intact stems may nevertheless lack the tertiary interaction that positions the stems correctly. A mutation can therefore leave a secondary-structure diagram nearly unchanged while abolishing activity. In a catalytic RNA this could result from a misplaced substrate or catalytic group; in a riboswitch it could produce a pocket that no longer accepts the signal molecule.

Consider a short stem in which a guanine on one side pairs with cytosine on the other. Replacing the guanine with adenine creates an A–C mismatch. If the stem is necessary for a function, the mutation may reduce that function. Introducing a second mutation that replaces the original cytosine with uracil restores a conventional A–U pair. If function is restored, the evidence supports the importance of pairing rather than an absolute requirement for the original two bases. This is the logic of compensatory mutation. It is more informative than simply observing that a single sequence change causes a defect. Nevertheless, a failed rescue does not prove that pairing was unimportant. The new A–U pair can differ in stacking, local geometry and stability; the original guanine might also have been making a separate contact with a ligand or another part of the RNA.

RNA folding should not be understood as a machine choosing one shape and remaining there forever. A population of molecules with identical sequences can occupy several conformations. Some are transient intermediates, some are incorrectly folded, and some are able to bind or react. Temperature, ionic conditions and other molecules influence the distribution among these conformations. Even when a particular structure has the lowest free energy, the RNA may have to break existing interactions before reaching it. The energy barriers separating conformations affect the speed of folding. This distinction between stability and speed is crucial to riboswitch function because the downstream part of an RNA is produced progressively during transcription.

One can illustrate this principle with three consecutive segments X, Y and Z. Suppose X and Y can pair, but Y and Z can also form a stable alternative stem. Y cannot simultaneously use all of the same pairing positions in both stems. When X and Y first emerge from RNA polymerase, an X–Y stem may form before Z exists. After Z emerges, an alternative fold becomes possible. Which fold is occupied at a biologically important moment depends on the stability of each structure and the time required to rearrange. A bound ligand can stabilize one conformation and thereby change which region is available to form another helix. The ligand is not necessarily the source of the energy needed to synthesize RNA or protein; its binding changes the energetic preference among structures.

3. What catalysis means

A chemical reaction changes the arrangement of atoms through breaking or forming covalent bonds. A reaction can be energetically favorable and yet proceed very slowly because the reactants must pass through an unstable arrangement on the way to products. A catalyst accelerates the reaction by making an alternative route with a lower activation free energy available. It does not alter the free-energy difference between the initial and final states under the same conditions. Thus, an enzyme cannot turn an unfavorable equilibrium into a favorable one solely by lowering the activation barrier. The principle applies equally to proteins and catalytic RNAs.

At the molecular level, catalytic RNA has fewer kinds of monomers than protein. A protein can employ amino acid side chains with diverse chemical properties, whereas an RNA has a limited collection of bases, sugar hydroxyls and phosphate groups. However, catalytic effectiveness depends not on how many different types of monomer exist but on whether appropriate groups can be arranged at an active site. RNA can place a substrate in an orientation favorable for reaction, reduce the freedom of motion that would otherwise have to be lost during the reaction, stabilize developing charge and participate in acid–base chemistry. Metal ions and other bound molecules can contribute to these functions. Different ribozymes use these strategies in different proportions; there is no single mechanism that applies to every catalytic RNA.

The difference between an enzyme and a receptor is illustrated by comparing two systems with the same substrate affinity. One system binds a molecule but leaves its covalent structure unchanged. The other binds the molecule in a geometry that facilitates bond cleavage and releases chemical products. A measurement of binding alone cannot establish that the second system is a catalyst. Product formation must be measured, and it should occur more rapidly than the uncatalyzed background. Conversely, a mutation that preserves substrate binding but reduces reaction speed can identify a group involved in the reaction rather than recognition. This separation of functions is useful when exam problems describe binding assays alongside catalytic assays.

The term active site refers to the particular molecular arrangement where the transformation occurs. It need not occupy a single contiguous stretch of sequence. Distant nucleotides can meet after folding and contribute different components of the active site. In some catalytic RNAs, metal ions are essential for generating the correct geometry or promoting charge stabilization. In others, nucleobases act as proton donors or acceptors. An RNA nucleophile may begin as a neutral hydroxyl group and become more reactive through proton transfer. Protonation states depend on pH and local environment. A base that usually behaves one way in dilute solution can have a different tendency to accept or donate a proton when it is buried in a folded catalytic pocket.

It is tempting to ask whether RNA catalysis is always an instance of cutting RNA. The answer is no. RNA cleavage is a common class of natural ribozyme reactions because RNA contains chemically accessible phosphodiester bonds. But the catalytic center of the ribosome performs acyl transfer to form a peptide bond. The substrate there includes amino acids and transfer RNAs. A ribozyme is defined by the catalytic role of RNA, not by the identity of the bond being formed or broken. The chemical versatility of natural catalytic RNAs should be appreciated without claiming that RNA can perform every known protein enzyme reaction with comparable efficiency.

The word enzyme is often associated with repeated catalytic cycles. RNase P can process one precursor tRNA and subsequently bind another. A self-cleaving ribozyme is different because its substrate and catalytic region initially belong to the same chain. After cleavage, the original intact molecule no longer exists. A self-splicing intron similarly alters the precursor in which it resides. In these cases the RNA provides the catalytic architecture for an intramolecular reaction even if the natural precursor does not go through many catalytic cycles. An excised catalytic domain can sometimes be adapted to act on external substrate molecules, but that is a separate experimental design. Lack of multiple turnover in the natural cis arrangement is not evidence against RNA catalysis.

Catalytic rate also has to be interpreted in relation to what is measured. If a population of properly folded precursor molecules is converted into products by a single irreversible rate-limiting step, its disappearance may approximately follow first-order behavior. However, not all precursors necessarily begin in the active structure, and some reactions have multiple steps. A time course that initially changes rapidly and later approaches a plateau below complete conversion can reflect the presence of inactive molecules rather than an equilibrium limit. It can also reflect substrate depletion, enzyme inactivation or product inhibition. Experimental conditions and an appropriate model are needed before a mechanistic conclusion is drawn.

4. Three reactions involving the RNA backbone

A phosphodiester bond can be attacked by a nucleophile at phosphorus. When water participates in cleavage, the overall process is hydrolysis. When one ester linkage is replaced by another as an alcohol group attacks phosphorus, the process is transesterification. These words identify different chemical events. They help distinguish the processing of precursor tRNA by RNase P from many self-cleavage and self-splicing reactions. A student who knows only that a piece of RNA has been removed cannot deduce the reaction type without knowing the attacking group and the nature of the products.

In the ordinary RNase P reaction, the nucleophile is derived from water. The bond at the junction between a 5′ leader and mature tRNA is hydrolyzed. The released leader acquires a 3′ hydroxyl end, and the mature tRNA has a 5′ phosphate. This product polarity is important. The mature tRNA is not produced by adding nucleotides to the original 5′ leader or by cutting the tRNA anticodon. The reaction recognizes the region that will become the beginning of the mature tRNA and removes a dispensable precursor extension. It generates an end needed for later steps in the lifetime of the molecule.

In a typical small self-cleaving ribozyme, the 2′ hydroxyl of a ribose immediately upstream of the scissile linkage attacks the adjacent phosphorus. The attacking oxygen is on the same nucleotide whose 3′ oxygen is already connected to phosphorus. As the bond to the downstream 5′ oxygen is broken, a cyclic phosphate can form between the attacking 2′ oxygen and the original 3′ oxygen of the upstream nucleotide. The downstream fragment receives a 5′ hydroxyl. This is an intramolecular transesterification rather than ordinary hydrolysis. Because the product ends differ from those of RNase P, analyzing the chemical termini is one way to test which broad class of reaction occurred.

A self-splicing intron carries out a pair of transesterification reactions. In the first step, an attacking hydroxyl group replaces the connection at the 5′ splice site and releases the 3′ hydroxyl of the upstream exon. In the second step, that newly available exon hydroxyl attacks the linkage at the 3′ splice site. The exons become joined. The topology and identity of the excised intron depend on the source of the first attacking hydroxyl group. A group I intron uses the 3′ hydroxyl of an external guanosine nucleotide or nucleoside, whereas the familiar branching pathway of a group II intron uses the 2′ hydroxyl of an adenosine located within the intron. These two reactions achieve a similar overall biological goal but leave distinct molecular evidence.

A common misconception arises when a question mentions that GTP is required for a group I splicing reaction. It may appear that the phosphate bonds of GTP must be hydrolyzed to provide energy, as in some motor proteins. The relevant part of the guanosine compound is actually its available 3′ hydroxyl, which becomes involved in the covalent chemistry. Free guanosine, GMP, GDP or GTP can serve in suitable group I systems, depending on the experimental conditions. The reaction is not equivalent to a GTPase cycle. Distinguishing a nucleotide used as a nucleophile from one consumed to drive an energy-dependent process prevents a mechanistic mistake that can affect several topics in molecular biology.

In both transesterification steps of splicing, one phosphodiester linkage is exchanged for another. The new covalent connection is formed as another connection is displaced. This explains why the reaction does not require an ATP-driven ligase simply to join the two exons. It does not mean that RNA folding and processing within a living cell require no resources. Some cellular splicing systems use proteins and ATP-dependent remodeling to achieve the right configuration, and these processes must not be confused with the bond-exchange chemistry itself. A meaningful answer to an energy question specifies which step requires energy and which chemical bond is being transformed.

5. The discovery that RNA can catalyze reactions

The conventional picture of enzymes as proteins was supported by a large body of biochemical research before the discovery of catalytic RNA. When a catalytic reaction was observed in a sample containing RNA and protein, it was reasonable to suspect that a protein supplied the active site. RNA might have been the substrate, a structural scaffold or a molecule that guided the protein to the correct site. A convincing discovery of RNA catalysis required more than the observation that RNA was present. Researchers had to exclude the possibility that the activity arose from contaminating protein and show that an RNA molecule could make a specific transformation occur.

Thomas Cech and colleagues investigated the processing of a ribosomal RNA precursor in the ciliate Tetrahymena. Part of the precursor is removed to produce a mature RNA. The researchers found that a precursor synthesized from a DNA template in vitro and subsequently deproteinized could undergo specific rearrangements under suitable conditions. The work of Kruger and colleagues, published in 1982, established that the intervening RNA sequence could participate in its own excision and subsequent rearrangements. The novelty was not merely that an intron had been removed. It was that a specific removal and joining process took place without the addition of a dedicated protein splicing enzyme.

Why did an in vitro transcript provide a particularly useful form of evidence? An RNA isolated directly from a biological processing complex could carry tightly associated proteins. Preparation of an RNA from a defined DNA template followed by removal of protein makes it possible to examine an RNA sequence independently of its original cellular environment. The transcription reaction initially requires a polymerase, which is itself a protein. Therefore, protein removal and appropriate controls are important; the use of an in vitro transcript alone would not be sufficient if polymerase or other enzymes remained in the reaction mixture. The evidence came from specific RNA product formation in the absence of an added protein catalyst, together with the characterization of the reaction and its RNA requirements.

A second discovery came from studies of RNase P, a cellular activity that processes the 5′ ends of precursor tRNAs. RNase P purified from bacteria contained RNA and protein. The central experimental question was whether the RNA played a structural or catalytic role. In 1983 Guerrier-Takada, Gardiner, Marsh, Pace and Altman reported that the RNA component of bacterial RNase P was itself catalytically active under suitable in vitro conditions. A purified RNA component was therefore capable of catalyzing processing of an external substrate. The result complemented the Tetrahymena discovery because it demonstrated an RNA-based enzyme that could act in trans on another RNA rather than only rearrange the chain that contained the catalytic structure.

The experiments were not claims that proteins are irrelevant to RNA biology. Bacterial RNase P normally functions as a ribonucleoprotein complex, and the associated protein enhances activity under physiological conditions. The existence of a catalytically competent isolated RNA establishes that the RNA can provide the essential chemical machinery. It does not imply that an isolated RNA performs equally well at every salt concentration, in every organism or inside a cell. This distinction between catalytic sufficiency in a defined experiment and physiological effectiveness is an important general lesson in interpreting molecular biology evidence.

Cech and Sidney Altman received the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of RNA's catalytic properties. The two lines of investigation changed the accepted description of biological catalysts. They also encouraged hypotheses about early evolution in which RNA could have provided both information storage and catalytic functions. The historical experiments did not reconstruct the origin of life, however. They established experimentally that a particular type of modern biomolecule can catalyze particular reactions. Evolutionary interpretations must remain separate from that direct experimental evidence.

6. RNase P: processing the beginning of a transfer RNA

A transfer RNA must possess a precisely arranged acceptor end and anticodon region to participate in translation. When its gene is transcribed, however, the initial RNA may contain nucleotides that do not belong to the mature molecule. Some bacterial tRNA genes are transcribed as individual precursors, whereas others form part of larger transcripts. In either case, the 5′ end of a mature tRNA must be generated at the correct position. If an extra nucleotide is left attached, the resulting RNA may fail to adopt the normal acceptor-stem arrangement or interact efficiently with the proteins that use it. RNase P is an endonuclease involved in generating this mature end.

An endonuclease cleaves a phosphodiester linkage within a nucleic acid chain rather than successively removing nucleotides from an exposed terminus. RNase P recognizes a precursor tRNA and cuts at the junction between the 5′ leader and the first nucleotide of the mature tRNA. The leader may vary in sequence and length between precursor molecules. It would therefore be inefficient for a processing enzyme to require the same complete leader sequence in every substrate. The structural features shared by precursor tRNAs provide a more broadly useful basis of recognition. A tRNA contains an acceptor-stem region, a T arm and an overall folded arrangement that can be recognized by the enzyme. Particular sequence features also matter, but the substrate is not defined by a single identical short leader in every tRNA.

In the familiar Escherichia coli system, the RNA component is commonly called M1 RNA and the associated protein C5. They form a ribonucleoprotein enzyme. The RNA creates the catalytic core, while the protein helps the enzyme operate efficiently with precursor substrates under normal cellular conditions. Substrate binding requires the precursor to be placed so that the scissile phosphodiester linkage is near the chemical groups and ions that promote cleavage. The RNA component contains regions that provide the structure for catalysis as well as regions that contribute to recognizing the substrate. Because of this arrangement, some mutations affect the interaction with the precursor, whereas others allow the precursor to bind but prevent efficient hydrolysis.

The exact placement of the cleavage site is an excellent example of geometric specificity. Imagine that the precursor attaches to the enzyme in a position shifted by one nucleotide. The same general hydrolysis reaction might still be chemically possible, but the product would have an incorrect 5′ end. Successful processing depends on positioning a particular phosphodiester linkage rather than merely binding the tRNA somewhere. The architecture of RNase P coordinates recognition of the tRNA body with alignment of the junction that will be cut. This is also why modifications to the acceptor stem or the leader can have different effects on substrate affinity, cleavage rate and cleavage-site choice.

Magnesium ions contribute to the folded and catalytically active state of bacterial RNase P. The folded RNA brings negatively charged phosphate groups into proximity, and a suitable ionic environment reduces repulsion. In addition, metal ions near the active site can contribute to activation of water and stabilization of charge during phosphodiester hydrolysis. It is helpful to distinguish these structural and chemical roles even though both can occur in a single experiment. If removal of magnesium causes the RNA to unfold, absence of reaction cannot establish that a particular magnesium ion directly participates in hydrolysis. Conversely, an RNA that retains its overall structure can still fail to react if the catalytic metal environment is disrupted.

The outcome of hydrolysis is a shorter, mature-ended tRNA and a released leader fragment. The enzyme is not covalently incorporated into these products and can bind another precursor. If the product tRNA remains associated with the enzyme for a long time, the rate of processing further molecules can be limited by product release rather than by the chemical cleavage step. This distinction matters when comparing initial reaction speed with the rate observed during many catalytic cycles. An enzyme can have a very rapid cleavage step and still have a relatively modest overall turnover if a different step limits the cycle.

A researcher can study RNase P using a labeled precursor of known length and then separate intact RNA and the processed products by electrophoresis. In a denaturing gel, molecules are separated primarily according to length rather than their original folded shapes, although labeling strategy determines which products are visible. If the label lies in the mature tRNA region, the processed tRNA will retain it, but a detached unlabeled leader will not be detected. Failure to observe the leader under these conditions is not evidence that it was never generated. If the label lies exclusively in the leader, the opposite detection pattern is expected. Such questions test the ability to track a chemical label through the mechanism rather than simply remember the name of the enzyme.

Suppose an investigator compares an intact bacterial RNase P complex with its isolated RNA and isolated protein components. The RNA is active in the presence of a sufficiently supportive magnesium concentration; the protein alone is inactive under the same conditions; the intact complex is active at a lower magnesium concentration. The observations would support a catalytic RNA that benefits from association with a protein. They would not justify concluding that the protein is dispensable in a living bacterium. To establish physiological dispensability one would need cellular evidence that RNA processing, growth and other relevant outcomes remain normal without it. A controlled biochemical reaction answers a question about molecular capacity, whereas a cellular experiment answers a different question about functional necessity.

The converse experiment is informative too. If a treated RNase P preparation loses activity after RNA degradation, the result is consistent with an important RNA component. Yet RNA degradation could also destroy a structural scaffold required by a protein active site. Thus an RNase-sensitivity experiment by itself cannot decide whether catalysis is RNA-based. The historical demonstration of catalytic sufficiency was stronger because the isolated RNA performed processing. This hierarchy of experimental evidence is useful for interpreting novel enzyme systems: evidence of requirement, evidence of sufficiency and evidence of direct chemical participation are related but not interchangeable.

RNase P should not be confused with RNase Z, which is associated with 3′ maturation of many precursor tRNAs, or with the machinery that adds a CCA sequence where required. A mature tRNA has several features that may have been generated by distinct processing activities. RNase P specifically addresses the 5′ leader. Nor should one assume that every organism uses the exact bacterial M1–C5 arrangement. Many archaeal and eukaryotic forms have more extensive protein components. Some organisms and organelles possess protein-only RNase P systems. Those cases reveal that a similar biological task can be accomplished by different catalytic architectures. The statement that bacterial RNase P is a classical ribozyme is correct; the assertion that every RNase P enzyme everywhere must contain catalytic RNA is too broad.

7. Group I introns: using a free guanosine to exchange phosphodiester bonds

When an intron interrupts a sequence that must be retained in a mature RNA, the cell faces two linked tasks: removing the intervening sequence and reconnecting the flanking segments accurately. Group I introns provide an example in which the intron RNA contains the structural information necessary to support both reactions. The classic Tetrahymena intron occurs in a ribosomal RNA precursor, which is a useful reminder that an intron is not exclusively a feature of messenger RNA genes. An exon is the retained portion of an RNA after splicing, whether or not that portion encodes a protein.

The starting molecule consists of a 5′ exon, the group I intron and a 3′ exon, all connected by ordinary RNA backbone bonds. As the intron folds, it brings the splice junctions into a catalytic arrangement. A short pairing interaction between the intron and adjacent exon sequences helps position the upstream splice site. An external guanosine compound binds in a pocket in the folded intron. Its ribose 3′ hydroxyl is positioned to attack the phosphorus at the bond linking the 5′ exon to the first nucleotide of the intron. The chemistry depends on suitable orientation and activation of the attacking group and on stabilization of the transition state.

The first transesterification generates a free 3′ hydroxyl at the end of the 5′ exon. Simultaneously, the external guanosine becomes covalently joined to the 5′ end of the intron. At this stage the intron is still attached to the 3′ exon. It would be incorrect to call the reaction finished merely because the upstream junction has been cleaved. The two retained exons must still be joined, and the downstream boundary must still be resolved. The folded intron maintains the relevant groups in a geometry that supports this second event.

In the second transesterification, the 3′ hydroxyl of the upstream exon attacks the phosphorus of the linkage at the intron–3′ exon junction. The upstream exon becomes covalently attached to the downstream exon. The intron is released, normally as a linear product of the two-step reaction, with the guanosine acquired during the first step. Further intron rearrangements, including circularization in certain systems, can occur after excision. It is necessary to distinguish the immediate splicing product from these secondary products. A circular intron observed later does not mean that the initial group I reaction formed the characteristic lariat of group II splicing.

The source of the attacking guanosine is a central feature of group I chemistry. It is not the first nucleotide of the intron that must serve as a branch-point adenosine. The free guanosine is recruited from the surrounding solution and supplies a reactive hydroxyl. This property suggests a useful experimental prediction. If a label is incorporated in the external guanosine and the reaction proceeds through the usual pathway, the label should enter the excised intron rather than the joined exons. To interpret such an experiment, one must distinguish a label attached to the guanosine portion from a label on a phosphate that could undergo a different reaction. The prediction concerns the fate of the guanosine moiety as a covalently incorporated nucleophile.

If the external guanosine binds but lacks a usable 3′ hydroxyl, the first reaction cannot proceed by its normal route. In contrast, a modification that specifically prevents the newly generated 3′ hydroxyl of the 5′ exon from reacting would allow the first intermediate to accumulate while blocking efficient exon joining. The products therefore identify which step is affected. Observing a free upstream exon but little joined product is different from observing intact precursor with no free exon at all. A time series that detects an intermediate before the final product provides stronger evidence for a sequential mechanism than a single measurement after the reaction is complete.

Although the intron is called self-splicing, it does not follow that every group I intron splices at a useful rate in every cellular context without assistance. RNA folding can be difficult for long precursors, and proteins can facilitate the attainment of an active structure. The term reflects the intrinsic catalytic capacity of the intron RNA under appropriate conditions. The classic in vitro demonstration establishes that this capacity exists. Physiological processing can rely on additional factors without changing the fundamental assignment of the catalytic RNA. In questions comparing an isolated intron to its cellular behavior, both the chemical ability of the RNA and the role of the cellular folding environment should be recognized.

The specificity of a group I intron also has evolutionary and biotechnological implications. Exchanging an RNA phosphodiester bond is not a random breakage event if substrate positioning is determined by base pairing and a conserved catalytic fold. In experimentally modified systems, a catalytic RNA derived from an intron can be separated from its original substrate and directed toward compatible RNA sequences. The general idea is that recognition can be adjusted while a catalytic core is retained. This is useful for understanding how RNA catalysts may be engineered, although the exact sequence constraints and performance of a designed molecule are not predictable from base pairing alone.

8. Group II introns: branching within the RNA chain

Group II introns solve the same broad processing problem through two transesterification reactions, but the initiating nucleophile differs. A particular adenosine within the intron provides a 2′ hydroxyl that attacks the 5′ splice junction. This adenosine is part of the same RNA chain as the substrate linkage. Its position in the folded intron brings its 2′ oxygen close to the phosphorus at the upstream exon boundary. Because the 2′ hydroxyl is used, the resulting connection is not the ordinary 3′–5′ linkage found along most of the RNA backbone.

The first reaction forms a 2′–5′ phosphodiester linkage connecting the branch-point adenosine to the 5′ end of the intron. The upstream exon gains a free 3′ hydroxyl. The intron now contains a branch, while its original backbone remains intact through the branch-point nucleotide. One can visualize this by following the chain from the first intron nucleotide through the branch-point region. An extra bond returns from the adenosine's 2′ oxygen to the first intron nucleotide, closing a loop with a tail extending toward the downstream splice junction. This branched structure is called a lariat. It is not simply a circular RNA made by joining the original 5′ and 3′ ends.

During the second reaction, the free 3′ hydroxyl of the upstream exon attacks the phosphorus at the downstream splice junction. As with group I splicing, this exchange joins the two exons and releases the intron. In the usual group II branching pathway, the released intron has the lariat topology generated in the first reaction. The difference between a group I and group II intron therefore resides not in whether exon joining occurs but in the origin of the initiating nucleophile and the topology of the excised product.

There is a strong relationship between this mechanism and the splicing of nuclear pre-mRNA by the spliceosome. The spliceosome also uses an intronic adenosine 2′ hydroxyl in a branching reaction and the free upstream exon hydroxyl in the second reaction. The catalytic environment of the spliceosome involves RNA components, including U2 and U6 small nuclear RNAs, together with many proteins. The mechanistic and structural parallels between group II introns and spliceosomal splicing support an evolutionary relationship. The exact sequence of evolutionary events is inferred from comparative evidence, not directly observed. In an examination the useful conclusion is that similar RNA-centered chemical strategies underlie these pathways, rather than that the spliceosome and a group II intron are identical molecular complexes.

Group II introns are found in bacteria and in some organellar genomes. The distribution of such introns is not uniform, and their cellular processing can depend on associated proteins. Some introns can also use a hydrolytic pathway instead of forming a lariat in the initial step. A statement describing the standard branch-point mechanism should therefore not be elevated into an assertion that every group II reaction always yields a lariat. For most textbook comparison problems, unless an alternative pathway is specified, the expected group II product is a branched intron and the expected group I product is a linear excised intron. The exception becomes important when experimental product structures are supplied explicitly.

An experiment with a group II intron can test the significance of the branch-point hydroxyl. If this group is made unavailable while the rest of the catalytic architecture is maintained, the usual branching reaction should be impaired. If exon joining still occurs through a hydrolytic alternative, the intron product may have a different topology. Thus complete loss of splicing is not the only possible result of altering the branch point. An informative analysis distinguishes total product yield from the relative abundances of branched and linear intron species. Electrophoretic mobility alone may not always establish topology, because a branched RNA can migrate differently from a linear RNA of equal nucleotide number. Additional evidence of linkage or branch-sensitive processing can strengthen the conclusion.

9. The ribosome as a catalytic ribonucleoprotein machine

The discovery of catalytic RNA might initially seem unrelated to protein synthesis. Yet the reaction that makes a protein chain longer is itself catalyzed within a large RNA-containing complex. The ribosome contains a small subunit involved in reading the messenger RNA and a large subunit that contains the peptidyl-transferase center. Proteins are indispensable to the assembly and function of normal ribosomes, but the catalytic center responsible for peptide bond formation is principally built from ribosomal RNA. In bacteria the relevant RNA is 23S rRNA, located in the 50S subunit. Eukaryotic cytosolic large subunits contain the corresponding 28S rRNA region.

The chemical problem differs fundamentally from intron excision. An amino acid attached to a transfer RNA in the aminoacyl site supplies an amino group. The growing peptide is attached to another transfer RNA in the peptidyl site by an ester linkage. The amino group of the incoming amino acid attacks the carbonyl carbon of the ester that attaches the peptide to the other tRNA. A new peptide bond is produced, and the growing chain is transferred onto the incoming tRNA. The departing tRNA becomes deacylated. The catalyzed event is therefore an acyl transfer, not a phosphodiester cleavage reaction.

The ribosome arranges the ends of the two tRNAs to favor this reaction. The ester linkage of the peptidyl-tRNA is an activated chemical connection generated ultimately through the energy-dependent charging of tRNA. It is unnecessary to imagine the ribosomal RNA hydrolyzing ATP each time it forms a peptide bond. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases use ATP to attach amino acids to tRNAs, and translation factors use GTP during other stages of the translation cycle. These energy-consuming processes contribute to the operation and fidelity of protein synthesis, but they are not identical to the bond-making reaction at the peptidyl-transferase center. The distinction will be developed more fully in the following subunit.

Structural studies published in 2000 by Nissen and colleagues examined the large ribosomal subunit of an archaeon together with substrate analogs. The location of conserved rRNA residues around the substrate analogs, and the absence of protein side chains in the immediate reaction environment, provided strong structural evidence for an RNA-based active site. The organism used in the structural work should not be confused with the standard E. coli numbering convention often used to describe conserved rRNA residues. The central conclusion is shared across these systems: the catalytic site for peptide bond formation is RNA-based.

It would be an overstatement to conclude that the entire ribosome is pure RNA or that all of its functions can be reproduced by an isolated fragment of rRNA. The assembled machine contains many proteins, and its behavior depends on interactions among its components. The structural evidence identifies the nature of the reaction center. It does not make the proteins dispensable for decoding, assembly, stability, communication among functional sites or interaction with translation factors. In addition, the exact contributions of individual RNA and substrate chemical groups to catalysis have been refined by later studies. For the present level, it is sufficient to understand substrate alignment, the participation of RNA in the active-site architecture and the activated ester chemistry, without asserting a single oversimplified acid–base mechanism for all stages of the reaction.

10. Small self-cleaving ribozymes and the importance of controls

The hammerhead ribozyme provides a compact example of RNA catalysis in which several helical regions meet around a conserved catalytic core. It was originally characterized in small RNAs associated with plant pathogens, including satellite RNAs and viroids, and related motifs occur in other biological contexts. In rolling-circle RNA replication systems that use self-cleavage, long RNA intermediates containing repeated units need to be separated into appropriate lengths. A catalytic motif carried by the RNA can help accomplish this processing. The ribozyme does not itself synthesize all of the replicated RNA or necessarily catalyze every subsequent joining step. Its particular chemical activity is site-specific backbone cleavage.

The hammerhead reaction uses the neighboring ribose 2′ hydroxyl in the attacking step, producing the upstream cyclic phosphate and downstream hydroxyl termini described earlier. Its surrounding stems and additional contacts help support the active arrangement. A minimal RNA fragment that retains a core sequence might be far less active than a more complete natural structure because important tertiary contacts have been removed. This explains why defining a conserved sequence is not equivalent to defining all of the determinants of activity. It also illustrates a general caution for experiments that test truncated RNA domains: a negative result may arise because a necessary supporting structure was omitted, rather than because the original molecule is incapable of catalysis.

The classical RNase P experiments provide a concrete example of how the ionic environment affects interpretation. Guerrier-Takada and colleagues reported activity of isolated bacterial RNA under particular in vitro ionic conditions, including high magnesium or a combination of magnesium and spermidine. At lower magnesium concentrations without spermidine in their reported conditions, the RNA and protein were both needed for activity. The important inference is not that there is one universal concentration of magnesium required by every ribozyme. Instead, the results show that a protein subunit can enhance the ability of a catalytic RNA to function in a less strongly stabilizing environment. Conditions in the reaction tube must always accompany statements about the sufficiency of isolated components.

A complementary control is to compare the cleavage rate of the structured RNA against the rate of an appropriate uncatalyzed substrate under identical pH, temperature and ionic conditions. RNA can undergo background chemical cleavage, especially when conditions favor deprotonation of ribose hydroxyl groups. If the substrate degrades at many positions without the proposed catalyst, the mere appearance of shorter molecules does not establish site-specific catalysis. Defined product lengths, appropriate background measurements and analysis of relevant mutations together provide stronger evidence. The purpose is to connect the observed transformation to the sequence and fold of the candidate RNA rather than to general instability of RNA in the reaction mixture.

11. From catalytic activity to metabolite recognition

The ribozymes considered so far provide evidence that RNA can bind a substrate and assist a chemical reaction. A further possibility is that an RNA can recognize another molecule and communicate information without altering that molecule covalently. This is especially useful in metabolism, where the concentration of a small molecule can indicate whether the cell should increase or decrease synthesis of particular enzymes. A bacterium that already possesses sufficient amounts of an essential cofactor need not continually make the enzymes required for its biosynthesis. The difficulty is to connect the availability of the cofactor to the expression of the genes that produce it.

One solution involves a regulatory protein. A metabolite binds to a protein, the protein changes its interaction with a DNA or RNA site, and gene expression changes. This strategy is widespread, but it is not the only possibility. A newly transcribed RNA can contain a region that directly recognizes the metabolite. If occupation of this recognition site changes the accessibility of another functionally important sequence in the same RNA, the molecule can respond to the signal without a dedicated metabolite-sensing protein. A conventional riboswitch is such a regulatory RNA element. Its existence means that the RNA transcript can carry both a message and a mechanism for determining how much of that message will be used.

The signal molecule is called a ligand. A ligand can be a nucleotide derivative, a vitamin-derived coenzyme, an amino acid, a metabolic intermediate or, in particular RNA sensors, an ion. Binding is selective because the three-dimensional pocket can recognize features such as the arrangement of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, shape, charge and metal-associated groups. A ligand need not be a nucleotide merely because it binds RNA. Nor does binding necessarily require that the ligand pair with the RNA by a conventional series of Watson–Crick base pairs. The recognition event may involve several nucleotides brought together from different parts of the folded molecule.

A typical bacterial riboswitch occurs in the 5′ untranslated region of a transcript, upstream of the protein-coding sequence. Transcription begins before the coding region is reached, and the regulatory RNA can begin folding during that interval. Its position is advantageous because it can influence the fate of the message before or during production of the coding region. Nevertheless, the definition of a riboswitch does not require that it always be in the 5′ untranslated region. Some eukaryotic examples occur in intronic or 3′ regions and regulate RNA processing. The location should be specified for the particular system rather than inferred from the name.

Riboswitches usually act in cis: the regulatory sequence controls expression from the same RNA molecule in which it is located. The signal molecule, however, is not permanently attached to that RNA and may diffuse among many transcripts. A change in metabolite concentration can therefore alter the behavior of numerous independently transcribed molecules. This combination of a shared cellular signal and a local regulatory element enables a population of messages to respond without every message having to recruit a separate dedicated regulatory protein.

In a cell, a riboswitch still operates in an environment filled with proteins. Transcription requires RNA polymerase, translation requires ribosomes and other factors, and RNA degradation may depend on nucleases. When a textbook says that a riboswitch directly senses a ligand without a protein, the claim concerns the recognition event: the RNA does not need a protein receptor to recognize the relevant molecule. It does not mean that the entire expression system operates without proteins. A well-designed experimental question may deliberately ask which part of the pathway is protein-independent. The correct response should distinguish signal recognition from the processes that carry out the regulatory consequence.

12. Aptamer and expression platform: two functions in a linked structure

The conventional riboswitch model separates the regulatory RNA into an aptamer and an expression platform. The aptamer is the region responsible for recognizing and binding the ligand. The expression platform is the region whose structure or accessibility determines the regulatory outcome. These terms describe functions and do not require that the two regions be completely separate in sequence. A nucleotide that helps stabilize the ligand-bound aptamer may also influence whether an alternative downstream helix forms. Their structural coupling is essential: binding at one location has to change a functional property elsewhere in the RNA.

The aptamer is usually a structured RNA domain containing a binding pocket. In a simple conceptual model, the pocket is assembled when several RNA segments adopt the appropriate arrangement. Some nucleotides directly contact the ligand, whereas others form the stems and junctions that support those contacting groups. A mutation that disrupts the scaffold may reduce affinity even if it leaves all direct ligand contacts unchanged. Conversely, a mutation at a direct contact position may prevent recognition while allowing the general secondary structure to remain intact. This distinction explains why determining the sequence of an RNA is only the beginning of understanding its regulatory behavior.

Binding can be understood in terms of the energetic relationship between possible conformations. Imagine an RNA that fluctuates between a configuration compatible with binding and a configuration that cannot accommodate the ligand. When the ligand binds the compatible configuration, the bound state becomes more populated. Even if an RNA samples that configuration only infrequently in the absence of ligand, sufficiently favorable binding can shift the overall distribution. In other cases, initial binding may be followed by further rearrangement of the RNA. These possibilities are often described as conformational selection and induced fit. For examination purposes it is important to understand that binding and folding are coupled, not to assume every switch follows one universal sequence of structural events.

The expression platform provides the connection from molecular recognition to gene expression. A simple platform can be a set of RNA segments capable of forming two alternative pairing arrangements. One arrangement makes a ribosome-binding site available; the other pairs that site with a complementary region and makes it less accessible. A different platform can contain sequences that form an intrinsic transcription terminator when certain segments become available. The consequences are distinct even when the aptamer recognizes the same chemical signal. Thus a student should not infer the regulatory output simply by identifying the ligand.

The distinction between these two regions can be investigated experimentally. An isolated aptamer may bind a ligand with high specificity even if it lacks the downstream expression platform. This observation establishes recognition capacity but does not demonstrate regulation. If the same aptamer is placed upstream of a reporter gene together with the appropriate platform, its ligand-dependent folding may influence reporter output. A platform mutation might eliminate this response while preserving binding by the aptamer. Such a result indicates that molecular recognition remains possible but the coupling between recognition and expression has been disrupted.

Conversely, a platform can be locked into a regulatory state by mutations that strongly favor one helix. The gene may then remain constitutively expressed or constitutively repressed, depending on the state. Adding a ligand will have little effect even if the aptamer can still bind it. The loss of a measurable response could therefore arise from a binding defect, a communication defect, or an output structure that is no longer able to switch. The combination of an independent binding assay and an expression assay is especially valuable for distinguishing these interpretations.

A terminology problem sometimes arises because any RNA that binds a molecule may be called an aptamer in a broad biochemical sense. An aptamer by itself is not necessarily a riboswitch. A laboratory-selected RNA might recognize a fluorescent molecule without regulating any gene. An endogenous RNA could bind a protein for structural purposes. To classify a regulatory element as a conventional riboswitch, there should be a demonstrable connection between direct recognition of the signal and a change in gene expression. The aptamer explains how the signal is detected; the platform explains how the detection event has a consequence.

13. Transcriptional riboswitches and premature termination

In bacteria, RNA polymerase synthesizes RNA progressively from its 5′ end toward its 3′ end. As the upstream parts of the nascent transcript emerge, they can fold before the downstream sequence has been synthesized. A transcriptional riboswitch makes use of this order of events. The aptamer region can become competent to recognize the ligand while the polymerase is still transcribing the regulatory leader. Later, a decision is made concerning whether transcription terminates at a site within that leader or continues into the downstream coding sequence.

A common bacterial intrinsic terminator contains a sequence capable of forming a stable RNA hairpin, followed by a uridine-rich tract. The hairpin and the properties of the RNA–DNA hybrid in the transcription complex favor release of the RNA from the polymerase under appropriate conditions. The mechanism does not require a stop codon; stop codons are signals for translation termination, not ordinary intrinsic transcription termination. The terminator is also not a promoter that turns off transcription initiation. Polymerase has already initiated and synthesized part of the RNA before it encounters this signal.

For a representative ligand-dependent OFF switch, consider a leader with two competing RNA structures. When ligand concentration is low, the aptamer region or associated switching segment can pair with part of the potential terminator sequence. This alternative structure is often called an antiterminator because it prevents the terminator stem from being fully assembled. If the polymerase progresses past the potential termination site, the coding region is transcribed. The cell can then produce more of the protein encoded by the message, assuming the subsequent steps of gene expression function normally.

At high ligand concentration, binding stabilizes an alternative aptamer conformation that prevents formation of the antiterminator. The relevant terminator segments become available to pair. As the polymerase transcribes the uridine-rich region, the intrinsic termination signal can promote premature transcript release. The resulting short RNA contains the leader but lacks the complete downstream coding sequence. Even if some leader RNA can be detected, the cell has fewer complete messages available for producing the target protein.

Notice that the ligand has not necessarily altered the activity of the promoter. If the promoter initiates the same number of RNA molecules in both conditions, the number of complete messages can nevertheless differ because different fractions of initiated transcripts reach the downstream coding region. A measurement made using a probe in the leader may detect both short terminated RNAs and long readthrough products. A probe in the coding region detects only molecules that extend far enough to include it. Consequently, two assays can yield apparently different impressions of transcription if their probe locations are not considered.

The mechanism also explains why a mutation in the terminator can prevent repression. If the stem can no longer form, polymerase may read through the leader despite normal ligand binding. Restoring the complementary pairing through a compensatory mutation may restore termination. However, successful termination requires more than any arbitrary hairpin. Its position relative to the polymerase, the nature of the downstream tract and the transcriptional context can influence its efficiency. A compensatory mutation can restore base pairing but still fail to recreate all of the relevant properties of the original terminator.

A different mutation might disrupt the antiterminator without affecting the terminator. In the simple OFF-switch model, the terminator may then form even when ligand is absent, causing constitutively low expression. The interpretation follows from identifying the helix that has been weakened and asking which competing structure becomes available. Memorizing that a mutation in a riboswitch causes either high or low expression is unhelpful; either outcome is possible. The specific sequence interaction and the normal regulatory architecture determine the direction of the response.

Not all transcriptional riboswitches are OFF switches. Some ligand-bound states favor readthrough rather than premature termination. In those arrangements, the ligand stabilizes a structure that interferes with terminator formation. The same basic physical principles apply, but the wiring of the expression platform is different. In an examination, the supplied structures and experimental data take priority over a generalization about feedback inhibition. Although many biosynthetic pathways are repressed by an abundant end product, other cellular tasks can require induction when a particular signal is present.

14. Translational riboswitches and accessibility of the initiation region

A second widespread regulatory output in bacteria is a change in the initiation of translation. The transcript can be produced in full, but the rate at which ribosomes initiate protein synthesis differs depending on the ligand-bound state of the leader. In many bacterial messages, a Shine–Dalgarno sequence upstream of the start codon can pair with a complementary region of the small-subunit ribosomal RNA. Proper accessibility and positioning help the ribosome recognize the initiation region. Not every bacterial message requires a canonical Shine–Dalgarno sequence, but it provides a useful model for understanding this class of switches.

In a representative translational OFF switch, the ligand-free RNA can adopt a configuration in which the ribosome-binding site is accessible. Ribosomes can engage the message and initiate translation if the remaining initiation requirements are satisfied. After ligand binding, the RNA adopts a configuration in which a complementary sequence pairs with the ribosome-binding site. This sequestration reduces its availability for interaction with the small ribosomal subunit. The start codon can remain completely unchanged; the defect is that initiation becomes less favorable because an essential recognition region has become inaccessible.

The fact that this is translational regulation means that intact RNA can remain present when protein production decreases. Suppose a gene produces equal numbers of full-length messages with and without ligand, but the amount of encoded protein falls markedly at high ligand. That pattern is consistent with a translational mechanism. It is not sufficient to prove it on its own, because protein stability could also change. Measuring translation from the message or using a reporter whose degradation properties are controlled can help discriminate among possibilities. The abundance of RNA and protein should therefore be treated as two related but distinct observations.

An ON switch operates with the opposite configuration. In the absence of ligand, the initiation region might be sequestered in a stem. Ligand binding stabilizes an alternate RNA arrangement that frees the ribosome-binding sequence and allows translation to occur. Such a switch could increase expression when a compound is present. This principle demonstrates why there is no inherent association between ligand binding and reduced gene expression. The aptamer's function is to recognize the signal; the pattern of communication with the expression platform determines whether the signal increases or decreases protein production.

A mutation within the ribosome-binding site itself requires particular care in interpretation. If it prevents the site from pairing with the complementary switching segment, the mutant might appear to favor an open conformation. But the same mutation could also weaken interaction with ribosomal RNA, reducing initiation. It is not always possible to change only one interaction by altering a multifunctional sequence. A more informative design would preserve the sequence required for ribosome recognition and instead modify the alternative pairing partner, if the experimental goal is to test sequestration. Even then, the influence of the altered region on folding of the entire leader has to be considered.

There can also be indirect effects on mRNA abundance. When translation initiation is strongly inhibited in bacteria, the transcript may become more susceptible to degradation in some contexts because ribosomes and RNA structure influence nuclease accessibility. A translational switch can therefore eventually lead to a decrease in steady-state RNA concentration as well as protein synthesis. A simple textbook distinction between transcriptional and translational control remains useful, but steady-state RNA abundance alone does not always identify the primary event. Direct observations of termination products, ribosome occupancy or transcript half-life can resolve more complex cases.

15. The timing of an RNA switch

A complete RNA molecule studied in a tube is not always equivalent to the nascent RNA that operates during transcription. In the cell, sequence becomes available in order. Upstream regions can begin forming structures before downstream pairing partners have emerged. A ligand that binds after a transcription termination decision has already been made cannot retroactively convert the short released RNA into a full-length transcript. The opportunity to respond to the ligand is therefore bounded in time for many transcriptional riboswitches.

Consider a simplified model in which the aptamer becomes accessible and the polymerase must travel another defined distance before reaching the point at which termination is committed. If the polymerase moves at an approximately constant speed, the time available is the distance in nucleotides divided by the elongation speed in nucleotides per second. A span of 60 nucleotides traversed at 30 nucleotides per second provides roughly two seconds. At 60 nucleotides per second, the same span provides roughly one second. These are illustrative values, not measurements for a particular natural riboswitch. They demonstrate why changes in transcription speed can alter the probability that ligand binding takes place in time.

A pause of the polymerase within this interval may increase the time during which the aptamer can fold and encounter the ligand. If ligand binding favors termination, a pause that extends the binding window could promote repression in an appropriately designed OFF switch. The same pause is not guaranteed to have this effect in every system. It might occur before the binding pocket has formed or after the outcome is already committed; a change in pause position can favor a different RNA structure. The reasoning must take into account both the order of structural events and the location of the pause.

The distinction between affinity and binding speed is also important. Two aptamers could have the same equilibrium dissociation constant but different association and dissociation rates. The equilibrium constant reports the ratio of these rates in a simple reversible model, not their individual values. An RNA with relatively slow association might require a longer time or a higher ligand concentration to bind during a short transcriptional window. An RNA with rapid association could encounter the ligand efficiently even when the time is brief. If both systems are allowed to equilibrate for sufficiently long periods, their fractional occupancy may become the same. Their behavior in a rapidly progressing transcription complex can nevertheless differ.

This is why the concentration giving half-maximal regulatory output need not match the dissociation constant measured for an isolated aptamer. The output depends on ligand recognition, folding, communication with the expression platform and the timing of the relevant expression step. In a simple equilibrium switch with perfect coupling, the two concentrations might be close. In a kinetic switch, a larger concentration may be needed to achieve enough binding before commitment, or other features of the pathway may shift the relationship. Treating a reporter dose–response curve as a direct measurement of molecular affinity is therefore unjustified unless the required model assumptions have been established.

The fate of an RNA molecule can also differ between transcriptional and translational switches in terms of reversibility. Once polymerase has released a prematurely terminated RNA, ligand dissociation cannot restore the missing coding region. New transcription is needed. An intact translationally regulated message might be able to alternate between accessible and inaccessible states while it remains present. However, some translational switches are also strongly affected by folding history, and RNA decay can remove the message. The statement that all translational switches are freely reversible would be as misleading as the statement that all transcriptional switches simply reflect equilibrium binding.

16. Natural examples of riboswitches

A useful set of natural examples begins with thiamine pyrophosphate, commonly abbreviated TPP or ThDP. This molecule is the diphosphate derivative of vitamin B1 and acts as a cofactor for enzymes involved in carbohydrate and related metabolism. Its concentration is an informative signal because a cell that has sufficient cofactor may not need to invest further resources in producing or acquiring it. Genes associated with thiamine biosynthesis and utilization are subject to different regulatory mechanisms across organisms, including TPP-responsive RNA elements.

In 2002 Winkler, Nahvi and Breaker reported direct binding of thiamine derivatives to RNA elements associated with thiamine metabolism in Escherichia coli. Their work supported the idea that a messenger RNA could act as a direct receptor rather than merely carry a signal recognized by a protein. In a representative bacterial system, the TPP-bound RNA rearranges the initiation region so that translation becomes less favorable. The metabolite therefore controls expression by altering the behavior of the message itself. The relevant ligand in a physiological TPP-responsive switch is the phosphorylated cofactor; vitamin B1 and TPP must not be treated as identical chemical species simply because their metabolism is related.

Ligand specificity has an important physiological implication here. A biosynthetic intermediate may be present before the cell has completed the production of the active cofactor. If the RNA responds most effectively to the completed cofactor, expression can remain appropriate until a sufficient amount of the functional product has accumulated. A compound related in structure might occupy the binding pocket but not produce the same regulatory output. Affinity and regulatory effect are experimentally distinguishable properties. The result depends on whether the ligand stabilizes the configuration that communicates with the expression platform, not merely whether it makes any contact with RNA.

Flavin mononucleotide, FMN, provides a second example. FMN is derived from riboflavin and participates in biological oxidation–reduction chemistry. An RNA element associated with riboflavin metabolism can bind FMN directly and use this signal to alter expression. Work published by Winkler, Cohen-Chalamish and Breaker in 2002 demonstrated protein-independent recognition of FMN and supported regulatory outcomes involving premature transcription termination or reduced accessibility of a translation-initiation region in different contexts. These alternatives illustrate a central principle of riboswitch design: related sensing functions can be associated with different outputs. Students should identify the actual expression platform rather than memorize a fixed mapping in which FMN necessarily controls only transcription and TPP necessarily controls only translation.

S-adenosylmethionine, abbreviated SAM, serves as a methyl-group donor in numerous cellular reactions. Regulatory RNA elements that recognize SAM can link the state of methionine-related metabolism to expression of relevant biosynthetic or transport genes. A common logic is that abundant SAM indicates that resources for these processes are already available, so further biosynthetic investment can be reduced. This is an example of feedback regulation operating through RNA. It should be distinguished from a reaction in which SAM directly donates a methyl group to the RNA. In an ordinary SAM-binding riboswitch, binding communicates the signal; the aptamer is not defined by catalyzing transfer of the methyl group.

Purine-responsive riboswitches demonstrate the selectivity that can arise from closely related ligand structures. Adenine and guanine differ in the pattern of chemical groups on their purine rings. A correctly folded RNA pocket can exploit these differences through complementary contacts. Different members of related purine-sensing RNA families can favor different ligands. The biological output may also differ: an adenine-responsive switch can activate an associated gene, while a guanine-responsive biosynthetic switch can repress expression in an appropriate regulatory architecture. The direction is not a necessary property of the chemical identity of the ligand. It is a property of the coupled sensing and expression system.

Other examples include RNA elements responsive to lysine, glycine, cyclic nucleotides and magnesium ions. These names are useful as representative evidence of chemical diversity, not as a list of families to memorize. A glycine signal can inform a cell about an available metabolic substrate; a magnesium signal can report an aspect of the ionic environment. In considering an ion-responsive RNA element, a particularly important question is whether magnesium specifically acts as the regulated signal or simply supplies the general ionic conditions needed for RNA to fold. All structured RNA molecules are affected to some extent by ionic conditions. Not all of them are magnesium-sensing riboswitches. A specific regulatory connection between an ionic signal and gene expression is needed for that designation.

A comparison of these examples helps reveal why RNA-mediated regulation can be valuable. A cell can respond to a changing intracellular condition without first producing a new protein receptor for each newly made transcript. The sensing RNA is transcribed as part of the message itself. The cost of synthesizing the regulatory segment must still be paid, and the regulatory response has limits imposed by folding and ligand availability. A riboswitch is not inherently superior to a protein-based regulatory system. Both have advantages in particular biological contexts, and their coexistence illustrates the diversity of mechanisms through which cells control resource use.

17. Catalysis as the regulatory output: the glmS exception

The distinction between ribozyme and riboswitch becomes especially informative in the case of the glmS regulatory RNA. The bacterial GlmS enzyme participates in producing glucosamine-6-phosphate, an important precursor in amino sugar metabolism and the formation of cellular envelope components. Accumulation of glucosamine-6-phosphate can therefore provide feedback about the product of the pathway. An RNA element in the glmS message responds to this metabolite in a way that differs from the conventional model of a mutually exclusive terminator and antiterminator.

Winkler and colleagues reported in 2004 that glucosamine-6-phosphate activates a naturally occurring self-cleaving RNA associated with glmS expression. The regulatory RNA catalyzes cleavage of a specific bond in its own transcript. This reaction produces a downstream RNA with a 5′ hydroxyl and an upstream cleavage product with a cyclic phosphate at its 3′ end. The downstream coding-containing fragment becomes susceptible to degradation in the relevant bacterial system, reducing expression. The cellular output therefore depends on an RNA processing and turnover event rather than directly requiring premature transcription termination or sequestration of a ribosome-binding sequence.

An interesting property of this example is that the metabolite contributes to the catalytic process, rather than acting only as a signal that reorganizes a separate expression platform. Its amino group participates in the chemical environment that promotes cleavage. A standard textbook model in which every riboswitch has two entirely distinct domains and functions solely by moving between alternative large structures would not adequately explain this system. The glmS RNA is a catalytic RNA with metabolite-dependent regulatory function. It provides a legitimate case in which the terms ribozyme and riboswitch can both be relevant.

The logic of feedback remains recognizable. The gene encodes an enzyme that helps produce the metabolite; increasing availability of the metabolite activates an RNA reaction that reduces the lifetime of the message used to make that enzyme. Decreased message availability tends to reduce subsequent protein production. This is distinct from immediate allosteric inhibition of the already existing enzyme protein. Existing GlmS molecules do not disappear the instant RNA cleavage occurs. The effect concerns production of additional enzyme through regulation of its message, and the total protein abundance changes according to synthesis and degradation over time.

A useful way to distinguish a glmS-like mechanism experimentally is to look for specific cleavage products. If a ligand merely masks the translation-initiation region, intact RNA might remain detectable and there would be no requirement for the characteristic cleavage termini. If the ligand promotes self-cleavage, defined fragments should appear in a suitable cell-free experiment. In a living cell, the fragments might disappear rapidly because downstream degradation is part of the regulatory pathway. Absence of a stable fragment in a cellular steady-state sample therefore does not establish absence of cleavage. A time-resolved experiment or a perturbation of the degradation pathway may reveal intermediates that are otherwise short-lived.

18. Beyond bacteria: an RNA sensor can regulate RNA processing

Most introductory examples of riboswitches involve bacteria because bacterial gene organization and transcription provide clear examples of regulatory leaders. The presence of riboswitches in eukaryotes demonstrates that the underlying recognition principle is not restricted to that particular transcriptional arrangement. Natural TPP-sensing RNA elements have been characterized in fungi and plants. Their location and downstream consequences differ from the familiar bacterial leader terminator.

In the fungus Neurospora crassa, a TPP-responsive element in a precursor messenger RNA regulates alternative splicing. Ligand binding influences which RNA sequence interactions are favored, thereby changing accessibility of splice sites and the pattern of processed messages. The regulatory decision takes place at the level of RNA maturation. It does not require a bacterial Shine–Dalgarno sequence or a bacterial intrinsic terminator. The same broad principle of ligand-dependent RNA structure is connected to the molecular machinery available in the eukaryotic cell.

In Arabidopsis, a TPP-responsive element associated with the THIC transcript lies in the 3′ untranslated region and influences RNA processing, including alternative splicing and 3′ end formation. The processed RNA products differ in their downstream properties, affecting message accumulation and protein production. Studies published in 2007 demonstrated this regulatory relationship. This example is particularly useful for correcting the idea that an untranslated region located downstream of a coding sequence is incapable of influencing gene expression. A regulatory event can alter message stability, processing or use without changing the amino acid sequence encoded by the principal reading frame.

The comparison also establishes an important boundary for the interpretation of evolution. The detection of a TPP-binding RNA in bacteria and eukaryotes does not imply that every organism uses the same regulatory output. Natural selection can preserve a ligand-recognition function while the way that function is connected to gene expression changes. The conserved activity can be considered separately from the platform, transcript location and cellular machinery used to carry out the response. A student examining a new organism should therefore avoid automatically applying the bacterial mechanism to a eukaryotic transcript merely because the aptamer binds the same ligand.

19. Quantitative reasoning: binding, rates and regulatory output

A simple equilibrium model is sufficient to understand many exam problems about RNA receptors. Let R denote a free RNA aptamer, L a free ligand and RL the complex. In a reversible one-to-one binding reaction, the equilibrium dissociation constant is Kd = [R][L]/[RL]. Square brackets represent concentrations in the same units, usually molar or micromolar units. Because the numerator has concentration squared and the denominator has concentration, Kd has units of concentration. A smaller Kd indicates stronger affinity under the stated conditions. This interpretation does not mean that an aptamer with a smaller Kd always produces a larger regulatory response, because downstream coupling can differ.

Suppose the total RNA concentration is Rt = [R] + [RL]. The fraction of RNA bound is f = [RL]/Rt. Rearranging the equilibrium expression gives [R] = Kd[RL]/[L]. Substitution into the total concentration expression and division by [RL] gives f = [L]/(Kd + [L]). The expression assumes a simple one-site binding equilibrium, with free ligand concentration known. If RNA is dilute relative to the ligand, the total added ligand concentration may approximate free ligand concentration. If the two are comparable, that approximation can fail because binding removes a significant fraction of ligand from solution.

As a hypothetical example, take Kd = 2 μM and free ligand concentration = 6 μM. The bound fraction is 6/(2+6) = 0.75. Three quarters of the RNA molecules are expected to be bound at equilibrium under the model. If ligand concentration falls to 0.5 μM, the bound fraction becomes 0.5/2.5 = 0.20. At free ligand equal to Kd, half of the molecules are bound. This relationship is an occupancy curve, not automatically a protein-expression curve. A transcriptional switch that operates far from equilibrium could yield quite different regulatory fractions at the same ligand concentrations.

For a simple illustrative equilibrium OFF switch, assume unbound molecules produce a normalized output of 1.0 and bound molecules produce an output of 0.1. The mean normalized output is the weighted sum of outputs from the two populations: Y = (1−f) × 1.0 + f × 0.1 = 1.0−0.9f. At f = 0.75, Y = 0.325. At f = 0.20, Y = 0.82. The model includes incomplete repression in the bound state. It shows that the fraction of bound RNA is not equal to the fraction of total expression lost unless specific assumptions about the outputs of each state are satisfied.

A similarly elementary kinetic model describes a self-cleaving RNA when all precursor molecules are initially active, cleavage is effectively irreversible during observation and one first-order step limits the process. If P is the concentration of intact precursor, its rate of disappearance is dP/dt = −kP. Separating variables and integrating from the initial concentration P0 to P(t) yields ln(P/P0) = −kt, and hence P(t)/P0 = exp(−kt). The cleavage fraction is 1−exp(−kt), and the half-life of intact precursor is ln(2)/k. If k = 0.20 min⁻¹, the half-life is approximately 3.47 minutes. After five minutes the remaining fraction is exp(−1), approximately 0.368, giving about 63.2 percent cleavage. These values are hypothetical and are not measurements of a named natural ribozyme.

The time course can help separate recognition from catalysis. For example, a mutant with half the cleavage rate of a wild-type RNA but the same final cleavage extent could have altered catalytic speed rather than a reduced number of active molecules. A mutant with the same initial rate constant for the active fraction but a smaller final extent might instead contain a larger population of inactive molecules. Such interpretations are hypotheses that require evidence. In a real experiment a plateau can also result from reversal, product effects or degradation. The simple model is valuable precisely because its assumptions can be checked against observations.

For ligand association during a short transcriptional window, a complementary simple model uses a pseudo-first-order rate kobs = kon[L], where kon has units of concentration⁻¹ time⁻¹. If the aptamer starts unbound, ligand remains constant and dissociation and other folding transitions are negligible during the window, the probability of at least one binding event by time t is 1−exp(−kon[L]t). At kon = 1 μM⁻¹ s⁻¹, [L] = 0.5 μM and t = 2 s, this probability is about 0.632. Halving the time to one second lowers it to about 0.393. This describes encounter probability under restrictive assumptions, not the exact fraction of terminated natural transcripts. To infer termination, one must also know whether binding occurs in the relevant active conformation and whether it successfully changes the platform before the commitment point.

These models connect the chapter with earlier principles of enzyme catalysis and binding equilibrium while staying within the intended examination level. There is no requirement to fit elaborate multistate kinetic schemes to understand the central consequences. Increasing ligand concentration can increase equilibrium occupancy and, under appropriate assumptions, increase the rate of association. Changing RNA structure can alter affinity, chemistry or communication independently. Changing transcription speed can alter the time available for a regulatory decision without changing the chemical structure of the ligand. In analytical questions, identifying which model is applicable is often more important than carrying out the final arithmetic.

20. From data to biological conclusions

Consider a hypothetical bacterial RNA that is suspected of being a transcriptional OFF riboswitch. In a cell-free transcription reaction without ligand, 90 out of 100 initiated transcripts read through the leader. With ligand, only 20 out of 100 read through, and a correspondingly larger short product appears at the expected termination site. An aptamer mutant shows 89 readthrough transcripts without ligand and 87 with ligand. An independent binding assay finds that the mutant no longer binds the ligand detectably at the tested concentration. These observations support a mechanism in which direct ligand recognition contributes to premature termination. They do not by themselves show which individual atoms contact the ligand.

Now consider a platform mutant whose isolated leader binds ligand comparably to the wild type but whose transcription reaction yields about 90 readthrough products in both conditions. The binding step is retained while the regulatory consequence is lost. This provides an experimentally distinct phenotype from the aptamer mutant. A third mutant with low readthrough in both conditions could be locked in the termination-favoring state. When interpreting such a dataset, the negative control and the constitutive-state mutant are useful because they show that the same loss of ligand responsiveness can arise at different mechanistic steps.

A second hypothetical experiment addresses a self-cleaving ribozyme. The wild-type RNA produces two defined fragments, while a mutant with a disrupted stem gives little cleavage. A second mutation restores the stem and substantially restores cleavage. This pattern supports a structural requirement. If a different point mutant binds a trans substrate with near-normal affinity but produces no detectable product, the altered nucleotide may be important in the catalytic arrangement rather than initial recognition. The absence of product could also reflect subtle conformational or alignment defects; it is not sufficient proof that the nucleotide directly transfers a proton. A conclusion about atomic chemistry requires evidence more specific than a single mutation and a binding assay.

RNA cleavage can also be tested using product identity. If a hypothetical 100-nucleotide precursor carries a 25-nucleotide leader and RNase P hydrolyzes precisely at the expected junction, the products should contain 25 and 75 nucleotides. An observed 76-nucleotide mature-region product would indicate cleavage one nucleotide upstream of the expected boundary if all other assumptions about the substrate and product are correct. A mobility shift on a non-denaturing gel, however, need not correspond exactly to a change of one nucleotide. Denaturing separation, known length markers and evidence about product termini help avoid interpreting a folded structure as an altered chain length.

A reporter assay is useful because regulatory consequences can be measured in a relatively simple way. The candidate RNA element can be associated with a reporter whose product is quantifiable. Appropriate control constructs include one lacking the regulatory element and one with a recognition-defective mutation. The ligand can affect cellular physiology through mechanisms unrelated to the RNA, so a change in reporter activity alone is not proof of direct sensing. An independent RNA-binding assay and evidence of the predicted RNA structural change provide complementary information. Measuring RNA abundance and protein output can help distinguish transcriptional, translational and stability-related mechanisms.

The ability of RNA to couple chemical recognition to a functional response motivates applications in biosensors and synthetic regulatory systems. A selected aptamer can be linked conceptually to a reporter output, or an engineered catalytic RNA can be directed toward a suitable RNA substrate. These applications illustrate the principles of recognition and coupling rather than guarantee that every design works in living cells. Specificity, folding, access to the target, cellular degradation and the behavior of the regulatory platform can all limit performance. A ligand analog that binds a bacterial RNA sensor may also be of interest in research on microbial metabolism, but binding alone does not establish useful antimicrobial activity or selectivity.

The discovery of ribozymes additionally makes plausible an evolutionary scenario in which early RNA-like molecules performed both informational and catalytic functions. This is often discussed as part of the RNA world hypothesis. The modern examples do not demonstrate that all earliest life used RNA alone or that any particular current ribozyme was present unchanged in the earliest organisms. They establish that RNA can carry sequence information, form recognition sites and support chemical transformations. The evolutionary hypothesis draws on these capacities, along with other evidence and open questions about replication, stability and the emergence of proteins.

The chapter brings together two complementary insights from RNA biology. A ribozyme reveals how a nucleotide sequence can fold into a chemically productive structure. A riboswitch reveals how an RNA can connect molecular recognition to a change in expression. The first principle prepares the student to understand the catalytic center of the ribosome; the second provides a foundation for studying more integrated gene-regulatory mechanisms. In both cases the useful mode of reasoning begins by identifying the RNA structure, follows the interactions and chemical or physical event, and ends by predicting a measurable biological consequence.

Part C: Analytical and quantitative practice

All numerical data and scenarios in this section are hypothetical educational examples, not reported measurements. Predict the result before opening each explanation.

36. A 130-nt precursor contains a 38-nt 5′ leader. Correct RNase P cleavage yields what product lengths?

Answer: 38 and 92 nt

130−38=92 nt. The leader and mature region account for the original 130 nucleotides. Cleavage itself adds no nucleotide.

37. An RNase P substrate is labeled only in the 5′ leader. Which product will a label-specific detector show after complete processing?

Answer: The released leader

The mature tRNA loses the labeled region. Absence of a visible mature tRNA in this detection mode is not evidence that processing failed.

38. A group I precursor has exons of 70 and 55 nt and an intron of 120 nt. One external guanosine is incorporated, with no secondary reaction. Predict the lengths of joined exons and excised intron.

Answer: 125 and 121 nt

70+55=125; 120+1=121. Total product nucleotide count is 246, one more than the precursor because a guanosine was incorporated.

39. A group II intron contains 120 nt and its exons total 125 nt. What changes in nucleotide count when the intron branches and is excised?

Answer: No nucleotide is added; 120-nt intron and 125-nt joined exons

The 2′ hydroxyl belongs to an existing intron nucleotide. An unusual linkage is formed but no external nucleotide is incorporated in the standard branch pathway.

40. A group I mutant produces free 5′ exon and intron still linked to 3′ exon, but little joined exon product. Which step is impaired?

Answer: Second transesterification

The first reaction already generated the upstream exon hydroxyl. Its attack on the downstream junction has not occurred efficiently. Lack of initial free exon would indicate a first-step problem.

41. A ribozyme substrate binds mutant and wild type equally well, but the mutant makes no product. What can and cannot be concluded?

Answer: A post-binding defect is likely; direct catalytic atom assignment is not established

The observed binding rules out gross loss of affinity under the assay conditions. Misalignment, a defective chemical group or a subtle folding problem remain possible.

42. A stem mutation G–C→A–C abolishes ligand response; a second mutation yielding A–U restores response. What is supported?

Answer: The paired structure contributes to regulation

The rescue despite sequence change favors a pairing-dependent role. It does not prove the stem is the only structural requirement or that every alternative pair will work.

43. For Kd = 4 μM and free ligand 12 μM, find equilibrium fractional occupancy.

Answer: 0.75

f=12/(4+12)=0.75. This applies to one-to-one equilibrium binding, not necessarily a cotranscriptional output.

44. At free ligand 0.25 μM, occupancy is 20%. Find Kd for the simple one-site model.

Answer: 1 μM

0.2=0.25/(Kd+0.25), so Kd+0.25=1.25 and Kd=1.00 μM.

45. For a switch whose unbound and bound outputs are 100 and 20 units, respectively, compute mean output when occupancy is 60%.

Answer: 52 units

Y=0.40×100+0.60×20=40+12=52. Assuming 60% repression would ignore residual bound-state output.

46. A self-cleaving RNA follows first-order irreversible loss with k=0.10 min⁻¹. What fraction remains after 10 min?

Answer: 0.368, or about 36.8%

P/P0=e^(−0.10×10)=e^(−1). Cleaved fraction is 63.2%, not 36.8%.

47. A homogeneous self-cleaving precursor has a 5-min half-life. Find its rate constant.

Answer: 0.139 min⁻¹

k=ln 2/5≈0.1386 min⁻¹. The half-life is independent of starting concentration only under the assumed first-order model.

48. A nascent aptamer can bind for 80 nt of elongation at 40 nt s⁻¹. An additional 1-s pause falls entirely inside this window. What is the window?

Answer: 3 s

80/40=2 s plus 1 s. Pauses outside the available binding interval would not necessarily add usable time.

49. Take kon=2 μM⁻¹ s⁻¹, ligand=0.5 μM and window=2 s. Under the encounter model with negligible dissociation, find binding probability.

Answer: About 0.865

kon[L]t=2×0.5×2=2; 1−e^(−2)=0.8647. This is not automatically termination probability.

50. Hypothetical transcription assay: 120 starts, 96 full-length without ligand and 30 full-length with ligand. Assuming all starts yield either full-length or terminated RNA, find termination percentages.

Answer: 20% without ligand; 75% with ligand

Without ligand (120−96)/120=0.20; with ligand (120−30)/120=0.75. The change concerns elongation outcome, not initiation frequency.

51. A switch mutant binds FMN normally but produces nearly all full-length RNA regardless of ligand. Its terminator stem has been disrupted. Explain.

Answer: The output platform is defective, not necessarily the ligand receptor

Normal binding does not guarantee termination. Disrupting the required hairpin lets polymerase read through even in the bound state.

52. A ligand reduces protein 10-fold but full-length RNA remains constant. What additional observation would strengthen translational rather than protein-stability control?

Answer: Reduced initiation/ribosome occupancy at comparable RNA levels with protein stability controlled

Steady-state protein is determined by production and loss. A translation-specific observation is needed to separate the two explanations.

53. A glmS mutant binds metabolite but cannot self-cleave. Predict a result in a controlled cell-free cleavage assay and a plausible expression phenotype in cells.

Answer: Loss of ligand-induced fragments; impaired ligand-dependent repression

Recognition is not sufficient for cleavage-mediated turnover. Other cellular regulatory processes could affect expression, so the expression prediction should be tested.

54. An RNA-dependent enzyme loses activity with EDTA and regains it with Mg²⁺. Is direct participation of Mg²⁺ in catalysis proved?

Answer: No

Metal chelation may disturb folding, substrate binding or the chemical step. Independent structural and chemical evidence is required to locate its role.

55. Ligand A and B each have Kd=1 μM. A has fast association and dissociation; B has slow association and dissociation. Which necessarily binds more rapidly during a short initially unbound window at equal free concentration?

Answer: A has the larger initial association rate, but regulatory outcomes need not follow that order

Equal Kd fixes the ratio koff/kon, not the individual rates. Rapid binding is favored initially for A, but retention and coupling determine the ultimate output.

Revision and active recall

Essential formulas

One-site equilibrium: Kd = [R][L]/[RL]; f = [L]/(Kd + [L]).

Weighted output: Y = (1−f)Yfree + fYbound.

Irreversible first-order cleavage: P/P0 = exp(−kt); t½ = ln(2)/k.

Short binding window: t ≈ distance/speed; probability ≈ 1−exp(−kon[L]t) when dissociation and competing folding can be neglected.

Mechanism retrieval

RNase P: hydrolysis of the 5′ leader junction. Group I: free G attacks, then exon 3′ OH joins the exons. Group II: internal A branches, then exon 3′ OH joins the exons. Ribosome: amino-group attack on activated ester. Conventional riboswitch: binding → RNA fold → expression platform → output. glmS: ligand-activated RNA cleavage → turnover.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

1. Catalytic RNA is not synonymous with all ncRNA. Many functional RNAs are structural or regulatory without performing chemistry.

2. A riboswitch is not necessarily a ribozyme. Conventional switches act through binding and structural coupling; glmS is an overlapping case.

3. RNase P does not remove the 3′ end. Its classical processing reaction removes the 5′ leader of pre-tRNA.

4. Do not equate all RNase P systems. Bacterial RNA-based enzymes and protein-only human mitochondrial RNase P solve similar tasks.

5. A group I guanosine is not a GTP-powered motor. Its 3′ hydroxyl participates in transesterification.

6. A lariat is not a simple end-to-end circle. The group II branch is a 2′–5′ linkage.

7. The peptidyl-transferase center is not the small subunit. Bacterial 23S rRNA in the large subunit provides the catalytic architecture.

8. Binding does not demonstrate output. Aptamer and platform defects are separable.

9. A transcription terminator is not a stop codon. One concerns release of RNA polymerase; the other concerns translation termination.

10. Ligand binding does not always repress expression. ON and OFF outcomes depend on the platform.

11. Kd is not necessarily the reporter EC50. Timing, coupling and residual expression affect regulatory output.

12. A 5′ UTR is not the only possible location. Eukaryotic TPP riboswitches can regulate RNA processing from other positions.

13. RNA catalysis does not mean cellular protein independence. The reaction center and the functioning cellular complex must be distinguished.

14. Do not infer direct metal chemistry from chelation. Folding and catalysis can both depend on ions.

Active recall

How can RNA catalyze chemistry despite four standard bases?

Folding positions chemical groups, substrate and sometimes metal ions to favor reaction.

How do RNase P and self-cleaving ribozyme products differ?

RNase P gives 3′ OH/5′ phosphate; a typical small self-cleaver gives cyclic phosphate/5′ OH.

What initiates group I splicing?

External guanosine 3′ OH.

What initiates canonical group II splicing?

Internal branch-point adenosine 2′ OH.

What joins the exons in both pathways?

The upstream exon 3′ OH attacks the downstream junction.

What is the difference between an aptamer and a platform?

Recognition versus functional coupling to gene expression.

How is an OFF transcription switch different from a translational switch?

The first prevents complete RNA production; the second primarily reduces use of intact RNA.

Why can faster elongation affect riboswitch sensitivity?

It may shorten the time for folding and ligand association before commitment.

Which natural example links sensing directly to self-cleavage?

glmS, activated by glucosamine-6-phosphate.

What is needed to establish direct sensing?

Independent ligand binding, appropriate controls and evidence connecting recognition to the regulatory output.

  • I can predict outcomes of aptamer, platform and compensatory mutations.
  • I can distinguish transcriptional termination, translation-initiation control and cleavage-mediated decay.
  • I can calculate occupancy, first-order cleavage and simple binding-window probabilities with correct assumptions.
