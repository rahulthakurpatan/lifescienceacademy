1. Why cells need stable but reversible contacts

An animal tissue is neither a collection of independent cells nor a single continuous cytoplasm. Each cell is surrounded by a plasma membrane that maintains its own internal chemical environment. At the same time, the cells of an epithelium must remain physically associated while experiencing changes in shape, mechanical stress and replacement of individual cells. The surface of the intestine illustrates this problem especially well. The epithelium has to maintain a continuous boundary even when neighboring cells change position or divide. If the membranes merely touched without forming specific connections, small movements could separate them. If all connections were permanent, however, cell division and tissue repair would become difficult. Cell adhesion is the biological solution to these competing requirements: it creates selective, regulated physical connections between cells and their surroundings.

Adhesion means that two surfaces resist separation because interactions exist between them. In cell biology the surfaces may be those of two cells, or one cell and the extracellular matrix. The interaction may be localized to a few molecules or distributed across a larger region in which many receptors cooperate. A recognizable contact in a microscope is not by itself evidence of strong adhesion. Two cells can be compressed together by their neighbors, or may collide briefly without forming a load-bearing junction. To demonstrate adhesion one must establish that some molecular interaction actually retains the surfaces together. Conversely, two cells can be genuinely adhesive before their contact has acquired the conspicuous organization of a mature junction. Adhesion molecules provide the initial biochemical specificity; junctions organize many such interactions into specialized cellular structures.

It is useful to distinguish three levels of explanation. At the molecular level we ask which extracellular surfaces of proteins recognize one another and what chemical conditions support their binding. At the cellular level we ask how these proteins reach the surface, accumulate in particular regions, and attach to the internal cytoskeleton. At the tissue level we ask how the resulting interfaces distribute force and allow different populations of cells to maintain recognizable boundaries. A change in one level need not produce an identical change in another. A purified protein can retain its ability to bind another molecule while a cell expressing it remains mechanically fragile because the intracellular attachment is defective. This distinction is central to interpreting experimental questions on cadherins.

The word signaling describes transmission of information that alters the state of a receiving cell. Adhesion and signaling are therefore not synonyms. A hormone may activate a cell without physically attaching it to the hormone-producing cell. A cadherin-mediated contact may primarily resist mechanical separation while also influencing the behavior of cytoskeletal proteins or the organization of other receptors. These two functions frequently cooperate. For example, when a cell detects that its neighbors are attached, the resulting local organization of its cortex may alter the forces that it generates and the direction in which it moves. Physical coupling thus changes the cellular conditions under which other signaling pathways operate. It does not follow that every adhesive interaction must initiate a conventional second-messenger cascade.

A cell adhesion molecule, abbreviated CAM, is generally a cell-surface protein participating in selective attachment. The term is functional rather than a single structural classification. Some CAMs bind another copy of their own type of protein; others recognize a structurally different molecule. Some operate largely at the contact between two cells, whereas others recognize extracellular matrix proteins. Their extracellular domains perform recognition and binding. In many cases an intracellular region connects directly or through adaptor proteins to a structural or regulatory network. Because recognition and intracellular anchorage are separable functions, cells can regulate their adhesive behavior without necessarily changing the identity of the protein that is expressed.

The ordinary plasma membrane is not a universal biological glue. Its outer surface is hydrated and covered by proteins and carbohydrate-containing structures collectively associated with the glycocalyx. Membranes can approach closely but they encounter steric and electrostatic barriers whose importance depends on their molecular composition. Adhesive proteins extend from the surface and provide a way for neighboring cells to recognize one another across this extracellular space. Binding does not require the lipid bilayers to merge. The two cells preserve their individuality, and their cytoplasms remain separated. This is why a cadherin junction cannot be equated with membrane fusion or a gap junction.

Stable adhesion is relative to the biological time scale and to the magnitude of forces applied. A contact maintained during an observation lasting several minutes may be considered stable compared with a rolling blood cell that repeatedly attaches and detaches. It is not necessary for every molecular bond within that contact to remain intact throughout the observation. Many surface proteins can dissociate and reassociate while the overall interface persists. A contact that survives the movement of a whole epithelium may contain thousands of bonds that individually have finite lifetimes. This principle makes it possible for a tissue to retain cohesion while remodeling its molecular components.

2. The four major families of adhesion receptors

The major CAM families encountered in standard animal cell biology are cadherins, selectins, members of the immunoglobulin superfamily and integrins. Their shared ability to participate in attachment should not obscure their different molecular architectures. Classification into these families is based on structural characteristics and evolutionary relationships, not merely on the strength of the attachment that they produce. When an examination question identifies a receptor only as an adhesion molecule, additional evidence is required to classify it. Its extracellular ligand, ion requirements, cytoplasmic partners and biological context can each provide clues.

Cadherins form a large protein superfamily characterized by extracellular cadherin domains. Classical cadherins, including E-cadherin and N-cadherin, are the principal examples for this chapter. Their extracellular interactions usually favor association with compatible cadherins on another cell, and calcium supports the conformation required for effective adhesion. Their intracellular organization permits coupling to actin-based structures. Desmosomal cadherins are related but use a different system of intracellular partners, linking their junctions to intermediate filaments. The common extracellular family relationship therefore does not require an identical intracellular function.

Selectins contain a carbohydrate-recognizing domain and typically bind appropriately modified sugars on glycoproteins or glycolipids at the cell surface. Their carbohydrate-binding activity normally requires calcium. A standard example is the temporary interaction between leukocytes and vascular endothelium during recruitment. These interactions can permit rolling because bonds are made and released as a moving cell encounters new positions on the vessel wall. The calcium requirement alone cannot distinguish a selectin from a cadherin. The kind of ligand and the character of the interface are needed to make the distinction. Selectins will be studied mechanistically in the following chapter.

Immunoglobulin-superfamily CAMs contain domains structurally related to immunoglobulin folds. Membership in this superfamily does not mean that every member is an antibody. Some proteins recognize identical molecules, while others recognize different partners, such as certain integrins. Many Ig-superfamily CAMs can perform their direct recognition functions without the extracellular calcium-binding arrangement characteristic of classical cadherins. This is a useful family-level comparison, not a guarantee about the ionic requirements of every complex in which an Ig-superfamily protein participates. For instance, an interaction between an Ig-like molecule and an integrin may be sensitive to the divalent cations required by the integrin.

Integrins are receptors assembled from an alpha and a beta subunit. Many bind extracellular matrix ligands such as fibronectin or laminin, establishing mechanical continuity between the cell and its substrate. Other integrins recognize proteins presented on another cell, so an integrin is not necessarily restricted to matrix attachment. Integrin binding is regulated by divalent cations and by changes in receptor conformation. Detailed mechanisms of affinity regulation and linkage to the cytoskeleton belong to Node 4.B.05. At this stage the important comparison is that classical cadherins often recognize related molecules directly on an opposing cell, while integrins are heterodimeric receptors whose ligand can be a matrix protein or a different cell-surface protein.

The distinction between homophilic and heterophilic interaction applies to the molecular partners, not to the cell lineages. Homophilic binding occurs when molecules of the same kind recognize each other. An epithelial cell and an engineered fibroblast could engage in homophilic E-cadherin adhesion if both display functional E-cadherin. A heterophilic interaction instead involves different molecular partners. The two cells could belong to the same tissue or even the same cell type and still use a heterophilic receptor–ligand pair. A question about cell sorting may use the expression profiles of cells to predict their recognition, but the correct first step is to identify the molecules available on both apposed surfaces.

It is also important to distinguish adhesion molecules from the structural proteins they recruit. Actin is not a classical CAM because it normally resides inside the cell and does not span the extracellular gap. A catenin is not the extracellular ligand for a cadherin; it associates with the intracellular portion of the cadherin complex. Likewise a calcium ion is not the opposing cell's adhesion receptor. Calcium modifies the structural and chemical environment of the extracellular cadherin domains. The direct recognition surface is formed primarily by proteins. Keeping these roles separate prevents the common error of assigning the wrong location or function to an adhesion component.

3. How calcium-dependent and selective adhesion were discovered

Early embryological observations demonstrated that cells removed from tissues did not always form random mixtures when brought together again. In suitable conditions dissociated cells could reassemble into aggregates in which particular cell populations became spatially associated. The resulting arrangements suggested that cells possessed properties allowing them to distinguish different contact environments. These observations did not by themselves reveal a receptor. They were compatible with differences in surface chemistry, motility, mechanical tension, extracellular matrix production or some combination of these factors. A molecular explanation required experiments in which adhesive behavior could be varied while other properties were monitored.

Masatoshi Takeichi's 1977 work examined the relationship between cell surface proteins and distinct calcium-dependent and calcium-independent adhesive behaviors. Studying adhesion under altered extracellular calcium conditions made it possible to distinguish classes of surface interaction. Calcium-dependent attachment was associated with particular membrane proteins, whose susceptibility to proteolysis depended on the ionic conditions. The experiment was important because it connected a manipulable chemical requirement to a molecular component of the cell surface. It was not simply an observation that calcium generally makes cells healthier or increases cellular movement. The implication was that certain surface proteins possessed a specific requirement for calcium in their adhesive function.

Consider the logic of a calcium removal experiment. A population of healthy cells may form clusters when suspended under conditions favorable for aggregation. If calcium is selectively reduced and clustering declines, there are at least two explanations: a calcium-dependent adhesive mechanism has been disrupted, or the treatment has altered another process necessary for aggregation. Reintroducing calcium and observing recovery strengthens the argument for a reversible calcium requirement, especially when cell viability is preserved. Nevertheless, this does not identify the protein responsible. To establish the molecular cause, investigators need additional evidence such as protein-specific inhibition, molecular identification or expression of the candidate protein in a cell that previously lacked the relevant behavior.

An especially informative approach is to introduce a defined cadherin gene into cells whose baseline cell–cell adhesion is weak. The introduced gene can be transcribed and translated, and the resulting protein can reach the plasma membrane. If these cells now show calcium-dependent aggregation while cells carrying an empty control vector remain weakly adhesive, the result establishes that expression of the cadherin can be sufficient to promote the measured interaction in that cellular background. The qualification matters: an engineered cell is not a test tube containing only one protein. It still provides membrane trafficking, cortical actin and potentially compatible intracellular partners. The result therefore demonstrates sufficiency in a particular system rather than independence from every other component.

Nose, Nagafuchi and Takeichi used recombinant cadherins in a 1988 study of cell sorting. Cadherin expression changed the aggregation properties of engineered cell populations and could influence their spatial associations when mixed with other cells. In an embryonic lung reconstitution system, cells expressing E-cadherin became associated with epithelial structures bearing the same cadherin more readily than the corresponding untransfected cells. This connected the identity of a surface protein with the organization of a multicellular population. It supplied direct experimental support for the idea that differential expression of adhesive receptors contributes to sorting. It did not establish that cadherin identity is the only determinant of embryonic tissue architecture.

The history also illustrates why correlation and causation are different. Immunostaining an intact tissue may reveal E-cadherin concentrated between epithelial cells. That is evidence for a spatial association between the molecule and the contact. If specific blocking of E-cadherin causes contact failure, the case for a functional requirement becomes stronger. If restoring the protein rescues the defect, a nonspecific effect becomes less likely. If introduction into an appropriate nonadhesive cell confers aggregation, the protein's role can be tested in the opposite direction. Together these types of evidence build a mechanism from several complementary observations rather than relying on a single impressive image.

The early distinction between calcium-dependent and calcium-independent adhesion remains useful because it allows simple experimental reasoning. But a modern experiment must also consider different calcium-sensitive receptor families. Removing extracellular calcium can alter cadherins, selectins, ion channels and cellular physiological conditions. Consequently, calcium dependence is a property to be explained, not a unique molecular identification test. In a controlled question where the only manipulated receptor is E-cadherin, a calcium effect can be interpreted more specifically than in an intact tissue containing multiple adhesion systems.

4. Molecular architecture of a classical cadherin

A classical cadherin is a membrane glycoprotein whose overall organization is suited to linking the environment outside a cell with machinery inside the cell. The mature protein contains an amino-terminal extracellular region, a single membrane-spanning segment and a carboxyl-terminal cytoplasmic region. In a typical vertebrate classical cadherin, the extracellular region consists of five successive cadherin domains, named EC1 to EC5 from the membrane-distal end toward the membrane-proximal end. Each domain is folded rather than an unstructured extension. Their combination creates an elongated extracellular structure capable of reaching an opposing cell without requiring the membranes to occupy the same space.

The order of the domains gives a convenient way to interpret mutations. An alteration in EC1 can interfere with the recognition surface while leaving the membrane-spanning and intracellular regions intact. A mutation in the membrane anchor may prevent normal surface display even if the extracellular domains could fold and bind in isolation. An alteration in the cytoplasmic tail may disrupt the recruitment of intracellular partners while the exterior still recognizes its ligand. These predictions are conditional on the mutant protein being expressed, folded and correctly localized. A mutation at a recognition site can also impair folding or trafficking, so a reduced aggregation result alone does not identify the exact cause.

The extracellular domains are joined by short linker regions that coordinate calcium ions. Calcium binding changes the behavior of the chain of domains, limiting the flexibility between them and maintaining a geometry that favors adhesive interactions. The importance of the linkers becomes obvious if the extracellular portion is imagined as a chain of rigid blocks joined by movable hinges. If all hinges are highly flexible, the distal recognition domain can occupy many orientations relative to the membrane. Constraining the hinges reduces this uncertainty and helps position the recognition surface in the extracellular space. The analogy concerns domain orientation rather than the detailed chemical structure of the protein.

Calcium coordination occurs through oxygen-containing groups supplied by the protein and, depending on the site, water molecules. Negatively charged side chains such as those of aspartate and glutamate are commonly important in ion coordination. The positive charge of calcium is compatible with these coordinating groups, but charge attraction is not the whole explanation. The precise spatial arrangement of oxygen atoms and the shape of the binding site matter. A calcium-binding mutation can therefore have a large effect even when it changes only one residue. Replacement of an acidic residue may remove a coordination group, distort a linker or affect neighboring sites. It is not equivalent to removing a single extracellular adhesion bond.

A typical mature classical cadherin presents its extracellular domains outside the cell and its cytoplasmic tail inside. The topology follows from synthesis and processing through the secretory pathway. The transmembrane segment anchors the protein in the bilayer while allowing lateral movement within the membrane. Glycosylation and maturation can influence folding, stability and surface delivery, though the detailed processing machinery is a separate topic in Unit 03. For interpreting an adhesion phenotype the essential point is that production of a cadherin polypeptide and functional presentation of its extracellular region are not the same measurement. A whole-cell immunoblot can detect a protein that is retained in intracellular compartments and therefore cannot participate in a contact.

Classical cadherin tails contain distinguishable regions for binding intracellular partners. A region close to the membrane binds p120-catenin, which contributes to regulation of surface stability and turnover. A more distal region associates with beta-catenin, providing a route through alpha-catenin and other proteins to the actin cytoskeleton. These are organized interactions rather than a covalent extension of the cadherin molecule into an actin filament. The physical connection can change with molecular state and force. The same basic cadherin architecture can consequently support an initial small adhesion site and a more mature mechanical interface.

The structural model should not be generalized to every member of the superfamily. Some cadherin-related proteins have many extracellular repeats, different modes of membrane attachment or intracellular domains unlike those of classical cadherins. The five extracellular domains and the familiar catenin-binding tail are therefore appropriate for standard E-cadherin and N-cadherin questions, not a universal definition of all cadherin-related proteins. Similarly, the fact that desmosomal cadherins share related extracellular domains does not imply that they use the same intracellular adaptors. Exam questions often exploit precisely this difference between family membership and junction-specific function.

5. Calcium-dependent recognition: from ion binding to adhesive competence

To understand the role of calcium in cadherin adhesion, we must distinguish the chemical event at an ion-binding site from the biological event at an interface between cells. Calcium binding takes place on individual cadherin molecules. Adhesion takes place when compatible molecules anchored on opposing cell surfaces interact. The ion-binding sites between extracellular domains influence the structure of each molecule, whereas the recognition interface joins two molecules. Both events are necessary for the characteristic behavior of a classical cadherin under normal extracellular conditions, but they are not the same reaction. In particular, removing calcium does not simply remove a metal bridge stretched between the two cells.

Free extracellular calcium is ordinarily present at concentrations much greater than the resting free calcium concentration in the cytosol of an animal cell. In a typical physiological setting, extracellular ionized calcium is of the order of millimolar, whereas resting cytosolic free calcium is around a tenth of a micromolar. The contrast helps explain why the extracellular cadherin domains can remain calcium occupied while intracellular calcium acts as a tightly regulated signal. The membrane separates these environments. A transient intracellular calcium signal is not automatically equivalent to removal of extracellular calcium, and a molecule binding calcium on the external side should not be assumed to act as a cytosolic calcium sensor.

When calcium concentration falls sufficiently, cadherin linkers become more flexible and extracellular domains may become more susceptible to proteolysis. The physical consequence is not merely that the molecule becomes shorter. Its distal domain can sample orientations that do not efficiently engage a partner on another cell. Calcium depletion may also destabilize the arrangement needed for cooperative lateral association of cadherins. The amount of correctly folded protein on the cell surface might initially remain unchanged even while adhesive competence declines. With continued exposure, other effects such as internalization or degradation can become important. A short treatment and a long treatment therefore need not yield the same molecular phenotype.

A useful way to examine this mechanism is to compare calcium readdition to the addition of a different divalent cation. If a binding site is specifically organized to coordinate calcium, another ion with the same charge need not restore its structural role. Ionic radius, preferred coordination geometry and hydration all influence whether a substitute fits the site. Magnesium, for example, differs from calcium in these properties. A treatment that restores general ionic strength without restoring the particular calcium-dependent conformation may fail to recover cadherin adhesion. Conversely, a response to calcium could also involve other surface proteins, which is why a receptor-specific experiment remains necessary.

Extracellular calcium can be removed experimentally by a chelating agent, a molecule that binds free metal ions through multiple coordinating groups. EGTA is commonly employed in calcium-sensitive biological systems because, under appropriate conditions, it preferentially binds calcium relative to magnesium. EDTA binds several divalent metal ions and therefore can have broader consequences. The precise free calcium remaining after chelation depends on the total amounts of ions and chelator, pH, competing species and binding equilibria. Adding a chelator at a stated total concentration does not tell us the exact free ion concentration without further chemical information. This distinction is especially important when a question supplies a defined calcium-buffered solution rather than simply stating that calcium was removed.

A calcium-switch experiment is a conceptual demonstration of both the reversible chemical requirement and the time needed to rebuild an organized contact. An epithelial cell population maintained under calcium-poor conditions may show poorly organized cadherin contacts. When extracellular calcium is restored, individual cadherin molecules can recover a favorable conformation, cells can reestablish adhesive interactions, and the associated cytoskeletal structures can reorganize. These events occur on different time scales. An early increase in molecular association may precede the development of a mature junction. A later appearance of a junctional belt is evidence of cellular organization, not a direct measurement of the time required for a calcium ion to bind.

The proper controls distinguish the immediate effect of calcium from irreversible injury. A parallel population kept in normal calcium provides a baseline. A population undergoing the same medium change without calcium depletion controls for mechanical disturbance. Measurements of membrane integrity and viability reveal whether poor aggregation is associated with injury. A comparison of total and surface cadherin amounts helps distinguish reduced protein availability from a change in its adhesive state. Recovery upon calcium readdition is particularly informative when it occurs without enough time for substantial synthesis of new protein. It suggests that at least part of the adhesive machinery was already present and that the chemical state of that machinery could be restored.

Calcium occupancy can be introduced quantitatively through a simplified binding equilibrium. Suppose an isolated regulatory site is either free or occupied by a calcium ion, and the concentration of free calcium is held constant by a large external reservoir. Under those assumptions, the occupied fraction is the free calcium concentration divided by the sum of that concentration and the dissociation constant. This expression follows from the definition of the equilibrium constant and conservation of the two forms of the site. Its important lesson is that occupancy does not normally change in direct proportion to calcium concentration over the whole concentration range. At very high free calcium, most sites are occupied and further increases have relatively little effect. Near the dissociation constant, changing the calcium concentration can strongly affect the occupied fraction.

Real cadherins are more complicated than this one-site teaching model. Multiple ions bind between successive extracellular domains, and the structural state of one region can influence neighboring regions. The fraction of sites occupied is not identical to the fraction of cells present in an aggregate. A small change in molecular competence can produce a large change in cellular attachment if the contact depends on many cooperating bonds. If a question describes a steep decrease in aggregation with reduced calcium, it is therefore premature to infer the number of calcium ions bound per cadherin from the shape of the cellular response alone. A molecular binding measurement and a cell aggregation measurement have different explanatory levels.

6. Homophilic recognition, trans binding and lateral organization

Classical cadherins have an extracellular recognition surface near the distal end of the molecule. An E-cadherin displayed on one epithelial cell can associate with an E-cadherin on an opposing cell. The adjective homophilic indicates that the interacting molecules are of the same kind. In the standard model, the interaction joins two different cell membranes across their intervening extracellular space. This orientation is described as trans. The term cis describes an interaction between molecules associated with the same membrane. The distinction is geometrical: both cis and trans interactions can involve proteins belonging to the same molecular class.

For type I classical cadherins, the best-characterized adhesive interface involves exchange of an amino-terminal beta strand between the EC1 domains of two partners. A conserved tryptophan residue near the amino terminus of the mature protein, commonly designated Trp2, participates in the exchange by entering a hydrophobic pocket in its partner. Each partner can contribute a strand to the other. The resulting configuration is often called a strand-swapped dimer. A dimer is simply an association of two molecular units. The exchange does not mean that a peptide is cleaved off one protein and permanently transferred to another. The beta strand remains part of its own polypeptide chain and contributes to a reversible noncovalent interface.

This structure helps explain both selectivity and reversibility. The hydrophobic pocket is shaped by specific residues, and the backbone and side chains of the interacting strands must be compatible with the partner surface. Hydrogen bonding and other noncovalent interactions also contribute to stabilization. A mutation replacing a key tryptophan with an amino acid lacking the same aromatic side chain may substantially change binding behavior. Because the interface involves an organized folded domain, however, a mutation need not abolish all possible molecular association. In particular, classical cadherins can occupy alternative binding configurations; the existence of an altered residual interaction would not invalidate the general importance of the usual strand-swapped adhesive state.

An exam-level account does not require memorization of every structural intermediate. It is enough to recognize that proteins can adopt encounter configurations before forming a more stable adhesive interface and that mutation may influence the probability or lifetime of different states. Consider two extracellular molecules that collide many times as the membranes fluctuate. If a particular orientation is needed for productive binding, only a subset of collisions will produce an effective interaction. A mutation that impairs the approach to that configuration can slow association, whereas a mutation that destabilizes the final state can increase dissociation. Both defects may reduce the final number of stable bonds, but a time-resolved experiment can distinguish them.

The term homophilic is a useful description of the usual recognition preference of many classical cadherins, but it is not a rule that every molecule binds exclusively to an identical molecule under all conditions. Different classical cadherins share substantial structural similarity, and some can engage in heterophilic interactions. An observed preference for contacts between like-expressing cells may arise from differences in affinity, surface density, cytoskeletal organization or several of these simultaneously. In a simplified question that explicitly states that E-cadherin binds only E-cadherin and N-cadherin binds only N-cadherin, the specified rule can be used. In an actual biological experiment, the extent of cross-binding has to be established rather than assumed.

Trans adhesion is amplified when cadherins accumulate within the contact. Once two opposing molecules associate, the membranes are locally held at a separation compatible with additional interactions. Cadherins on the same membrane can form lateral associations and can be retained or organized by the underlying cytoskeleton. The resulting contact is a population of many interacting receptors, not simply a row of completely independent molecules. Lateral organization can favor the formation of extended adhesive arrays. The contribution of cis interactions depends on the cadherin type, the organization of the membrane and the mechanical state of the junction. A trans-binding defect and a cis-organization defect may therefore produce different microscopic appearances despite both weakening the contact.

Imagine two mutants with normal total surface expression. In the first, EC1 recognition is severely impaired. Molecules may diffuse into a zone of membrane apposition without efficiently forming trans bonds. In the second, extracellular trans recognition is largely preserved but lateral organization is weakened. The second mutant may support initial cell association yet fail to concentrate receptors effectively or maintain a broad mechanically competent interface. A brief binding assay could show a greater difference for the first mutant than for the second, while a prolonged resistance-to-separation assay could reveal severe defects in both. The example demonstrates why the measured property and time scale must accompany any claim about adhesion.

The availability of partner molecules is a further constraint on recognition. A cell may display abundant functional E-cadherin, but if its neighbor displays no compatible extracellular partner, it cannot form normal homophilic E-cadherin bonds across that boundary. In a mixture of cells, a population expressing both E-cadherin and N-cadherin may provide compatible surfaces to more than one population. The actual spatial result also depends on which receptor is concentrated at a given interface and whether each partner is mechanically coupled inside its respective cell. It is therefore necessary to analyze both cells in a proposed pair, rather than infer adhesive behavior from the genotype of only one.

A simple purified-protein assay might immobilize an extracellular cadherin domain on one surface and expose it to a compatible soluble domain. Such an assay can establish direct molecular recognition in the absence of a living cell's cytoskeletal machinery. It cannot establish the mechanical strength of a cell–cell junction or its capacity for remodeling. An aggregation assay using living cells provides information about collective behavior but introduces variation from trafficking, movement, cytoskeletal tension and other receptors. The two approaches complement one another: one narrows the mechanism to a molecular interaction, and the other asks whether that interaction is effective in its biological context.

7. The intracellular link: catenins, cortical actin and force transmission

A cadherin bridge between the external surfaces of two cells would have limited mechanical usefulness if its intracellular ends were not organized. A membrane protein can move laterally in the lipid bilayer, and a local force can rearrange the membrane or pull a weakly anchored region out of shape. The actin cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments located inside the cell that helps support membrane shape and generate force. Classical cadherin-based junctions establish a functional connection between this network and the adhesive interface. The connection is not usually accomplished by a cadherin binding directly to an actin filament. It depends on a group of cytoplasmic proteins known as catenins and their associated partners.

Beta-catenin associates with a region of the cytoplasmic tail of a classical cadherin. Alpha-catenin, in turn, associates with the cadherin-bound beta-catenin complex and participates in coupling the junction to actin. This is the origin of the standard textbook sequence cadherin, beta-catenin, alpha-catenin and actin. The sequence is a useful map of functional relationships, but it should not be pictured as an inflexible molecular chain with the same connections at every instant. The actin-associated state of alpha-catenin depends on its conformation, the state of the complex, other recruited proteins and mechanical force. Junctional coupling is an actively regulated system.

Alpha-catenin helps explain how a mechanical influence applied from outside the cell can be translated into local changes inside it. Tension transmitted through a junction can change the conformation and accessibility of binding regions in alpha-catenin. In appropriate conditions this favors recruitment of vinculin, an actin-associated protein that can strengthen the junctional connection to the cytoskeleton. The principle is called mechanosensitivity: the state of a molecular complex depends on the physical forces acting through it. It does not require a specialized mechanosensitive ion channel in every case. Mechanical coupling can itself provide a mechanism for reorganizing a contact.

The protein p120-catenin binds a region of the classical cadherin tail closer to the membrane. One important function is regulation of cadherin turnover at the plasma membrane. If p120 binding is lost, cadherins in many cell contexts become more susceptible to internalization and subsequent removal from the surface. This can reduce the number of available adhesive receptors even when the extracellular binding interface has not been altered. p120 also participates in regulation of signaling and cytoskeletal behavior. Its role cannot be reduced to that of a second copy of beta-catenin. Distinct regions of the same receptor tail recruit different proteins with different primary effects.

Consider how a mutation affecting each of these intracellular interactions could be distinguished. A cadherin that reaches the surface but cannot efficiently bind beta-catenin may exhibit poor organization of the associated cytoskeleton. A defect in alpha-catenin may alter force transmission despite the presence of both extracellular cadherin and beta-catenin at a contact. A defect disrupting p120 association may produce reduced surface cadherin abundance. These phenotypes are not guaranteed to be completely independent, because loss of one interaction can alter the behavior of the rest of the complex. They nevertheless provide a rational starting hypothesis for interpreting molecular localization and cell adhesion measurements together.

A cell contains cortical actin beneath much of its plasma membrane. At an adherens junction, cadherin-associated complexes couple to a locally organized part of this network. Actin filaments can interact with myosin motors, which use ATP hydrolysis to generate tension. The resulting actomyosin contractility can change cell shape, move a contact boundary and redistribute loads. When two cells are connected through cadherins, contraction of one can influence the mechanics of the other. The entire route of force transmission extends from the actomyosin machinery inside one cell through its cadherin-associated complexes and extracellular cadherin bonds to the corresponding structures in its neighbor.

The requirement for ATP in actomyosin function should not be confused with a direct requirement for ATP hydrolysis by extracellular cadherin binding. The adhesive interaction is a noncovalent association whose equilibrium depends on the energies and concentrations of the molecular states. The living cell expends energy in synthesis, transport, maintenance of ion gradients, receptor turnover and active cytoskeletal remodeling. A purified cadherin ectodomain may bind a compatible partner without an ATP supply, whereas a living cell depleted of ATP may be unable to organize or maintain a normal junction. These observations are consistent because they test different levels of the system.

Beta-catenin also participates in the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, where its regulated accumulation and association with nuclear transcriptional partners alter gene expression. This second role is a useful connection to earlier signaling material. Cadherin-associated beta-catenin and signaling-competent beta-catenin should not be treated as interchangeable without considering the cell's regulatory state. Loss of a junction does not automatically mean that all released beta-catenin reaches the nucleus. Cytoplasmic degradation pathways, the availability of binding partners and Wnt pathway activity determine the outcome. An experiment that detects more nuclear beta-catenin after altering adhesion must therefore include evidence that distinguishes release from changes in synthesis, degradation or signaling.

Microscopy can reveal whether cadherin and catenin signals overlap at a cell boundary. Such overlap is evidence of colocalization within the resolution of the method. It cannot establish that two proteins bind directly. A biochemical association experiment can reveal that proteins are present in a common recovered complex, but proteins may be connected indirectly through other components. Testing interaction between purified domains or using well-characterized mutations provides additional evidence for a specific direct binding event. Mechanical assays then address whether the molecular complex performs the predicted load-bearing function. The combination of location, biochemical association and functional perturbation is stronger than any single observation.

The relationship between actin organization and cadherin adhesion is bidirectional. Adhesive interactions create a site at which the cortex can become organized; cortical remodeling can bring more receptor molecules into contact or stabilize the contact region. A defect in actin can therefore alter adhesion without changing the extracellular specificity of the cadherin. Equally, loss of a cadherin can disturb local cortical organization even when actin polymerization elsewhere in the cell remains possible. This reciprocal relationship accounts for the coordinated changes observed when epithelial contacts are assembled, remodeled or broken.

8. Adherens junctions as organized cell–cell mechanical interfaces

An adherens junction is a specialized cell–cell contact in which classical cadherins and their associated cytoplasmic proteins connect adjacent cells to actin-based structures. It is not defined simply by a high concentration of protein at the cell surface. Its organization allows mechanical interactions to be transmitted over the contact. In a polarized epithelial sheet, adherens junctions often form a circumferential belt near the apical part of each cell, below the tight junction. The term zonula adherens describes this belt-like organization. Adherens junctions can also occur as more localized structures, particularly in cells that do not have a typical epithelial architecture.

The position of the junction contributes to tissue organization. An epithelial cell has an apical region facing a lumen or external environment and a basal region attached to underlying tissue. The lateral surface faces neighboring cells. By connecting the lateral surfaces near the apical boundary, the adherens belt helps coordinate changes in the shape of the cells within the sheet. When the associated actin–myosin network contracts, the apical regions may change their perimeter, influencing tissue geometry. The resulting force is transmitted through connections to neighboring cells. The full developmental pathways that regulate tissue bending belong to developmental biology; here the central principle is that a specialized adhesive junction mechanically couples individual cells.

The assembly of a junction begins when cells encounter one another. Cell movement and membrane protrusions can create points of close apposition, allowing compatible extracellular domains to interact. Cadherins and associated proteins become locally organized as the interface grows. Actin remodeling supports expansion and stabilization of the contact. These events are mutually dependent rather than rigidly separated into completely independent stages. A weak contact may be sufficient to begin cortical reorganization, which in turn improves the conditions for additional receptor binding. The mature junction is therefore an emergent structure built through interactions between adhesion, movement and local mechanical organization.

An adherens junction is distinct from a tight junction. Tight junction proteins, including claudins, regulate passage through the paracellular space and contribute to separation of membrane domains in polarized epithelia. The main defining function of an adherens junction is mechanical attachment and associated regulation of the actin cortex. Both types can influence the formation and stability of the other, so a cadherin defect can secondarily impair epithelial barrier properties. This does not mean that E-cadherin forms the primary selective paracellular seal. A permeability phenotype is not sufficient by itself to identify which junctional protein was affected.

A gap junction has another distinct function. It provides channels connecting the cytoplasms of neighboring cells, permitting selected ions and small molecules to pass. An adherens junction can hold cells together without allowing such direct cytoplasmic exchange. The existence of stable contact may help provide an appropriate spatial setting for gap-junction organization, but the cadherin ectodomain is not a channel spanning the two membranes. A question that describes electrical coupling or transfer of a small fluorescent tracer through channels is primarily testing a different form of intercellular communication.

Adherens junctions also differ from matrix-associated focal adhesions. Both connect to actin, but their extracellular partners and geometry are different. The adherens junction joins the membrane of one cell to the membrane of its neighbor through cadherin-based interactions. A focal adhesion connects a cell to extracellular matrix through integrins and their associated proteins. A cell migrating across a matrix while retaining contact with its neighbors can use both structures simultaneously. The direction and distribution of forces depend on the balance between substrate traction, cortical contractility and cell–cell adhesion. A change in migration speed may therefore originate in either system or in their coordination.

In a mature epithelial sheet, the adhesive interface has to accommodate occasional changes in neighbors and cell shape. Existing receptors can be removed and new molecules can arrive while other parts of the interface remain connected. When a cell divides, its immediate contacts must reorganize to accommodate the two daughter cells. When a cell is extruded from a sheet, surrounding cells can rearrange their junctions to preserve continuity. This capacity for remodeling is a direct consequence of reversible interactions and active cellular regulation. The persistence of a junction should not be confused with the permanent residence of its individual components.

The force transmitted through an adherens junction is not uniformly beneficial at every magnitude. Within a suitable range, tension can promote recruitment and organization of load-bearing molecules. If the applied forces exceed the capacity of the interface or act in a geometry that concentrates stress, junctional damage can occur. A defect in cytoskeletal coupling may be difficult to detect when cells are undisturbed but become obvious when the sheet is stretched. Testing adhesion only under static conditions can therefore miss a biologically important failure of mechanical resilience.

9. Desmosomal cadherins and the mechanical resilience of tissues

A tissue that experiences repeated stretching must do more than maintain an ordinary adhesive interface. It must resist disruption when force is transmitted over large regions of the tissue. Desmosomes are specialized cell–cell junctions particularly suited to this requirement. Like adherens junctions, they employ members of the cadherin superfamily to connect adjacent cells. The intracellular machinery to which those molecules attach is different. Instead of primarily coupling to actin, a desmosome connects to intermediate filaments. The distinction explains why two cadherin-containing junctions may have complementary rather than redundant functions.

Intermediate filaments are cytoskeletal polymers whose organization provides mechanical resilience. Their subunit composition varies with cell type. Keratins are important intermediate filament proteins in epithelial cells, while desmin is prominent in muscle cells. A desmosome in the epidermis links the intermediate filament network of one cell to that of a neighbor through a specialized adhesive complex. It does not create a single intermediate filament running uninterrupted through both plasma membranes. The extracellular part of the force-bearing route is formed by adhesion proteins. Intracellular filament networks remain distinct, with adaptor complexes connecting them to the membrane.

Two principal families of desmosomal cadherins are desmogleins and desmocollins. They are integral membrane proteins with extracellular cadherin domains and intracellular regions capable of participating in the assembly of a junctional plaque. A plaque is the organized concentration of proteins at the cytoplasmic face of a junction. Desmosomal cadherins participate in specific adhesive interactions across neighboring membranes; different isoforms and combinations of desmogleins and desmocollins can contribute to this interaction. It is not appropriate to model every desmosomal bond as an exclusive homophilic interaction between identical molecules, because heterophilic interactions can be important. The characteristic homophilic model of E-cadherin should thus be taught as a classical cadherin example, not imposed uncritically on all cadherin-containing contacts.

On the intracellular side, plakoglobin and plakophilins help organize the desmosomal plaque, and desmoplakin connects this complex to intermediate filaments. Plakoglobin is structurally related to beta-catenin and is sometimes called gamma-catenin. The similar names reflect molecular relationships, but the conventional functional map of a desmosome is distinct from that of an adherens junction. Desmoplakin is especially important when interpreting perturbations because it lies toward the filament-attachment end of the mechanical pathway. A cell can retain desmosomal proteins at its membrane yet fail to distribute force effectively through the intermediate filament network if the attachment between plaque and filament is compromised.

Consider a pair of epithelial cells with functional desmoglein and desmocollin ectodomains but defective desmoplakin. Initial molecular recognition between the surfaces may remain possible. Nevertheless, the resulting adhesive structures may be mechanically weak because tension cannot be transferred normally to the intermediate filament networks. In contrast, a defect in an extracellular desmosomal recognition domain can impair the connection between the surfaces even if desmoplakin and intermediate filaments are normally expressed. These two mutations interfere with different parts of a continuous mechanical path. The distinction is analogous to separating the ligand-binding and cytoskeletal-coupling functions of a classical cadherin, but the relevant proteins and filaments are different.

The familiar description of a desmosome as a spot-like junction is useful because it contrasts with the circumferential adherens belt of many epithelial cells. Individual desmosomes distribute attachment through localized regions of the membrane, while the intracellular filament network spreads mechanical load over wider regions. Their mechanical effectiveness is thus not restricted to the area immediately beneath a single junction. When an epithelium is stretched, multiple sites cooperate to transmit force. A defect can produce a failure at a particular contact or a more extensive loss of tissue integrity depending on the distribution of the affected protein and the load placed on the tissue.

The epidermis offers a clear physiological context. Its cells need to remain attached despite movement and external mechanical stress. Desmosomal interactions and their linkage to keratin networks contribute to the resistance of the tissue to separation. The heart provides another useful example because cardiomyocytes repeatedly generate force. Intercellular regions of cardiac muscle contain specialized adhesion structures that maintain mechanical continuity, including desmosomal components. Gap junctions in these regions perform a separate electrical coupling function. A question about mechanical disruption of heart muscle contacts therefore need not imply a primary defect in ion-channel-mediated communication.

There is a subtle qualification to the calcium requirement of desmosomal adhesion. Desmosomal cadherins have calcium-dependent extracellular structural properties, and normal junction assembly requires suitable conditions. However, mature desmosomes in certain epithelia can enter a strongly adhesive state that is relatively resistant to acute extracellular calcium depletion. This property is often described as hyperadhesion. It does not mean that calcium-binding domains have disappeared or that classical cadherins behave identically. It illustrates that the calcium sensitivity of an organized junction may depend on its maturation state. A test comparing newly formed contacts and mature tissue may therefore obtain different results after the same short calcium depletion.

Loss of adhesion at a desmosome is biologically distinct from failure of the tight-junction seal. A mechanically fragile epithelial layer can initially retain aspects of barrier organization, and a leaky layer can have intact desmosomes. Over time, failure in one system can indirectly disrupt the other because tissue integrity is a coordinated property. The most informative experimental design distinguishes direct structural failure from secondary consequences. Mechanical challenge, localization of the relevant junctional proteins and observation of the plane of cell separation can help identify the primary defect. A single measure of tissue damage should not be interpreted as proof of failure in every junction type.

Adherens junctions and desmosomes therefore solve complementary mechanical problems. Actin-associated contacts provide an effective means of coordinating contractile forces and rearranging cell shape. Intermediate filament-associated contacts help distribute stress and maintain durable cohesion. Neither system should be described as completely static, and neither is always mechanically stronger in every possible experiment. Their roles depend on cell type, organization and the nature of the imposed load. The correct comparison identifies the extracellular cadherins, intracellular connectors, associated filament class and characteristic function rather than assigning an absolute ranking to the junctions.

10. E-cadherin, N-cadherin and selective tissue recognition

The names of classical cadherins often reflect the tissues or experimental contexts in which they were originally characterized. E-cadherin is strongly associated with epithelial cell adhesion, while N-cadherin is familiar from neural and other tissues, including cardiac muscle. In humans, their genes are CDH1 and CDH2 respectively. These associations are helpful for organizing knowledge, but the names do not define exclusive expression domains. N-cadherin is not found only in neurons, and E-cadherin does not determine an entire epithelial identity on its own. A cell may express more than one cadherin, and expression can change as its developmental or physiological state changes.

Both E-cadherin and N-cadherin have the basic type I classical cadherin architecture. Their extracellular regions mediate calcium-supported recognition, their transmembrane regions anchor them in the cell surface, and their cytoplasmic tails recruit classical cadherin-associated proteins. Consequently, a comparison between these two molecules should not assign actin coupling to one and intermediate filament coupling to the other. Both generally participate in actin-associated adherens junctions. It is the desmosomal cadherin class, not N-cadherin, that characteristically uses the desmosomal plaque and intermediate filament route.

A tissue's cadherin expression pattern affects which interfaces can form efficiently. If a population displays E-cadherin and another population displays N-cadherin, there may be a preference for contacts within each population. This preference can contribute to the formation of boundaries. But the exact magnitude of the preference cannot be predicted solely from the names of the proteins. Different classical cadherins can exhibit cross-interactions, and the balance of forces at the interface can differ among cell types. The expression level of each receptor, its availability at the surface, its localization at contacts and its association with the cytoskeleton all influence the observed behavior.

Expression of a cadherin and the strength of cadherin-mediated adhesion should be treated as related but distinct properties. Imagine two populations that contain the same amount of E-cadherin in a whole-cell extract. In population A most of the protein is localized at the plasma membrane and enriched at cell contacts. In population B a substantial fraction is retained intracellularly, and the remaining surface pool is not organized into mature junctions. A whole-cell immunoblot could show similar bands, although population A has more effectively organized cell–cell adhesion. Surface labeling and junctional microscopy provide additional information necessary to understand the difference.

There is also a distinction between the average amount of a protein and its local density. A cadherin that is distributed uniformly over the plasma membrane may have a relatively small density at any particular contact. When molecules accumulate at an interface, the local density rises even if the total number of surface molecules remains constant. This change can increase the opportunity for trans binding, lateral association and mechanical reinforcement. The behavior of the contact therefore depends on the spatial distribution of the adhesive receptors. A measurement of total protein cannot reveal this redistribution without a complementary spatial assay.

A change in cadherin expression can be associated with a change in cell behavior. For example, during certain developmental transitions a cell population may reduce its epithelial adhesive characteristics and acquire a different pattern of contacts. In some contexts reduced E-cadherin expression and increased N-cadherin expression accompany changes in cell motility. This is often described as a cadherin switch. The switch is a useful conceptual connection between adhesion and cell state, not a universal diagnostic rule. Some cells migrate while retaining E-cadherin-based contacts, some changes in motility occur without a simple E-to-N replacement, and N-cadherin itself can sustain substantial adhesion.

The mechanical meaning of a cadherin switch depends on the surrounding cells. A migrating cell that loses compatibility with its old neighbors may establish new contacts with cells displaying another compatible receptor. The new adhesive interactions may help guide movement rather than simply weaken adhesion everywhere. If a cell expresses both cadherins temporarily, its contact repertoire can be broader than that of a cell expressing only one. In a simple experimental design, one can compare the behavior of otherwise matched cells expressing individual receptors and then introduce both receptors to test how the interaction pattern changes. It is necessary to monitor expression levels, because altered receptor abundance can otherwise be mistaken for altered specificity.

Other classical cadherins reinforce the general idea that adhesive recognition can be regulated according to tissue context. VE-cadherin, for example, is a major adhesive component of vascular endothelial junctions. It supports the organization of the cell layer lining blood vessels. Its presence should not be confused with E-cadherin merely because both occur in cohesive cell sheets. It also illustrates why a receptor's physiological role has to be understood in terms of the cell population in which it is expressed and the mechanical and signaling demands of that population. Exhaustive memorization of all cadherin isoforms is not necessary for the basic examination distinction.

When interpreting an unfamiliar diagram, begin with molecular topology. A protein with extracellular cadherin repeats, a membrane-spanning segment and a classical catenin-binding tail is compatible with a classical cadherin. If the same diagram shows an actin-associated junction, either E-cadherin or N-cadherin may be plausible, depending on the tissue context. The extracellular domain alone does not always reveal the specific isoform. A question must provide the expression pattern, an antibody specificity, a sequence feature or another distinguishing observation. Conversely, the designation E-cadherin provides enough information to rule out its being an integrin heterodimer or the primary channel of a gap junction.

11. Cell sorting, tissue boundaries and the balance of interfacial forces

Cell sorting is the process by which initially mixed populations become spatially organized according to differences in their cellular properties. In a simple aggregation experiment, cells of two distinguishable populations can be mixed and allowed to establish contacts. If the cells remain randomly distributed, there is no detectable sorting under those conditions. If cells of each type preferentially associate with their own population, distinct regions may develop. The observation is especially informative when the starting mixture is demonstrably random, the cells remain viable and their movement can be followed over time.

One way to explain sorting is through selective recognition. Suppose a hypothetical cell type A expresses only a cadherin that binds the same cadherin, while cell type B expresses another receptor that binds only its own type. If both populations are motile enough to rearrange and no other adhesive mechanism dominates, contacts between members of the same population are favored. A mixed arrangement containing many A–B interfaces can be replaced by an arrangement containing more A–A and B–B contacts. This prediction follows from the specified molecular compatibility, but it does not determine whether the final aggregate forms two adjacent domains or one population envelops the other. The shape depends on additional interfacial properties.

Differential adhesion can also arise without changing receptor identity. Suppose both populations express the same functional cadherin, but one displays a greater surface density. A contact between two high-density cells can potentially support more adhesive bonds than a contact involving a low-density cell, provided other conditions are similar. Differences in the energetic or mechanical properties of the interfaces can then influence spatial organization. This distinction separates qualitative differences in the kind of receptor expressed from quantitative differences in the abundance or effective activity of a receptor.

Malcolm Steinberg's differential adhesion hypothesis provided a conceptual framework for interpreting the liquid-like behavior of rearranging cell aggregates. A population of cells can change its collective shape and spatial arrangement as individual contacts are exchanged. Under suitable conditions the aggregate exhibits an effective tissue surface tension, and arrangements that reduce unfavorable interfaces can be favored. The analogy to the behavior of liquids is valuable because it connects microscopic cell interactions to macroscopic organization. The analogy is not exact: living cells consume energy, generate forces, respond to their environment and may not have enough mobility to approach a mechanically relaxed state.

In a controlled study published by Steinberg and Takeichi in 1994, differences in the expression level of the same P-cadherin were used to examine cell sorting and aggregate organization. The use of a shared receptor type made it possible to study the importance of quantitative differences in adhesive expression rather than attributing the result solely to distinct cadherin identities. Later measurements by Foty and Steinberg used engineered cadherin-expressing aggregates to relate cadherin levels to measured aggregate surface tension in their experimental system. These studies support the idea that receptor abundance can contribute to collective organization. Their results should not be turned into a universal numerical law for arbitrary tissues with different cell shapes, active forces and receptor systems.

A useful idealized model treats a tissue aggregate as having an effective energy cost for each kind of interface. If a unit area of A–A contact has an effective energy cost denoted gamma AA, a unit area of B–B contact has cost gamma BB, and an A–B interface has cost gamma AB, then the relative favorability of different arrangements can be compared. Imagine exchanging two equal-area heterotypic contacts for one A–A contact and one B–B contact. The energy change is proportional to gamma AA plus gamma BB minus twice gamma AB, under the assumptions that other interface areas and contributions remain fixed. When this quantity is negative, the exchange is favorable in this simplified description. The model shows why the heterotypic interface must be evaluated relative to the average properties of both homotypic interfaces.

This effective interface model does not directly measure the free energy of a single cadherin bond. It compresses many physical contributions into a tissue-scale parameter. Among those contributions are molecular adhesion, cortical tension and the energetic or mechanical consequences of deforming cells. It also ignores the detailed kinetics of rearrangement. A system can have an energetically favored arrangement yet fail to reach it because cells cannot readily exchange neighbors. Cell sorting is therefore not guaranteed simply because a static energy comparison favors a segregated arrangement. It requires an accessible path by which the configuration can change.

The tendency of a more cohesive population to occupy an interior region is sometimes presented as a simple rule. In classic liquid-like aggregate models, a population with higher effective tissue surface tension can be enveloped by a lower-surface-tension population when their mutual adhesion and interfacial tensions permit wetting. But knowing only which population has greater homotypic adhesion is insufficient to specify the final shape. A population may adhere poorly to the other population, in which case complete envelopment may not occur. A substrate can also alter the configuration by providing additional adhesive interactions. Thus an examination question that asks about interior versus exterior location must state or imply the assumptions of its sorting model.

Modern cellular interpretations emphasize that cortical contractility and interfacial tension are active determinants of sorting. Myosin-generated tension influences the shape of each cell and the mechanical state of its contacts. Cadherins can modulate these properties by organizing associated cortical structures. As a result, stronger cadherin expression may alter collective organization both through extracellular adhesive interactions and through changes in cytoskeletal tension. A purely passive adhesion model may explain some observations but fail in systems where active mechanical regulation is a major contributor. The more general lesson is that effective tissue behavior reflects the whole adhesive and mechanical system.

To distinguish these contributions, imagine an experimental comparison in which two populations have matched surface cadherin levels but differ in cortical contractility. If they sort into separate domains, the result challenges a model that assigns all sorting to cadherin abundance alone. An intervention that equalizes contractility while preserving cadherin levels could test whether the mechanical difference contributes causally. However, contractility-modifying interventions may also change cell shape, motility or receptor organization, so these variables require examination. A careful inference identifies the controlled conditions and does not assume that a single manipulation has only one cellular consequence.

Time-lapse observations are especially useful because an endpoint image can have several explanations. Apparent segregation might result from selective adhesion and rearrangement, but it could also result from differential proliferation, death or migration away from the field of view. If cells can be tracked from an initially random distribution and are seen exchanging neighbors without substantial changes in cell number, the case for active sorting becomes stronger. Fluorescent markers must also be chosen so that they do not themselves alter the adhesive properties being measured. Reciprocal labeling of the two populations is a straightforward way of testing whether the marker has influenced the outcome.

In real tissues, boundaries can also be stabilized by differences in gene expression, local extracellular matrix, physical barriers and guidance signals. Cadherin-based recognition is one important component of the explanation. Its relevance is especially clear when a controlled change in cadherin type or quantity changes the organization of an otherwise matched cell population. Developmental biology will consider how such properties are regulated in an organism. Here the important achievement is to understand how molecular compatibility and mechanically active cell interfaces can translate into spatial organization without treating cadherins as a complete explanation of all pattern formation.

12. Regulation of stable contacts during movement and tissue maintenance

A stable cell contact must be capable of adapting to its environment. If adhesive receptors remained permanently in the same position at a junction, the surface would become unable to accommodate a growing cell, a dividing neighbor or a change in tissue shape. Living cells instead maintain junctions as dynamic assemblies. The population of receptors and associated cytoplasmic proteins may remain concentrated at a boundary even as individual molecules arrive, leave and exchange partners. Junctional stability is therefore a property of the system over time, not the immobility of all its constituents.

Cells regulate cadherin availability through production, surface delivery, retention, internalization and recycling or degradation. The balance between these processes determines the steady-state number of molecules available for adhesive interactions. If synthesis increases but the rate of internalization also increases, the surface population need not increase. Similarly, if internalization slows while delivery remains constant, the surface pool can grow without a corresponding increase in gene transcription. An experimental result showing increased cadherin mRNA is thus insufficient to establish stronger cell–cell adhesion. Protein abundance, localization and functional measurements are needed to connect changes in expression with the behavior of the junction.

p120-catenin contributes to the control of surface retention in many classical cadherin systems. Its association with the membrane-proximal tail can limit access to mechanisms that promote internalization. If this association is disrupted, cadherins may be removed from the surface more readily. The resulting defect differs fundamentally from a mutation that changes the binding energy of the external recognition interface. In the first case, fewer molecules may be available to form otherwise normal bonds; in the second case, abundant molecules may be unable to form the expected bonds. A measurement of surface expression can help separate these hypotheses.

Actomyosin remodeling also regulates the organization of the contact. Small GTPases and their associated pathways can influence actin polymerization, contractile tension and the direction of movement. These processes were introduced in general signaling and cytoskeleton contexts elsewhere in the curriculum. Their relevance here is that an extracellular adhesive interaction can be reinforced, reshaped or disrupted through changes occurring inside the cell. It is not necessary for every regulatory pathway to directly modify the cadherin molecule. A change in the cortex beneath the contact may be sufficient to alter the geometry and mechanical properties of the entire interface.

A positive reinforcing relationship can occur when an initial adhesive contact organizes actin, and the organized cortex in turn promotes recruitment or retention of additional adhesive molecules. This is a useful example of feedback in cellular organization. It should not be interpreted as an unlimited increase in adhesion. The number of available receptors is finite; the balance of delivery and removal constrains their distribution; mechanical forces may become disruptive rather than reinforcing. Regulatory systems help a cell maintain an appropriate degree of cohesion rather than simply maximize adhesion in every circumstance.

An epithelial wound provides a conceptual example of regulated contact remodeling. Cells close to the free edge encounter a different environment from cells in the interior of the sheet. They may change shape and generate movement toward the open space while retaining connections with other cells. In collective migration, adhesion between neighboring cells allows mechanical influences to be transmitted across the group. It can also coordinate movement so that a multicellular layer advances without completely dissociating into individual cells. This example corrects the idea that any movement requires total loss of cadherin-mediated adhesion. The nature of movement matters: individual migration and collective migration can involve different arrangements of cell–cell contacts.

Very strong retention of particular contacts can inhibit rearrangement when a cell must exchange neighbors, whereas inadequate adhesion can prevent coordinated force transmission or cause cells to detach. Therefore, the relationship between adhesion strength and tissue movement is not necessarily monotonic. At one extreme, contacts are too weak to sustain the organized group; at another, contacts may resist the rearrangements needed for movement. The optimal behavior depends on the geometry, timing and forces involved. If an exam supplies a graph in which movement is greatest at intermediate adhesion levels, the graph is compatible with a trade-off between mechanical coordination and the ability to rearrange.

Contact-dependent regulation of cell proliferation provides a further illustration of the difference between structural and signaling roles. In some cultured cell types, increasing cell density is associated with reduced proliferation or changes in growth signaling. Cadherin-mediated contacts can participate in these responses by organizing local signaling and mechanical conditions. However, density also changes nutrient access, soluble signal concentration, cell shape and substrate interactions. A dense culture that proliferates slowly does not by itself prove that E-cadherin is the direct cause. A receptor-specific perturbation, rescue and careful control of cell density are needed to isolate its contribution.

Changes in external calcium, receptor-blocking molecules, mechanical stress and cell-state transitions can all disturb adhesion. Their effects may appear at different time scales. Acute calcium depletion can alter ectodomain competence before gene expression changes; a transcriptional alteration may require time for existing proteins to turn over; a strong mechanical load may immediately damage an interface. A time course thus helps distinguish mechanisms that produce similar final appearances. The principle extends to other signaling systems: the temporal order of changes provides evidence about causality, provided that observations are made at appropriate intervals and with suitable controls.
