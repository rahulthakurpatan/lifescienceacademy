1. The biological problem of reversible cell contact

A cell in a tissue and a cell carried by flowing blood encounter very different physical situations. An epithelial cell must remain joined to its neighbours for long periods while the sheet bends, stretches and renews itself. A white blood cell, or leukocyte, often needs to remain free in the circulation until it encounters a particular vascular surface. It must then attach without immediately forming an irreversible union, evaluate the local environment and, under appropriate conditions, stop. Adhesion molecules therefore cannot be understood simply as molecular glue. They form regulated interfaces whose physical properties must match the behaviour of the cells expressing them.

The inner surface of a blood vessel is formed by endothelial cells. Their exposed membrane faces flowing blood and their opposite surface faces the underlying tissue. Imagine a circulating leukocyte encountering this surface. The two cell membranes cannot ordinarily fuse or make uniform direct contact because membrane lipids, surface proteins and the hydrated carbohydrate coat keep them apart. A useful adhesive interaction requires extracellular domains extending from one surface to encounter compatible structures on the other. These structures must bind before the flowing cell is swept away. Once they bind, mechanical load will be transmitted through the contact.

A stable attachment is not necessarily the best solution to this initial problem. A molecule that forms a very durable bond but associates slowly may fail to capture a passing cell. A molecule that associates during a brief encounter but dissociates rapidly can arrest the forward motion momentarily and provide time for another association. If these short-lived contacts occur successively, a cell can travel along the surface while remaining close enough to receive further information. Such behaviour is termed rolling adhesion. Rolling is not free swimming and it is not the same as stationary adhesion.

The family of adhesion molecules particularly adapted to such interactions is the selectin family. The name reflects their carbohydrate-binding activity and their role in selective recognition. Selectins usually recognize particular carbohydrate-containing structures attached to membrane molecules on another cell. They are capable of forming reversible bonds in mechanically dynamic situations. A second group of molecules, the immunoglobulin-superfamily adhesion molecules, includes important endothelial partners for leukocyte integrins. These proteins permit cells to establish stronger or more sustained associations and also participate in many other forms of cell recognition.

The preceding chapter described cadherins. In an ordinary adherens junction, cadherins expressed on opposing epithelial cells recognize compatible cadherins and contribute to tissue cohesion. The dynamic contact considered here has a different design requirement. Selectins are not cadherins that happen to be expressed in blood vessels. Their extracellular binding domain recognizes a different kind of chemical structure, and their role depends importantly on bond kinetics and presentation at the surface. The difference between these families provides an example of how molecular architecture influences cellular behaviour.

A ligand is a molecular entity recognized by a binding site. In a selectin interaction the ligand is often a glycan, a short or long chain of sugars carried by a glycoprotein or glycolipid. A counter-receptor is a surface molecule on the opposing cell that participates in the binding interaction. These terms describe opposite sides of a contact and do not necessarily indicate which cell receives a signal. Both cells can change their behaviour as a result of the same physical interaction. An adhesion receptor can also be a signaling participant; the two functions are not mutually exclusive.

The word adhesion refers to a physical association between surfaces. Recognition refers to the discrimination between compatible and incompatible molecular features. Signaling means a process by which information changes cellular activity. These processes frequently occur together, but an experiment may isolate one from another. A particle coated with a ligand can bind to purified selectin without any intracellular metabolism. A living leukocyte interacting through the same ligand can also respond biochemically. Observing binding in the first experiment is evidence about molecular recognition, not evidence that the particle has signaled.

In circulation the initial contact is especially sensitive to distance, orientation and time. A large number of molecules in the wrong membrane region may be less useful than a smaller number extending towards the other surface. A leukocyte's surface projections, known as microvilli, can present certain adhesion proteins beyond its bulk glycocalyx. The cell is deformable, so its shape and membrane extensions can change when a bond is loaded. These physical variables explain why measuring an isolated receptor's affinity in solution cannot completely predict whole-cell adhesion.

An important conceptual distinction is between an interaction that is selective and one that is exclusive. Different selectins can recognize overlapping sets of glycans, and an individual leukocyte may possess several ligand carriers. A cell therefore does not necessarily lose all adhesive behaviour when one receptor is removed. Redundant interactions may provide partial compensation, and the importance of a given route varies with the tissue, cell type and inflammatory state. Predicting the phenotype of a mutation requires identifying the actual molecular partners that were present before the mutation.

The central theme of this chapter is the division of adhesive work. A reversible molecular tether allows a cell to encounter an endothelial surface. Additional interactions increase the probability that the encounter develops into stable attachment. Local signaling helps regulate the transition. This is a functional sequence rather than a universal rigid schedule: individual bonds can operate simultaneously, and not every cell uses every molecular class. The most useful examination strategy is to locate the impaired step and identify which remaining steps are intrinsically possible.

2. How the selectin family and rolling behaviour were discovered

The history of adhesion research illustrates the distinction between a cellular phenomenon and the molecular explanation for it. Observing that leukocytes associate with vascular walls establishes that some physical interaction occurs. It does not identify the surface protein responsible. Similarly, observing increased attachment to activated endothelial cells suggests that the endothelium has changed, but it does not establish whether the cause is new protein production, mobilization of previously stored material, altered cell geometry or a change in the leukocytes themselves. Identifying the responsible molecule required ways of interfering with particular surface components.

Monoclonal antibodies were important in this development because an antibody raised against a cell-surface antigen can sometimes interfere with its biological function. If an antibody binds an activated endothelial surface and reduces leukocyte adhesion, it provides a means of identifying a candidate adhesion molecule. The interpretation requires controls. An irrelevant antibody of the same class should not produce the same specific reduction. The endothelial monolayer must remain intact, and the test must distinguish interference with recognition from nonspecific damage to either cell. An antibody that binds but does not block function is also informative about accessible epitopes.

In 1987, Michael Bevilacqua and colleagues reported an inducible endothelial leukocyte adhesion molecule, subsequently called ELAM-1 and now E-selectin. Their antibody-based observations linked an endothelial surface component appearing after activation to leukocyte attachment. The significance was not that all adhesion was explained by one molecule. Rather, they could distinguish an induced molecular contribution from the background adhesiveness of unstimulated endothelial cells. This discovery made it possible to ask whether the induced protein was chemically related to other known receptors.

The 1989 molecular characterization by Bevilacqua and colleagues identified a domain arrangement in ELAM-1 related to carbohydrate-recognition proteins and complement-regulatory protein domains. Other researchers had been investigating lymphocyte homing receptors and a protein stored in platelet and endothelial secretory granules, then called GMP-140. Comparison of the proteins revealed a shared architecture. The older names arose from distinct experimental systems; the modern family names reflect the related proteins rather than three unrelated adhesion mechanisms. ELAM-1 is E-selectin, GMP-140 or PADGEM is P-selectin, and the lymphocyte homing receptor LECAM-1 is L-selectin.

Lymphocyte homing research established that cells circulating through the blood do not enter every tissue indiscriminately. Particular endothelial regions, including high endothelial venules in lymphoid organs, present surface molecules that favour the attachment of appropriate lymphocytes. Blocking the homing receptor recognized by the MEL-14 antibody in mice provided an experimental route for connecting specific molecules with this selectivity. The broader lesson is that a vascular surface is not a passive wall: it can display a chemically encoded set of attachment opportunities.

The existence of related proteins raised a second question. Were they simply additional members of a large set of cell adhesion molecules, or did they support a distinctive adhesive behaviour? Conventional static cell adhesion assays could show that cells remained attached after incubation, but such assays did not reproduce the fleeting collisions and mechanical loading experienced in the circulation. A cell that can attach when placed on a surface for several minutes may be unable to attach while passing that surface in less than a second. The two assays answer different questions.

Lawrence and Springer addressed this distinction in a landmark 1991 study using artificial lipid surfaces with purified adhesion molecules and leukocytes under controlled flow. They compared adhesion involving the molecule then designated CD62, now identified as P-selectin, with integrin-dependent interactions involving ICAM-1. The experimental design separated molecular components of the endothelial surface instead of allowing the entire complex endothelium to contribute uncontrolled variables. Rolling could be supported by the selectin-containing surface, whereas the conditions permitting strong integrin-dependent attachment differed. In particular, forming an association under flow was not equivalent to surviving shear after the association had already formed.

This work supplied a mechanistic explanation for a sequence of leukocyte recruitment events. The initial tether increases the time available to encounter the wall; subsequent activation-dependent adhesive interactions can support much stronger retention. The experiment was a demonstration under specified conditions, not proof that no integrin can ever mediate initial capture. Later observations show that some alpha-four integrin interactions can support rolling and that different endothelial beds use different combinations of adhesion molecules. The historical model is valuable precisely because it identifies experimentally separable functions that can be compared in new situations.

Determining the chemical identity of selectin ligands required another kind of experiment. Researchers compared binding to cells with different glycosylation states, used enzymes that remove particular carbohydrate groups and assessed whether defined glycan structures supported recognition. These approaches revealed the importance of sialylated and fucosylated structures such as sialyl Lewis X. A reduction in adhesion after removing a sugar residue suggests that the residue contributes to recognition, although it may also affect the presentation or stability of the larger carrier. Testing purified components and restoring the missing modification helps distinguish these possibilities.

Structural work provided a complementary line of evidence. In 2000, Somers and colleagues reported structures of selectin extracellular domain fragments interacting with sialyl Lewis X and with a modified peptide from P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1, PSGL-1. Atomic models showed that the binding interface is a defined molecular surface and that the carbohydrate and, for the physiological P-selectin interaction, additional peptide modifications contribute to the association. Such structural evidence is much more specific than a general claim that carbohydrates are sticky. It also explains why the correct sugar presented on an unsuitable carrier may not reproduce the full interaction.

A further discovery concerned the mechanical behaviour of these bonds. Most familiar chemical intuition suggests that applying force should shorten the lifetime of a noncovalent bond. In 2003, Marshall and colleagues combined force measurements and flow experiments to show that the lifetime of a P-selectin interaction with PSGL-1 first increased over one force range and then decreased at higher forces. This provided direct evidence for a catch-to-slip transition. It did not mean that every selectin contact is strengthened by all forces. The result defined conditions under which a simple always-weakening model is insufficient.

These studies form an instructive progression from cell observation, to inhibitory antibodies, to sequence comparison, to reconstituted flow systems, to molecular structural and mechanical evidence. No single method answers every question. Antibody inhibition identifies a functional contribution; a domain sequence suggests a biochemical family; a purified surface tests sufficiency in a defined context; an atomic structure locates possible contacts; a force experiment reveals how bond lifetime changes under load. A student should be able to match each kind of result with the inference it supports and recognize the conclusions that remain outside its scope.

3. Selectin architecture: how the recognition domain is presented

E-selectin, P-selectin and L-selectin are single-pass membrane glycoproteins. Their extracellular amino-terminal regions extend away from the membrane, followed by a membrane-spanning segment and a relatively short cytoplasmic region. A membrane protein of this orientation can present a ligand-binding site into the extracellular environment while remaining mechanically connected to the cell. A soluble fragment containing the recognition domain may retain its ability to bind, but it lacks the normal membrane anchor needed to transmit tension between two cells.

The amino-terminal carbohydrate-recognition region belongs to the C-type lectin family. In the case of selectins, calcium participates directly in recognition of the carbohydrate ligand. The term lectin means a protein that recognizes carbohydrates; it does not mean that the protein is itself a carbohydrate. Lectins occur in many biological settings and not all lectins bind the same sugars or require calcium. When an examination statement uses the phrase C-type lectin in relation to selectins, the relevant property is their calcium-dependent carbohydrate-binding interface.

Immediately after the lectin domain lies an epidermal growth factor-like domain, abbreviated EGF-like domain. The name describes a characteristic structural motif rather than a claim that the selectin is a receptor for epidermal growth factor. The relative orientation of the lectin and EGF-like regions influences the conformation of the extracellular binding apparatus. Mechanical loading can alter this relationship in ways that affect the lifetime of certain selectin-ligand complexes. Thus a mutation outside the residues that touch the glycan can still change adhesion.

Following the EGF-like domain are repeated extracellular modules related to short consensus repeats found in complement-regulatory proteins. These modules are also called complement control protein repeats or sushi domains. The common human forms of E-selectin, P-selectin and L-selectin contain six, nine and two such repeats respectively. P-selectin consequently has a relatively long repeated extracellular stalk, whereas L-selectin has a shorter one. The length difference is not a simple ranking of adhesive strength. It changes how the recognition domain can reach across the interface and how it is presented relative to other surface molecules.

The repeated modules are not equivalent to multiple separate carbohydrate-binding pockets. A single selectin polypeptide has an amino-terminal lectin domain, and the stalk modules support its positioning. A diagram showing nine P-selectin repeats must therefore not label them as nine ligand-binding sites. If the stalk is shortened experimentally while the lectin site remains functional in a solution assay, binding between whole cells may nevertheless decrease because the site has become less accessible. Comparing soluble binding to cell-based binding tests this geometrical interpretation.

The transmembrane segment is hydrophobic and lies within the lipid bilayer. It allows the extracellular portion and cytoplasmic tail to remain associated with the membrane. The cytoplasmic tail does not bind an extracellular glycan directly. Instead, tail sequences can influence intracellular sorting, membrane retention and interactions with cellular machinery. In P-selectin, for example, the tail is important in routing the protein to appropriate intracellular storage compartments and controlling its residence at the surface. In L-selectin, the cytoplasmic region and near-membrane processing influence surface behaviour and shedding.

These membrane functions are distinguishable by mutation. Suppose a substitution abolishes calcium coordination in a lectin domain without reducing the number of proteins on the cell surface. Such a mutation primarily impairs recognition. A substitution that changes intracellular targeting can produce a different result: purified extracellular protein may bind normally, but the intact cell does not display enough receptor in the appropriate location. A deletion that changes stalk length can leave both abundance and intrinsic affinity largely intact but reduce encounter efficiency. Each phenotype reduces adhesion through a different intermediate step.

All three selectins are glycoproteins. Glycosylation of a receptor can contribute to correct folding, stability and surface expression, while glycosylation of its ligand can create the actual structure recognized at the binding site. These are separate roles. A defect in protein maturation may reduce receptor number, whereas a defect in ligand glycosylation may leave the receptor number unchanged but remove the matching chemical feature on the opposing surface. Surface staining for a protein alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The distribution of receptors across the membrane also matters. A membrane protein may diffuse laterally, encounter neighbouring molecules and become locally enriched in particular membrane structures. The location of a binding site near the tip of a leukocyte microvillus can improve its chances of encountering an endothelial ligand before the cell body is close to the wall. The opposing surface has its own organization and glycocalyx. The final probability of attachment is therefore determined by molecular density, orientation, accessibility, deformation and local fluid motion, not just the total number of receptor molecules in the cell.

A structural comparison with the immunoglobulin superfamily is especially useful. A selectin carries a specialized carbohydrate-recognition head connected to an extracellular stalk, whereas a typical immunoglobulin-superfamily CAM contains one or more immunoglobulin-like domains, often organized serially along its extracellular part. Both can be single-pass membrane proteins and both can be glycosylated, so neither membrane topology nor glycosylation alone identifies the family. The decisive features are domain architecture and binding mechanism. Cadherins constitute a third architecture with repeated extracellular cadherin domains; integrins contain two different associated subunits. These distinctions recur throughout cell communication questions.

4. The chemistry of glycan recognition and ligand construction

Carbohydrates on cell surfaces carry information because monosaccharides can be linked in many distinct ways. Two molecules containing the same kinds and numbers of sugars can differ in linkage position, anomeric configuration and branching. A protein recognizing one arrangement need not recognize another. In contrast to a polypeptide, whose amino acid sequence is directly specified by a template during translation, a complex glycan is assembled through successive enzymatic reactions. The expression and localization of glycosyltransferases and the supply of activated sugar donors influence which carbohydrate structures appear at the membrane.

A representative selectin-binding determinant is sialyl Lewis X, abbreviated sLeX or sLeˣ. It is a tetrasaccharide motif containing N-acetylneuraminic acid, galactose, fucose and N-acetylglucosamine. Its usual linkage notation is Neu5Acα2–3Galβ1–4(Fucα1–3)GlcNAc. The fucose is attached to the N-acetylglucosamine and the sialic acid is attached to galactose. The notation is a structural description: it specifies which monosaccharides are joined and the linkage configuration. Listing the four monosaccharides without the linkages does not define the same molecular epitope.

A selectin does not recognize any negatively charged sugar indiscriminately. The positions of hydroxyl groups and other chemical features must fit the binding interface. Calcium coordinated within the lectin domain helps organize the contact, notably with hydroxyl groups of the fucose in a canonical selectin-binding glycan. Hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts and electrostatic contributions help provide the specificity of the complete interface. Although calcium is essential to the normal recognition mechanism, the interaction is not a covalent joining of the receptor to its ligand.

A chelator is a chemical compound that binds metal ions. EDTA reduces the concentration of available divalent cations, including calcium and magnesium. When a selectin-dependent interaction is tested in the presence of EDTA, removal of available calcium is expected to impair lectin-dependent binding. However, EDTA is not a selectin-specific inhibitor. It can also disrupt cadherin contacts and integrin-dependent interactions. A conclusion about which molecule was responsible therefore requires family-specific reagents or a defined purified binding system, not simply a comparison with EDTA alone.

The distinction between direct calcium dependence and indirect sensitivity is important. An immunoglobulin-like ligand such as ICAM-1 does not use a selectin-like calcium-dependent lectin domain to recognize its binding partner. Nevertheless, an ICAM-1 interaction with an integrin can depend on divalent cations because of the integrin's own binding mechanism. If EDTA disrupts the interaction, it does not follow that ICAM-1 is a C-type lectin. The overall adhesive pair inherits requirements from both molecular partners.

The glycan determinant must be presented in a context that permits recognition. A glycoprotein carrier consists of a polypeptide with covalently attached carbohydrate chains; a glycolipid carries carbohydrate attached to a lipid. Several different carriers can present related selectin-binding motifs, and the same carrier polypeptide may occur in both functional and nonfunctional glycoforms. The amino acid sequence of a carrier and its glycosylation state must therefore be considered separately.

P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1, PSGL-1, is a prominent example. It is a mucin-like membrane glycoprotein on many leukocytes. The extended, heavily O-glycosylated extracellular region helps display its amino-terminal recognition features. For efficient physiological binding to P-selectin, an appropriately modified O-glycan and nearby sulfated tyrosine residues in the amino-terminal portion contribute to recognition. The protein is thus not merely an interchangeable hook carrying a sugar; the spatial combination of carbohydrate and peptide features can determine the quality of the interaction.

Tyrosine sulfation and sugar sulfation are distinct post-translational chemical processes. Addition of a sulfate group to a tyrosine side chain changes the charge and recognition properties of a polypeptide. Addition of sulfate to a sugar changes the chemistry of a glycan. These modifications are not identical to phosphorylation of a cytoplasmic tyrosine by a signaling kinase. In PSGL-1, extracellular tyrosine sulfation contributes to P-selectin recognition; in certain L-selectin ligands, sulfated carbohydrate structures are particularly important. Confusing the two modifications obscures the molecular basis of receptor selectivity.

The biochemical pathway generating a functional ligand begins inside the cell. Sugar donors must be synthesized, transported where needed and used by the appropriate enzymes during secretory pathway processing. Fucose residues are added using GDP-fucose as a donor; the transport of GDP-fucose into the Golgi lumen is crucial for many fucosylation reactions. A defect in this transport can leave a ligand carrier protein present but deficient in the required carbohydrate epitope. The result can be a failure of rolling adhesion even when the selectin on the endothelial cell is completely normal.

Glycan recognition is consequently a property of both genetic and post-translational regulation. A gene encoding PSGL-1 specifies a polypeptide that can serve as a carrier. Other genes encode the glycosylation and sulfation machinery that generate the binding surface. Two cell types expressing comparable amounts of the same carrier can display different selectin-binding activities. Conversely, a change in glycosylation can affect several carriers at once. In a genetic experiment, rescue of a common modifying enzyme may restore multiple adhesive interactions rather than only a single protein-protein pair.

Consider an experiment in which leukocytes bind poorly to P-selectin despite normal staining for PSGL-1. Three hypotheses remain plausible: the antibody detects a region unrelated to the functional site, required glycan modifications are missing, or the surface carrier is present but incorrectly distributed. A glycan-specific antibody, binding assay with purified selectin, microscopy of surface distribution and rescue of the glycosylation defect could distinguish these mechanisms. Normal protein expression is an essential control, but it is not sufficient proof of normal receptor function.

Sialyl Lewis X is a useful model determinant rather than an exhaustive universal ligand list. E-selectin recognizes a range of appropriately glycosylated protein and lipid carriers, and the relative importance of individual carriers varies among cells and species. P-selectin typically depends strongly on PSGL-1 in physiological leukocyte interactions, yet artificial surfaces bearing appropriate carbohydrates can support some binding even without the full physiological carrier. L-selectin recognizes specialized glycosylated endothelial ligands and can also participate in leukocyte-leukocyte interactions. These variations are not contradictions of the carbohydrate-recognition principle; they show that a shared chemical motif is embedded in distinct biological presentations.
