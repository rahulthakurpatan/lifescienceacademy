1. The biological problem of reversible cell contact

A cell in a tissue and a cell carried by flowing blood encounter very different physical situations. An epithelial cell must remain joined to its neighbours for long periods while the sheet bends, stretches and renews itself. A white blood cell, or leukocyte, often needs to remain free in the circulation until it encounters a particular vascular surface. It must then attach without immediately forming an irreversible union, evaluate the local environment and, under appropriate conditions, stop. Adhesion molecules therefore cannot be understood simply as molecular glue. They form regulated interfaces whose physical properties must match the behaviour of the cells expressing them.

The inner surface of a blood vessel is formed by endothelial cells. Their exposed membrane faces flowing blood and their opposite surface faces the underlying tissue. Imagine a circulating leukocyte encountering this surface. The two cell membranes cannot ordinarily fuse or make uniform direct contact because membrane lipids, surface proteins and the hydrated carbohydrate coat keep them apart. A useful adhesive interaction requires extracellular domains extending from one surface to encounter compatible structures on the other. These structures must bind before the flowing cell is swept away. Once they bind, mechanical load will be transmitted through the contact.

A stable attachment is not necessarily the best solution to this initial problem. A molecule that forms a very durable bond but associates slowly may fail to capture a passing cell. A molecule that associates during a brief encounter but dissociates rapidly can arrest the forward motion momentarily and provide time for another association. If these short-lived contacts occur successively, a cell can travel along the surface while remaining close enough to receive further information. Such behaviour is termed rolling adhesion. Rolling is not free swimming and it is not the same as stationary adhesion.

The family of adhesion molecules particularly adapted to such interactions is the selectin family. The name reflects their carbohydrate-binding activity and their role in selective recognition. Selectins usually recognize particular carbohydrate-containing structures attached to membrane molecules on another cell. They are capable of forming reversible bonds in mechanically dynamic situations. A second group of molecules, the immunoglobulin-superfamily adhesion molecules, includes important endothelial partners for leukocyte integrins. These proteins permit cells to establish stronger or more sustained associations and also participate in many other forms of cell recognition.

The preceding chapter described cadherins. In an ordinary adherens junction, cadherins expressed on opposing epithelial cells recognize compatible cadherins and contribute to tissue cohesion. The dynamic contact considered here has a different design requirement. Selectins are not cadherins that happen to be expressed in blood vessels. Their extracellular binding domain recognizes a different kind of chemical structure, and their role depends importantly on bond kinetics and presentation at the surface. The difference between these families provides an example of how molecular architecture influences cellular behaviour.

A ligand is a molecular entity recognized by a binding site. In a selectin interaction the ligand is often a glycan, a short or long chain of sugars carried by a glycoprotein or glycolipid. A counter-receptor is a surface molecule on the opposing cell that participates in the binding interaction. These terms describe opposite sides of a contact and do not necessarily indicate which cell receives a signal. Both cells can change their behaviour as a result of the same physical interaction. An adhesion receptor can also be a signaling participant; the two functions are not mutually exclusive.

The word adhesion refers to a physical association between surfaces. Recognition refers to the discrimination between compatible and incompatible molecular features. Signaling means a process by which information changes cellular activity. These processes frequently occur together, but an experiment may isolate one from another. A particle coated with a ligand can bind to purified selectin without any intracellular metabolism. A living leukocyte interacting through the same ligand can also respond biochemically. Observing binding in the first experiment is evidence about molecular recognition, not evidence that the particle has signaled.

In circulation the initial contact is especially sensitive to distance, orientation and time. A large number of molecules in the wrong membrane region may be less useful than a smaller number extending towards the other surface. A leukocyte's surface projections, known as microvilli, can present certain adhesion proteins beyond its bulk glycocalyx. The cell is deformable, so its shape and membrane extensions can change when a bond is loaded. These physical variables explain why measuring an isolated receptor's affinity in solution cannot completely predict whole-cell adhesion.

An important conceptual distinction is between an interaction that is selective and one that is exclusive. Different selectins can recognize overlapping sets of glycans, and an individual leukocyte may possess several ligand carriers. A cell therefore does not necessarily lose all adhesive behaviour when one receptor is removed. Redundant interactions may provide partial compensation, and the importance of a given route varies with the tissue, cell type and inflammatory state. Predicting the phenotype of a mutation requires identifying the actual molecular partners that were present before the mutation.

The central theme of this chapter is the division of adhesive work. A reversible molecular tether allows a cell to encounter an endothelial surface. Additional interactions increase the probability that the encounter develops into stable attachment. Local signaling helps regulate the transition. This is a functional sequence rather than a universal rigid schedule: individual bonds can operate simultaneously, and not every cell uses every molecular class. The most useful examination strategy is to locate the impaired step and identify which remaining steps are intrinsically possible.

2. How the selectin family and rolling behaviour were discovered

The history of adhesion research illustrates the distinction between a cellular phenomenon and the molecular explanation for it. Observing that leukocytes associate with vascular walls establishes that some physical interaction occurs. It does not identify the surface protein responsible. Similarly, observing increased attachment to activated endothelial cells suggests that the endothelium has changed, but it does not establish whether the cause is new protein production, mobilization of previously stored material, altered cell geometry or a change in the leukocytes themselves. Identifying the responsible molecule required ways of interfering with particular surface components.

Monoclonal antibodies were important in this development because an antibody raised against a cell-surface antigen can sometimes interfere with its biological function. If an antibody binds an activated endothelial surface and reduces leukocyte adhesion, it provides a means of identifying a candidate adhesion molecule. The interpretation requires controls. An irrelevant antibody of the same class should not produce the same specific reduction. The endothelial monolayer must remain intact, and the test must distinguish interference with recognition from nonspecific damage to either cell. An antibody that binds but does not block function is also informative about accessible epitopes.

In 1987, Michael Bevilacqua and colleagues reported an inducible endothelial leukocyte adhesion molecule, subsequently called ELAM-1 and now E-selectin. Their antibody-based observations linked an endothelial surface component appearing after activation to leukocyte attachment. The significance was not that all adhesion was explained by one molecule. Rather, they could distinguish an induced molecular contribution from the background adhesiveness of unstimulated endothelial cells. This discovery made it possible to ask whether the induced protein was chemically related to other known receptors.

The 1989 molecular characterization by Bevilacqua and colleagues identified a domain arrangement in ELAM-1 related to carbohydrate-recognition proteins and complement-regulatory protein domains. Other researchers had been investigating lymphocyte homing receptors and a protein stored in platelet and endothelial secretory granules, then called GMP-140. Comparison of the proteins revealed a shared architecture. The older names arose from distinct experimental systems; the modern family names reflect the related proteins rather than three unrelated adhesion mechanisms. ELAM-1 is E-selectin, GMP-140 or PADGEM is P-selectin, and the lymphocyte homing receptor LECAM-1 is L-selectin.

Lymphocyte homing research established that cells circulating through the blood do not enter every tissue indiscriminately. Particular endothelial regions, including high endothelial venules in lymphoid organs, present surface molecules that favour the attachment of appropriate lymphocytes. Blocking the homing receptor recognized by the MEL-14 antibody in mice provided an experimental route for connecting specific molecules with this selectivity. The broader lesson is that a vascular surface is not a passive wall: it can display a chemically encoded set of attachment opportunities.

The existence of related proteins raised a second question. Were they simply additional members of a large set of cell adhesion molecules, or did they support a distinctive adhesive behaviour? Conventional static cell adhesion assays could show that cells remained attached after incubation, but such assays did not reproduce the fleeting collisions and mechanical loading experienced in the circulation. A cell that can attach when placed on a surface for several minutes may be unable to attach while passing that surface in less than a second. The two assays answer different questions.

Lawrence and Springer addressed this distinction in a landmark 1991 study using artificial lipid surfaces with purified adhesion molecules and leukocytes under controlled flow. They compared adhesion involving the molecule then designated CD62, now identified as P-selectin, with integrin-dependent interactions involving ICAM-1. The experimental design separated molecular components of the endothelial surface instead of allowing the entire complex endothelium to contribute uncontrolled variables. Rolling could be supported by the selectin-containing surface, whereas the conditions permitting strong integrin-dependent attachment differed. In particular, forming an association under flow was not equivalent to surviving shear after the association had already formed.

This work supplied a mechanistic explanation for a sequence of leukocyte recruitment events. The initial tether increases the time available to encounter the wall; subsequent activation-dependent adhesive interactions can support much stronger retention. The experiment was a demonstration under specified conditions, not proof that no integrin can ever mediate initial capture. Later observations show that some alpha-four integrin interactions can support rolling and that different endothelial beds use different combinations of adhesion molecules. The historical model is valuable precisely because it identifies experimentally separable functions that can be compared in new situations.

Determining the chemical identity of selectin ligands required another kind of experiment. Researchers compared binding to cells with different glycosylation states, used enzymes that remove particular carbohydrate groups and assessed whether defined glycan structures supported recognition. These approaches revealed the importance of sialylated and fucosylated structures such as sialyl Lewis X. A reduction in adhesion after removing a sugar residue suggests that the residue contributes to recognition, although it may also affect the presentation or stability of the larger carrier. Testing purified components and restoring the missing modification helps distinguish these possibilities.

Structural work provided a complementary line of evidence. In 2000, Somers and colleagues reported structures of selectin extracellular domain fragments interacting with sialyl Lewis X and with a modified peptide from P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1, PSGL-1. Atomic models showed that the binding interface is a defined molecular surface and that the carbohydrate and, for the physiological P-selectin interaction, additional peptide modifications contribute to the association. Such structural evidence is much more specific than a general claim that carbohydrates are sticky. It also explains why the correct sugar presented on an unsuitable carrier may not reproduce the full interaction.

A further discovery concerned the mechanical behaviour of these bonds. Most familiar chemical intuition suggests that applying force should shorten the lifetime of a noncovalent bond. In 2003, Marshall and colleagues combined force measurements and flow experiments to show that the lifetime of a P-selectin interaction with PSGL-1 first increased over one force range and then decreased at higher forces. This provided direct evidence for a catch-to-slip transition. It did not mean that every selectin contact is strengthened by all forces. The result defined conditions under which a simple always-weakening model is insufficient.

These studies form an instructive progression from cell observation, to inhibitory antibodies, to sequence comparison, to reconstituted flow systems, to molecular structural and mechanical evidence. No single method answers every question. Antibody inhibition identifies a functional contribution; a domain sequence suggests a biochemical family; a purified surface tests sufficiency in a defined context; an atomic structure locates possible contacts; a force experiment reveals how bond lifetime changes under load. A student should be able to match each kind of result with the inference it supports and recognize the conclusions that remain outside its scope.

3. Selectin architecture: how the recognition domain is presented

E-selectin, P-selectin and L-selectin are single-pass membrane glycoproteins. Their extracellular amino-terminal regions extend away from the membrane, followed by a membrane-spanning segment and a relatively short cytoplasmic region. A membrane protein of this orientation can present a ligand-binding site into the extracellular environment while remaining mechanically connected to the cell. A soluble fragment containing the recognition domain may retain its ability to bind, but it lacks the normal membrane anchor needed to transmit tension between two cells.

The amino-terminal carbohydrate-recognition region belongs to the C-type lectin family. In the case of selectins, calcium participates directly in recognition of the carbohydrate ligand. The term lectin means a protein that recognizes carbohydrates; it does not mean that the protein is itself a carbohydrate. Lectins occur in many biological settings and not all lectins bind the same sugars or require calcium. When an examination statement uses the phrase C-type lectin in relation to selectins, the relevant property is their calcium-dependent carbohydrate-binding interface.

Immediately after the lectin domain lies an epidermal growth factor-like domain, abbreviated EGF-like domain. The name describes a characteristic structural motif rather than a claim that the selectin is a receptor for epidermal growth factor. The relative orientation of the lectin and EGF-like regions influences the conformation of the extracellular binding apparatus. Mechanical loading can alter this relationship in ways that affect the lifetime of certain selectin-ligand complexes. Thus a mutation outside the residues that touch the glycan can still change adhesion.

Following the EGF-like domain are repeated extracellular modules related to short consensus repeats found in complement-regulatory proteins. These modules are also called complement control protein repeats or sushi domains. The common human forms of E-selectin, P-selectin and L-selectin contain six, nine and two such repeats respectively. P-selectin consequently has a relatively long repeated extracellular stalk, whereas L-selectin has a shorter one. The length difference is not a simple ranking of adhesive strength. It changes how the recognition domain can reach across the interface and how it is presented relative to other surface molecules.

The repeated modules are not equivalent to multiple separate carbohydrate-binding pockets. A single selectin polypeptide has an amino-terminal lectin domain, and the stalk modules support its positioning. A diagram showing nine P-selectin repeats must therefore not label them as nine ligand-binding sites. If the stalk is shortened experimentally while the lectin site remains functional in a solution assay, binding between whole cells may nevertheless decrease because the site has become less accessible. Comparing soluble binding to cell-based binding tests this geometrical interpretation.

The transmembrane segment is hydrophobic and lies within the lipid bilayer. It allows the extracellular portion and cytoplasmic tail to remain associated with the membrane. The cytoplasmic tail does not bind an extracellular glycan directly. Instead, tail sequences can influence intracellular sorting, membrane retention and interactions with cellular machinery. In P-selectin, for example, the tail is important in routing the protein to appropriate intracellular storage compartments and controlling its residence at the surface. In L-selectin, the cytoplasmic region and near-membrane processing influence surface behaviour and shedding.

These membrane functions are distinguishable by mutation. Suppose a substitution abolishes calcium coordination in a lectin domain without reducing the number of proteins on the cell surface. Such a mutation primarily impairs recognition. A substitution that changes intracellular targeting can produce a different result: purified extracellular protein may bind normally, but the intact cell does not display enough receptor in the appropriate location. A deletion that changes stalk length can leave both abundance and intrinsic affinity largely intact but reduce encounter efficiency. Each phenotype reduces adhesion through a different intermediate step.

All three selectins are glycoproteins. Glycosylation of a receptor can contribute to correct folding, stability and surface expression, while glycosylation of its ligand can create the actual structure recognized at the binding site. These are separate roles. A defect in protein maturation may reduce receptor number, whereas a defect in ligand glycosylation may leave the receptor number unchanged but remove the matching chemical feature on the opposing surface. Surface staining for a protein alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The distribution of receptors across the membrane also matters. A membrane protein may diffuse laterally, encounter neighbouring molecules and become locally enriched in particular membrane structures. The location of a binding site near the tip of a leukocyte microvillus can improve its chances of encountering an endothelial ligand before the cell body is close to the wall. The opposing surface has its own organization and glycocalyx. The final probability of attachment is therefore determined by molecular density, orientation, accessibility, deformation and local fluid motion, not just the total number of receptor molecules in the cell.

A structural comparison with the immunoglobulin superfamily is especially useful. A selectin carries a specialized carbohydrate-recognition head connected to an extracellular stalk, whereas a typical immunoglobulin-superfamily CAM contains one or more immunoglobulin-like domains, often organized serially along its extracellular part. Both can be single-pass membrane proteins and both can be glycosylated, so neither membrane topology nor glycosylation alone identifies the family. The decisive features are domain architecture and binding mechanism. Cadherins constitute a third architecture with repeated extracellular cadherin domains; integrins contain two different associated subunits. These distinctions recur throughout cell communication questions.

4. The chemistry of glycan recognition and ligand construction

Carbohydrates on cell surfaces carry information because monosaccharides can be linked in many distinct ways. Two molecules containing the same kinds and numbers of sugars can differ in linkage position, anomeric configuration and branching. A protein recognizing one arrangement need not recognize another. In contrast to a polypeptide, whose amino acid sequence is directly specified by a template during translation, a complex glycan is assembled through successive enzymatic reactions. The expression and localization of glycosyltransferases and the supply of activated sugar donors influence which carbohydrate structures appear at the membrane.

A representative selectin-binding determinant is sialyl Lewis X, abbreviated sLeX or sLeˣ. It is a tetrasaccharide motif containing N-acetylneuraminic acid, galactose, fucose and N-acetylglucosamine. Its usual linkage notation is Neu5Acα2–3Galβ1–4(Fucα1–3)GlcNAc. The fucose is attached to the N-acetylglucosamine and the sialic acid is attached to galactose. The notation is a structural description: it specifies which monosaccharides are joined and the linkage configuration. Listing the four monosaccharides without the linkages does not define the same molecular epitope.

A selectin does not recognize any negatively charged sugar indiscriminately. The positions of hydroxyl groups and other chemical features must fit the binding interface. Calcium coordinated within the lectin domain helps organize the contact, notably with hydroxyl groups of the fucose in a canonical selectin-binding glycan. Hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts and electrostatic contributions help provide the specificity of the complete interface. Although calcium is essential to the normal recognition mechanism, the interaction is not a covalent joining of the receptor to its ligand.

A chelator is a chemical compound that binds metal ions. EDTA reduces the concentration of available divalent cations, including calcium and magnesium. When a selectin-dependent interaction is tested in the presence of EDTA, removal of available calcium is expected to impair lectin-dependent binding. However, EDTA is not a selectin-specific inhibitor. It can also disrupt cadherin contacts and integrin-dependent interactions. A conclusion about which molecule was responsible therefore requires family-specific reagents or a defined purified binding system, not simply a comparison with EDTA alone.

The distinction between direct calcium dependence and indirect sensitivity is important. An immunoglobulin-like ligand such as ICAM-1 does not use a selectin-like calcium-dependent lectin domain to recognize its binding partner. Nevertheless, an ICAM-1 interaction with an integrin can depend on divalent cations because of the integrin's own binding mechanism. If EDTA disrupts the interaction, it does not follow that ICAM-1 is a C-type lectin. The overall adhesive pair inherits requirements from both molecular partners.

The glycan determinant must be presented in a context that permits recognition. A glycoprotein carrier consists of a polypeptide with covalently attached carbohydrate chains; a glycolipid carries carbohydrate attached to a lipid. Several different carriers can present related selectin-binding motifs, and the same carrier polypeptide may occur in both functional and nonfunctional glycoforms. The amino acid sequence of a carrier and its glycosylation state must therefore be considered separately.

P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1, PSGL-1, is a prominent example. It is a mucin-like membrane glycoprotein on many leukocytes. The extended, heavily O-glycosylated extracellular region helps display its amino-terminal recognition features. For efficient physiological binding to P-selectin, an appropriately modified O-glycan and nearby sulfated tyrosine residues in the amino-terminal portion contribute to recognition. The protein is thus not merely an interchangeable hook carrying a sugar; the spatial combination of carbohydrate and peptide features can determine the quality of the interaction.

Tyrosine sulfation and sugar sulfation are distinct post-translational chemical processes. Addition of a sulfate group to a tyrosine side chain changes the charge and recognition properties of a polypeptide. Addition of sulfate to a sugar changes the chemistry of a glycan. These modifications are not identical to phosphorylation of a cytoplasmic tyrosine by a signaling kinase. In PSGL-1, extracellular tyrosine sulfation contributes to P-selectin recognition; in certain L-selectin ligands, sulfated carbohydrate structures are particularly important. Confusing the two modifications obscures the molecular basis of receptor selectivity.

The biochemical pathway generating a functional ligand begins inside the cell. Sugar donors must be synthesized, transported where needed and used by the appropriate enzymes during secretory pathway processing. Fucose residues are added using GDP-fucose as a donor; the transport of GDP-fucose into the Golgi lumen is crucial for many fucosylation reactions. A defect in this transport can leave a ligand carrier protein present but deficient in the required carbohydrate epitope. The result can be a failure of rolling adhesion even when the selectin on the endothelial cell is completely normal.

Glycan recognition is consequently a property of both genetic and post-translational regulation. A gene encoding PSGL-1 specifies a polypeptide that can serve as a carrier. Other genes encode the glycosylation and sulfation machinery that generate the binding surface. Two cell types expressing comparable amounts of the same carrier can display different selectin-binding activities. Conversely, a change in glycosylation can affect several carriers at once. In a genetic experiment, rescue of a common modifying enzyme may restore multiple adhesive interactions rather than only a single protein-protein pair.

Consider an experiment in which leukocytes bind poorly to P-selectin despite normal staining for PSGL-1. Three hypotheses remain plausible: the antibody detects a region unrelated to the functional site, required glycan modifications are missing, or the surface carrier is present but incorrectly distributed. A glycan-specific antibody, binding assay with purified selectin, microscopy of surface distribution and rescue of the glycosylation defect could distinguish these mechanisms. Normal protein expression is an essential control, but it is not sufficient proof of normal receptor function.

Sialyl Lewis X is a useful model determinant rather than an exhaustive universal ligand list. E-selectin recognizes a range of appropriately glycosylated protein and lipid carriers, and the relative importance of individual carriers varies among cells and species. P-selectin typically depends strongly on PSGL-1 in physiological leukocyte interactions, yet artificial surfaces bearing appropriate carbohydrates can support some binding even without the full physiological carrier. L-selectin recognizes specialized glycosylated endothelial ligands and can also participate in leukocyte-leukocyte interactions. These variations are not contradictions of the carbohydrate-recognition principle; they show that a shared chemical motif is embedded in distinct biological presentations.

5. P-selectin: rapid deployment of a preformed receptor

A useful way to understand the individual selectins is to ask where each is located before an adhesive encounter, how it becomes available, and what it recognizes. P-selectin, also designated CD62P, is an especially clear example of regulation by intracellular trafficking. The P in its name refers to platelets, but the receptor is also an important component of endothelial cells. Restricting it to platelets because of its name would omit a major part of its biological function.

Platelets are small anucleate cellular fragments produced from megakaryocytes. They contain secretory granules that can discharge or expose components when stimulated. P-selectin is associated with the membrane of platelet alpha granules. Endothelial cells contain specialized rod-shaped secretory organelles called Weibel–Palade bodies, which store P-selectin together with other proteins including von Willebrand factor. The two cell types have different physiological roles, but both exploit storage to make a membrane protein available quickly.

The membrane of a secretory granule separates its internal lumen from the cytoplasm. When a granule fuses with the plasma membrane, the face that previously faced the granule lumen becomes continuous with the extracellular face of the cell membrane. An extracellular lectin domain that was directed into the granule lumen is consequently exposed to the outside of the cell after fusion. This topological continuity explains how secretion can rapidly alter the adhesive properties of a cell without requiring newly translated P-selectin to travel from the endoplasmic reticulum.

Stimuli such as thrombin can promote P-selectin exposure on both activated platelets and endothelial cells; histamine is a familiar stimulus for endothelial exocytosis. The immediate response uses an existing pool of proteins. Transcription and translation are slower routes for changing receptor abundance, although later expression and replenishment can contribute in particular contexts. A rapid increase in surface P-selectin should not automatically be interpreted as an increase in the total number of P-selectin polypeptides inside the cell.

Imagine measuring P-selectin with two antibody staining conditions. A nonpermeabilized cell exposes only epitopes accessible from the external medium. A permeabilized cell can additionally reveal intracellular pools if the assay preserves the relevant epitopes. Shortly after an exocytotic stimulus, surface staining may increase substantially while total staining remains approximately constant. Such a pattern supports redistribution of an existing protein pool. A later rise in both measurements could reflect additional synthesis, but further controls would be needed to establish the source of the increased protein.

Stored P-selectin is not permanently exposed after a stimulus. Receptors at the surface may be internalized, recycled or delivered to degradative pathways. The balance between insertion and removal determines surface abundance. This regulation is useful because a vessel wall should not remain maximally adhesive after the local initiating signal subsides. A defect in internalization can prolong surface exposure without increasing transcription. Conversely, a sorting defect that reduces granule storage may reduce the capacity for rapid inducible exposure even if the cell produces the protein.

A simple time-course experiment can distinguish these mechanisms. In a hypothetical endothelial culture, one measures surface P-selectin at baseline and at two, ten and sixty minutes following stimulation. One culture receives an inhibitor of protein synthesis during the short assay, another receives a perturbation known to impair granule fusion, and a third receives vehicle alone. Persistence of the early increase during short-term inhibition of synthesis, together with loss of that increase when fusion is prevented, supports mobilization of pre-existing molecules. The investigator must separately confirm that the perturbations did not destroy cell viability or the antibody-binding epitope.

When endothelial P-selectin is exposed to blood, its lectin domain can recognize appropriately modified PSGL-1 on passing leukocytes. The extended extracellular portions of both molecules help the binding regions meet despite the separation between membranes. Receptor-ligand engagement creates a bridge that transmits force from the flowing cell to the vascular wall. A transient bridge can slow a cell even when it does not stop the cell permanently.

On an activated platelet the same basic recognition system can connect a platelet to a leukocyte. This is an example of heterotypic adhesion, meaning association between different cellular types. The consequences depend on the state of the cells and on the other signals present in the local environment. The adhesive bridge is a mechanism for selective encounter; it is not in itself a complete explanation of inflammation, clot formation or all responses of the participating cells.

A question may ask what happens after selective inhibition of P-selectin-mediated binding. In an assay containing only P-selectin and functional leukocyte ligands, the expected defect is reduced tethering and rolling. In an intact vessel, E-selectin and other adhesive mechanisms may support some residual interactions. This difference illustrates the value of examining an isolated molecular system before generalizing to a complex tissue. The magnitude of the phenotype cannot be specified from the identity of the inhibited molecule alone.

P-selectin therefore exemplifies three connected ideas: rapid control by membrane trafficking, recognition of a chemically modified extracellular ligand, and transient adhesion capable of withstanding mechanical load for a limited time. Its biological specificity arises from the combination of these properties. A receptor with the same carbohydrate affinity but no inducible endothelial exposure would not perform precisely the same regulatory task.

6. E-selectin: inducible endothelial recognition

E-selectin, or CD62E, was originally identified in activated endothelial cells and was called ELAM-1. In the standard model of acute inflammatory endothelial activation, cytokines such as interleukin-1 and tumour necrosis factor increase E-selectin expression. This response commonly requires changes in gene expression and synthesis of the receptor, in contrast to the rapid mobilization of preformed P-selectin. The distinction is between mechanisms of availability, not between one receptor that is regulated and another that is unregulated.

The process begins when endothelial cells receive soluble signals from their environment. Receptor signaling alters the activity of transcriptional regulators; expression of the E-selectin gene can then rise, followed by translation, processing through the secretory pathway and delivery to the plasma membrane. The exact signaling cascades are not required to understand this chapter. What matters is that the surface state of the endothelial cell reflects earlier communication events. A passing leukocyte can therefore read an induced adhesive surface as evidence of changes in the underlying biological environment.

E-selectin's time course is usually slower than the immediate exposure of stored P-selectin. In textbook comparisons, P-selectin can become available rapidly after stimulation, whereas substantial E-selectin induction develops over a longer interval, often measured in hours. These are qualitative distinctions, not a universal clock. Different species, tissues and stimuli exhibit different expression patterns, and constitutive E-selectin expression occurs in some vascular settings. A graph drawn for a particular experimental condition should be interpreted using the condition specified rather than converted into a universal rule.

E-selectin recognizes appropriately sialylated and fucosylated carbohydrate determinants on several different carriers. Functional E-selectin ligands can include PSGL-1 and other glycoproteins; their relative importance differs among leukocyte populations. For example, CD44 can occur in a glycoform capable of E-selectin binding, and ESL-1 has important roles in certain experimental systems. The protein name alone is not enough to determine whether the ligand is functional in a particular cell. It must present compatible glycans at the relevant surface.

This diversity of ligand carriers is helpful for understanding an apparently paradoxical experiment. Removing PSGL-1 may strongly impair P-selectin-dependent interactions but leave appreciable rolling on E-selectin. If another correctly glycosylated carrier remains available to E-selectin, the second phenotype is not surprising. Removing a common fucosylation activity, by contrast, may impair several E-selectin ligands simultaneously. A deletion of an individual carrier and a deletion of a shared modifying enzyme therefore test different levels of the recognition system.

The endothelial E-selectin surface can support capture and rolling, and E-selectin-dependent interactions often contribute to relatively slow rolling under appropriate conditions. Slow rolling increases the time available for local communication and can be accompanied by signals influencing subsequent integrin-dependent adhesion. This observation is not equivalent to saying that E-selectin permanently immobilizes every contacting cell. Rolling velocity depends on the density and type of ligands, the local mechanical conditions and the participation of other adhesive interactions.

It is also important not to assign an invariant rolling speed to a selectin. The statement that E-selectin can promote slow rolling is a biological tendency in defined settings, not a guarantee that every E-selectin-expressing surface produces slower movement than every P-selectin-expressing surface. A surface bearing a high density of one receptor can have different mechanical behaviour from a surface bearing a low density of another. Fair comparisons require controlling receptor density, ligand availability, the cell population and wall shear stress.

A culture study comparing E-selectin induction with P-selectin mobilization should use measurements appropriate to the proposed mechanism. Quantitative measurement of messenger RNA can identify changes in gene expression but cannot establish surface availability by itself. Immunoblotting of total protein can detect protein accumulation but does not reveal which side of the membrane is exposed. Surface antibody staining or direct ligand-binding assays are needed to establish the functional accessible pool. Under flow, a measurement of rolling events then connects the molecular observations to behaviour.

A hypothetical result illustrates the logic. At ten minutes after a stimulus, total E-selectin RNA remains at its baseline value while surface P-selectin staining is elevated. At four hours, surface E-selectin and E-selectin RNA both increase. Inhibition of transcription prevents much of the late E-selectin increase while leaving the early mobilization of P-selectin relatively intact. These observations support distinct regulatory routes. They do not establish that every late response is transcription-dependent or that the inhibitor has no additional effects, so appropriate viability and assay-specific controls remain essential.

If a leukocyte lacks the glycosylation needed to bind E-selectin, simply increasing endothelial expression may not rescue the missing recognition determinant. Conversely, providing the correct leukocyte glycan does not guarantee capture when the endothelial receptor is absent. Successful adhesion requires both sides of the interface. This two-sided logic is useful in interpreting mixed-cell experiments in which one cell population is genetically normal and the other is altered.

E-selectin links local signaling to physical recognition. It provides a way for endothelial activation to create a spatially restricted adhesive surface that can influence circulating cells. It also demonstrates that the information conveyed by an adhesion molecule can lie in the regulation of its abundance, even when the chemical mechanism of ligand binding remains unchanged.

7. L-selectin: leukocyte expression and specialized vascular ligands

L-selectin, designated CD62L, differs from the two vascular selectins in its usual cellular distribution. It is expressed on many leukocytes, including numerous lymphocyte populations and neutrophils. The orientation of a typical L-selectin-dependent interaction is therefore reversed relative to the standard endothelial P-selectin example: the lectin receptor is on the circulating cell, and an appropriate glycan-bearing ligand may be on the endothelial surface. A diagram should make the sides explicit rather than suggest that all selectins occupy the vessel wall.

An important physiological example is lymphocyte homing through high endothelial venules, specialized vessels in lymphoid tissues. Unlike the relatively flattened endothelial cells of many ordinary vessels, these endothelial cells have a characteristic morphology and display a specialized set of adhesion ligands. Their surface helps circulating lymphocytes enter the appropriate lymphoid tissue. The location-specific expression and modification of ligands contributes to this routing, while subsequent signaling and adhesive interactions regulate progression beyond the initial encounter.

The term peripheral node addressin, or PNAd, describes a group of endothelial glycoproteins bearing particular carbohydrate determinants associated with L-selectin recognition in peripheral lymph nodes. It is not the name of one universal receptor protein. Relevant carriers include endothelial sialomucins such as CD34 and others, but not every molecule of CD34 in every tissue functions as an L-selectin ligand. Appropriate carbohydrate structures, including sulfated and fucosylated determinants, are needed. This is another example of the difference between a carrier protein and the actual recognition surface.

A mucin-like glycoprotein contains regions with extensive carbohydrate modification that can project from a membrane. Such an architecture makes it suitable for displaying glycans in a cell-contact interface. L-selectin binds compatible glycans on specialized endothelial molecules, with the exact sulfate, sialic acid and fucose arrangement important for many physiological ligands. The sulfated form of sialyl Lewis X often discussed in lymph node homing includes a sulfate modification on the carbohydrate structure. It should not be confused with the sulfated tyrosines that contribute to PSGL-1 recognition by P-selectin.

L-selectin also participates in interactions outside the classic lymph node homing example. It can recognize appropriately modified ligands on other leukocytes, including PSGL-1 in particular contexts. This can support secondary capture, in which a circulating leukocyte encounters an already interacting leukocyte rather than initially binding directly to an endothelial ligand. The mechanism illustrates how a cell adhesion molecule can contribute to the assembly of a local group of cells without being a universal homophilic receptor.

When leukocytes become activated, L-selectin can be rapidly removed from their surface by proteolytic shedding of its extracellular portion. ADAM17 is a well-established sheddase in many settings. Shedding differs from internalization: cleavage releases the extracellular portion rather than transferring the intact protein from the surface to an intracellular compartment. Both processes can reduce surface receptor abundance, but they produce different molecular products and are detected by different experimental approaches.

A decrease in surface CD62L following activation does not necessarily indicate reduced transcription or a reduction in the number of leukocytes. One can test a shedding mechanism by measuring surface receptor levels together with soluble extracellular receptor fragments in the surrounding medium. An increase in soluble fragments with a decrease in surface staining is consistent with ectodomain cleavage. Measuring total protein with an antibody directed against the cytoplasmic tail can additionally reveal whether the membrane-associated remnant remains. Such experiments require suitable time controls because newly synthesized receptor and degradation can also influence the measurements.

A mutation that prevents shedding may lead to prolonged L-selectin exposure and altered adhesive interactions. Whether the resulting cell enters more or less efficiently into a particular tissue is a separate question. Persistent initial binding could help capture in one situation while interfering with the transition to later behaviours in another. It would be incorrect to predict that a longer receptor residence time always improves total leukocyte recruitment. The relevant endpoint depends on the sequence of steps being measured.

L-selectin provides a useful case for reasoning about tissue specificity. Suppose two endothelial surfaces display equal amounts of the same mucin-like carrier protein but only one can synthesize the sulfated glycan determinant recognized by L-selectin. A lymphocyte expressing functional L-selectin is expected to attach preferentially to the surface bearing the compatible glycan. The specificity is created by differential processing of a carrier, not necessarily by differential expression of a unique carrier gene. This distinction connects adhesion biology to the glycosylation mechanisms learned earlier in the curriculum.

Another exam trap is to interpret the abbreviations E, P and L as complete expression maps. P-selectin is not restricted to platelets, E-selectin is predominantly associated with endothelium, and L-selectin is characteristically expressed by leukocytes, not by every cell in the body. Even within a cell class, activation state and differentiation can change expression. A family comparison should use representative distribution, regulation and binding properties rather than rigid statements about every individual cell.

Together the three selectins demonstrate three complementary strategies for creating a selective encounter: storage and rapid surface mobilization, induction of endothelial expression, and receptor expression on the mobile cell with tissue-specific ligand presentation. These strategies can overlap within a biological system. A leukocyte may express L-selectin while simultaneously carrying ligands for endothelial E-selectin and P-selectin. The same cell can thus present a receptor in one contact and a counter-receptor in another.

8. From a molecular tether to rolling under shear

Blood flow imposes a mechanical requirement that is largely absent from static adhesion assays. The velocity of fluid in a simple vessel is lower near the wall than near its centre because of the interaction of the fluid with the boundary. A cell close to the wall experiences a velocity difference across its diameter. The resulting hydrodynamic forces and torque tend to translate and rotate the cell. Adhesive bonds opposing this motion are mechanically loaded.

Shear rate describes the gradient in fluid velocity perpendicular to the direction of flow. Its unit is reciprocal seconds. Shear stress is the tangential force per unit area arising from that gradient. For a Newtonian fluid, the wall shear stress is the dynamic viscosity multiplied by the wall shear rate. The viscosity measures resistance to deformation during flow. Blood is a suspension of cells and is not an ideal Newtonian fluid in all conditions; nonetheless the simple relationship is useful in a defined flow-chamber experiment using a Newtonian medium.

A leukocyte tether begins when a receptor and compatible ligand meet within a region of sufficient proximity and bind for long enough to oppose the fluid-driven movement. The initial tether need not stop all translational motion. The cell may pivot, deform or extend a membrane structure while the bond bears load. New contacts can form closer to the leading part of the region facing the surface as the cell continues moving. Older contacts are loaded and eventually break. The sequence of bond formation, extension and rupture results in rolling.

A useful diagram of this process shows the direction of fluid motion parallel to the vessel wall, the leukocyte body above it and at least one molecular tether connecting the two surfaces. The position of the adhesive contact changes relative to the rotating cell. An arrow indicating the fluid force is not itself a molecular bond; similarly, an arrow representing rolling direction is not evidence that the cell is actively crawling. The reader should be able to identify the separate roles of fluid movement and receptor-ligand association.

The rolling cell need not behave as a rigid spherical wheel. The membrane, cytoskeleton and surface projections can deform. A membrane tether can extend from the leukocyte as a loaded contact is pulled away from the main cell body. This extension changes the geometry of force transmission and can help sustain adhesion over distances that a rigid contact would not permit. The amount of deformation depends on cell properties and the experimental situation. A schematic circular cell is therefore a teaching representation of the overall sequence, not a complete mechanical model.

The number of bonds present at any instant is not necessarily constant. An initial tether may involve only a small number of molecular associations, followed by formation of further bonds. Some loaded interactions break while others are established. If the rate at which new contacts become load-bearing is too low relative to the rate of rupture, the cell detaches. If contacts are replenished effectively, it can maintain rolling over a longer surface. This dynamic balance explains how reversible microscopic bonds can produce persistent macroscopic adhesion.

Receptor abundance affects the encounter process in a manner that is distinct from intrinsic bond affinity. A higher surface density of compatible receptors makes it more likely that a passing ligand will find a receptor within reach. It may also permit more simultaneous bonds and distribute the imposed load among them. However, density does not automatically change the chemical dissociation constant of one isolated receptor-ligand pair. A cell can exhibit stronger apparent adhesion because it forms multiple bonds without changing the chemistry of any individual bond.

The same reasoning applies to the distribution of ligand carriers. A leukocyte displaying the proper glycan at an accessible surface projection may establish contacts more efficiently than a leukocyte displaying the same number of motifs in a recessed membrane region. Surface glycan density, receptor accessibility and cell mechanics all influence the encounter rate. A measured reduction in rolling after altering the cytoskeleton could therefore arise without changing receptor transcription or carbohydrate chemistry.

Rolling is conveniently quantified by tracking cell trajectories in time-lapse images. One may measure the fraction of incoming cells captured, the velocity of captured cells, the length of the rolling path, or the fraction that remain attached after a defined interval. These are different variables. A treatment may reduce the number of captured cells but have little effect on the speed of the few that do roll. Conversely, it may preserve capture while increasing rolling velocity. An experiment that reports only the total number of cells attached at the end cannot distinguish these outcomes.

The measurement of rolling velocity must also be interpreted relative to the local flow. A cell moving more slowly than an unbound cell in the same near-wall region may be undergoing repeated adhesion. But a slow absolute velocity alone is not proof of adhesion because the surrounding fluid may also be moving slowly. Tracking nonadhesive control particles or cells under matched conditions provides a reference. A stationary cell, by contrast, can be operationally classified as arrested if its displacement remains below a specified threshold during an observation window.

An increase in shear stress can have several effects at once. It can alter encounter frequency, load existing bonds more strongly, promote cell deformation and change the time a ligand spends within binding distance. These competing effects mean that the relation between shear and rolling is not necessarily monotonic. The molecular response of particular selectin bonds to force, discussed in the next section, supplies one explanation for why a modest increase in force may sometimes improve rather than weaken rolling adhesion.

The outcome of a rolling experiment should finally be connected back to the biological objective. Maintaining a cell close to the vascular surface provides time and spatial access for the cell to encounter other molecules. The purpose of rolling is not simply to reduce velocity. It allows the cell to sample a particular local environment without making every initial encounter an irreversible commitment. The selective use of reversible adhesion is thus a form of information processing at the scale of moving cells.

9. Quantitative reasoning: affinity, lifetime, flow and mechanical load

A receptor-ligand association can be described chemically before considering the more complicated geometry of a rolling cell. Let R represent a free receptor, L a free ligand and RL the associated complex. For a simple reversible one-to-one interaction, association occurs at a rate proportional to the concentrations of free R and free L; dissociation occurs at a rate proportional to the concentration of RL. At equilibrium, the rates of formation and disappearance of the complex are equal. This dynamic equilibrium does not imply that individual complexes remain permanently bound.

The association rate constant, conventionally written k_on, has units of inverse molar concentration per second for a simple bimolecular reaction in solution. The dissociation rate constant k_off has units of inverse seconds. Equating the association and dissociation rates gives k_on[R][L] = k_off[RL]. Rearrangement defines the equilibrium dissociation constant K_D = [R][L]/[RL] = k_off/k_on. Its units are concentration. Under the assumptions of this simple equilibrium model, a smaller K_D indicates higher affinity.

If the ligand concentration is maintained in excess so that binding does not appreciably deplete it, the fraction of receptor occupied is the concentration of RL divided by the total receptor concentration, [R] + [RL]. Substitution of the equilibrium relationship gives the fractional occupancy θ = [L]/(K_D + [L]). At a free ligand concentration equal to K_D, half the available receptors are occupied. This result is useful for an equilibrium assay, but a membrane-bound ligand on a flowing cell is not a uniform three-dimensional ligand solution. Applying the formula directly to receptor number per square micrometre would mix different physical descriptions.

Equal equilibrium affinity does not imply equal association and dissociation kinetics. Imagine a hypothetical receptor A with an association rate constant of one hundred thousand per molar per second and a dissociation rate constant of one per second. Receptor B has an association rate constant of ten thousand per molar per second and a dissociation rate constant of one tenth per second. Both have a K_D of ten micromolar. Yet the first complex forms and breaks ten times faster than the second at the same free ligand concentration. In a very brief collision, these receptors need not have the same probability of capturing a ligand.

For a simple bound state whose dissociation probability per unit time remains constant, the fraction of initially formed complexes surviving at time t obeys dS/dt = −k_off S. Integrating gives S(t) = exp(−k_off t) when S(0) equals one. The mean bound lifetime is the integral of this survival function over all positive times, equal to 1/k_off. The half-life is ln(2)/k_off. These quantities are not identical: the mean lifetime of an exponential distribution exceeds the half-life. The model assumes independent complexes with a constant dissociation rate and no rebinding.

For example, a hypothetical bond with k_off = 2 per second has a mean lifetime of 0.5 seconds and a half-life of approximately 0.347 seconds. After 0.5 seconds, exp(−1), or approximately 36.8 percent, of the original bound population remains under the stated assumptions. This calculation describes individual complexes, not the fraction of rolling cells still attached, because a rolling cell can form new bonds while its earlier bonds disappear. Rebinding and multivalent contacts invalidate the direct identification of cell retention with single-bond survival.

If a ligand experiences brief, independent opportunities to bind during a collision, an elementary probability model can illuminate the effect of encounter duration. Suppose the effective rate of first productive bond formation during contact is a constant λ per second. The probability of no bond after time t is exp(−λt), so the probability of at least one bond is 1 − exp(−λt). For λ = 5 per second, a contact lasting 0.02 seconds gives a probability of about 0.0952, while a contact lasting 0.2 seconds gives about 0.632. The same molecular system becomes much more effective when the cell remains within binding distance longer.

This first-event model treats all eligible encounters as equivalent and assumes a constant effective rate. Actual cell contacts contain multiple receptors and ligands, moving membranes and changing geometries. The parameter λ is therefore not automatically the solution association rate constant. It summarizes opportunity and molecular recognition within a specified experimental context. An increase in accessible surface density could raise λ even without changing the chemistry of the binding site. Such a model helps interpret why rapid rolling can reduce the chance of subsequent firm adhesion.

The relationship between binding affinity and binding free energy is also important. For a standard-state convention with concentration C° equal to one molar, the standard free energy of association is ΔG° = RT ln(K_D/C°). The argument of a logarithm must be dimensionless. At a fixed temperature, a smaller K_D gives a more negative standard association free energy. This relationship describes equilibrium thermodynamics under the model assumptions. A loaded adhesive contact continuously driven by flowing fluid is not at equilibrium, so mechanical lifetime cannot in general be inferred from ΔG° alone.

Avidity is the effective strength or stability of an association involving multiple binding contacts. Even when each individual bond is relatively short-lived, a cell can remain attached if other bonds continue to bear load and new bonds form as old ones rupture. In a simplified population of three independent, preformed bonds with survival probability 0.4 after a specified time, the probability that at least one remains is one minus the probability that all three have broken, or 1 − 0.6³ = 0.784. Actual loaded bonds are not necessarily independent because loss of one may redistribute force to the others. The calculation demonstrates the principle without claiming to predict a living cell exactly.

Wall shear stress can also be calculated in a simple physical system. In a Newtonian flow medium with dynamic viscosity 0.001 pascal seconds and a wall velocity gradient of 200 reciprocal seconds, the shear stress is 0.2 pascals. A pascal is one newton per square metre, and one pascal equals ten dynes per square centimetre, so the result is also two dynes per square centimetre. Shear stress should not be confused with the force on a single molecular bond, which is measured in newtons or piconewtons and depends on geometry and force sharing.

For a shallow rectangular flow channel, a useful approximation is τ = 6μQ/(wh²), where μ is dynamic viscosity, Q volumetric flow rate, w channel width and h channel height. It assumes steady fully developed laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid between broad parallel boundaries, with width much larger than height. The relation follows by integrating the parabolic velocity profile across the gap. When pressure drop drives flow, the velocity curvature is constant and the velocity is zero at both walls; the integrated flow rate fixes the curvature. The wall gradient is six times the mean speed divided by the gap height, giving the stated relation.

Consider a channel one millimetre wide and one hundred micrometres high, with viscosity 0.001 pascal seconds and flow of twenty microlitres per minute. In SI units the flow is 3.333 × 10⁻¹⁰ cubic metres per second, the width is 0.001 metres and the height is 0.0001 metres. Substitution gives 0.2 pascals. If the channel height is halved while the other quantities remain fixed, the approximation predicts a fourfold increase in wall shear stress, to 0.8 pascals. This illustrates why comparing pump settings alone is insufficient when comparing adhesion across chambers of different geometry.

A rolling velocity can be calculated from measured displacement divided by elapsed time. If a cell moves thirty micrometres along the vessel wall during six seconds, its average rolling velocity is five micrometres per second. A control cell travelling freely at one hundred micrometres per second moves twenty times faster under those specified conditions. Such ratios are descriptive rather than universal biological constants. They become interpretable only when the compared cells experience similar local flow and when the method reliably distinguishes rolling from pauses, detachment and free transit.

Together these quantitative relationships separate at least four measurable properties: equilibrium occupancy, association opportunity, individual-bond lifetime and macroscopic cell behaviour. An examination problem may deliberately provide information about one property and ask whether a claim about another follows. A smaller dissociation constant alone does not prove faster capture; a long-lived individual bond alone does not prove a higher rolling fraction; a reduction in rolling velocity alone does not prove stronger equilibrium affinity. The assumptions behind each equation determine which inference is justified.

10. Catch bonds, slip bonds and the limits of a simple adhesion model

Mechanical loading changes the energy landscape of an adhesive interaction. In the simplest slip-bond picture, an applied tensile force tends to favour dissociation by reducing the effective barrier to separation along a relevant reaction coordinate. A common simplified expression is k_off(F) = k₀ exp(Fx_b/k_BT), where k₀ is the unloaded dissociation rate, F is tensile force, x_b is an effective distance to the transition state, k_B is Boltzmann's constant and T is absolute temperature. The exponent is dimensionless because both Fx_b and k_BT have units of energy.

For a positive x_b in this model, raising force raises the dissociation rate and lowers the mean lifetime. If the exponent increases by ln(2), the dissociation rate doubles and the mean lifetime halves. The model is useful because it makes a precise qualitative prediction, but it assumes a simple force-dependent dissociation pathway. It is not a complete description of a selectin interaction that can occupy different conformational states or dissociate through more than one pathway.

A catch bond is defined operationally by an increase in lifetime with increasing tensile force over a specified range. This does not mean that the bond becomes permanent or that it is impossible to break. Many catch-bond systems eventually show a transition to slip-like behaviour at higher load, so the lifetime-versus-force curve can rise to a maximum and then fall. The definition concerns lifetime under force; it does not require a particular change in equilibrium affinity measured without force.

One intuitive mechanism involves a force-dependent rearrangement of the bound complex. If modest tensile force favours a configuration that dissociates less readily, the mean lifetime can increase even though force would eventually promote separation at higher values. For selectins, the relative orientation of the lectin and EGF-like domains is an important part of mechanistic models. The structural comparison of different conformations supports the idea that the binding interface and its adjacent domain arrangement are coupled. It would be an overstatement to treat this illustrative mechanism as a complete universal description of all selectin-ligand pairs.

The 2003 work of Marshall and colleagues supplied direct force-dependent measurements of P-selectin binding to PSGL-1. Their results showed a range in which increased force lengthened the observed bond lifetime followed by a range in which greater force shortened it. These results challenged the assumption that every adhesive bond behaves as a simple slip bond across all relevant forces. The experimental finding refers to the measured molecular interaction and conditions. Other selectins and ligands can exhibit different force-response properties.

A hypothetical force experiment might report average lifetimes of 0.2, 0.6, 0.9 and 0.3 seconds at increasing tensile forces of 5, 15, 25 and 45 piconewtons. These illustrative numbers show catch-like behaviour between the first three points and slip-like behaviour between the last two. The highest measured lifetime occurs at 25 piconewtons, but the exact maximum might lie between sampled forces. Without additional measurements one should not claim that 25 piconewtons is precisely the optimal force.

There are important experimental qualifications. A force-measuring probe may form more than one bond, and multiple bonds can increase apparent lifetime. A changing loading rate may also influence rupture measurements differently from a constant-force experiment. Single-bond interpretations require conditions that minimize or identify multiple simultaneous contacts and a clearly specified measurement protocol. The lifetime of a complex held under constant force is distinct from the rupture force measured during steadily increasing force.

A flow-chamber observation alone is insufficient to demonstrate a molecular catch bond. Rolling can become more effective at moderate shear because of improved molecular encounter, changes in cell deformation or more favourable contact geometry. A nonmonotonic whole-cell response is consistent with catch-bond behaviour, but multiple mechanisms can create the same shape. A direct single-bond force measurement or a suitably controlled combination of assays provides stronger evidence about the molecular cause.

Conversely, a molecular catch bond does not guarantee that whole-cell rolling will improve indefinitely as shear increases. Fluid force can reduce contact time, detach cells or exceed the range in which the bond exhibits catch-like behaviour. The geometry of a cell also means that the same wall shear stress does not always impose the same force on each bond. An increase in bond number can redistribute load, and membrane extensions can alter the lever arm. The molecular force-response curve is one component of the whole-cell mechanical system.

The biological significance of these properties is that adhesion must remain effective under a range of physical conditions while still allowing movement and eventual detachment. A molecular system that can withstand modest load through regulated lifetime may be better suited to rolling than a system that simply loses all binding as soon as force appears. However, the functional value depends on association kinetics, receptor density, the presence of other ligands and the cell's capacity to respond to local signals. Mechanical adaptability and chemical specificity work together.

11. The immunoglobulin superfamily: an extracellular recognition scaffold

The immunoglobulin superfamily, often abbreviated IgSF, is a large group of proteins sharing domains related to those first characterized in immunoglobulins. An immunoglobulin-like domain is a folded protein module, typically built from two opposing beta sheets forming a beta sandwich. Many such domains are stabilized by an intramolecular disulfide bond. The common fold can support a wide variety of molecular interactions because the sequences and exposed surfaces can differ substantially among family members.

The name does not imply that every member of this superfamily is an antibody. An antibody is a particular kind of immunoglobulin with specialized roles in antigen recognition and immune defence. Many IgSF proteins are membrane adhesion molecules or receptors with functions unrelated to antibody secretion. They do not necessarily possess antibody heavy and light chains, antigen-binding variable regions arranged like an antibody, or the ability to undergo the genetic rearrangements that generate antibody diversity.

An Ig-like adhesion molecule commonly displays a series of extracellular domains in an extended arrangement. Such an arrangement can project the binding surface away from the membrane while providing opportunities for interaction with partners on another cell. The number of domains and the location of the binding interface differ among members. Some proteins have additional extracellular motifs, and some family members exist in alternative isoforms with different membrane anchoring or cytoplasmic properties. The shared fold is a structural classification, not a specification of one universal binding mechanism.

Protein-protein recognition by an Ig-like domain can involve hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals contacts and contributions from hydrophobic surfaces. Specific amino acid side chains and the arrangement of loops may determine partner recognition. Glycosylation can influence protein folding, accessibility and interactions, but the presence of glycosylation does not transform an Ig-like domain into a selectin carbohydrate-recognition domain. A glycoprotein classification and a structural domain classification answer different questions.

The textbook distinction between cadherins, selectins and Ig-superfamily CAMs often emphasizes calcium dependence. Many classical Ig-family adhesive interactions are independent of extracellular calcium, whereas classical cadherins and selectins require calcium for characteristic aspects of their function. This is a useful family-level contrast. It must not be converted into a universal rule that every adhesion involving an Ig-like molecule is unaffected by divalent cations, because the binding partner can impose its own cation requirements.

Neural cell adhesion molecule, NCAM, provides an example of Ig-superfamily adhesion outside the vascular system. NCAM is expressed in neural and other tissues, and its extracellular region commonly contains five Ig-like domains followed by two fibronectin type III domains. It can participate in homophilic association, in which one NCAM molecule binds another NCAM molecule on an opposing surface. Different isoforms can differ in cytoplasmic regions or membrane anchoring, so it is not correct to describe every NCAM molecule as having an identical long cytoplasmic tail.

Homophilic adhesion should be distinguished from homotypic adhesion. The former describes the identities of molecular partners, whereas the latter describes the identities of the cells. Two cells of different types can in principle express the same adhesion molecule and interact homophilically at the molecular level. Conversely, two cells of the same type may express different compatible molecules and interact heterophilically at the molecular level. The terms are not synonyms, and distinguishing them allows a student to interpret a binding diagram accurately.

Some Ig-family CAMs mediate heterophilic binding to an entirely different protein family. ICAM-1 binding to leukocyte LFA-1 is a central example. ICAM-1 is an IgSF protein, whereas LFA-1 is an integrin composed of two subunits. Their interaction does not imply that both molecules belong to the same structural family. The identity of the receptor on one surface cannot be inferred merely by knowing the structural classification of its binding partner.

NCAM also illustrates the role of glycan modification in controlling surface interactions. Polysialic acid is a polymer of sialic acid residues that can be attached to NCAM. Its bulky and highly hydrated character can reduce close membrane association and modulate adhesive interactions during development. This is different from the short specific glycan determinant recognized by selectins. In one context carbohydrate modification contributes to a direct recognition site; in another, a large hydrated polymer changes the physical environment of an adhesive interface.

The Ig-superfamily fold is evolutionarily useful because a relatively stable structural scaffold can support diversification of external binding surfaces. The existence of related domains in many animal proteins does not imply that every organism possesses the same mammalian selectin-dependent vascular recruitment mechanism. Plants and bacteria face different problems of adhesion, and animals without the same circulatory organization use different cellular arrangements. Comparative reasoning should preserve the distinction between the broad principle of molecular recognition and the specific vertebrate examples considered here.

An examination question may show three membrane proteins with distinct extracellular architectures: a lectin-like head and repeated stalk, serial Ig-like beta-sandwich modules, and a paired alpha and beta subunit arrangement. These correspond respectively to the characteristic selectin, IgSF CAM and integrin organizations. The diagram does not reveal every ligand or signaling property of the proteins. It provides structural evidence for classification, which must then be combined with information about binding partners and biological context.

12. ICAM and VCAM: connecting recognition to stronger cell association

Intercellular adhesion molecule 1, ICAM-1, is a prominent member of the immunoglobulin superfamily. It is also called CD54. The common human form is a membrane glycoprotein displaying five extracellular Ig-like domains, followed by a single transmembrane segment and a cytoplasmic tail. It can be expressed by endothelial cells and a variety of other cell types. Its surface abundance can increase during inflammatory activation, creating additional opportunities for interaction with leukocytes.

The principal textbook leukocyte binding partners of ICAM-1 are LFA-1 and Mac-1. LFA-1 is the integrin αLβ2, also designated CD11a/CD18. Mac-1 is the integrin αMβ2, or CD11b/CD18. The two share the beta-two subunit but have different alpha subunits and binding properties. This distinction becomes important in genetic experiments: a mutation in CD18 can impair multiple beta-two integrins, whereas a mutation restricted to CD11a primarily affects LFA-1. One should not equate a shared subunit defect with a mutation in only one alpha chain.

ICAM-1 presents different extracellular regions to its partners. LFA-1 recognition involves the amino-terminal Ig-like domain, whereas Mac-1 recognizes a distinct region involving another extracellular domain. A mutation can therefore affect one interaction preferentially without eliminating all ICAM-1-dependent binding. The exact residues are not required for the curriculum, but the domain-specific logic is important. Loss of a single extracellular domain need not have the same consequences as complete loss of the protein from the cell surface.

ICAM-2 is a related IgSF adhesion molecule that can also bind LFA-1 and contribute to endothelial and leukocyte interactions. It is often discussed in the context of more constitutive surface expression compared with strongly inducible ICAM-1, though expression depends on cell type and environment. The presence of several ICAM family members provides opportunities for overlapping function. A blocking experiment directed only against ICAM-1 may leave some LFA-1-mediated interactions intact if a compatible alternative ligand is present.

Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, VCAM-1, is also an IgSF membrane protein. It is designated CD106 and is often induced on activated endothelial cells. Its common full-length human isoform contains seven extracellular Ig-like domains; alternative forms can contain fewer. Like ICAM-1, it can provide an extracellular binding surface for leukocyte integrins, but the characteristic partner differs. VCAM-1 binds the integrin α4β1, commonly known as VLA-4, and can also interact with α4β7 in relevant contexts.

The labels VLA-4 and VCAM-1 can be confused because both occur in the same adhesive pair and both contain a numeral. VLA-4 is the integrin, while VCAM-1 is the Ig-superfamily CAM. They are not different names for one molecule. In the standard endothelial interaction, VLA-4 is on the leukocyte and VCAM-1 is on the endothelial cell. Drawing the two membrane surfaces and writing the molecule names on their respective sides is an effective way to avoid this confusion.

The choice between ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 interactions depends partly on the integrin repertoire of the leukocyte. A cell expressing functional alpha-four beta-one integrin can interact with VCAM-1, whereas one lacking that integrin cannot be assumed to use the same route simply because it expresses LFA-1. Conversely, a leukocyte with functional LFA-1 can bind ICAM-1 under appropriate conditions. The endothelial state matters equally: receptor activation on the leukocyte cannot create an absent ligand on the vascular surface.

These adhesive pairs are not uniformly strong under every condition. Integrin ligand-binding competence is regulated, and multiple interactions can increase effective adhesion. A leukocyte may display an integrin before contact but have a relatively low capacity for firm binding until local signals modify its adhesive state. This explains why measuring integrin abundance alone is insufficient to predict arrest. The detailed mechanisms of integrin conformational activation and cytoskeletal linkage belong to Node 4.B.05; here the essential point is that the binding partner must be functionally competent as well as present.

Although Ig-family adhesion is often described as calcium independent, the integrin side of an ICAM-1 or VCAM-1 interaction has its own divalent-cation dependence. Magnesium and other cations can influence integrin-ligand recognition, and experimental use of manganese can promote certain integrin binding states. It follows that removal of divalent cations can reduce the interaction even though the IgSF molecule is not itself using a selectin-like calcium-binding lectin mechanism. The cation requirement of the pair must be assigned to the correct molecular component.

IgSF molecules can participate in cellular signaling as well as physical adhesion. Engagement and clustering of endothelial ICAM-1 can influence the local cellular interface and cytoskeletal organization. An adhesion molecule need not contain a catalytic enzyme domain to participate in signaling: association with other membrane or cytoplasmic proteins can transmit information. Conversely, the observation that a surface protein participates in signaling does not establish that its intrinsic enzymatic activity is responsible. The distinction between direct catalytic action and recruitment of signaling machinery is a general principle of receptor biology.

Consider a hypothetical endothelial culture in which ICAM-1 expression is eliminated but VCAM-1 remains intact. A leukocyte population whose firm attachment depends predominantly on LFA-1 may show reduced arrest, while another population with functional VLA-4 may retain a route to adhesion. If both populations roll on preserved selectin ligands, the difference in firm adhesion arises downstream of initial capture. The specific outcome depends on alternative ICAM family members and other adhesive mechanisms, which must be controlled before drawing an absolute conclusion.

The ICAM and VCAM examples demonstrate heterophilic recognition between structurally distinct protein families. Together with NCAM, they also demonstrate why the Ig superfamily cannot be assigned a single adhesive timescale or partner type. Some members mediate homophilic tissue contacts, some recognize integrins and some contribute to interfaces with signaling functions. Structural family, binding chemistry, partner identity and whole-cell behaviour are complementary descriptions rather than interchangeable labels.

13. Integrating the adhesive sequence from capture to passage

The selectins and Ig-superfamily molecules can now be placed in a single physiological framework. In a representative postcapillary venule, the endothelial surface separates circulating blood from the surrounding tissue. A local change in tissue conditions can cause nearby endothelial cells to alter the molecules exposed to the blood. This local regulation is essential because recruitment should preferentially occur where the appropriate signals are present rather than throughout the entire vascular system.

The delivery of a leukocyte to the near-wall region precedes molecular recognition. Movement within blood and interactions with other blood cells influence the probability that a leukocyte encounters the endothelium. The redistribution of leukocytes toward the vessel periphery is often called margination. It is a physical contribution to contact opportunity, not another class of adhesion receptor. A cell can fail to reach the wall despite possessing functional receptors, and changes in flow conditions can alter recruitment before any molecular bond forms.

Capture occurs when compatible molecules on the two surfaces interact during a sufficiently close encounter. In the standard model, selectin recognition of properly modified glycans helps establish the first tether. The loaded contact slows the cell relative to freely moving cells. Successive contacts then permit rolling. These steps expose the cell repeatedly to a restricted endothelial surface and prolong the time during which it can receive local signals.

Chemokines are soluble signaling proteins that can influence cell migration and adhesive behaviour. Some chemokines are retained or presented on endothelial surfaces through associations with extracellular structures, making the signals accessible to cells close to the wall despite blood flow. A rolling leukocyte encountering a compatible chemokine can respond through its chemokine receptors. Such signals may increase the binding competence of leukocyte integrins. Selectin engagement itself can contribute to signaling and to integrin-dependent slow rolling in certain contexts. The signal and the adhesive contact therefore cooperate rather than operate as completely isolated events.

The next functional transition is arrest. Activated leukocyte integrins interact with compatible endothelial IgSF partners, such as LFA-1 with ICAM-1 or VLA-4 with VCAM-1. A larger or more stable collection of contacts can resist hydrodynamic forces and maintain the leukocyte close to one position. An arrested cell is not necessarily permanently fixed, and subsequent movement on the surface may occur. Firm adhesion means resistance to displacement under defined conditions, not the formation of a new permanent tissue junction.

Some arrested leukocytes crawl along the endothelial surface before crossing the endothelial barrier. Crawling involves active cell movement and dynamic adhesive interactions, unlike the largely flow-driven rolling behaviour. The direction of crawling need not coincide with the direction of blood flow. The molecular requirements vary among leukocyte populations; in some settings Mac-1 interactions with ICAM-1 contribute substantially. This brief overview serves to distinguish the two forms of movement without duplicating the cell motility and immunology chapters.

Passage across the endothelial barrier is called transendothelial migration or diapedesis. The route can be between adjacent endothelial cells or, in some settings, through an endothelial cell. Ig-superfamily proteins such as PECAM-1, also known as CD31, can participate at endothelial junctions. Other adhesive and regulatory proteins contribute as well. Diapedesis requires rearrangements beyond the initial selectin bond and is not automatically accomplished when a leukocyte stops. For this chapter it is sufficient to recognize that arrest and barrier passage are experimentally distinguishable outcomes.

The sequence is often represented as a cascade: encounter, tethering, rolling, activation, arrest, crawling and passage. The arrows should be interpreted as typical functional progression, not as a universal irreversible sequence in which one molecular family disappears completely before the next is engaged. Selectin and integrin interactions can overlap, and alpha-four integrins can contribute to initial rolling on suitable ligands in some biological contexts. The cascade is a model for organizing the dominant events and for locating perturbations.

A conditional probability framework helps explain why an early defect can produce a large reduction in a later measurement. Suppose that, of the cells approaching a defined endothelial surface, a fraction p is captured; of those captured, a fraction q arrests; and of those arrested, a fraction s crosses the barrier during a fixed observation interval. If these are sequential conditional fractions measured in the same cohort, the overall crossing fraction is pqs. For hypothetical values 0.6, 0.5 and 0.4, the result is 0.12, or twelve percent. A reduction in p from 0.6 to 0.2, with the conditional later fractions unchanged, reduces overall crossing to four percent.

These conditional quantities must be distinguished from independently measured marginal fractions. If an experiment reports arrest as a percentage of all incoming cells, that percentage already includes the consequences of capture and should not be multiplied by the capture fraction again. A useful habit is to identify the denominator of each measurement before performing a calculation. Similarly, the relationship above assumes a specified cohort and observation interval; repeated encounters, reverse transitions and cells entering by alternative paths require a more complex model.

This framework clarifies why an adhesion defect may indirectly impair later biological outcomes without directly affecting the molecular machinery of those later steps. A cell that seldom reaches the endothelial surface has fewer opportunities to engage ICAM-1 even if its LFA-1 is fully functional. An assay that forces cells into contact with an ICAM-1-coated surface may reveal intact firm adhesion that could not be observed during normal flowing encounters. The distinction between the capacity to perform a step and the probability of reaching that step is central to analytical reasoning.

14. Experimental designs that separate recognition, expression and function

A static adhesion assay places cells on a surface under conditions allowing sustained contact, then measures cells retained after a standardized wash. Such an assay can test whether a particular molecular pair contributes to retention after contact has been established. It is relatively straightforward to compare surfaces bearing different ligands or cells with different receptors. However, the prolonged opportunity for contact may obscure a defect that is important only during brief encounters under flow.

A controlled flow chamber instead exposes a surface to a defined moving suspension. The surface can consist of cultured endothelial cells or a simplified substrate bearing specified adhesive molecules. Imaging allows an investigator to identify capture events, rolling trajectories and arrest. A reconstituted surface provides better control over molecular composition, whereas an intact endothelial monolayer retains biological features such as signaling, surface topography and regulation. Results from the two systems are complementary rather than interchangeable.

A useful hypothetical experiment compares three defined surfaces: P-selectin alone, ICAM-1 alone and P-selectin together with ICAM-1. The test leukocytes carry functional PSGL-1 and LFA-1. One additionally controls whether the leukocyte integrin has been activated. Under chosen shear conditions, the selectin surface may support rolling, while the ICAM-1 surface alone may capture relatively few freely moving cells. On the combined surface, capture can be followed by arrest if the integrin is competent. The result would support a division of adhesive functions under those conditions. It would not establish that ICAM-1-dependent initial capture is impossible in every setting.

This experiment can be extended with selective blocking antibodies. An antibody blocking P-selectin recognition should reduce initial tethering on the combined surface. An antibody blocking LFA-1 recognition of ICAM-1 may preserve much of the selectin-mediated rolling while reducing the fraction of captured cells that arrest. An irrelevant isotype control tests the nonspecific contribution of adding immunoglobulin. An independent measurement of cell viability and receptor surface abundance helps exclude generalized damage or loss of the relevant cell population.

When interpreting a blocking antibody, the site at which it binds matters. An antibody recognizing a receptor can fail to block its ligand-binding surface. Conversely, an antibody may change receptor distribution or promote clustering rather than simply mask the site. It is desirable to compare results from more than one reagent or a complementary genetic perturbation. A gene deletion removes the protein or its functional product more broadly, whereas a blocking reagent may selectively interfere with a particular interaction. Agreement between independent approaches strengthens the causal conclusion.

A genetic rescue provides another level of evidence. A leukocyte with a defective glycosylation enzyme can be compared with an otherwise similar cell in which a functional copy of the enzyme is restored. If ligand-associated fucosylation and rolling behaviour both recover, the result supports a causal link between the glycosylation defect and the adhesive phenotype. The rescue should be compared with an empty-vector control and, where feasible, with an inactive version of the enzyme. Restoration of cell health alone could otherwise be mistaken for specific restoration of recognition.

Flow cytometry can quantify surface expression using fluorescent probes. Fluorescence intensity depends on antibody binding, instrument settings and the amount of accessible antigen. It is not automatically a direct count of receptors unless appropriate calibration is performed. A probe recognizing the carrier protein and a probe recognizing a functional glycan epitope report different features. In a mutant with normal PSGL-1 staining but low glycan staining, the findings suggest a post-translational defect rather than simple loss of the carrier.

Direct binding of a soluble selectin fragment can provide a functional complement to staining. A calcium-dependent binding signal lost after chelation or selective glycan removal is consistent with lectin-dependent recognition. The assay must include a control for nonspecific adherence of the labeled protein and for the integrity of the cell surface. Binding of a multimeric selectin fusion protein can reflect avidity rather than the affinity of one isolated lectin domain, so conclusions about single-molecule kinetics require additional measurements.

A quantitative comparison can be made using a fixed number of incoming cells. For example, suppose a control chamber receives 500 cells, captures 200 and arrests 100. A mutant chamber receives the same number, captures 50 and arrests 25. The captured fractions are forty and ten percent, while the conditional arrest fraction is fifty percent in both. These teaching data suggest that the main detected defect lies before or at capture. They do not prove that every downstream adhesive process is normal; the surviving captured cells may be a selected subset. Measuring downstream function after equalizing contact opportunity helps address that limitation.

In a different hypothetical experiment, both control and mutant chambers capture 200 of 500 incoming cells. The control arrests 100 and the mutant arrests only 20. The conditional arrest fractions are fifty and ten percent. This pattern instead identifies a defect after capture in the measured system. A selective loss of an integrin-binding interaction is consistent with the data, but the same pattern might result from defective chemokine responsiveness or impaired cell mechanics. Follow-up assays must distinguish those alternatives.

Scientific evidence becomes strongest when methods operating at different levels agree. An altered glycan structure, reduced purified receptor binding, impaired rolling under matched flow conditions and restoration after genetic rescue provide a linked chain of evidence. An isolated reduction in adhesion is less specific. The ability to identify the appropriate next control is more valuable in analytical examinations than memorizing a list of laboratory techniques without understanding what each measures.

15. Perturbations, comparative significance and integration

Inherited adhesion disorders provide natural examples of why the stages of the recruitment model must be separated. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I is associated with defects in ITGB2, which encodes the beta-two integrin subunit CD18. This can reduce the expression or function of several leukocyte integrins, including LFA-1 and Mac-1. Selectin-dependent tethering may still occur, but firm attachment and later migration steps can be impaired. The expected phenotype is not a direct loss of the selectin carbohydrate-binding site.

Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type II involves defective fucosylation associated with mutations in SLC35C1, encoding a GDP-fucose transporter of the Golgi. When the supply of GDP-fucose to the relevant luminal enzymes is compromised, formation of functional selectin-binding glycan determinants can be impaired. This defect acts at a different level from loss of CD18. The leukocyte may express the protein carriers of selectin ligands, yet fail to display the necessary carbohydrate structures. Its ability to establish initial rolling contacts is consequently compromised in relevant settings.

Both defects can reduce delivery of leukocytes to tissues, but their shared outcome does not establish an identical molecular cause. In a flow assay with defined P-selectin and ICAM-1 surfaces, the two defects can be compared by measuring capture and arrest separately. A fucosylation defect is expected to compromise selectin-dependent capture, while a beta-two integrin defect compromises the relevant ICAM-dependent arrest route. Alternative ligands and adhesive pathways can modify these predictions, so a specific experimental design must define the molecules available.

An elevated number of leukocytes in circulating blood can coexist with ineffective delivery to a tissue. This is a compartmental problem: the blood count describes the population within the sampled circulation, while recruitment depends on the transfer of cells between blood and tissue. If a transfer step is impaired, accumulation in one compartment and depletion in another are compatible outcomes. This relationship provides a mechanistic explanation without requiring detailed clinical diagnosis or treatment, which lies outside the syllabus.

Other perturbations can be analyzed without referring to disease. A receptor lacking the extracellular lectin site may fail to recognize a glycan despite normal surface localization. A ligand-carrier mutant may fail to extend its recognition region into an accessible position. An endothelial cell with deficient inducible E-selectin expression may lose a later adhesive response while retaining the capacity to mobilize stored P-selectin. A leukocyte with defective signaling to integrins may retain both receptor abundance and selectin-dependent rolling but fail to develop efficient arrest. The experimental endpoint must be chosen to distinguish the proposed causes.

The physiological importance of reversible recognition extends beyond the immediate vascular illustration. In lymphoid tissues, selective interactions help route cells to appropriate environments. In developing neural tissues, IgSF-mediated adhesion helps organize cell contacts, with the strength and selectivity of those contacts subject to molecular regulation. These two contexts differ in geometry, timescale and function. Their common principle is that cells use defined extracellular recognition surfaces rather than indiscriminate attachment.

Comparing animals with plants and bacteria reinforces the specificity of this mechanism. Plant cells have walls and can remain connected through structures such as plasmodesmata; the mammalian vascular rolling system should not be projected onto them. Bacteria use many surface adhesins to interact with surfaces and other cells, but the existence of an adhesion function does not make every bacterial adhesin a selectin. Functional analogy does not establish structural homology or identical molecular mechanisms. The vertebrate examples in this chapter illustrate a broader problem of selective association under particular physical constraints.

Cell adhesion can be studied or applied using recombinant receptor fragments, glycan probes, engineered expression systems and controlled cellular assays. For instance, a substrate displaying a known glycan can be used to test whether an isolated lectin domain retains functional recognition after mutation. Such an application builds directly on the chemistry of the binding interface. The same experimental concept can be adapted to compare the effect of ligand density or presentation without assuming that an artificial surface perfectly reproduces a natural endothelium.

There is also a conceptual link to the extracellular matrix, the subject of the following chapter. In the current examples, molecules on two opposing cell surfaces provide the major adhesive partners. Cells can also attach to extracellular proteins that are not anchored in another cell's membrane. The physical forces, receptor accessibility and regulation of binding remain relevant, but the nature of the opposing substrate changes. The extracellular matrix provides a structural environment and a source of information, and integrins participate in recognizing both matrix components and selected cell-surface proteins.

The four major CAM families can therefore be compared along independent axes: structure, chemistry of recognition, cellular location, partner identity, cation requirements and functional behaviour. Classical cadherins have characteristic repeated domains and calcium-dependent cell-cell contacts; selectins have carbohydrate-recognition lectin domains and contribute to transient adhesive encounters; IgSF CAMs have Ig-like domains and diverse partner relationships; integrins are alpha-beta heterodimers with regulated adhesion to selected matrix or cell-surface partners. The most defensible answers preserve those independent distinctions instead of forcing every member of a family into one universal role.
