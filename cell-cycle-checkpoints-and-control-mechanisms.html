1. Why order requires an active control system

A dividing cell must accomplish three different tasks without confusing their order. It must decide whether to reproduce, duplicate a complete set of genetic information, and distribute the two copies to separate daughter cells. None of these tasks is guaranteed merely because the preceding task has begun. DNA synthesis can start yet fail to finish. A chromosome can have two sister chromatids yet fail to attach to the mitotic spindle. A cell can receive abundant growth factors while carrying damaged DNA. The control problem is therefore not simply to initiate events but to prevent a dangerous next event when its prerequisites have not been satisfied.

The phase and DNA-content conventions introduced in 2.D.01, the cyclin–CDK control system, and the structure of chromosomes and kinetochores provide the necessary foundation. Their key relationships are recalled here so that the mechanisms can be followed without interrupting the argument.

The cell cycle alternates between interphase and cell division. During G1, a diploid somatic cell normally contains two sets of chromosomes and the DNA amount designated 2C. S phase duplicates DNA, producing sister chromatids held together. G2 follows with 4C DNA and the same chromosome number as G1, counting each pair of joined sisters as one chromosome. During M phase the sisters separate. The distinction matters because a cell with 4C DNA has not necessarily entered mitosis; it may be waiting in G2. Nor does a cell with nearly 4C DNA necessarily possess an intact, fully replicated genome. A few damaged sites are invisible in a measurement of total DNA.

A cell-cycle checkpoint is a regulatory dependency that delays or prevents a transition when a condition is unsuitable. In contrast, a phase is a period defined by the main cellular activities occurring during it. A phase can last its normal duration with no stress-induced delay, or become prolonged because a checkpoint is signaling. The same checkpoint machinery may operate throughout a phase rather than only at a single instant. The term checkpoint does not imply a physical gate on a chromosome, nor a protein that counts every nucleotide. It describes a functional control relationship.

A useful distinction is between the accelerator and the brake. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) are enzymes that phosphorylate specific proteins; cyclin binding and other regulatory changes determine their activity. Their activation drives transitions such as S-phase entry and mitotic entry. Checkpoint pathways typically modify those same drivers or inhibit another transition-promoting system. Thus a cyclin can be abundant but the corresponding CDK can remain inactive. This explains why measuring cyclin abundance alone is insufficient to decide whether a cell has permission to divide.

Most checkpoint pathways contain a sensor or initiating complex that responds to an abnormal molecular structure, signaling proteins that relay or amplify that information, and effectors that change the activity of the cell-cycle machinery. A fourth functional requirement is the ability to recover once the problem is resolved. These are roles, not necessarily four separate proteins. For example, a kinase recruited to damaged chromatin participates in sensing and simultaneously starts a signaling cascade. The important exam task is to follow the sign of each regulatory step until the final driver is identified.

A prerequisite can be enforced by an intrinsic physical dependency or by an active inhibitory mechanism. Consider a fork that cannot synthesize DNA when a nucleotide is absent: the absence of substrate directly prevents polymerization. In contrast, there is no simple chemical law requiring nuclear-envelope breakdown to wait for the last DNA segment to finish replication. A regulatory mechanism imposes that order. Removing it may allow the biochemical events of mitosis to begin even though replication has failed. Checkpoints therefore improve reliability without making failure physically impossible.

2. How experiments reveal a checkpoint

Suppose a population of cells stops dividing after DNA is damaged. Two explanations are initially possible. The damage may have disabled the biochemical machinery of division directly, or a regulatory system may be actively preventing division. The observable delay alone cannot distinguish them. One must change the control mechanism while leaving the original defect present.

Studies of budding yeast provided a particularly powerful way to approach this problem. A genetic mutation can remove a specific regulatory function while retaining many other cellular activities. Hartwell and Weinert's 1989 review of checkpoint experiments brought together evidence that some ordering dependencies disappear when checkpoint genes are defective. Cells lacking an effective damage-dependent delay can enter subsequent stages with lesions still present. The important inference is not that the mutation repairs the damage, but that it removes the regulatory dependency that normally prevents the next event. In 1988 Weinert and Hartwell showed that the budding yeast gene RAD9 was required for normal damage-induced division arrest. Subsequent experiments documented viable rad9 deletion strains with radiation sensitivity and defective arrest. These results distinguish the capacity to divide under ordinary conditions from the capacity to delay after injury; they do not establish that RAD9 alone repairs DNA.

In a teaching version of the experiment, compare wild-type and checkpoint-deficient cells in the absence and presence of a DNA-damaging treatment. The untreated cultures establish whether both strains can ordinarily progress. If both are initially healthy, but after damage the wild type delays while the mutant progresses, the mutation is implicated in the delay. Independent measurements of damage must show that both cultures experienced the insult; otherwise the mutant might simply have received less damage. Restoring a functional copy of the affected gene should restore the response, strengthening the causal inference. Survival and chromosome integrity must be measured separately from progression because a cell that divides prematurely is not necessarily a cell that divides successfully.

Another way to test whether an arrest is caused by active control is to remove the abnormal condition and follow recovery. If the transition resumes after the signal is removed, the initial delay was reversible under those conditions. However, removing the signal experimentally is different from actually correcting the damage. A mutation that suppresses a checkpoint may produce rapid progression but poor survival. An experiment must distinguish these two possibilities rather than assuming that every resumed cell is healthy.

A classic example of such a distinction is the spindle assembly checkpoint. An unattached chromosome is mechanically unable to segregate correctly, but that failure need not prevent other chromatids from separating. The regulatory system makes anaphase wait globally. Experiments discussed later show that an individual unattached kinetochore can generate a signal capable of delaying separation of the entire chromosome set. This is a cellular decision rather than a local mechanical obstruction.

Different organisms have distinct names for their checkpoint components. Budding yeast Rad9 is a checkpoint mediator, whereas human RAD9 is part of a different checkpoint complex; identical-looking gene names do not guarantee identical roles. The main mechanistic examples below refer to mammalian somatic cells unless stated otherwise. The conserved principle is coupling cell-cycle transitions to the condition of the genome and spindle, not universal use of every mammalian protein.

3. The restriction point: deciding whether to enter a proliferative cycle

A newly produced mammalian cell does not inevitably copy its DNA again. Many cells can remain outside active proliferation in a reversible resting state, often called G0 or quiescence. Others undergo differentiation and lose proliferative capacity for long periods. The decision to proceed is important for tissue homeostasis: if every cell that completed mitosis immediately entered S phase again, tissue size would be difficult to regulate.

A mitogen is an extracellular signal that promotes cell proliferation. In many cultured mammalian cells, mitogenic signaling promotes synthesis and accumulation of D-type cyclins. These associate with CDK4 or CDK6. Among the important substrates of these kinases is the retinoblastoma protein, Rb. Rb controls the activity of E2F transcription factors, which regulate genes needed for S phase. In the classical model, active Rb binds and restrains activating E2Fs. Appropriate phosphorylation of Rb reduces its restraint, allowing increased expression of genes involved in DNA synthesis and cyclin E. Cyclin E associates with CDK2; further Rb phosphorylation and E2F-dependent expression reinforce the transition. This positive feedback helps make commitment more robust than a simple proportional response to extracellular signal intensity.

The restriction point is an operationally defined commitment decision. In Arthur Pardee's classical studies of mammalian cell proliferation, cells subjected to unsuitable growth conditions could remain viable in a resting state rather than continue an inappropriate cycle. Experiments on mitogen dependence can distinguish cells that have not yet committed from cells that can complete the ongoing cycle after growth signals are withdrawn. A simple model places this point in G1: withdrawal before commitment prevents timely S-phase entry; withdrawal after commitment permits many cells to continue through the current cycle despite the absence of mitogens. The model does not imply that all cell types commit at exactly the same time or that a committed cell is immune to subsequent DNA damage or replication stress.

It helps to distinguish three related questions. Is the extracellular environment sufficiently supportive to initiate proliferation? Is the intracellular DNA sufficiently intact to start replication? Has replication actually been completed before mitosis? The restriction point primarily concerns the first of these. DNA damage checkpoint pathways can also regulate G1/S entry, often through the same CDKs and Rb system, but the input is different. Consequently, the terms restriction point and G1 DNA-damage checkpoint should not be treated as strict synonyms.

Growth factors, energy supply, nutrient availability and tissue context influence the proliferation decision through several signaling routes. In a normal mammalian cell with little mitogenic stimulation, lower cyclin D activity and inhibitors such as p27 can help maintain Rb-mediated repression and delay S-phase entry. A mitogen cannot provide all the required building blocks by itself. Even a strong mitogenic signal may fail to sustain proliferation during nutrient limitation. Nor is contact inhibition simply a mechanical barrier preventing cells from increasing in number; cell contacts can modify signaling and gene expression.

Consider Rb loss. The normal inhibitory link between Rb and E2F is weakened or absent, so a cell may express S-phase genes under conditions that would otherwise favor arrest. This does not prove that Rb-deficient cells can proliferate without nutrients, repair all damage, or ignore every later checkpoint. The Rb pathway represents one major control layer, not the entire cell cycle. Conversely, a form of Rb that remains bound to E2F despite CDK activity can obstruct S-phase entry even if growth signals are present. Such contrasting perturbations reveal the direction of the regulatory links.

4. DNA damage signals: from abnormal DNA to regulatory kinase activity

DNA is chemically vulnerable despite its stable double-helical structure. Normal metabolism, reactive oxygen species, ultraviolet irradiation, ionizing radiation and certain chemicals can generate lesions. A lesion is a physical or chemical alteration to DNA; a mutation is a persistent change in its sequence. A lesion can be repaired without leaving a mutation, or miscopied and converted into a mutation. Thus preventing an injured cell from entering S phase can reduce the probability that damage becomes a permanent heritable change.

The initiating signal depends on the molecular structure generated by a lesion rather than the everyday name of its cause. A DNA double-strand break is a discontinuity in both strands of a DNA duplex. In mammalian cells, the MRN complex (MRE11, RAD50 and NBS1) participates in recognition and processing of such breaks and promotes activation of ATM, a protein kinase named for the disease ataxia-telangiectasia. ATM phosphorylates multiple substrates, including proteins involved in checkpoint signaling. One important downstream checkpoint kinase is Chk2. These modifications change the activity and interactions of proteins around the damage and elsewhere in the nucleus.

The second major initiating pathway is centered on ATR, a related protein kinase. During replication, exposed single-stranded DNA is normally limited and protected. When DNA synthesis stalls while unwinding or DNA processing continues, longer regions of single-stranded DNA may become exposed. Replication protein A (RPA), a single-stranded DNA-binding protein, coats these regions and helps recruit ATR through its partner ATRIP. Additional regulatory interactions activate ATR at appropriate DNA structures. Activated ATR promotes signaling through Chk1, which can alter replication-origin firing, stabilize stressed forks and restrain CDK activation.

The textbook distinction ATM–Chk2 for double-strand breaks and ATR–Chk1 for replication-associated single-stranded DNA is a valuable starting point, not a rule of absolute exclusivity. A double-strand break can undergo processing that produces single-stranded DNA coated with RPA; this can recruit ATR as well. A stalled replication fork can collapse and produce a break, recruiting ATM. Thus the response can evolve as the structure of the DNA changes. A question describing ultraviolet damage during S phase may primarily test ATR-dependent fork stress, while the same physical exposure can generate other signals depending on cell-cycle phase and subsequent processing.

Another useful molecular marker is phosphorylated histone H2AX, commonly called gamma-H2AX or γH2AX. Damage-associated kinases can phosphorylate H2AX in chromatin near affected sites. The modification helps organize recruitment of response proteins and can be visualized as nuclear foci using antibodies. A focus is an indirect readout of a molecular response, not a direct count of broken DNA ends. Different lesions, replication stress and other conditions can affect the signal. Consequently, a larger number of foci is evidence to interpret alongside the treatment, phase and other measurements, not automatic proof of a specified number of double-strand breaks.

The kinase relay makes a localized problem capable of changing the behavior of the whole cell. The affected DNA structure need not directly touch every CDK molecule. Activated signaling components phosphorylate downstream targets, which can change the stability, location or enzymatic activity of cell-cycle regulators. The result may be rapid inhibition of a driver, a slower transcription-dependent response or both. Understanding these time scales is especially important when interpreting the effect of removing p53 or inhibiting Chk1.

5. G1/S DNA-damage checkpoint: the p53–p21 and Rb–E2F connection

Why is replication of a damaged template dangerous? A DNA polymerase can stall at an abnormal base, incorporate an inappropriate nucleotide opposite it, or require an alternative mechanism to bypass it. If a lesion is present before replication, preventing initiation allows repair while the original sequence information may still be recoverable. The G1 DNA-damage checkpoint therefore reduces the chance of turning lesions into permanent sequence changes and reduces the burden placed on replication forks.

In mammalian cells, p53 is a transcription factor that plays a central role in many damage responses. Under unstressed conditions p53 is often kept at relatively low levels by MDM2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that promotes its turnover. Ubiquitin is a small protein attached to substrates through an enzyme cascade; appropriate ubiquitin chains can target proteins to the proteasome for degradation. This continuous turnover allows the cell to adjust p53 levels rapidly without needing to initiate all p53 synthesis only after damage occurs.

When damage activates ATM/ATR-related signaling, modifications of p53, MDM2 and related regulators can reduce MDM2-mediated p53 degradation and increase p53 transcriptional activity. Stabilized p53 activates several genes, among them CDKN1A, which encodes the CDK inhibitor p21. A CDK inhibitor is not a phosphatase; it can bind cyclin-CDK complexes and reduce their ability to phosphorylate substrates. In the G1 checkpoint context, increased p21 restrains cyclin E–CDK2 and other relevant CDK activity. As a result, phosphorylation of Rb is reduced relative to what would occur during unrestricted progression, and Rb can continue to restrain E2F-dependent transcription.

This is a chain of regulatory effects: damage increases checkpoint signaling, signaling stabilizes or activates p53, p53 induces p21, p21 reduces CDK activity, lower CDK activity favors Rb-dependent repression, and the S-phase transition is delayed. Notice that p53 does not itself directly inhibit CDK2 in this classical pathway. Nor does p21 necessarily inhibit synthesis of cyclin E. A western blot showing unchanged cyclin E protein but elevated p21 and diminished CDK activity would be compatible with checkpoint operation.

Some G1/S responses are faster than p53-dependent transcription. Chk kinases can promote inhibition or degradation of Cdc25A, a phosphatase that normally removes inhibitory phosphate groups from CDKs. Reducing Cdc25A helps keep CDK activity low. A cell with defective p53 may therefore retain some rapid checkpoint signaling even though a sustained p21-dependent G1 arrest is weakened. The precise phenotype depends on the damage, cell type, timing and remaining pathways. This distinction is a frequent source of overgeneralized answers: p53 loss does not abolish all checkpoints.

An instructive genetic test compares wild-type, p53-null and p21-null cells. After damage, an intact p53 pathway should increase p21. In p53-null cells the p53-dependent increase is impaired. In p21-null cells p53 may still accumulate and regulate other genes, but the particular p21-mediated route to CDK inhibition is lost. Introducing p21 in a p53-deficient cell can restore inhibition downstream without restoring all other p53 functions. This logic is called pathway ordering or epistasis: a downstream intervention may bypass the missing upstream signal for a particular measured outcome.

Rb and p53 are both frequently described as tumor suppressors, but they do not have interchangeable roles. Rb is a major brake on proliferation-associated transcription. p53 integrates stress signals and can promote arrest or other fates. In the classical G1 checkpoint pathway, p53 can influence Rb indirectly through p21 and CDKs. A defect in Rb may therefore weaken one consequence of p53 signaling, while leaving p53-dependent expression of other target genes intact.

6. Intra-S checkpoint: protecting replication already in progress

Once a cell enters S phase, stopping all DNA synthesis indefinitely would be a poor solution to every problem. Many replication origins have already fired and nascent DNA chains have been produced. A stalled replication fork is the region where synthesis has slowed or stopped despite a partly completed duplication process. If an unstable fork collapses, damaged or broken DNA may be generated. The intra-S response must therefore coordinate a delay with preservation of the replication machinery itself.

An origin is a site where DNA replication begins, whereas a fork is a moving boundary between replicated and unreplicated DNA. A eukaryotic chromosome contains multiple potential origins. During a normal S phase, a regulated subset initiates and produces forks that move into neighboring DNA. Replication licensing before S phase prepares possible origins for use; subsequent CDK activity helps initiate replication and prevents inappropriate re-licensing. The detailed molecular mechanism belongs to DNA replication, but this distinction is necessary here: a reduction in new origin firing is not equivalent to a reduction in the speed of every existing fork.

Consider a shortage of deoxyribonucleotides, the building blocks of DNA. DNA polymerases may slow because substrate availability is limited. Unwinding and synthesis can become uncoupled, exposing RPA-coated single-stranded DNA. The ATR–Chk1 pathway responds to this structure. It can restrain CDK-dependent initiation at additional origins and help maintain existing replication structures until the shortage is relieved. These actions reduce the risk of creating too many new forks competing for a limited supply of nucleotides and replication proteins.

The response is not simply a complete stop. Some existing forks continue, some slow, some pause, and nearby dormant origins can be used in particular stressed regions. A dormant origin is a licensed origin that is normally not needed but can help complete duplication if neighboring forks fail. Suppression of excessive or late origin firing at the genome level can coexist with local backup initiation where needed. The apparently contradictory observations are resolved by recognizing that regulation acts at different scales.

A rapid inhibitory branch involves Chk1 and Cdc25A. Cdc25A normally contributes to activation of S-phase CDKs by removing inhibitory phosphates. Chk1-dependent phosphorylation can promote Cdc25A degradation, allowing inhibitory phosphorylation of relevant CDKs to persist. Reduced initiation activity slows the creation of new forks. ATR–Chk1 signaling also has functions that protect or stabilize replication intermediates; this cannot be reduced solely to Cdc25A regulation. If checkpoint signaling is disabled during stress, initiation can become excessive, available replication factors may become limiting and fork stability can deteriorate.

A typical experimental perturbation is hydroxyurea, which inhibits ribonucleotide reductase and reduces production of deoxyribonucleotides. Treated cells may accumulate in S phase and incorporate less nucleotide label. A checkpoint-deficient culture may exhibit additional origin firing or elevated DNA damage despite reduced overall DNA synthesis. It would be wrong to infer that absence of arrest guarantees higher replication output. Without adequate substrates, synthesis remains physically constrained even after regulatory brakes are removed. This is another instance of the distinction between intrinsic biochemical requirements and checkpoint-dependent ordering.

A second experimental readout is a short pulse of a labeled thymidine analogue such as EdU. Cells incorporating the label are actively synthesizing DNA during the pulse. In an S-phase population with low EdU incorporation, the combined observation of intermediate DNA content and weak synthesis can suggest replication stress. But EdU alone does not distinguish slow individual forks from fewer active origins, and some repair synthesis may incorporate nucleotide analogues. To separate origin density and fork speed, scientists can analyze individual labeled DNA fibers, which record newly synthesized DNA in stretches. Sequential pulses give information about ongoing versus newly initiated replication, with the interpretation depending on the label order and pulse duration.

The intra-S checkpoint and G2/M checkpoint share an important goal but differ in their immediate outputs. Intra-S signaling manages ongoing replication and origin use; G2/M signaling restrains entry into mitosis. A lesion arising in S phase may activate both as the cycle progresses. The checkpoint does not belong exclusively to one DNA lesion or one kinase, and a cell can experience a prolonged S phase followed by a further G2 delay.

7. G2/M checkpoint: why abundant mitotic cyclin is not permission to enter mitosis

By the end of S phase, DNA content approaches 4C in a diploid somatic cell. Entry into mitosis involves widespread phosphorylation, chromosome condensation, reorganization of microtubules and, in many animal cells, nuclear-envelope breakdown. These events radically alter the cellular environment. If replication has left incompletely duplicated regions or DNA breaks, premature mitotic entry risks broken, bridged or incorrectly inherited chromosomes. The G2/M checkpoint couples mitotic entry to the condition of the replicated genome.

The transition-promoting complex is cyclin B–CDK1, historically associated with maturation-promoting factor (MPF). Cyclin binding is necessary for its activity, but binding does not guarantee that the complex is active. The kinase Wee1 can place an inhibitory phosphate on CDK1; related kinases can contribute to inhibitory phosphorylation. Cdc25 phosphatases remove such inhibitory phosphates. CDK-activating kinase also contributes an activating phosphorylation at a different site. It is therefore possible for CDK1 to carry an activating modification yet remain restrained by an inhibitory one. A question that simply says CDK1 is phosphorylated is underspecified unless the site or effect is given.

In an undamaged cell, initial activation of some cyclin B–CDK1 can promote additional Cdc25 activity and reduce the influence of Wee1, creating positive feedback. Each additional active CDK1 molecule helps shift the balance toward further activation. This regulatory architecture permits a rapid switch-like increase in mitotic CDK activity once the conditions favor entry. The checkpoint must restrain this positive feedback before the transition becomes self-reinforcing.

When unreplicated DNA or damage generates ATR–Chk1 or ATM-associated signaling, the response inhibits appropriate Cdc25 functions and can favor persistence of Wee1-dependent inhibitory phosphorylation. In mammalian textbook examples, checkpoint-dependent phosphorylation of Cdc25C can promote binding to 14-3-3 proteins, which changes its availability and localization; regulation of Cdc25A and other Cdc25 family members also contributes. The particular dominant member varies by system. The common output is reduced capacity to remove inhibitory phosphates from CDK1. Thus cyclin B may accumulate while mitotic entry is delayed because CDK1 remains inactive.

This checkpoint is particularly easy to understand using a two-enzyme balance. Wee1 adds an inhibitory phosphate, and Cdc25 removes it. Increasing the influence of Wee1 or decreasing the influence of Cdc25 favors the inactive form. Inhibiting Wee1 experimentally may allow damaged cells to enter mitosis prematurely. In contrast, inhibiting Cdc25 in an undamaged cell can delay mitosis even without an upstream checkpoint signal. The first manipulation bypasses a brake; the second imposes the brake's downstream state. Neither manipulation by itself establishes that DNA has been repaired.

A sustained G2 delay can also involve transcriptional responses, including p53-dependent targets. The immediate control through inhibitory phosphorylation can occur without waiting for gene transcription. A p53-deficient cell may still show a rapid G2 delay through Chk1 and Cdc25, yet fail to maintain it as effectively as a normal cell under particular conditions. Conversely, a cell can possess p53 but be unable to implement a rapid checkpoint if a relevant upstream signaling function is absent. A mechanistic answer should identify which arm is tested rather than claiming that one protein controls every aspect of the arrest.

Checkpoint recovery occurs after the abnormal DNA structures decline and the signal no longer needs to suppress mitotic entry. Phosphatases and other regulatory mechanisms reverse checkpoint-associated modifications, and the CDK activation balance again shifts toward Cdc25-dependent activation. The distinction between recovery and inappropriate escape is crucial: a cell may enter mitosis despite persistent damage if checkpoint signaling is forcibly removed or attenuated. Such entry does not demonstrate successful repair.

An S-phase cell with nearly 4C DNA may also exhibit checkpoint signaling. Total DNA amount alone cannot establish that the checkpoint activated in G2. Combining DNA content with a replication label and markers of mitotic entry is more informative. A 4C, EdU-negative, phospho-histone H3-low cell is consistent with G2 arrest, whereas a 4C cell with condensed chromosomes and high mitotic markers is more consistent with M phase. Neither combination is an absolute definition across all cell types, but the joint measurements substantially narrow the explanation.

8. The spindle assembly checkpoint: coordinating a whole chromosome set

Replication produces two sister chromatids per chromosome. Their centromeric regions assemble kinetochores, multiprotein structures that link chromosomes to spindle microtubules. During mitosis, a pair of sisters should ultimately attach to opposite spindle poles, a configuration termed bi-orientation. Cohesin holds sister chromatids together until the correct stage. The cell must preserve this linkage until it is ready for anaphase, because cohesin cleavage is effectively irreversible during the ongoing division.

An unattached kinetochore is a strong source of the spindle assembly checkpoint signal. This is especially important during prometaphase, when spindle microtubules are still establishing attachments. In the canonical mammalian mechanism, checkpoint proteins including Mad and Bub family members accumulate at unattached kinetochores. Mps1 kinase helps initiate the signaling process at such sites. Mad2 changes its interactions with Cdc20, and together with BubR1 and Bub3 forms the mitotic checkpoint complex, commonly abbreviated MCC. The MCC restrains Cdc20-dependent activation of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome, APC/C.

APC/C is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. At the appropriate transition, its coactivator Cdc20 promotes recognition and ubiquitination of particular substrates. One such substrate is securin, an inhibitory binding partner of the protease separase. When securin is degraded and other restraints are relieved, separase cleaves the relevant cohesin subunit that holds sister chromatids together. The sisters are now free to move apart. APC/C also promotes cyclin B destruction; declining CDK1 activity supports exit from mitosis. The spindle checkpoint therefore controls both chromosome-separation machinery and the timing of mitotic exit through regulation of APC/C–Cdc20.

The negative signaling chain is instructive. Unattached kinetochore activity increases checkpoint complex formation. The complex inhibits APC/C–Cdc20 action, which preserves securin and cyclin B. Securin restrains separase, and cohesin remains intact. This is why a spindle-damaged cell can have high cyclin B, strong mitotic CDK activity, condensed chromosomes and yet no anaphase. That signature is distinct from a G2 checkpoint, in which cyclin B may also be high but CDK1 activity is restrained and mitotic entry has not occurred.

When kinetochores form appropriate stable attachments, production of the inhibitory signal declines and existing inhibitory complexes are dismantled or turned over. APC/C–Cdc20 can become active, securin is degraded, separase acts, and chromosome segregation proceeds. The spindle checkpoint does not itself pull the chromosomes to opposite poles. It controls the timing of release; spindle mechanics carry out movement. This difference explains why removing the checkpoint in a cell treated with a spindle-disrupting drug does not restore normal segregation.

One unattached kinetochore can delay anaphase despite attachments elsewhere. Rieder and colleagues used live observations of vertebrate somatic cells in 1994 to relate anaphase timing to the persistence of unattached kinetochores. Their 1995 follow-up used targeted laser ablation of kinetochore regions. Removing the source of the inhibitory signal could permit anaphase despite the unresolved attachment situation. The experiment illustrates why the checkpoint cannot be understood merely as chromosomes physically blocking separation. The remaining cell possesses the capacity to initiate anaphase; a local signal can prevent it. These experiments established the existence and location of an inhibitory control, not the entire molecular composition of the subsequently characterized checkpoint complex.

Attachments must also be accurate. In a monotelic attachment, one sister kinetochore is attached and the other is not. In syntelic attachment, both sisters attach toward the same pole. In merotelic attachment, one kinetochore has microtubules connected to both poles. The latter can sometimes escape robust checkpoint detection because the kinetochore is occupied even though the geometry is wrong. Aurora B kinase participates in correction of inappropriate attachments by destabilizing unsuitable microtubule connections, allowing new attachment attempts. This error-correction process works with the checkpoint but is not identical to it. Tension and attachment geometry influence correction and stable occupancy; the checkpoint is not a perfect universal tension meter.

An experiment using nocodazole, which disrupts spindle microtubules, can reveal the importance of the checkpoint. Wild-type cells that reach mitosis frequently remain arrested with unattached kinetochores. If Mad2 function is lost, many cells can initiate inappropriate mitotic exit or anaphase-like events despite the spindle defect. The cells may not form viable daughters. If the separase cleavage target in cohesin is made resistant, however, APC/C activation need not produce normal sister separation even after the checkpoint is satisfied. In the first case the brake is missing; in the second the brake can be released but the mechanical release mechanism fails. Such perturbation comparisons are more informative than memorizing the names Mad2 and BubR1.

9. Recovery, persistent arrest, senescence and apoptosis

A checkpoint-induced delay has a purpose only if it changes the consequences of a problem. For a repairable lesion, the desirable outcome is a temporary reduction of progression, repair, reduction of the damage signal and resumption of proliferation. Repair is the biochemical correction of damaged DNA, whereas checkpoint recovery is restoration of cell-cycle progression following reduction of the inhibitory response. Although they are often coordinated, the processes are not synonymous. Repair pathways can operate while a cell is not arrested, and checkpoint escape can occur while lesions persist.

The p53–MDM2 relationship provides an example of negative feedback within damage signaling. p53 can induce MDM2 expression; MDM2 in turn restrains p53. When activating signals diminish, this feedback contributes to returning p53 activity toward the unstressed state. Other recovery mechanisms reduce phosphorylation of checkpoint proteins and restore normal CDK activation. Persistent damage may maintain signaling or cause repeated activation. The details vary with the kind and persistence of the lesion, so the response is not adequately described by a universal fixed waiting time.

If damage remains extensive or if cellular stress is sustained, a transient arrest may be replaced by a durable proliferation arrest called cellular senescence. Senescent cells remain metabolically active and can change their gene expression and interactions with surrounding tissue. This distinguishes senescence from apoptosis, in which a cell undergoes an organized cell-death process. It also differs from ordinary quiescence, which in many contexts is a reversible state entered in response to environmental or developmental signals. No single marker conclusively identifies every senescent cell: persistent loss of proliferative capacity together with appropriate molecular and cellular indicators gives stronger evidence than DNA content alone.

p53 can activate genes that favor apoptosis as well as those that favor arrest. Which outcome occurs depends on cell type, severity and duration of stress, signaling context and the state of survival machinery. A damaged cell is not inevitably killed because p53 rises, nor does absence of p53 guarantee survival. Other death pathways can act. The detailed mitochondrial and receptor-linked apoptotic mechanisms are treated separately in 2.D.08; here the key principle is that checkpoint signaling can influence cell fate beyond a temporary pause.

Prolonged spindle checkpoint activation presents a related but distinct problem. Cells with persistent attachment defects can remain in mitosis for a long interval, die during mitosis, or undergo mitotic slippage, in which gradual loss of mitotic cyclin activity allows exit despite an unsatisfied checkpoint. The resulting cell may have an abnormal chromosome complement or increased DNA content depending on whether segregation and cytokinesis occurred. Slippage is not evidence of correct spindle attachment; it is an example of the limits of checkpoint enforcement. The ability to stop is not unlimited, and different cells can respond differently to the same duration of arrest.

A checkpoint-deficient cell may initially appear to grow faster in culture because it avoids delays. Over many divisions, however, it may accumulate mutations, rearrangements or chromosome-number abnormalities, impairing genome stability. On the other hand, excessive or persistent inhibitory signaling can compromise tissue renewal. Normal tissue function therefore requires a balance between sufficient proliferation and quality control. The same protective mechanisms can become harmful if a necessary repair is indefinitely delayed or if cells cannot re-enter the cycle after the danger has passed.

10. Putting the four decisions together

The restriction point is chiefly a decision about proliferative commitment, whereas the G1/S damage checkpoint checks whether the genome is suitable to begin duplication. During S phase the intra-S response manages the execution of replication itself. Before mitosis the G2/M response restrains mitotic CDK activation. During mitosis the spindle checkpoint restrains APC/C–Cdc20 until kinetochore signals decline. These controls are coupled to one another but not interchangeable. The same damaged cell can trigger several of them at different times.

A simple way to reason through a perturbation is to identify the next event that is normally prevented. A CDK1 mutant resistant to inhibitory phosphorylation primarily compromises the ability to suppress mitotic entry through that modification. A Cdc20 mutant that escapes MCC inhibition primarily compromises anaphase restraint. Neither directly substitutes for the missing DNA polymerase activity in a replication-stressed cell. If a question combines a checkpoint mutant and an execution-defective mutant, the visible phenotype depends on whether the downstream event is still physically possible.

Consider two cells with abundant cyclin B. The first has damaged DNA, inactive CDK1 and an intact nuclear envelope. The second has condensed chromosomes, active CDK1, retained securin and unattached kinetochores. They cannot be assigned the same stage on the basis of cyclin B levels. The first is consistent with premitotic G2 delay; the second with mitotic spindle checkpoint delay. The regulated output is more informative than the protein abundance. After removal of the underlying problem, the first must activate CDK1 to enter mitosis, while the second must activate APC/C–Cdc20 and then decrease CDK1 activity to leave mitosis.

These mechanisms occur in the context of normal cell-cycle control. Checkpoints do not replace the need for cyclins, CDKs, cohesin, microtubules or replication proteins. They make the use of those systems conditional on the state of earlier processes. This conceptual distinction prepares the analysis of mitotic stages and chromosome separation in the following chapters.

11. Reading checkpoint experiments rather than merely identifying markers

The most common difficulty in checkpoint problems is that a measurement is not identical to a biological process. A DNA-content histogram reports the amount of fluorescent DNA stain associated with each measured object. Under conditions where dye binding is proportional to DNA content, a diploid G1 population forms a peak at 2C, and G2 and early mitotic cells form a peak at 4C. S-phase cells occupy the interval between them. A peak is a population distribution, not a record of an individual cell moving through time. An increased 4C fraction can therefore result from G2 delay, mitotic delay or, in particular populations, tetraploid G1 cells.

Accurate interpretation assumes that intact single cells or nuclei are being compared. Two adjacent 2C cells passing together through a detector may produce a signal resembling one 4C cell. Excluding doublets and assessing viability reduces this error. A fragmented or dying population can introduce low-DNA events; these cannot all be counted as normal G1 cells. Polyploidy can occur naturally in some tissues or after division failure.

A short EdU pulse adds a measurement of DNA synthesis over a defined interval. A mitotic marker such as histone H3 phosphorylation, together with microscopy of chromosome condensation and spindle morphology, helps distinguish G2 from M phase. A high 4C peak accompanied by a low mitotic index and low CDK1 activity supports premitotic delay. A high 4C peak accompanied by condensed chromosomes, abundant mitotic markers and persistent securin supports a mitotic delay. A 4C peak with sustained absence of cycling markers after cytokinesis failure may instead represent a new ploidy state.

Checkpoint activation is most convincingly demonstrated when molecular activity and the physiological response are measured together. A rise in Chk1 phosphorylation after replication stress is consistent with ATR-dependent signaling, but a functional consequence must also be shown, such as restraint of inappropriate origin initiation or delayed phase progression. Measuring the same response in a checkpoint-defective strain and after genetic rescue makes the inference more specific. A kinase inhibitor alone is weaker evidence because its concentration and specificity may affect unrelated targets.

Time courses are valuable because causation requires a plausible sequence. If p53 increases, then p21 increases, then CDK2 activity decreases, then fewer G1 cells enter S phase, the ordering fits the classical pathway. If CDK2 inhibition precedes detectable p21 induction, a faster parallel mechanism may operate. Neither observation alone demonstrates that p21 is irrelevant. Test necessity using cells lacking p21; test whether a downstream intervention can bypass a missing signal by restoring p21 in a p53-deficient background. Rescue of arrest is not rescue of every p53 function.

The same logic applies to spindle perturbations. If a drug destroys spindle microtubules and wild-type cells retain securin, one might conclude that the drug directly inhibits APC/C. But if the same drug fails to maintain securin in cells lacking checkpoint signaling, a checkpoint-dependent effect is supported. Test whether securin can be degraded when kinetochores attach normally to distinguish a persistent checkpoint from a defect in the destruction machinery. A nondegradable securin mutant can preserve separase inhibition even with functional APC/C and checkpoint satisfaction.

Population fractions require care. A fraction can rise because cells enter a phase faster, leave it more slowly or preferentially survive while other cells die. A single snapshot cannot measure a transition rate without assumptions. A synchronized starting population followed by timed observations, or direct tracking of individual cells, provides stronger evidence of delay. Synchronization itself can perturb the system, especially when replication inhibitors are used, so matched controls and appropriate recovery intervals matter.

For DNA fiber reasoning, consider two consecutive pulses of distinguishable nucleotide labels. A continuous segment of first label followed by second label on a fiber is consistent with a fork that continued during both pulses. A new origin can generate bidirectional tracks during the second pulse without an earlier labeled segment at that origin. A shorter second segment relative to the first for a known pulse duration can indicate reduced fork movement. But an isolated short segment may also reflect a fork starting late in a pulse or ending early. Track selection, equal pulse intervals and suitable controls determine which interpretation is justified. This is why the intra-S checkpoint cannot be characterized solely by total label incorporation.

12. Quantitative reasoning: phase fractions, delays and checkpoint escape

Checkpoint mechanisms do not require a universal mathematical law. Nevertheless, examination questions frequently ask students to compare proportions or calculate the extent of a response. The following datasets are hypothetical teaching examples rather than published experimental measurements. Each calculation uses a defined denominator because changes in the measured fraction can otherwise be mistaken for changes in the underlying process.

Worked example 1 — phase occupancy. Among 2,000 intact singlet cells, 860 are G1, 540 are S and 600 are G2/M. A phase percentage equals its count divided by the total, multiplied by 100. G1 is 860/2,000 × 100 = 43%; S is 27%; G2/M is 30%. If 90 of the 600 G2/M cells are independently identified as mitotic, the mitotic index is 90/2,000 × 100 = 4.5%, not 15%. The latter value, 90/600 × 100 = 15%, is conditional on 4C content. Assuming the remaining 510 cells are G2, the G2 fraction is 25.5%.

Worked example 2 — distinguish two 4C accumulations. Three cultures each contain 1,000 analyzed cells. The untreated culture has 250 4C cells, of which 50 are mitotic. DNA damage gives 650 4C cells, of which 30 are mitotic. Spindle disruption gives 650 4C cells, of which 590 are mitotic. Both treated samples have 65% 4C cells, but their mitotic indices are 3% and 59%. Their estimated G2 fractions are 62% and 6%, respectively. Assuming appropriate markers and no other 4C states, the first pattern supports a premitotic delay and the second supports a mitotic delay.

Worked example 3 — relative suppression of S-phase entry. A synchronized culture contains 800 viable G1 cells initially. During a fixed interval, 480 control cells enter S but only 120 damaged cells enter S. The entry fractions are 0.60 and 0.15. The absolute decrease is 0.45, or 45 percentage points. Relative suppression equals (0.60 − 0.15)/0.60 = 0.75, or 75%. The result describes entry within this particular interval, not permanent arrest.

Worked example 4 — checkpoint escape and survival. Follow 200 damaged wild-type cells and 200 checkpoint-deficient cells. Twenty wild-type cells and 150 mutant cells enter mitosis with persistent damage markers. Escape fractions are 10% and 75%; their ratio is 7.5. If only 18 of the 150 mutant entrants later form viable colonies, conditional colony formation is 18/150 = 12%, not 9%. The latter number is 18/200 and refers to the original mutant cohort. Rapid entry cannot be equated with successful recovery.

Worked example 5 — time to anaphase. In a live-cell record, nuclear-envelope breakdown occurs at minute 0, the last unattached kinetochore attaches at minute 42 and anaphase begins at minute 58. The interval after final attachment is 16 minutes. Another cell attaches its last kinetochore at minute 75 and starts anaphase at minute 91. The total interval is prolonged by 33 minutes, matching the difference in attachment time, whereas the post-attachment interval remains 16 minutes. The observations fit an attachment-dependent delay followed by a finite interval for signal loss and downstream events. Two cells cannot establish a universal timing rule.

Worked example 6 — replication pulse interpretation. Two groups receive equal 20-minute EdU pulses. Mean background-corrected fluorescence among S-phase cells is 100 arbitrary units in controls and 40 under stress. Under matched labeling efficiency and linear detection, relative incorporation is 40/100 = 0.40, a 60% decrease. This measures incorporated label, not fork velocity. If there are half as many active forks, the 40% total signal is consistent with 80% average incorporation per fork, 0.40/0.50 = 0.80, under simplifying assumptions. A single bulk fluorescence reading cannot prove that all forks are 60% slower.

Worked example 7 — quantify a phenotypic rescue. In a matched experiment, S entry fractions for damaged wild type, p53-null and p53-null expressing p21 are 0.12, 0.68 and 0.20. The mutant excess over wild type is 0.56. Rescue reduces entry by 0.48 relative to the mutant. Thus 0.48/0.56 = 0.8571, approximately 86% of the excess entry is eliminated. This is an operational measurement of rescue of the observed phenotype, not a fraction of all p53 functions restored.

The general rule is to establish what has been counted, identify the comparison group and express the answer in correct units. Percentages and fold changes are dimensionless; delays are measured in time units; fluorescence depends on calibration. No artificial equation for checkpoint strength is needed.

13. Conservation, biological context and the limits of checkpoint control

The main checkpoint problems are widely shared by eukaryotes, but different lineages solve them using overlapping rather than identical proteins. Budding yeast passes through a commitment transition called Start, analogous in function to the mammalian restriction point, but not governed by the mammalian Rb–E2F and p53–p21 circuit. Yeast Mec1 and Tel1 have ATR- and ATM-related functions, respectively. Logical relationships established by yeast experiments can therefore illuminate mammalian pathways without requiring every protein name and interaction to be identical.

Plants also regulate division in response to developmental and environmental conditions and use CDKs and DNA-damage checkpoint signaling, but the mammalian p53 pathway must not be imposed on them. A question about a plant or yeast cell should be answered using the components specified for that organism, or at the level of the conserved control principle when no pathway is specified. Early embryonic cell cycles in some organisms are unusually rapid, with reduced or absent conventional gap phases. The response can differ from that in adult mammalian somatic cells, and developmental changes can establish stronger controls later. Absence of a long G1 period does not establish absence of every form of quality control.

Checkpoints are not perfect detectors of all errors. A chemically modified base may escape detection; a small amount of unreplicated DNA may persist into mitosis; an incorrectly oriented but occupied kinetochore may escape the spindle checkpoint. Detection depends on the structures that generate the signal, the effective regulatory threshold and the downstream machinery. Normal-looking progression therefore does not prove an undamaged genome. Repair, error correction and surveillance also operate beyond a simplified phase-transition diagram.

A checkpoint-deficient cell can retain repair capacity but lose the time normally available to use it. A repair-deficient cell may possess a functional checkpoint and remain arrested because the initiating structures persist. A double mutant may both fail to repair and fail to delay. These causal combinations can all reduce long-term viability yet produce different short-term phase distributions. In normal proliferative tissues, recovery enables renewal without transmitting avoidable genomic injury; prolonged arrest or elimination can prevent propagation of persistent damage. None of these responses guarantees protection at the level of the whole organism.

Before moving on, reconstruct the framework from memory. Start with the biological problem, identify the initiating molecular condition, connect it to the signaling pathway and follow its effect on the transition driver. Predict what happens when the signal is removed, when the driver is forcibly activated and when the execution machinery itself is defective. This method prevents the common mistake of equating checkpoint bypass with successful cell division.

14. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style questions. Attempt each before opening its explanation.

1. Which statement best defines a cell-cycle checkpoint?

  1. A period defined by DNA synthesis
  2. A regulatory dependency that delays a transition if a prerequisite is unsuitable
  3. A constitutive DNA repair enzyme
  4. A physical chromosome structure
Answer and reasoning

B. The checkpoint is a control relationship. A describes S phase, C may repair damage without controlling progression, and D describes a structure rather than regulation.

2. A mitogen is withdrawn before the classical mammalian restriction point. Which is the most likely consequence in a responsive cell?

  1. Reduced G1 CDK-dependent commitment
  2. Direct separase activation
  3. Immediate chromosome doubling
  4. Obligatory apoptosis
Answer and reasoning

A. Loss of mitogenic input can reduce cyclin D–CDK4/6 support and maintain Rb restraint. B is a mitotic event, C cannot follow directly from withdrawal, and D is not inevitable.

3. In the classical G1 damage pathway, which molecule directly inhibits relevant cyclin–CDK activity downstream of p53?

  1. E2F
  2. MDM2
  3. p21
  4. Securin
Answer and reasoning

C. p21 is a CDK inhibitor. E2F is a transcription factor, MDM2 regulates p53 turnover and securin restrains separase.

4. Which structure most directly favors ATR-dependent replication-stress signaling?

  1. RPA-coated single-stranded DNA
  2. A completed cleavage furrow
  3. An intact fully attached kinetochore
  4. A mature ribosome
Answer and reasoning

A. Exposed ssDNA coated with RPA favors ATR recruitment. B concerns cytokinesis, C does not provide this replication-stress signal, and D is part of translation.

5. Which enzyme normally removes inhibitory phosphorylation from cyclin B–CDK1?

  1. Wee1
  2. Cdc25
  3. Separase
  4. DNA ligase
Answer and reasoning

B. Cdc25 is a phosphatase that removes inhibitory CDK phosphates. Wee1 adds one, separase cleaves cohesin, and DNA ligase joins DNA ends.

6. Which is the immediate regulated output of the spindle checkpoint's MCC?

  1. DNA polymerase
  2. Rb
  3. APC/C–Cdc20 activity
  4. RNA polymerase II
Answer and reasoning

C. MCC restrains APC/C–Cdc20, delaying substrate destruction. The other proteins do not implement this checkpoint's immediate anaphase restraint.

7. Assertion: A 4C DNA-content peak does not by itself identify mitotic arrest. Reason: G2 and early mitotic cells commonly contain the same DNA amount.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Both phases contain the same genome amount in a diploid cycling cell. DNA content does not report chromosome condensation or CDK activity.

8. Which molecule is normally destroyed to release a major inhibition of separase?

  1. Rb
  2. RPA
  3. Chk1
  4. Securin
Answer and reasoning

D. Securin restrains separase and is an APC/C substrate. Rb regulates transcription, RPA binds ssDNA and Chk1 transmits checkpoint signals.

9. Which distinction between senescence and quiescence is most accurate?

  1. Senescence is always death
  2. Quiescence is always irreversible
  3. Senescence usually involves durable arrest, while quiescence is often reversible
  4. Both necessarily contain 4C DNA
Answer and reasoning

C. Senescent cells can remain metabolically active despite durable arrest; quiescence is often reversible. Neither state is defined by DNA content alone.

10. Assertion: Removing a checkpoint can permit premature division. Reason: Checkpoint loss always restores damaged molecular structures.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

C. Removing regulatory restraint may enable a transition even when the lesion persists. Repair and checkpoint control are different functions.

11. Which comparison is correct?

  1. Wee1 removes inhibitory CDK1 phosphate
  2. Cdc25 directly cleaves cohesin
  3. p21 can restrain G1 CDKs, whereas MCC restrains APC/C–Cdc20
  4. Mad2 is a mammalian G1 mitogen receptor
Answer and reasoning

C. The inhibitors act at distinct transitions. Wee1 adds inhibitory phosphate, separase cleaves cohesin, and Mad2 acts in mitotic checkpoint signaling.

12. Which observation is compatible with an unsatisfied spindle checkpoint?

  1. High securin and active mitotic CDK1 with unattached kinetochores
  2. Complete absence of cyclin B in ordinary metaphase
  3. S-phase initiation without replication machinery
  4. Obligatory p53 activation in every eukaryote
Answer and reasoning

A. The spindle checkpoint restrains APC/C-dependent securin destruction after entry into mitosis. B is more consistent with exit, C is physically implausible, and D falsely universalizes a mammalian pathway.

15. Part C — Experimental, mechanistic and quantitative reasoning

Original examination-style questions. Attempt each before opening its explanation.

1. After equal irradiation, wild-type cells delay S entry; p53-null cells do not sustain the delay; p53-null cells expressing p21 again delay S entry. Which inference is strongest?

  1. p21 acts upstream of p53
  2. p21 can implement a downstream arrest function independent of p53 expression
  3. p21 repairs all lesions
  4. p53 has no other functions
Answer and reasoning

B. A downstream rescue supports p21 as an arrest effector. It neither reverses pathway order (A), demonstrates repair (C), nor restores all p53 outputs (D).

2. After damage, cyclin B is unchanged, inhibitory CDK1 phosphorylation increases and mitotic entry declines. Which manipulation most directly bypasses the indicated brake?

  1. Increase Wee1 activity
  2. Inhibit Cdc25 further
  3. Introduce CDK1 resistant to the relevant inhibitory phosphorylation
  4. Stabilize securin
Answer and reasoning

C. Resistance at the inhibitory site may weaken the G2 brake, although other restraints remain. A and B reinforce inhibition. D affects chromatid separation after mitotic entry.

3. Two 1,500-cell samples each contain 900 4C cells. X has 60 mitotic cells and Y has 810. What are the inferred G2 fractions of the whole samples if other 4C states are excluded?

  1. 56% and 6%
  2. 60% and 60%
  3. 4% and 54%
  4. 93.3% and 10%
Answer and reasoning

A. X has (900−60)/1500 = 56% G2; Y has (900−810)/1500 = 6%. B is the 4C fraction; C gives mitotic indices; D uses the 4C class as denominator.

4. Hydroxyurea-treated wild-type cells reduce origin firing. In ATR-defective cells under the same treatment, additional origins fire but damage rises. Why does higher initiation not prove recovery?

  1. Origin firing repairs every break
  2. Additional forks compete for limited substrates and fork protection can fail
  3. ATR is a DNA polymerase
  4. Hydroxyurea only disrupts mitotic microtubules
Answer and reasoning

B. Checkpoint bypass does not restore nucleotide supply and can compromise fork integrity. A confuses initiation with repair, C confuses kinase and polymerase, D misidentifies the perturbation.

5. A cell with an unattached kinetochore undergoes anaphase after laser ablation removes that kinetochore's signaling region. What is justified?

  1. All chromosomes are now correctly attached
  2. The destroyed kinetochore was required for the inhibitory signal in that context
  3. The spindle was rebuilt by the laser
  4. DNA replication was incomplete
Answer and reasoning

B. Loss of the localized signal permits progression despite unresolved attachment. A and C equate signal removal with mechanical correction; D lacks evidence.

6. A mutant Cdc20 activates APC/C despite MCC. A second mutation makes securin nondegradable while retaining its inhibitory function. What is predicted during spindle stress?

  1. Cdc20 bypass necessarily ensures segregation
  2. Securin can still restrain separase despite APC/C activation
  3. CDK1 cannot be activated in G2
  4. DNA synthesis must accelerate
Answer and reasoning

B. The second perturbation blocks a downstream release step. A ignores this and the spindle defect, while C and D address unrelated transitions.

7. In a fixed interval 360/600 controls and 90/600 damaged cells enter S. What is relative suppression of S entry?

  1. 45%
  2. 15%
  3. 75%
  4. 25%
Answer and reasoning

C. Control entry is 60%; damage entry is 15%. Relative suppression is (60−15)/60 = 75%. A is the percentage-point decrease, B is treated entry, D is residual entry relative to control.

8. After replication stress, Chk1 phosphorylation increases in wild type but not in a kinase mutant. Both show RPA-coated ssDNA. Which additional result best supports a checkpoint signaling defect?

  1. The mutant had more DNA before treatment
  2. Restoring wild-type kinase rescues Chk1 activation and checkpoint delay under matched stress
  3. Another cell type expresses RPA
  4. The mutant grows in untreated medium
Answer and reasoning

B. Rescue links the gene to signaling while matched stress addresses an alternative explanation. A, C and D do not establish causality during stress.

9. A 4C cell has condensed chromosomes, high phospho-histone H3 and retained securin. Another 4C cell has intact nuclear envelope, low mitotic marker and high inhibitory CDK1 phosphate. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. First G2, second M
  2. Both necessarily G2
  3. First mitotic delay, second G2 delay
  4. Both necessarily senescent
Answer and reasoning

C. The combined markers distinguish stage and restrained driver. A reverses the signatures, B relies on DNA content, D requires evidence of durable arrest.

10. One kinetochore is connected to microtubules from both poles. Why might anaphase nevertheless occur?

  1. Merotelic attachment may escape a strong unattached-kinetochore signal
  2. The cell lacks centromeres
  3. Every erroneous attachment activates p53 before anaphase
  4. Cohesin cleavage must be impossible
Answer and reasoning

A. An occupied kinetochore may escape robust checkpoint detection even when geometry is incorrect. B contradicts the observation; C overgeneralizes damage control; D confuses geometry with cohesin cleavage.

11. In a mutant, irradiation increases p53 but not p21. Forced expression of p21 reduces CDK2 activity. Which is most appropriate?

  1. p53 protein abundance proves its transcriptional activity
  2. The p21 arm is compromised upstream of p21 expression, but downstream CDK inhibition remains possible
  3. CDK2 activates all p53 targets
  4. The spindle checkpoint is necessarily defective
Answer and reasoning

B. The phenotype could arise from p53 transcriptional dysfunction or other failure of p21 induction. Rescue establishes downstream competence for CDK inhibition. A confuses abundance with activity; C and D are unsupported.

12. In live-cell movies, final attachment and anaphase occur at 36 and 52 min in control versus 62 and 78 min in a second group. Which conclusion fits?

  1. Post-attachment delay rose by 26 min
  2. Both post-attachment delays are 16 min; attachment timing explains the total-time difference
  3. Second group lacks all spindle checkpoint activity
  4. First group never reached anaphase
Answer and reasoning

B. 52−36 = 16 and 78−62 = 16 min. A confuses total with post-attachment timing. C is not established and D contradicts the observation.

13. Two strains receive identical DNA damage. A cannot repair lesions but has an intact checkpoint; B retains repair capacity but lacks the principal delay. Which early phenotype is most likely?

  1. A may sustain arrest; B may enter the next phase before repair is complete
  2. A must enter immediately; B must arrest indefinitely
  3. Both repair completely because they divide
  4. Neither has an intact cyclin system
Answer and reasoning

A. Persistent lesions can maintain signaling in A, whereas B may bypass delay. B reverses the logic; C equates division and repair; D wrongly generalizes specific defects.

14. 40 of 400 damaged wild-type cells enter M prematurely versus 280 of 400 mutant cells. Of the mutant entrants, 35 form colonies. Which pair is correct?

  1. Escape fold change 7; conditional colony formation 12.5%
  2. Escape fold change 2.8; conditional colony formation 8.75%
  3. Escape fold change 7; conditional colony formation 70%
  4. Escape fold change 40; conditional colony formation 35%
Answer and reasoning

A. Escape is 10% versus 70%, giving 70/10 = 7. Colony formation among entrants is 35/280 = 12.5%. 8.75% is 35/400, using the original cohort as denominator.

15. A kinase inhibitor increases the 4C fraction. Which additional measurement best distinguishes G2/M restraint from spindle checkpoint activation?

  1. Repeat DNA staining alone
  2. Measure mitotic CDK activity, condensation and securin retention together
  3. Count ribosomes only
  4. Measure cell diameter alone
Answer and reasoning

B. The first checkpoint restrains entry, the second acts after entry. Joint molecular and morphological readouts discriminate the transition. A repeats the ambiguity; C and D lack phase specificity.
