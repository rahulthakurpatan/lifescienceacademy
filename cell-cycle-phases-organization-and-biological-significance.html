1. Why a cell must prepare before it divides

A living cell is not merely a container filled with enzymes. It is an organized system that must preserve information while continually replacing its components. The nucleus contains instructions for building proteins and functional RNAs, whereas membranes, ribosomes, mitochondria and many other structures make those instructions useful. A daughter cell must inherit enough of both the information and the machinery to maintain life. Simply pinching a cell into two equal pieces would not guarantee that each piece receives a complete genome. The biological problem of division is therefore fundamentally different from the mechanical problem of cutting an object into two parts.

Consider a fertilized egg developing into an animal, a root meristem producing new plant cells, or the basal layer of an epithelium replacing cells lost from the surface. Each of these examples involves production of new cells, but the timing and purpose differ. During development, repeated divisions can increase cell number and create populations that later specialize. In an adult tissue, division can replace lost cells without increasing the total population indefinitely. In a unicellular eukaryote, division can itself constitute reproduction. The common requirement is faithful inheritance of the nuclear genome.

The most useful way to understand this requirement is to separate three tasks: making a complete additional copy of the genetic information, distributing the two copies to different nuclear territories, and physically partitioning the cell. If segregation occurred before copying, the daughter cells could each lose some of the information necessary for survival. If copying occurred repeatedly without segregation, DNA content could increase without an increase in cell number. If the nuclear division succeeded but physical partitioning failed, a single cell could contain two nuclei. These outcomes demonstrate why the different operations must be ordered rather than treated as interchangeable meanings of division.

The cell cycle is the ordered succession of events that a proliferating cell undergoes between equivalent points in successive rounds of division. A convenient starting point is the formation of a daughter cell; the endpoint is the formation of its daughters. A circular diagram represents repeated progression, not an obligatory fate for every cell. Some cells cease proliferating temporarily; others differentiate and normally never enter another cycle. Cell growth also does not automatically mean cell division. A muscle cell may enlarge without producing two daughter cells, while early embryos can increase cell number with little increase in total embryonic mass.

The framework developed here concerns the conventional eukaryotic mitotic cycle. The chromosome terminology from chromosome architecture and the organization of the nucleus will be used throughout. Prokaryotes have distinct modes of genome replication and division; meiosis has a different sequence of nuclear divisions. Both are important comparisons, but neither should be forced into every detail of the standard somatic cycle.

2. How experiments revealed a cycle within interphase

Early observations with light microscopes made nuclear division particularly striking. Chromosomes become conspicuous as cells enter mitosis, whereas the genetic material is more dispersed during the interval between divisions. A microscope image of a nonmitotic nucleus, however, does not reveal whether that nucleus has duplicated its DNA. This limitation initially made it tempting to regard the interval as a single resting period. The discovery of a discrete DNA-synthesis interval changed that interpretation.

Alma Howard and Stephen Pelc studied proliferating cells in broad bean root tips using radioactive phosphorus and autoradiography in the early 1950s. A radioactive isotope acts as a tracer: its chemical behavior permits incorporation into cellular molecules, while its radiation provides a means of detection. By removing other phosphorus-containing material from fixed tissues, they could examine retained nuclear labeling associated with DNA. In autoradiography, radioactive emissions expose photographic material near the labeled structure, leaving a localized signal after development.

The essential logic was temporal. If chromosomes were copied continuously throughout interphase or only during visible nuclear division, the relationship between nuclear labeling and subsequent mitotic figures would differ. Howard and Pelc observed labeling in only a subset of interphase nuclei and a delay before labeled dividing cells appeared. This supported an interval of synthesis followed by an interval before mitosis. They distinguished the postmitotic, presynthetic interval from the postsynthetic, premitotic interval. The terms G1, S and G2 describe this sequence rather than three easily recognized nuclear shapes. Their root-tip estimates were particular to their material and conditions, not universal durations.

A useful conceptual extension is a short pulse of a DNA precursor such as tritiated thymidine. A pulse is a brief exposure to a label; a chase replaces it with unlabeled precursor. Cells incorporating label into replicating nuclear DNA during the pulse retain a detectable mark as they subsequently move through the cycle. An unexposed specimen establishes background signal; matching exposed samples collected at successive times reveal the redistribution of label. A short pulse makes the temporal interpretation sharper than continuous exposure, because long exposure can mark cells that were in different phases when labeling began. DNA repair can also incorporate precursors, so the interpretation concerns the strong genome-replication signal under the relevant conditions, not an absolute claim that DNA synthesis occurs nowhere outside S phase.

The experiment distinguishes a measured event from a named phase. DNA labeling reports synthesis while a mitotic figure reports chromosome organization. Combining these observations demonstrates a succession that cannot be deduced from either measurement alone. Modern DNA-content measurements, introduced below, add an independent way to ask how much nuclear DNA is present rather than whether synthesis is occurring at that instant.

Later mammalian cell-fusion experiments by Rao and Johnson provided a complementary insight. When an S-phase cell and a G1-phase cell shared a common cytoplasm while their nuclei initially remained distinct, the G1 nucleus could be induced to begin replication early. A G2 nucleus in an analogous combination did not normally undergo a second complete S phase simply because S-phase cytoplasm was present. This indicated both diffusible phase-promoting activities and a difference in the replication competence of nuclei. These experiments do not by themselves identify every molecular regulator; their importance is that progression is a regulated biological state, not merely an elapsed-time label. The molecular switches responsible for such transitions are the subject of the next chapter.

3. The organization of interphase and M phase

The standard cycle has two broad intervals. Interphase includes G1, S and G2. During it a cell performs much of its ordinary metabolism, increases or adjusts its cellular material, and prepares the chromosomes for distribution. M phase includes nuclear division, called mitosis, and usually the associated partitioning of the cytoplasm, called cytokinesis. In many animal cells cytokinesis starts before mitosis has completely finished, so the two events can overlap. Treat them as distinguishable processes rather than two obligatorily nonoverlapping time blocks.

The letters G denote gaps between the major observable DNA-synthesis and division events. They do not mean that cellular chemistry stops. The first gap follows the previous division and precedes S. The second follows the completion of replication and precedes mitosis. S denotes the interval during which the nuclear genome is duplicated. The phase labels are operationally defined by the events occurring in the cell; they are not simply arbitrary quarters of a clock.

Why are these phases arranged in that order? The previous division establishes the starting complement of chromosomes in a daughter nucleus. G1 allows the cell to assess its internal condition and external environment while building the materials required for a new replication round. S establishes two sister copies of each original chromosome. G2 provides an interval for completing preparations and checking whether duplication has occurred sufficiently for division. M physically separates sister copies so that each new nucleus receives the original chromosome complement. The next G1 begins with that restored complement. The successful repetition of this pattern maintains genome content across a lineage despite a temporary doubling of DNA in each cycle.

The convenient diagram of a cycle must not be mistaken for a drawing to scale. A long G1 can occupy most of a cell's cycle, and M phase can occupy only a small portion. Some cycles substantially reduce or omit conventional gaps. Cell-cycle exit, commonly represented as G0 branching from G1, introduces another distinction: a cell that is alive but not participating in a proliferative cycle is not necessarily delayed at a checkpoint because of damage. It may be in a normal tissue-specific noncycling state.

Two broad types of input affect progression. Extracellular inputs include nutrients, growth-promoting signals and tissue context. Internal inputs report whether the necessary prior operations, such as chromosome duplication, have succeeded. These inputs influence the regulatory activities that permit a transition. The cell cycle is therefore better understood as a sequence of gated processes than as a clock that necessarily advances every cell at the same rate.

4. Chromosome number, chromatids, ploidy and C-value

The chromosome count and the amount of DNA answer different questions. Before examining individual phases, it is necessary to learn a bookkeeping system that continues to work even when the DNA has been copied. Imagine a diploid cell containing two types of chromosomes, A and B. It inherited one chromosome of each type from each parent. Its chromosome complement therefore contains A of maternal origin, A of paternal origin, B of maternal origin and B of paternal origin. The two versions of A are homologues; the two versions of B are another homologous pair. Homologous chromosomes generally carry corresponding gene loci but need not carry the same alleles.

A chromosome is an organized DNA-protein unit that can be inherited as a distinct chromosome. In an ordinary monocentric chromosome the centromere defines the region required for proper segregation. During replication each chromosome becomes a duplicated unit consisting of two sister chromatids. A chromatid contains one continuous double-stranded nuclear DNA molecule in this simplified intact-chromosome model. Sister chromatids are the products of copying one chromosome; they are not the maternal and paternal homologues. The copies remain physically associated, through cohesin-mediated cohesion, until the appropriate stage of division. The two associated sisters count as one duplicated chromosome. Once the sisters disjoin, each becomes an independent daughter chromosome. The common exam rule to count centromeres must therefore be interpreted as counting independently segregating chromosome units rather than counting every replicated centromeric DNA sequence or every kinetochore.

Ploidy describes the number of complete chromosome sets. In the simple diploid system just described, one set contains two chromosome types, so n = 2 and 2n = 4. Copying the DNA creates two sisters for each chromosome but does not create new homologous sets in the ordinary G2 nucleus. The cell is still diploid. After a normal mitotic division, each new nucleus is also diploid. Chromosome count and ploidy are related, but changes in chromosome number are not always changes in the number of complete sets. Aneuploidy, for example, refers to an imbalance involving particular chromosomes rather than a balanced increase of the whole set.

The symbol C refers to the DNA amount in one unreplicated haploid genome of the organism, ordinarily expressed as a mass or number of base pairs. A standard diploid G1 nucleus contains two such complements, or 2C. The fully duplicated diploid G2 nucleus contains 4C. Here 2n has remained constant while C has doubled. A normal haploid cell undergoing its own mitotic cycle can progress from n, 1C to n, 2C; thus 2C does not universally mean diploid and 4C does not universally mean G2. In a tetraploid organism, ploidy and the choice of the reference genome must be stated with particular care; in exercises below C always means the specified haploid reference DNA amount.

The word chromatid requires a convention when chromosomes are not duplicated. Many examination questions call an unreplicated chromosome a single-chromatid chromosome. Other sources reserve the word chromatid for a member of an attached sister pair. Here we use “DNA-bearing units (one per unduplicated chromosome or two per fully duplicated chromosome)” where ambiguity matters. In S phase different regions and chromosomes can be incompletely replicated, so total DNA content is a more direct quantitative measurement than an attempt to assign a fractional number of completed chromatids.

A common erroneous inference is that a chromosome containing two DNA molecules must count as two chromosomes. It confuses a copy with an independent segregation unit. Visualize four folders, each containing a unique set of pages. Photocopying each folder and keeping the original and copy bound together produces four bundles, each now carrying two copies. Later separating the bundles into individual folders creates eight independent units in one space; distribution puts four in each daughter. The analogy represents count and inheritance, not the molecular structure of a chromosome.

5. G1: restoring capacity and deciding whether to proliferate

At the completion of a conventional symmetric division, a daughter has inherited one nuclear genome complement and a fraction of the parent's cytoplasm. It needs the machinery and resources to support another round of genome duplication and division. In G1 it continues transcription, translation, energy metabolism, membrane synthesis and organelle maintenance. Cells often increase in size during this interval, but growth does not occur exclusively in G1. S and G2 cells can also synthesize substantial amounts of RNA, protein and other cellular materials. The distribution of growth across the cycle depends on cell type and nutritional conditions.

A proliferating cell must obtain nucleotides for DNA synthesis, amino acids for proteins, ATP for energetically demanding reactions, and sufficient membrane and cytoskeletal components for the next division. Mitochondria supply much of the ATP in many aerobic cells; ribosomes synthesize proteins, and the endoplasmic reticulum and other organelles support cell maintenance. Most organelles do not duplicate in a single all-or-none S-like interval. Their growth, division, remodeling and partitioning are coordinated over the cycle, with mechanisms that differ among organelles. The cell is preparing as an integrated system, not merely enlarging a nucleus.

A cell can continue through G1 only when the signals governing proliferation permit it. In many mammalian cells, extracellular mitogens stimulate intracellular pathways that favor progression toward DNA replication. Mitogens promote cell-cycle entry or progression, whereas growth factors in the broader sense can support mass accumulation and survival; one signal can have overlapping effects. Availability of nutrients and cell attachment can also affect proliferation. The relevant conditions depend on the organism and cell type. A dividing plant meristem does not use an identical combination of extracellular signals to an animal fibroblast.

A particularly useful experimental idea in mammalian cell culture is the restriction point. Early in G1, withdrawal of appropriate growth-promoting signals can prevent entry into S. Later, under classic experimental conditions, cells that have passed the restriction point can continue through the current cycle despite withdrawal of those signals. This commitment is not immunity to every form of stress: replication or damage responses may still delay progression. The exact molecular mechanisms and the distinction between restriction and damage checkpoints are developed in subsequent chapters. Here the conceptual point is that G1 is not merely a waiting interval; it includes a decision to initiate a resource-intensive and potentially hazardous process.

The DNA state is especially simple for a conventional diploid G1 nucleus. Its complement is 2n and 2C. Replication origins can be prepared for later activation, but widespread genome duplication has not yet begun. Repair-associated DNA synthesis, mitochondrial DNA replication and RNA synthesis do not convert a G1 cell into an S-phase cell. S phase refers specifically to the programmed replication of the nuclear genome. This distinction matters when interpreting an experimental signal that reports synthesis of nucleic acids without distinguishing DNA from RNA or organellar DNA from nuclear DNA.

A cell that has inadequate resources or receives insufficient proliferative stimulation may remain outside the active cycle rather than entering S. The resulting 2C DNA measurement can look identical to that of an actively cycling G1 cell. To distinguish those biological states we must consider the G0 branch.

6. G0, quiescence, differentiation and re-entry

The existence of a nondividing cell is not a contradiction of the cell-cycle model. A tissue contains cells with different duties, and it would be disadvantageous for all of them to replicate continuously. G0 is a conventional term for a state outside the active proliferative cycle, typically entered from G1 in the standard diploid model. The cell continues metabolism and can perform its specialized functions. It is not necessarily dormant in the sense of being metabolically inactive.

Quiescence is a reversible nonproliferative state. Many resting lymphocytes can enter a proliferative program after appropriate activation. Hepatocytes in an undamaged adult liver divide infrequently but can re-enter the cycle when tissue regeneration is required. In culture, some cells become quiescent when deprived of mitogens and resume cycling when the conditions change. Re-entry requires restoration of proliferative activity and progression through the necessary preparatory events before S; it is not a direct jump from a 2C nucleus to mitosis. The details of exit and entry can vary, and not all quiescent populations behave identically.

Terminal differentiation describes acquisition of a specialized cell state that is ordinarily associated with durable loss of proliferative ability. Most mature neurons in the adult central nervous system are postmitotic; they remain biologically active while performing highly specialized functions. Some differentiated cells, such as hepatocytes, are not terminally postmitotic, demonstrating that differentiation and permanent cycle exit are not synonyms. Mature mammalian red blood cells have expelled their nuclei altogether. They are an extreme example of why a standard G0 DNA histogram description cannot simply be applied to every nondividing cell type.

A third state is cellular senescence, a durable cell-cycle arrest often triggered by stresses or particular developmental signals. Unlike simple reversible quiescence, a senescent cell usually does not resume proliferation merely because a mitogen is supplied. Senescent cells can remain metabolically active and alter their interactions with surrounding cells. Detailed mechanisms of senescence are taught elsewhere. For the present chapter, distinguish the evidence required to identify each state: response to a proliferative stimulus, capacity for re-entry, differentiated phenotype and the circumstances of arrest. A 2C reading alone supplies none of this information. Senescence and stress-induced arrest can also occur with other DNA contents.

G0 is commonly drawn as an offshoot of G1 because many noncycling cells possess the unreplicated chromosome complement typical of G1. The arrow back should be interpreted conditionally: cells capable of reversible re-entry can return, whereas a permanently postmitotic cell generally cannot. G0 is not an obligatory fifth stage that every cell completes between G1 and S, and it is not a phase of mitosis.

Suppose a sample of 1,000 intact diploid cells contains 700 cells with 2C DNA. If 400 of those are reversible quiescent cells and 300 are cycling G1 cells, a DNA-content assay alone records the combined 700. Counting all 700 as cells preparing for an immediate S phase overestimates the active G1 population by 400. A re-entry assay or a proliferation-associated marker can supply additional evidence. A marker such as Ki-67 is often used to help distinguish cycling from quiescent populations, but its expression depends on cell state and assay conditions; no single marker is a universal proof of reversibility.

7. S phase: duplicating the genome without changing ploidy

Entry into S initiates an organized program of nuclear DNA replication. DNA consists of complementary strands; during replication the parental strands provide templates for synthesis of new complementary strands. Each completed duplex contains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand in the standard semiconservative mechanism. The detailed chemistry, enzymes and proof of semiconservative replication belong to Unit 3. What matters in this chapter is how the copying event changes the amount and physical organization of DNA.

A eukaryotic chromosome is too long to be copied efficiently from a single point. Replication is initiated at many regions, and replication forks advance along the DNA. Different regions begin and complete copying at different times. Therefore, an S-phase nucleus does not instantly switch from 2C to 4C. Its total nuclear DNA progressively increases through intermediate values while its chromosome complement remains 2n. Early S cells have synthesized relatively little additional DNA; late S cells have completed most of the duplication. The increase need not be uniform in time because the number and activity of forks and the genomic regions being replicated can vary.

Replication must be accompanied by reorganization of chromatin. DNA associated with histones and other proteins must become accessible to replication machinery. As DNA is copied, existing and newly produced histones participate in re-establishing nucleosomes on the daughter duplexes. This process helps restore chromosomal packaging and cellular identity. The topic connects directly with histones and nucleosomes. It would be misleading to imagine naked daughter DNA molecules waiting until M phase for their first association with proteins.

A second requirement is the physical association of the copies. Cohesin complexes help establish sister-chromatid cohesion in association with replication. This allows the cell to recognize and orient paired sisters during later segregation. The attachment does not mean that the DNA molecules are chemically one continuous molecule. Each completed sister contains its own DNA duplex. The crucial distinction is between molecular duplication and the physical organization of the resulting chromosome unit.

In many animal cells centrosome duplication is coordinated with progression through the G1/S and S-phase interval, ensuring that appropriate microtubule-organizing structures can be established for mitosis. This is not a universal requirement for a centrosome in all eukaryotes. Higher plants can assemble mitotic spindles without animal-type centrioles. Nor is centriole duplication identical to DNA replication: it is a separate structural event under cell-cycle control. In the same way, mitochondria and plastids have their own genomes whose replication does not define nuclear S phase.

The cell must avoid both failure to copy a genomic region and inappropriate recopying of a region during the same cycle. Otherwise the resulting chromosome may contain missing or excessive DNA. Replication initiation is therefore restricted to a coordinated program, and responses to incomplete or damaged DNA can slow progression. A high DNA content alone cannot prove that every region is copied correctly; a small missing or duplicated segment may not be resolved by a total-DNA measurement. Molecular safeguards are introduced here only to explain why a normal S phase is a single round of genome duplication.

A diploid nucleus begins S at 2C and normally ends at 4C. A nucleus measured at 3C has acquired an additional 1C, which is half of the 2C DNA required to complete that round. Under the assumptions of normal genome replication and no DNA loss, it is halfway through duplication in terms of DNA mass. That does not necessarily mean it is halfway through the elapsed duration of S. If synthesis was initially slow and later fast, half the additional DNA could have been reached later than half the elapsed time. This distinction between amount and rate is a recurring principle in quantitative cell biology.

8. G2: a duplicated genome must become competent for division

At the end of normal S phase, the cell has a doubled amount of nuclear DNA, but it is not necessarily ready to separate the chromosomes immediately. G2 is the interval between completion of genome replication and entry into mitosis. The cell can continue growth, synthesize or activate components needed for division, and respond to problems that would make segregation unsafe. The chromatin remains predominantly interphase chromatin until the changes of early mitosis begin.

An intact chromosome that contains two completed sisters must be converted into a structure suitable for mechanical segregation. Later, its kinetochore attachments must connect with the spindle in an appropriate orientation. The production and regulation of proteins required for these processes are coordinated in advance. For example, activation of mitotic regulatory proteins promotes the transition into a state in which chromosomes condense and the division machinery reorganizes. This does not imply that the visible events of prophase have already happened in G2. They define entry into M phase and are treated separately.

The cell also needs a way to delay entry into mitosis when replication is incomplete or DNA damage has been detected. Without such a delay, forcing partially replicated chromosomes to condense and segregate can produce chromosome breakage, missing DNA or other abnormalities in the daughter nuclei. The G2/M control system therefore couples the permission to enter mitosis to the prior state of the genome. This is a functional description; the detailed regulatory molecules are assigned to Nodes 2.D.02 and 2.D.03.

In the standard diploid G2 cell, chromosome number is still 2n, DNA content is 4C and every fully replicated chromosome contains two sister chromatids. The same bulk DNA content is found in early mitosis. A 4C nucleus may be in G2, or a 4C cell may be undergoing mitosis, or it may represent a different ploidy state altogether. A microscope showing a diffuse interphase nucleus rather than condensed mitotic chromosomes supplies a second dimension of evidence, but even morphology may be ambiguous near transitions. Mitotic-associated protein modifications or direct observation of progression can provide further discrimination.

Now consider an intervention that blocks entry into mitosis without directly inhibiting DNA synthesis. Cells already in S may finish copying, and cells in G1 may later enter S. Over time, cells can accumulate at 4C because they continue reaching G2 but cannot proceed. In contrast, a drug that directly prevents DNA synthesis can produce accumulation at the G1/S boundary or within S, depending on when and how it acts. The shape of the population DNA histogram reflects both entry into and departure from a phase. Accumulation in a phase does not establish the direct molecular target of an intervention.

9. M phase and restoration of daughter-cell DNA content

The defining problem of M phase is the reliable partition of the genome produced during S. Mitosis is nuclear division, and cytokinesis is division of the cytoplasm. Their molecular details and characteristic stages will receive separate treatment in Nodes 2.D.04 and 2.D.05. A brief overview is necessary here to understand why a doubling of DNA in one part of the cycle does not lead to a permanent doubling in each generation.

At the beginning of mitosis, chromosomes compact into mechanically manageable units. In cells with open mitosis, the nuclear envelope breaks down so that spindle microtubules can interact with the chromosomes. Some eukaryotes undergo closed or partially closed mitosis, so nuclear-envelope breakdown is not a universal definition of mitosis. At metaphase in a typical animal cell, sister-containing chromosomes are organized at the spindle equator with their sister kinetochores oriented toward opposite spindle poles. The chromosome number is still 2n and the nuclear genome in the whole cell contains 4C.

Anaphase begins when sister association is released and the sisters segregate toward opposite poles. At this point the unit of counting changes. Before disjunction, each pair of sisters was one duplicated chromosome. After disjunction, each separated sister is an individual daughter chromosome. If the entire still-undivided cell is counted, there are temporarily twice as many independent chromosome units as before separation. However, each spindle pole is receiving only the normal 2n complement. No additional nuclear DNA synthesis is required for this increase in the number of countable units.

During telophase the chromosome groups form distinct daughter nuclei. Each normal daughter nucleus has 2n chromosomes and 2C DNA. Before the cytoplasm separates, the two nuclei together still contain 4C. Cytokinesis partitions the cell so that each daughter now contains one of these nuclei and its own share of cellular machinery. The cell count changes from one to two, while the sum of nuclear DNA in both daughters equals the nuclear DNA in the parent just before cytokinesis.

These relationships explain why a phrase such as “the DNA content in telophase is 2C” can be either correct or incorrect depending on what was measured. Per daughter nucleus, 2C is correct in the usual diploid example. Per entire binucleate cell before cytokinesis, 4C is correct. Similarly, calling the whole anaphase cell “tetraploid” simply because it temporarily contains 4n countable chromosomes can obscure the fact that the chromosomes occupy two segregating diploid complements. A true tetraploid interphase nucleus has four chromosome sets within one nucleus. The safest answer explicitly identifies the counting compartment.

Cytokinesis failure demonstrates why mitosis and cell division cannot be treated as synonyms. If nuclear division occurs normally but cytokinesis fails, an animal cell can contain two diploid nuclei. It contains 4C in total if both nuclei are in their unreplicated state, but each nucleus is 2C. Later behavior can differ: cells can arrest, divide abnormally or undergo additional cycles depending on context. This is not automatically identical to one tetraploid nucleus produced by a failure of chromosome segregation or subsequent nuclear fusion.

The usual sequence also explains why chromosome number in the daughters remains constant across generations. Replication doubles the DNA within a parent, and mitotic partitioning divides that pool between the daughters. It is an alternation between increase in genome material and redistribution, not a progressive accumulation of additional chromosomes in every daughter.

10. How cell-cycle organization changes with biological context

The conventional four-phase sequence is a powerful reference model, but organisms do not all use identical phase durations or even visibly distinct gaps. Early embryonic cleavage provides a particularly clear example. In many rapidly developing embryos, maternal stores of proteins, RNAs and other materials allow early cycles to alternate quickly between genome replication and division. Conventional G1 and G2 intervals can be greatly shortened or absent. Cleavage produces progressively smaller cells because cytoplasm is partitioned repeatedly without a corresponding increase in the overall mass of the embryo. The nuclear genome still has to be copied and distributed; the preparatory tasks are supported by materials already present in the egg.

As development proceeds, cycles can become longer and acquire more obvious gap phases. New developmental requirements include regulated growth, gene expression and communication among cells. The details vary among species and developmental stages; it would be wrong to state that all embryonic cells lack G1 and G2 or that adult cells always have a 24-hour cycle. The important comparison is between the invariant need for DNA inheritance and the flexible timing of supporting events.

Another departure from the usual pattern is the endocycle, in which cells alternate DNA replication and gap-like states without completing ordinary mitosis and cytokinesis. Repeated rounds may generate nuclei containing substantially more DNA than the original diploid G1 nucleus. Endoreduplication occurs naturally in several plant tissues, including some cells undergoing expansion. The increase in genome copy number can accompany changes in cell size and biosynthetic capacity, although the relationship is not universal or necessarily causal in every case. Endomitosis is a related but distinguishable process in which cells enter aspects of mitosis without completing normal division; megakaryocytes are a familiar example of polyploidization associated with modified division behavior. These are exceptions to ordinary mitotic inheritance, not failures of the definition of S phase.

A 4C reading can therefore have different meanings. A normal diploid G2 nucleus and an unreplicated tetraploid G1 nucleus contain the same DNA mass when measured relative to the same reference genome. They differ in chromosome organization and ploidy. Karyotypic evidence, prior cell history or additional markers are needed to separate them. A cell that undergoes repeated DNA synthesis without ordinary division may produce an 8C population, but an 8C event can also arise from technical aggregation of cells or from other biological states. Interpretation must use evidence rather than assign a phase from a number alone.

Meiosis also modifies the standard sequence. In a conventional diploid meiotic precursor, DNA replication occurs before meiosis I; two nuclear divisions follow with no intervening round of genome-wide DNA replication. The first separates homologous chromosomes, leaving haploid complements of duplicated chromosomes; the second separates sister chromatids. The resulting n, 1C nuclei illustrate that ploidy and DNA amount are independent descriptors. Detailed pairing, recombination and meiotic regulation belong to the later meiosis chapters. For now, the contrast shows that “one S followed by one M” describes a typical mitotic cycle rather than every cellular reproductive program.

Prokaryotic binary fission should not be described as mitosis simply because it results in two cells. Bacteria do not form the canonical eukaryotic mitotic apparatus, and in rapidly growing bacteria successive rounds of chromosome replication may overlap. Their growth and division strategy belongs to Subunit 2.E. The standard G1–S–G2–M model is a reference for eukaryotic nuclear organization, not a universal set of labels for all life.

11. Measuring DNA content with flow cytometry

A single photograph of a proliferating tissue provides a useful but incomplete view of the cycle. Mitotic chromosomes are often recognizable, whereas distinguishing G1 from G2 by nuclear appearance alone can be unreliable. Flow cytometry provides a complementary measurement by analyzing individual cells or nuclei as they pass through a detector. In a conventional DNA-content assay, a DNA-binding fluorophore is used under conditions in which the measured fluorescence is approximately proportional to the amount of nuclear DNA.

Fluorescence is light emitted after excitation by an appropriate light source. A dye such as propidium iodide can bind nucleic acids; in a standard DNA-content assay RNA is removed or its contribution otherwise controlled because RNA can distort the intended DNA signal. The instrument records the signal for each event. A suitable reference population or known DNA-content peak helps establish the relative fluorescence scale. In a simple diploid cycling sample, if the 2C peak is centered at 100 arbitrary fluorescence units, the 4C peak should be near 200 units under a linear, properly calibrated measurement. The units are relative instrument readings, not chromosome counts.

After collecting many events, the experimenter constructs a histogram. The horizontal axis represents fluorescence or inferred DNA amount. The vertical axis represents the frequency of events within a fluorescence interval. Cells near the 2C peak have the DNA content expected for G1 or G0; cells spread between the peaks have values compatible with S; cells near the 4C peak are compatible with G2 or M in the standard model. S cells form a distribution because cells in a population have completed different fractions of replication.

Peak height is not the same as the fraction of cells in a phase. A tall, narrow peak can contain fewer cells than a broad, lower region. The relevant quantity is the number of events assigned to a phase, or the integrated area over the corresponding distribution after accounting for overlap and background. For ideal discrete examples, these numbers may be provided directly. Real histograms require suitable gating and often model-based estimation because S-phase events overlap the edges of the G1 and G2/M peaks.

DNA-content assays have important sources of ambiguity. Two attached G1 cells can pass the detector as a single event and produce a signal near 4C. Appropriate singlet discrimination, which uses features of the signal pulse to exclude aggregates, can reduce this artifact. Cell debris and fragmented DNA can appear below the main 2C peak; such a sub-G1 population can be consistent with DNA fragmentation during apoptosis but is not by itself definitive evidence of a particular death pathway. Sample preparation, staining variability, mixed ploidy and instrument settings can all alter the apparent histogram.

The method is therefore powerful when combined with independent information. A brief EdU or BrdU pulse identifies active DNA synthesis under suitable conditions. A mitotic-associated marker, such as phosphorylation of histone H3 at a relevant site, or direct observation of mitotic chromosome morphology, can help separate M-phase cells from G2. Proliferation markers and re-entry assays help distinguish G0 from G1. None of these measurements is identical to the others: DNA content measures amount, precursor incorporation measures ongoing synthesis, and a marker or image reports a particular molecular or structural state. Their complementarity is precisely why they can resolve ambiguities.

12. Tracking cells through time: pulses, synchronization and indices

A histogram collected at one instant is a snapshot of a population, not a direct movie of a single cell. To determine whether cells advance normally through the cycle, a researcher can compare measurements over time. If cells are initially enriched for one phase, they may move as a recognizable cohort. The gradual loss of a 2C cohort, its appearance across the S region and its later accumulation near 4C support the expected progression. After division, a new 2C cohort should appear if the cells remain viable and proliferative.

Synchronization refers to obtaining a population enriched for a particular cell-cycle stage. It can be achieved in different ways, including selection based on physical or biological properties or reversible arrest and release. The method itself may disturb the cycle. A reversible block may stress cells or alter their subsequent timing. Appropriate comparisons include an untreated population and verification that the cells can recover and complete the expected sequence. Synchrony also decays because individual cells do not all progress at exactly the same rate. Consequently, the movement of a synchronized cohort is informative but not necessarily an exact representation of unperturbed phase durations.

A short pulse of EdU or BrdU marks cells actively replicating DNA during the exposure, provided the assay detects nuclear incorporation and controls relevant background. The labeling index is the number of labeled cells divided by the number of cells assessed. In a sufficiently short pulse, this can approximate the fraction actively in S phase. A long pulse labels cells that enter S during exposure as well as those already in S, producing a larger value. If replication is partially inhibited, reduced incorporation may mean slower synthesis per cell rather than an equivalent reduction in the fraction of cells that have entered S. This is why incorporation intensity and labeling fraction should not be conflated.

The mitotic index is the number of cells identified as mitotic divided by the total number of cells examined. In a root-tip preparation, the numerator may be scored using chromosome morphology. It is often reported as a percentage. A low mitotic index can indicate that mitosis is short compared with the other phases or that many cells are noncycling. A high index can arise because cells enter mitosis frequently, because mitosis takes longer, or because cells cannot leave mitosis. A spindle-disrupting intervention can therefore raise the mitotic index even as the rate of successful cell division falls.

Classical pulse–chase reasoning provides an additional way to estimate phase intervals. In an ideal synchronized or appropriately sampled population, a short pulse labels an S-phase cohort. Cells that were near the end of S at the time of labeling are among the first labeled cells to enter mitosis. The delay to their appearance provides information about the postsynthetic interval, subject to transit time, detection threshold and variability. Following labeled mitotic figures across time provides further information about the breadth of the labeled cohort. In an actual asynchronous population, quantitative interpretation requires an explicit model rather than assigning every observed delay to a single exact phase length.

Single-cell observations offer a different way to study timing. A cell can be followed through successive phase-associated events, and elapsed times measured directly. This avoids some ambiguity from population proportions but introduces the need for reliable event definitions and tracking. Population DNA-content analysis and single-cell time-lapse observations answer complementary questions. Combining them can reveal whether a high phase fraction represents longer residence, altered entry, failed exit or a change in the mixture of proliferating and nonproliferating cells.

13. Quantitative mastery: amounts, fractions and populations

The most reliable method for a calculation is to write the biological assumptions first. Determine whether the organism is haploid, diploid or another ploidy; identify the reference C amount; decide whether the question counts a whole cell, a single nucleus, one spindle pole or a population; and distinguish whether the number of cells is changing. All calculations in this section are illustrative rather than experimental data.

Worked example 1 — chromosomes versus DNA molecules. A diploid organism has 2n = 12. In G1, its nucleus contains 12 chromosomes, 12 intact chromosomal DNA duplexes and 2C DNA. At metaphase, S is complete, so there are still 12 chromosomes but each contains two sister chromatids: 12 × 2 = 24 chromosomal DNA duplexes, and DNA content is 4C. At anaphase the sisters become independent chromosomes. The entire undivided cell contains 24 daughter chromosomes and 4C DNA. Each pole receives 24/2 = 12 chromosomes and 4C/2 = 2C. After cytokinesis each daughter therefore has the original diploid complement. Notice that the chromosome count increases at sister disjunction without an increase in DNA mass.

Worked example 2 — converting DNA mass. If a haploid genome contains 3.2 pg of nuclear DNA, C = 3.2 pg. A diploid G1 nucleus contains 2 × 3.2 = 6.4 pg; a completed G2 nucleus contains 4 × 3.2 = 12.8 pg. A measured nuclear DNA content of 9.6 pg corresponds to 9.6/3.2 = 3C. Under the normal diploid S-phase model the fraction of the replication requirement completed is (9.6 − 6.4)/(12.8 − 6.4) = 3.2/6.4 = 0.5. Thus 50% of the additional DNA mass has been synthesized. A picogram is 10−12 grams. No chromosome number can be inferred without the organism's karyotype.

Worked example 3 — fluorescence interpolation. In a calibrated linear DNA assay, the 2C peak is at 80 fluorescence units and the 4C peak at 160. A singlet from the same diploid population is measured at 140 units. Its relative DNA content is (140/80) × 2C = 3.5C. The fraction of nuclear replication completed is (140 − 80)/(160 − 80) = 60/80 = 0.75, or 75%. The relation follows by subtracting the starting DNA and dividing the added DNA by the amount needed to reach the completed state. This assumes a linear response, appropriate background correction and no abnormal DNA gain or loss. It does not show that 75% of the time spent in S has elapsed.

Worked example 4 — phase fractions. Suppose 10,000 valid singlet events are classified by an ideal histogram as 5,800 at G0/G1 DNA content, 2,700 in S and 1,500 in G2/M. The percentages are 100 × 5800/10000 = 58%, 27% and 15% respectively, summing to 100%. If additional measurements show that 400 cells are mitotic, the mitotic index relative to the whole analyzed population is 4%. Under the stated classification and matching denominator, the estimated G2 count is 1,500 − 400 = 1,100, or 11%. Without that additional measurement, the 15% G2/M region cannot be divided reliably.

Worked example 5 — estimating phase times in a simplified model. Let T be the total cycle duration and ti the duration of phase i. In a model in which cycling cells are sampled uniformly over cycle age, and all cells have the same phase durations, the probability of finding a cell in phase i is the fraction of the cycle occupied by that phase: fi = ti/T. Therefore ti = fiT. If T = 20 h and the stipulated uniform-age sample has 45% G1, 35% S, 15% G2 and 5% M, the estimated durations are 9 h, 7 h, 3 h and 1 h. Their sum is 20 h. This common examination approximation should not be presented as a law for every growing population.

An exponentially expanding asynchronous population has more recently born cells than old cells because each successful division generates two daughters. Even with identical cycle duration T, its age distribution is not uniform. In the ideal deterministic binary-division model with no death, a population grows as exp(kt), where k = ln 2/T. The number born at a time a hours before sampling is proportional to exp(−ka), and survivors of that cohort remain in the age interval from 0 to T. Normalizing gives an age density p(a) = 2k exp(−ka), because the integral of exp(−ka) from 0 to T is (1 − exp(−kT))/k = 1/(2k). A phase spanning ages a to b then has fraction 2[exp(−ka) − exp(−kb)]. This optional correction is useful for understanding why a snapshot fraction need not equal a duration fraction; the model is not valid when cycle durations vary greatly or cells enter and leave quiescence.

Worked example 6 — age-distribution correction. In that ideal exponentially growing model, a phase occupies the first half of a fixed 20-hour cycle. Its population fraction is 2(1 − exp(−ln2/2)) = 2(1 − 1/√2) ≈ 0.586, or 58.6%, not 50%. The last half contains about 41.4%. The asymmetry occurs because young cells are numerically overrepresented. When an examination explicitly assumes uniform sampling, use the simpler relation; when it specifies exponential age structure, apply the corresponding model.

Worked example 7 — cell numbers and total nuclear DNA. A culture starts with 200 diploid G1 cells. Assume every cell completes three synchronized divisions, with no death, fusion or quiescence. Each division doubles cell number, so after three divisions N = 200 × 2³ = 1,600 cells. Just after division each contains 2C, giving total nuclear DNA of 3,200C in the culture. Just before the third division there are 800 cells with 4C each, giving the same total 3,200C. The first two rounds of synthesis have increased the total DNA pool compared with the original 400C; the final cytokinesis merely redistributes the existing pool.

Worked example 8 — growth fraction and mitotic index. A tissue contains 2,000 viable nucleated cells, of which 500 belong to the actively cycling population. Microscopy identifies 50 mitotic figures. The mitotic index relative to all cells is 50/2000 = 2.5%. Relative only to the cycling cells it is 50/500 = 10%. If that cycling population is idealized as uniformly distributed over a 16-hour cycle, the estimated mitotic interval is 0.10 × 16 = 1.6 h. Using the whole-tissue 2.5% instead gives 0.4 h and incorrectly treats the noncycling cells as part of the active age distribution.

The exponential age formula above is not needed for every basic question. Its significance is conceptual: phase residence time and observed abundance are related through population dynamics, not simply identical. Whenever measurements are made in a real tissue, one must consider quiescence, death, variation in cycle duration and whether the sample is expanding or at steady size. The assumptions are as important as the arithmetic.

14. Reasoning from perturbations rather than memorizing phase labels

Imagine three cultures treated with different interventions. Initially each contains a similar mix of G1, S and G2/M cells. In culture A, DNA synthesis is prevented after cells begin replication. As cells reach the affected stage they can no longer move normally toward 4C. Some may accumulate near 2C and others at intermediate DNA contents; the pattern depends on the precise intervention and starting positions. A short precursor pulse showing reduced incorporation strengthens the conclusion that synthesis is affected. It would not establish whether the drug acts directly on a replication enzyme or indirectly through a shortage of nucleotide precursors.

In culture B, genome replication finishes, but the transition from G2 into mitosis is prevented. Cells can accumulate with 4C nuclear DNA while showing predominantly interphase nuclear morphology. In culture C, cells enter mitosis but are unable to complete chromosome segregation. They can also accumulate at 4C, this time with condensed chromosomes and a high mitotic index. The two DNA histograms might look nearly identical. Their difference becomes apparent only when phase-associated structural or molecular evidence is added. This comparison provides the simplest preparation for distinguishing G2/M controls from the spindle assembly checkpoint.

An untreated control is essential because phase proportions can change for reasons unrelated to the intervention, including nutritional depletion, density-dependent changes or handling stress. Matched sampling times help distinguish an initial transient response from later accumulation. A viability measurement helps establish whether loss of cells from a region represents progression, selective death or poor recovery of material. If a treatment produces an unexpected 8C population, one should first evaluate aggregate exclusion, then ask whether abnormal genome duplication or failed division is biologically plausible. A numerical peak is a starting observation, not a mechanism.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which a treated culture contains 20% G0/G1, 10% S and 70% G2/M at 12 hours, whereas a matched untreated culture contains 55%, 30% and 15%. Both samples consist of viable singlets. This result strongly supports accumulation of cells with approximately 4C DNA in the treated culture. It cannot by itself distinguish a G2 entry defect, an M exit defect, or other explanations for a high 4C fraction. If only 3% of treated cells display mitotic morphology, a predominately premitotic accumulation becomes more plausible than a widespread metaphase arrest. If instead 65% show condensed chromosomes, an M-phase accumulation becomes more plausible. In either case direct time-lapse observation and additional molecular evidence can test the proposed mechanism.

A useful related distinction concerns cell proliferation and cell number. The number of living cells in a tissue changes according to the balance of production, death and—in some tissues—movement into or out of the sampled region. An increased proportion of S-phase cells is not necessarily proof of a faster increase in total cell number. A long S phase can cause cells to accumulate even when completed divisions are less frequent. Conversely, rapid mitosis can produce a small mitotic fraction even in a highly proliferative population. The rate of completion must be assessed with measurements that follow cell numbers or track division events over time.

These arguments convert the phase diagram into an experimental reasoning tool. Start from a defined readout, identify what it directly measures, state the assumptions, and propose at least one alternative explanation. Then choose a second measurement that separates the alternatives. The same approach applies throughout cellular biology: a structural image, biochemical marker and functional assay each address different aspects of the underlying process.

15. The cycle in tissues and the logic of its control

Normal multicellular organization requires control of proliferation as well as the ability to divide. In a renewing epithelium, progenitor cells can generate replacements for cells lost at the surface. Differentiated cells can perform their tissue functions while dividing rarely or not at all. In a stable adult organ, cell production may balance cell loss, so the absence of net tissue growth does not imply the absence of cell divisions. In a developing organ, production may exceed loss, whereas during regeneration a change in proliferative activity contributes to restoration of tissue structure.

The sequence of phases is vulnerable to different kinds of disruption. A cell entering S without adequate resources may experience difficulties completing DNA replication. A cell entering mitosis with incomplete chromosomes may distribute damaged or unequal DNA complements. A cell that initiates segregation before correct orientation of chromosomes risks missegregation. A cell that completes nuclear division but fails cytokinesis may acquire an abnormal nuclear organization. These outcomes are biologically different even though all concern a failure of coordinated progression.

Cancer provides a familiar illustration of why cell-cycle regulation matters, but a cancer cell is not simply a cell with a short cycle. Tumor growth can involve altered proliferation signals, reduced cell loss, changes in checkpoint responses and other processes. Many tumor cells cycle slowly or are temporarily nonproliferative. The detailed molecular and tissue-level mechanisms belong to Unit 4. For this chapter, the useful conclusion is that accurate cell-number regulation requires both control of entry and fidelity of the individual division cycle.

A phase transition must depend on the appropriate biological conditions, not merely on the amount of time that has elapsed. The fusion experiments discussed earlier suggest that regulatory activities can be transferred through cytoplasm and that chromosome state influences responsiveness. This leads naturally to the next two questions: which molecules turn phase-specific events on and off, and how does a cell detect an unfinished or damaged process? Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases provide much of the first answer; checkpoint networks provide the second. In the following chapters these systems will be linked to the phase organization established here.

Before moving on, reconstruct the cycle without relying on its labels. Begin with an unreplicated nucleus. Explain the need for resources and permission to copy, the increase in DNA mass while chromosome units remain paired, the preparation for mechanical segregation, the change in chromosome count when sisters separate, and the restoration of the original DNA complement in each daughter. Then explain why a noncycling 2C cell, a cycling G1 cell, and a 2C cell of another ploidy may be indistinguishable in an isolated DNA measurement. A complete understanding of this foundation is the basis for reasoning about regulation rather than memorizing regulatory protein names.

16. Part B — Conceptual and assertion–reason practice

Original examination-style practice; these are not previous-year questions. Select the best answer before opening its explanation.

1. Which event defines the start of S phase in the standard cycle?

  1. Genome-wide nuclear DNA replication begins
  2. Chromosomes separate at the spindle
  3. Cytokinesis ends
  4. All protein synthesis begins
Answer and reasoning

A. S phase is the scheduled duplication of nuclear DNA. B is anaphase, C marks the division boundary, and protein synthesis is not restricted to S.

2. A normal diploid cell has 2n = 8. How many chromosomes are present in metaphase?

  1. 4
  2. 8
  3. 16
  4. 32
Answer and reasoning

B. Eight duplicated chromosome units remain. A is the haploid number, C is the number of sister chromatids, and D doubles that count again.

3. Which statement correctly distinguishes homologues and sister chromatids?

  1. Homologues are always identical
  2. Sisters are different parental homologues
  3. Sisters arise by replication of the same chromosome
  4. Both terms refer only to haploid cells
Answer and reasoning

C. Sisters are copies of one chromosome, whereas homologues carry corresponding loci and may have different alleles. A and B conflate distinct relationships; D incorrectly restricts them to haploidy.

4. Which is usually true of reversible G0 in a conventional diploid cell?

  1. The cell is necessarily dead
  2. DNA must be 4C
  3. It is an obligatory stage of mitosis
  4. It can re-enter the proliferative cycle under suitable conditions
Answer and reasoning

D. Reversibility defines quiescence. A is wrong because metabolism continues, B ignores the common 2C state, and C confuses exit with mitosis.

5. A 4C peak in a standard diploid DNA histogram includes which populations?

  1. G2 and M
  2. G0 and G1 only
  3. Only early S
  4. Only cells with tetraploid nuclei
Answer and reasoning

A. Both G2 and early M have replicated DNA. B corresponds to 2C, C contains intermediate values, and D confuses DNA amount with ploidy.

6. What changes immediately when sister chromatids disjoin?

  1. Total DNA doubles
  2. The whole-cell number of independent chromosome units increases
  3. Each pole becomes tetraploid
  4. DNA polymerase starts S phase
Answer and reasoning

B. Independent chromosome count changes without new DNA synthesis. A and D mistake segregation for replication; C ignores distribution of a diploid set to each pole.

7. In many early embryonic cleavage cycles, why can the gap phases be very short?

  1. Nuclear DNA is unnecessary
  2. Chromosomes do not replicate
  3. Maternal stores support rapid alternation of replication and division
  4. Mitosis is always replaced by meiosis
Answer and reasoning

C. Maternal resources reduce the need for long preparatory growth intervals. A and B contradict genome inheritance; D is not the cleavage mechanism.

8. Which measurement directly reports the amount of nuclear DNA in a calibrated DNA-content experiment?

  1. Number of mitotic figures
  2. Ki-67 positivity alone
  3. Cell diameter
  4. DNA-associated fluorescence per singlet
Answer and reasoning

D. Stoichiometric DNA staining gives a relative DNA quantity. A reports morphology, B a proliferation-associated marker and C size, none of which directly measures DNA content.

9. Assertion: S phase doubles DNA content in a normal diploid cell. Reason: Replication makes sister DNA copies while chromosome units remain associated.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Copying each chromosome produces the second DNA complement. Sister association explains why the doubling does not double chromosome number. B is incorrect because the reason describes the copying mechanism; C and D reject true statements.

10. Which statement about a high mitotic index is defensible?

  1. It always means faster division
  2. It may result from delayed exit from mitosis
  3. It proves all cells are in S phase
  4. It is independent of the noncycling fraction
Answer and reasoning

B. Longer residence in mitosis can increase its observed fraction without successful division. A assumes residence is unchanged, C confuses phase identities and D ignores the denominator.

17. Part C — Quantitative and experimental interpretation

Original examination-style practice; these are not previous-year questions. Select the best answer before opening its explanation.

1. An organism has 2n = 16 and 1C = 2 pg. What are the chromosome count and DNA amount per undivided cell in metaphase?

  1. 32 and 4 pg
  2. 16 and 4 pg
  3. 16 and 8 pg
  4. 32 and 8 pg
Answer and reasoning

C. Metaphase has 2n = 16 duplicated chromosomes and 4C = 8 pg. A and D count chromatids as chromosomes; B uses G1 DNA amount.

2. For the same organism, at normal anaphase what is the count per spindle pole?

  1. 8 chromosomes, 2 pg
  2. 32 chromosomes, 8 pg
  3. 16 chromosomes, 8 pg
  4. 16 chromosomes, 4 pg
Answer and reasoning

D. Each pole receives 2n = 16 chromosomes and 2C = 4 pg. A describes a haploid set, B describes the whole undivided cell, and C assigns the entire DNA pool to one pole.

3. A linear DNA assay has 2C at 120 units and 4C at 240 units. A singlet at 210 units has completed what fraction of normal diploid replication?

  1. 75%
  2. 87.5%
  3. 50%
  4. 25%
Answer and reasoning

A. (210 − 120)/(240 − 120) = 90/120 = 0.75. B divides by final total fluorescence rather than the added amount. C and D use incorrect intervals.

4. A sample contains 4,000 valid cells: 2,000 at 2C, 1,200 between peaks and 800 at 4C. Independent morphology identifies 200 mitotic cells, all in the 4C class. What percentage is G2?

  1. 20%
  2. 15%
  3. 5%
  4. 30%
Answer and reasoning

B. G2 count = 800 − 200 = 600; 600/4000 = 15%. A includes M, C is the mitotic fraction, D is S.

5. A treated diploid sample accumulates 4C events with diffuse interphase chromatin; the untreated sample does not. Which is most consistent?

  1. Direct proof of a particular CDK mutation
  2. Increased completed cytokinesis
  3. Delay before mitosis after replication
  4. All cells are in G0
Answer and reasoning

C. The 4C interphase morphology supports G2 accumulation. A overclaims a molecular cause, B would tend to restore 2C daughters, and D is inconsistent with ordinary G0 DNA content.

6. A 4C histogram peak grows after treatment; microscopy shows many metaphase-like cells. Which additional conclusion is warranted?

  1. S never occurs
  2. DNA has necessarily been copied twice
  3. All 4C events are tetraploid G1
  4. Mitotic residence or arrest is a plausible explanation
Answer and reasoning

D. Morphology supports M-phase accumulation, potentially prolonged residence. A is inconsistent with prior replication, B is not implied by 4C, and C ignores mitotic morphology.

7. A short DNA-label pulse is followed by a chase. Labeled nuclei appear in mitosis after a delay. What does this support?

  1. S and mitosis are separated by a postsynthetic transit interval
  2. DNA is synthesized only during mitosis
  3. All G1 cells were labeled during the pulse
  4. The label disappears as soon as S ends
Answer and reasoning

A. A labeled S cohort later reaches M, supporting temporal ordering and an intervening interval under the tested conditions. B and C conflict with pulse specificity; D conflicts with retained label.

8. When S and G1 nuclei share cytoplasm, G1 may begin synthesis; a G2 nucleus exposed to S cytoplasm normally does not repeat an entire S phase. What follows?

  1. Replication is controlled only by age
  2. Diffusible activity and nuclear competence both matter
  3. G2 DNA is absent
  4. The G1 and G2 nuclei have identical replication competence
Answer and reasoning

B. The different responses imply cytoplasmic promotion and phase-specific nuclear competence. A ignores transferable influence, C is false and D contradicts the different outcomes.

9. A culture starts with 125 diploid G1 cells and undergoes four complete synchronous divisions without loss. Final cell number and total nuclear DNA just after division?

  1. 500, 1000C
  2. 1000, 2000C
  3. 2000, 4000C
  4. 2000, 8000C
Answer and reasoning

C. 125 × 2⁴ = 2000; each daughter has 2C, total 4000C. A and B use too few divisions; D assigns 4C to each postdivision G1 cell.

10. A phase lasts the first half of a deterministic cycle in a balanced exponentially expanding binary population. What fraction is expected under the age-density model in this chapter?

  1. 25%
  2. 41.4%
  3. 50%
  4. 58.6%
Answer and reasoning

D. 2(1 − 1/√2) ≈ 0.586. A is unrelated, B is the last half, and C assumes uniform ages.

11. A tissue has 1,000 cells, 250 actively cycling, 25 mitotic. With a uniform-age cycling population and T = 12 h, estimate M duration.

  1. 1.2 h
  2. 0.3 h
  3. 3 h
  4. 2.5 h
Answer and reasoning

A. Within the cycling fraction fM = 25/250 = 0.10; 0.10 × 12 = 1.2 h. B uses the whole tissue denominator. C treats the growth fraction as M fraction; D mistakes a percentage for time.

12. Two G1 cells attached to each other pass a DNA detector together. What is the strongest concern?

  1. Their nuclei necessarily fuse
  2. They can mimic a 4C singlet event
  3. They must be in S
  4. DNA fluorescence must vanish
Answer and reasoning

B. Summed signals can resemble 4C. Singlet discrimination helps exclude aggregates. A is not necessary, C confuses amount with synthesis and D contradicts the signal addition.

13. A cell completes normal nuclear division but fails cytokinesis. What is the immediate usual outcome?

  1. Two separate haploid cells
  2. One 8C nucleus
  3. One binucleate cell with two 2C diploid nuclei
  4. A normal single 2C cell
Answer and reasoning

C. Normal segregation gives two 2C nuclei but failed partition leaves them in one cell. A wrongly assumes reduction, B implies extra synthesis or fusion, and D overlooks the second nucleus.

14. A 2C cell and another 2C cell are indistinguishable by DNA histogram. One resumes S after mitogen stimulation; the other does not. Which inference is justified?

  1. Both are necessarily terminally differentiated
  2. Both are necessarily actively in S
  3. DNA staining directly determines reversibility
  4. Re-entry behavior provides information that DNA amount cannot
Answer and reasoning

D. The assay distinguishes responses despite identical DNA amount. A is contradicted by re-entry, B by the ordinary 2C state and C by the identical histogram readings. Failure to re-enter under one condition alone does not establish terminal differentiation.
