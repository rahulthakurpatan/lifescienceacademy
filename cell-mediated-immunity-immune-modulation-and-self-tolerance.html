1. Why immune defense must distinguish a target from its surroundings

A virus that has entered a living epithelial cell presents a different defensive problem from a virus that is still moving through extracellular fluid. In the fluid, an antibody may bind a viral surface molecule and prevent attachment to a susceptible cell. Once viral proteins are being synthesized inside a host cell, circulating antibodies generally cannot enter the cytoplasm in sufficient quantities to neutralize that intracellular machinery. The host has to recognize a change in the cell itself, limit the production of new virus particles, and sometimes remove the infected cell altogether. Removing a host cell is a costly action because that cell belongs to the organism. The ability to recognize which cells should be eliminated, while leaving their uninfected neighbors functional, is therefore a central problem of cell-mediated immunity.

Consider a second problem. A macrophage has engulfed a bacterium, but the bacterium survives inside the membrane-bound compartment in which it was captured. The infected macrophage is already a defensive cell. Destroying it is not necessarily the most useful first action. A more efficient solution may be to strengthen the macrophage's own antimicrobial machinery. The lymphocyte that provides this help does not have to enter the phagosome or recognize the bacterium directly. It can receive information about the organism from the macrophage's surface and deliver signals that improve intracellular killing. The two problems illustrate distinct uses of adaptive T cells: removal of an infected host cell and improvement of the defensive capacity of another cell.

An effector is a cell or molecule that actually produces a biological consequence. In an adaptive immune response, a lymphocyte first recognizes an antigen, becomes activated, and differentiates. Its subsequent effector activity is distinct from the recognition and proliferation that produced it. A cytotoxic T lymphocyte, abbreviated CTL, is an activated T cell capable of initiating the death of a suitable target cell. Most conventional CTLs are derived from CD8-positive T cells. Helper T cells derived from conventional CD4-positive T cells instead frequently act through cytokine secretion and membrane-associated signals. The term helper does not mean that these lymphocytes are dispensable. Their ability to coordinate other cells can determine whether recognition of an organism results in successful elimination.

Cell-mediated immunity refers especially to adaptive immune functions that depend on T cells and their coordinated cellular effectors. The expression does not imply that every reaction performed by a cell belongs exclusively to this category. Antibody-coated targets can be ingested by phagocytes or attacked by natural killer cells; those reactions depend on antibody recognition even though a cell carries out the effector step. Conversely, soluble cytokines released by T cells may operate without sustained physical contact. The important distinction is which recognition system directs the response and how that recognition is translated into an effective biological action.

The antigen receptor of a conventional alpha-beta T cell detects a molecular complex rather than a free protein. A fragment of a protein, called a peptide, is displayed by a major histocompatibility complex molecule, abbreviated MHC. Most nucleated mammalian cells display class I MHC molecules with peptides sampled from intracellular protein turnover. Professional antigen-presenting cells also display class II MHC molecules, which frequently carry peptides derived from material acquired into endosomal compartments. The rules are not absolute because cross-presentation and additional processing routes connect compartments. Nevertheless, the distinction provides a reliable starting point for understanding why conventional CD8 T cells survey many tissues and CD4 T cells frequently direct the activities of specialized antigen-presenting cells.

The molecular identity of a displayed peptide is not sufficient by itself to decide whether a lymphocyte should attack. Many self-derived peptides are continually displayed even in healthy tissues. The lymphocyte repertoire contains antigen receptors generated by genetic recombination, so self-reactive receptors can arise as an unavoidable consequence of producing diverse recognition structures. Selection during development removes or redirects many dangerous lymphocytes, but it does not guarantee that no self-reactive lymphocyte ever reaches a peripheral organ. Additional controls must operate after development. These controls explain why recognition, activation and attack are separate biological decisions.

An immune response must also have a termination phase. The disappearance of an organism does not instantly eliminate the many activated cells that arose during its infection. Nor does cessation of an activating signal automatically remove soluble mediators already present in tissues. If activated lymphocytes continue dividing, secreting cytokines or recruiting phagocytes after the threat has been removed, the tissue may suffer more damage from the defense than from the original disturbance. Immune modulation therefore encompasses increases and decreases in immune activity, adjustments in its location and duration, and changes in the relative contributions of different cell populations. Self-tolerance is the condition in which the immune system avoids damaging responses against the organism's own constituents. These properties are interconnected, but they are not interchangeable: a response may be appropriately directed against a foreign antigen yet still be excessive.

2. How experiments established antigen-specific cellular defense

Early immune experiments often relied on the transfer of protective activity between animals. If cell-free serum obtained from an immune animal protected another animal, a soluble substance could account for at least part of that protection. If protection instead required viable lymphocytes, a cellular mechanism became a plausible explanation. Transfer experiments do not establish that an immune response is exclusively cellular or exclusively humoral. Rather, they test whether the material being transferred contains a component sufficient to reproduce a particular measured effect under the conditions of the experiment.

The discovery of MHC restriction provided a way to define the target of T-cell recognition. In 1974, Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty reported experiments using lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in mice [1]. Cytotoxic lymphocytes generated during infection were tested against infected target cells from different genetic backgrounds. A central observation was that viral infection alone was not sufficient for efficient recognition: the appropriate relationship between effector and target MHC was also required. These observations helped replace the idea of a T cell responding merely to a viral molecular structure with the modern concept of recognition of a peptide in an appropriate self-MHC context.

An instructive way to reconstruct the logic is to consider four groups of target cells. Let the responding lymphocytes come from an immunized mouse expressing class I allele A. Targets may express allele A or allele B, and each may be infected or uninfected. A model in which the T cell responds to the viral antigen independently of MHC predicts that both infected groups will be susceptible. A model in which the T cell recognizes allele A independently of infection predicts that both allele-A groups will be susceptible. A conventional antigen-specific restricted CTL is instead expected to attack mainly the infected allele-A group. The other three groups test different alternative explanations. This four-group matrix is a simplified teaching reconstruction; it is not a transcription of measured values from the historical paper.

A complication arises if the effector population is not a pure antigen-specific T-cell population. Activated lymphocytes may include cells capable of reacting to foreign MHC molecules, and natural killer cells can attack certain targets without a rearranged peptide-specific T-cell receptor. An experimentally observed attack on an MHC-mismatched target therefore does not automatically invalidate MHC restriction. It may indicate an additional effector population or an alloreactive T-cell clone. The investigator must define the cell population, measure the relevant receptor expression, and include appropriate negative controls before interpreting exceptions.

Genetic disruption experiments subsequently distinguished recognition from the molecular execution of cytotoxicity. In 1994, Kägi and colleagues reported that cytotoxic activity mediated by T cells and natural killer cells was substantially impaired in mice lacking perforin [2]. This provides causal evidence that a cytotoxic granule component is important. It does not imply that every target in every tissue becomes completely resistant when perforin is absent. Alternative killing mechanisms can contribute, and different target populations vary in susceptibility. The contrast between loss of recognition and loss of execution remains useful: a perforin-deficient CTL may still recognize its peptide–MHC ligand, but it cannot execute normal perforin-dependent granule-mediated killing.

The history of immune regulation illustrates a complementary experimental strategy. Instead of asking what is necessary to produce an attack, investigators asked whether a defined cell population was necessary to restrain attack. In 1995, Shimon Sakaguchi and colleagues reported experiments identifying a CD4-positive, CD25-positive population that contributed to maintenance of immunological self-tolerance [3]. Removing this population from transferred T cells permitted pathological immune responses in recipient mice, while the regulatory population could prevent such effects in appropriate transfer settings. This established that self-tolerance is not merely the passive consequence of eliminating every autoreactive cell during development. Some lymphocytes actively restrain other lymphocytes in the periphery.

Two independent reports in 1995 demonstrated the severe immune consequences of disruption of the gene encoding CTLA-4 in mice [4], [5]. This type of observation reverses a common assumption: an immune receptor that inhibits activation is not necessarily an undesirable obstacle to defense. Its absence can produce uncontrolled lymphocyte expansion and destructive tissue inflammation. The relevant comparison is not simply whether knockout lymphocytes proliferate more than normal cells. The decisive organism-level question is whether proliferation remains appropriate in time and location.

3. From primed CD8 T cells to recognition of peripheral targets

A naive CD8 T cell and a differentiated CTL can express the same antigen-specific T-cell receptor but behave very differently after binding antigen. The naive cell generally needs signals from an appropriately activated antigen-presenting cell. Antigen presentation, costimulation and cytokines cooperate to initiate proliferation and differentiation. A differentiated CTL already contains much of the machinery needed for immediate effector activity. It can therefore respond to peptide–MHC on a target that would not be effective at priming naive T cells. This distinction resolves the apparent paradox that infected epithelial cells, which usually do not provide the full costimulatory context required for priming, can nonetheless be killed by antigen-specific effector T cells.

Dendritic cells help bridge the anatomical separation between priming and attack. They can acquire antigen from infected tissues and activate T cells in draining lymphoid organs. If the dendritic cell itself synthesizes microbial protein, the conventional intracellular route can supply peptides to class I MHC. If the antigen is acquired from another infected cell, cross-presentation may instead deliver suitable peptides to class I molecules. Cross-presentation is a mechanism of peptide display; cross-priming is the resulting activation of naive CD8 T cells when the other necessary conditions are satisfied. A dendritic cell may cross-present antigen without producing effective immunity if costimulation is insufficient or the surrounding environment is tolerogenic.

Once differentiation has occurred, effector cells leave lymphoid sites and circulate or migrate into tissues. A chemokine gradient can help direct their movement, and adhesion molecules facilitate passage through endothelium and contacts within tissue. These mechanisms determine access, whereas the T-cell receptor determines molecular specificity. A chemokine receptor defect can reduce the number of CTLs reaching an infected tissue without preventing those same cells from killing appropriate targets when the cells are brought together in a culture assay. This type of comparison distinguishes a trafficking defect from a defect intrinsic to effector function.

At the target interface, a conventional T-cell receptor recognizes features contributed by both peptide and MHC. CD8 acts as a coreceptor by interacting with a relatively conserved portion of class I MHC and by helping position signaling machinery close to the engaged receptor complex. CD8 does not provide peptide-specific recognition by itself. An isolated CD8 molecule cannot replace a TCR with an unrelated binding specificity. Conversely, the contribution of CD8 to a particular response varies with the properties of the TCR–peptide–MHC interaction. It is therefore incorrect to assume that loss of CD8 causes precisely the same quantitative reduction for every clone under all assay conditions.

A target cell displays many different peptide–MHC complexes at the same time. Only a fraction may contain the cognate antigen, meaning the peptide–MHC ligand recognized by the particular CTL. The abundance of this ligand depends on antigen synthesis and degradation, processing, peptide transport, binding to class I MHC and surface persistence. A cell that produces abundant viral protein need not display a correspondingly large amount of every viral peptide. Different epitopes can be produced with different efficiencies. This makes the density of cognate peptide–MHC a more direct recognition variable than the total amount of viral protein inside a cell.

Recognition has a physical as well as chemical component. Protein–protein binding depends on complementarity of molecular surfaces and the sum of reversible noncovalent interactions. Contact between two moving cells also depends on adhesion and membrane organization. Integrins such as LFA-1 on the lymphocyte can bind ICAM molecules and help stabilize productive contact. This interaction is not a substitute for the specific antigen receptor, but it gives that receptor time and spatial opportunity to generate signals. If a mutation reduces adhesion, short contacts may become insufficient for normal degranulation even though the TCR still binds its ligand in a purified binding assay.

The organized interface between a lymphocyte and its interacting cell is called an immunological synapse. Its components include antigen receptors, coreceptors, adhesion proteins and the cytoskeleton that supports localized secretion. The arrangement is dynamic; granule release can sometimes precede full centrosome polarization. An idealized pattern of a central receptor region surrounded by an adhesion ring is a useful representation of some mature contacts, not a universal architecture that every successful killing event must display. The essential functional point is the coupling of recognition at the membrane to directed delivery of effector molecules. The next stage of the response depends on that spatial coupling.

4. The perforin–granzyme pathway: delivering a death signal

A cytotoxic granule is a specialized membrane-bound compartment within an effector lymphocyte. It combines features of a secretory organelle and a lysosome. Unlike an ordinary lysosome whose contents are primarily used within its own cell, the cytotoxic granule stores molecules that can be exported during interaction with an appropriate target. Perforin is a pore-forming protein and granzymes are proteolytic enzymes. Their functions complement one another: perforin helps overcome a membrane barrier, whereas granzymes alter proteins inside the target cell. The destructive molecules must be stored and delivered in a way that avoids premature activity against the lymphocyte producing them.

The granule environment is acidic, and its contents are organized in association with negatively charged matrix components such as the proteoglycan serglycin. Granule packaging, processing of proteases and membrane separation all contribute to safe storage. The extracellular medium differs chemically from the granule lumen. When secreted, perforin encounters calcium-containing fluid at a more nearly neutral pH. These conditions favor its association with target membranes and formation of functional assemblies. Thus the change in compartment is a functional part of secretion: molecules that are safely sequestered in one environment become active in another. A defect that changes storage or processing might reduce killing even when synthesis of the relevant protein remains detectable.

Recognition of antigen stimulates rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton and helps orient the secretory apparatus toward the contact. Microtubules contribute to the transport of cytotoxic granules, which accumulate in the region facing the target. The response also depends on docking, priming and membrane fusion. These events are regulated rather than being a simple rupture of the granule membrane. A rise in intracellular calcium in the effector cell contributes to regulated exocytosis. The membranes of secretory granules fuse with the plasma membrane of the lymphocyte, exposing their luminal contents to the space between the two cells. The fusion event does not directly mix the cytoplasm of the lymphocyte with the cytoplasm of the target.

Polarized secretion raises the local concentration of cytotoxic molecules while limiting the total amount that escapes into the surrounding tissue. It also creates a mechanistic relationship between cell contact and delivery. A lymphocyte may contain normal amounts of perforin and granzyme B but be unable to release them at the contact because its trafficking or fusion machinery is defective. Such a cell differs fundamentally from one lacking the TCR ligand or from one unable to synthesize the effector proteins. In a degranulation assay, surface exposure of a normally granule-associated membrane protein such as CD107a can provide evidence that fusion occurred. This readout does not establish that perforin was functional or that the recipient cell received cytosolic granzymes.

Perforin contains a membrane-interacting domain that is important for calcium-dependent membrane association and a pore-forming region related to the membrane attack complex/perforin family. Multiple molecules can associate within a membrane to form a pore. The pore changes the permeability of the membrane, which can allow ions to move according to their electrochemical gradients. Perforin must not be confused with the terminal complement membrane attack complex. Both involve pore-forming proteins, but complement assembly takes place through its extracellular activation cascade, whereas perforin is supplied by a cytotoxic lymphocyte. Eliminating a complement protein does not directly remove the perforin stored in a CTL.

A simplified textbook diagram often shows granzyme moving through a perforin-generated opening in the target membrane. The central idea is that perforin promotes delivery of granzyme to the target cytosol, not that the granzyme freely crosses an intact lipid bilayer. Experimental work has also demonstrated a pathway involving transient plasma membrane damage, uptake of perforin and granzyme into vesicles, and perforin-dependent escape of granzyme from the endosomal compartment [12], [13]. The exact route can depend on the experimental system. For examination reasoning, avoid claiming that all physiological granzyme delivery necessarily occurs by direct passage through a large permanent plasma membrane hole.

Once a granzyme reaches the target cytoplasm it can cleave suitable protein substrates. Granzyme B is a serine protease with a preference for cleavage after particular aspartate residues in its substrates. Its actions can activate executioner caspases and can promote mitochondrial amplification through cleavage of Bid, a proapoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family. Caspases are themselves proteolytic enzymes that dismantle selected cellular components. The target cell, rather than the lymphocyte, contains much of the machinery that executes the final programmed death. Not every granzyme acts on the same substrates or requires exactly the same death pathway, and the details can differ between species.

A cell undergoing apoptosis commonly exhibits chromatin condensation, cell shrinkage and membrane blebbing, followed by removal of cellular material by phagocytes. The membrane often remains sufficiently intact during early apoptosis to prevent immediate uncontrolled release of cytosolic contents. This contrasts with rapid lytic injury. The distinction is important at tissue scale: a targeted death that can be followed by efficient clearance may disrupt fewer neighboring cells than indiscriminate destruction. However, apoptosis is not a guarantee that inflammation cannot occur. The response depends on the nature of the infection, clearance of dying cells and accompanying cytokine secretion.

After delivering a sufficient stimulus, a CTL can detach from the target before the target completes its death program. It can then engage additional susceptible cells. This property is sometimes described as serial killing. The capacity is not unlimited: the lymphocyte requires adequate effector stores, metabolic resources and access to additional targets, and its responses may change during prolonged antigen exposure. A single CTL is not consumed in every successful attack, so a one-to-one numerical correspondence between lymphocyte number and killed target number is not a general law. Conversely, not every contact with a target leads to commitment to death.

There are several experimentally distinguishable points of failure in this pathway. If antigen recognition fails, antigen-triggered signaling and degranulation can both be reduced. If membrane fusion fails, upstream activation may remain normal while delivery is impaired. If perforin is defective, degranulation may be detectable without efficient cytosolic granzyme action. If an inhibitory protein in the target prevents the action of a particular granzyme, recognition and delivery may proceed normally but target death may be reduced. The stages form a causal sequence, but a measurement at any one stage should not be treated as a complete measure of all the others.

Suppose an inhibitor reduces calcium availability outside the two interacting cells. A reduced level of killing cannot be interpreted immediately as specific evidence that perforin no longer forms pores. Calcium can also influence lymphocyte activation, secretion, target membrane repair and adhesion under certain conditions. An investigator could separate the possibilities by measuring effector degranulation independently of target apoptosis and by testing a condition in which already secreted effector molecules are examined. The significance of a reagent follows from its effects on the relevant system, not simply from the name of the molecule to which it is usually associated.

5. Fas–FasL signaling and alternative routes to target-cell death

The second major contact-dependent strategy used by CTLs is to activate a death receptor on the surface of the target. Fas, also named CD95 or APO-1, belongs to the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily. It possesses an extracellular region that recognizes its ligand and a cytoplasmic region that can participate in death-signaling complexes. Fas ligand, abbreviated FasL or CD95L, is a corresponding member of the TNF ligand family that can be expressed by activated T cells. Recognition of cognate antigen helps focus the action of the T cell, but the actual activation of Fas requires the presence of a suitable Fas molecule on the target.

Fas engagement promotes the assembly of a signaling complex that recruits the adaptor FADD through death-domain interactions. FADD in turn supports recruitment of initiator procaspase-8 through complementary protein-interaction domains. Concentrating appropriate procaspase molecules within such a complex favors their activation. Active initiator caspases can then activate downstream executioner caspases and, in some cell types, engage mitochondrial amplification. It is not necessary for Fas ligand to cross the plasma membrane of the target or enter its nucleus. The extracellular ligand changes the assembly of signaling proteins on the cytoplasmic side of the receptor.

The logic of Fas signaling differs from that of the perforin–granzyme pathway. In granule-mediated killing, the effector cell exports proteins that must ultimately influence intracellular substrates in the target. In Fas-mediated killing, the effector cell engages a surface receptor that transmits a death signal through the target's own machinery. Both can converge on apoptosis, but convergence at a late stage does not make their upstream events identical. A caspase measurement, for example, may show that the target is dying without establishing which of the two routes initiated death.

Fas expression is an important limiting condition. A target with very little functional Fas can remain susceptible to granzyme-mediated killing if it displays the cognate antigen and supports granzyme entry. Conversely, a target lacking susceptibility to perforin-mediated granule delivery may still die when an activated CTL engages Fas, provided the receptor and downstream signaling remain functional. Resistance to one pathway therefore cannot be generalized automatically to complete resistance to the effector cell. Some targets resist both pathways, and cytokines secreted by the effector may still change their behavior in the absence of direct cytotoxicity.

Fas ligand is not exclusively a weapon used against infected cells. Fas-mediated apoptosis also contributes to regulation of some activated lymphocyte populations. Repeated stimulation can increase susceptibility to death-receptor-mediated elimination under suitable conditions. This illustrates how a molecular system used to execute an immune response can also help restrict its duration and amplitude. The biological consequence depends on which cell expresses the receptor, which expresses the ligand, whether they meet, and how the intracellular death machinery is regulated. The same named receptor can thus participate in both defense and homeostasis.

A useful experimental design compares wild-type effector cells with perforin-deficient cells against targets that either express functional Fas or lack it. A reduction when only perforin is absent supports a contribution from the granule pathway. A further reduction when Fas is also absent from the target supports a contribution from the death-receptor route. The informative result is the pattern across conditions, not a claim that the percentages contributed by the two mechanisms must sum arithmetically to one hundred. The same cell may be susceptible to either route, and the rates and probabilities of the two pathways can interact.

The interpretation also depends on the time at which cytotoxicity is measured. Granule-mediated killing can be initiated rapidly because differentiated CTLs have preformed effector contents. Fas-dependent killing may have different kinetics depending on ligand expression, receptor density and the target's intracellular machinery. A comparison at an early time point may underestimate a slower pathway. At a late time point, spontaneous target death may become substantial, reducing the ability to distinguish specific killing. Appropriate spontaneous-death controls and time courses are therefore integral to interpreting such an experiment.

The death receptor family illustrates an important exception to simplistic interpretations of ligand binding. Expression of a receptor does not prove that its signal will cause cell death. The downstream proteins required to transmit the signal may be absent, inhibited or subject to alternative regulation. Similarly, expression of FasL on an activated lymphocyte does not demonstrate that a particular target died through Fas. Functional perturbations are required to assign the actual causal contribution. For an examination question, distinguish a necessary component of a proposed pathway from a marker merely associated with the cells involved.

6. Comparing adaptive CTL killing, NK cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent defense

Cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells share the ability to secrete perforin and granzymes, but their recognition systems are different. A conventional CTL responds to a particular peptide–MHC complex using its rearranged T-cell receptor. Natural killer cells integrate signals from multiple germline-encoded activating and inhibitory receptors. In many settings, recognition of normal class I MHC by inhibitory NK receptors reduces the likelihood of attack. Reduced expression of relevant class I ligands can remove this inhibition, but loss of an inhibitory ligand is not always sufficient to produce cytotoxicity because activating signals and cellular context also matter.

The comparison reveals a complementarity between the two mechanisms. A virus-infected cell that reduces surface MHC I may become less visible to a conventional CD8 T cell. At the same time, it may become more susceptible to an NK response if it has lost inhibitory ligands and retains sufficient activating signals. It would be misleading to state that every class-I-low cell is automatically killed by every NK cell. NK receptors differ in specificity, normal tissues can vary in their expression of ligands, and mechanisms of NK education influence responsiveness. The key prediction is a possible shift in the balance of recognition between the adaptive and innate cytotoxic compartments.

A different route to NK-cell cytotoxicity uses antibodies. An IgG antibody can recognize an antigen on a cell surface through its Fab regions, leaving its Fc region available for binding by Fc receptors. CD16, an Fc receptor expressed by many NK cells, can respond to appropriately clustered IgG on a target. This phenomenon is called antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity or ADCC. The antibody determines antigen specificity, while the NK cell supplies cytotoxic effector machinery. No conventional peptide–MHC recognition by a rearranged NK-cell antigen receptor is required. It is therefore an example of cooperation between humoral recognition and an innate cellular effector.

Antibody-mediated neutralization is mechanistically different from ADCC. In neutralization, binding prevents a pathogen or its product from performing a necessary biological interaction. For example, an antibody may obstruct a viral surface protein required for cell entry. Neutralization does not necessarily require direct death of the cell displaying an antigen. Opsonization instead makes a target easier for a phagocyte to recognize and ingest. Complement-dependent cytotoxicity uses a soluble cascade that can damage susceptible membranes after suitable activation. Although all of these outcomes may reduce the number of viable pathogens, their intermediate steps and genetic dependencies differ.

Imagine a pathogen that remains mainly extracellular. Specific antibodies may reduce access to susceptible cells, while complement and phagocytes help remove microbial material. A deficiency of perforin need not directly abolish antibody binding or complement opsonization. Now imagine an organism that replicates in the cytosol of a nucleated cell. The adaptive CD8 response gains particular importance because class I presentation samples intracellular proteins. But humoral immunity can still limit the spread of extracellular infectious particles, and innate interferons can suppress replication without killing the infected cell. The immune system normally uses cooperating functions rather than selecting one exclusively.

Some CD4 T cells can acquire direct cytotoxic capacity, and not every CD8 T cell is actively cytotoxic. Coreceptor expression predicts a conventional recognition relationship more reliably than it predicts the entire functional state of an individual cell. In addition, unconventional T-cell populations can recognize ligands other than typical classical peptide–MHC complexes. These exceptions do not eliminate the usefulness of the conventional CD8/class I and CD4/class II framework. They establish its domain: it describes the predominant organization of conventional alpha-beta T-cell responses rather than every lymphocyte in every vertebrate tissue.

When interpreting an experiment in which a mixed lymphocyte population destroys a target, use the recognition mechanism to identify the effector. Target lysis alone cannot distinguish NK killing from CTL killing. Dependence on cognate peptide–MHC supports the conventional CTL mechanism; dependence on antibody coating and CD16 supports ADCC; sensitivity to the balance of NK activating and inhibitory ligands supports innate recognition. Measuring the effector population and perturbing one of these recognition steps is more informative than relying on an endpoint shared by all three pathways.

7. Th1 cells and the activation of macrophage antimicrobial functions

Macrophages are capable of engulfing particles, digesting cellular debris and responding to microbial molecular patterns. Nevertheless, the basal antimicrobial capacity of a macrophage may be inadequate when a pathogen survives after uptake. Some organisms interfere with maturation of the phagosome, resist antimicrobial chemicals or tolerate the conditions within a membrane-bound compartment. An adaptive immune response can strengthen the defenses of the macrophage rather than requiring an antibody to penetrate that compartment. This is a characteristic function of type 1 helper T cells, conventionally abbreviated Th1.

A macrophage that has ingested microbial material can process proteins and display suitable peptides using class II MHC. A differentiated Th1 cell with the appropriate TCR can then recognize the peptide–MHC complex. CD4 contributes to conventional class II recognition. The antigen therefore serves as a molecular link between the infection and the helper cell's response. The cytokine released by the helper cell is not itself specific for the microbial peptide. The specificity lies in the TCR-dependent decision to produce or direct the cytokine signal. Nearby cells that express the corresponding cytokine receptor may respond even if they do not display the cognate peptide.

Interferon gamma, commonly written IFN-γ, is a central mediator of classical macrophage activation. It is a type II interferon, distinct from the type I interferons IFN-α and IFN-β that participate prominently in antiviral states. The receptor for IFN-γ is expressed by many cells. Binding activates receptor-associated signaling proteins, leading through JAK and STAT1 to altered gene expression. In macrophages, this can promote programs that improve antimicrobial functions and antigen presentation. The pathway is an example of how extracellular protein binding modifies nuclear transcription without the cytokine itself traveling to the nucleus as a transcription factor.

A second important contact-dependent input is delivered through CD40 ligand on an activated T cell and CD40 on the macrophage. CD40 belongs to the TNF receptor superfamily and transmits signals through associated intracellular proteins. It is not an antigen receptor and does not determine which microbial peptide the T cell recognizes. The contact provides an additional activation cue whose effect can cooperate with IFN-γ and signals generated by innate microbial recognition. The relative requirement for these inputs varies with the macrophage state and the microorganism involved. The full response is better understood as integration of multiple cues than as a single universal molecular switch.

Among the functions enhanced in an activated macrophage are mechanisms associated with antimicrobial reactive oxygen species, changes in phagosome handling, production of antimicrobial enzymes and other transcriptionally regulated effector molecules. Nitric oxide generated by inducible nitric oxide synthase is an important antimicrobial mechanism in many experimental mouse macrophage systems. Human macrophages do not always reproduce the same magnitude or pattern of nitric oxide production, so results from a particular murine model should not be treated as a quantitative universal mammalian rule. This comparative limitation does not weaken the central concept that T-cell signals can increase the intracellular microbicidal capacity of a phagocyte.

Enhanced antimicrobial function does not mean that all microbes are killed simply because IFN-γ is present. A microorganism may resist particular effector molecules, occupy a compartment inaccessible to them, or alter the responsiveness of the host cell. The outcome depends on both the macrophage and the pathogen. Equally, increased antigen presentation alone is not a direct demonstration of antimicrobial killing. A macrophage may display more MHC molecules yet fail to eliminate a pathogen. An experiment should measure viable organisms or a validated measure of replication alongside the cellular activation markers.

There is reciprocal communication between macrophages, dendritic cells and Th1 cells. Innate microbial recognition can promote production of interleukin-12 by antigen-presenting cells. IL-12 helps support type 1 responses and IFN-γ production by appropriate T cells and natural killer cells. In turn, IFN-γ can strengthen macrophage activity and promote a cellular environment supportive of further type 1 responses. Such cooperation helps maintain the response while microbial cues persist. It also creates a risk of amplification beyond the original need, especially when recruitment of additional cells expands the number of cytokine-producing sources.

The term classically activated macrophage is useful for describing a pattern of inflammatory antimicrobial activation. It should not be read as the claim that every macrophage in a living tissue belongs permanently to one of two sharply separated categories. Tissue origin, metabolic state, microbial products and multiple cytokines shape macrophage behavior. Cells can adopt mixed or changing functional programs. For the NET/SET examination, the most reliable distinction is between a type 1 response that promotes antimicrobial activity through IFN-γ and other cooperating signals, and regulatory or repair-associated influences that can restrain inflammation or support restoration of tissue integrity.

The activated macrophage faces a trade-off. Chemical products that damage microbes may also damage host molecules if they escape into neighboring tissue. Cytokines released to recruit help can contribute to swelling and local dysfunction. The aim is not simply to maximize antimicrobial output under all circumstances but to produce adequate control of infection while limiting harm. Persistent stimulation by an intracellular organism can keep macrophages and T cells interacting for extended periods. The resulting chronic inflammatory organization and its pathogen-specific consequences are developed in later chapters. Here the essential mechanism is the conversion of adaptive antigen recognition into enhanced innate cellular activity.

Consider two macrophage cultures infected with the same number of organisms. One culture is exposed to conditioned medium from stimulated Th1 cells; the other receives otherwise identical medium from unstimulated cells. If microbial survival is lower in the first culture, a soluble product is a plausible contributor. Neutralizing IFN-γ and comparing the result with an appropriate control antibody can test whether this cytokine is necessary for the observed effect. Adding recombinant IFN-γ to a separate culture can test sufficiency under the defined conditions. The results are not automatically equivalent: a molecule can be necessary for the full effect without being sufficient to reproduce it alone.

A contact-dependent contribution can be investigated by comparing direct coculture with a separation system that allows diffusible molecules to pass but prevents the cells from touching. A reduction in activation in the separated condition supports the importance of contact or short-range delivery, but it may also reflect changes in local cytokine concentration. Blocking CD40–CD40L interactions supplies a more focused test of that particular pathway. The strongest interpretation combines contact, cytokine and receptor perturbations with a functional microbial readout rather than relying on a single expression measurement.

8. Th17-dependent recruitment and the delayed cellular response

Not every helper T-cell response is directed toward a pathogen that survives inside a macrophage. An extracellular bacterium or fungus growing at an epithelial barrier can create a problem of local recruitment. Phagocytes must reach the affected site, and the epithelial layer may need to produce additional defensive molecules. Type 17 helper T cells, usually called Th17 cells, contribute to these tasks. The name reflects their characteristic production of members of the interleukin-17 family, particularly IL-17A and IL-17F. The cytokines act on other cells to help organize a protective environment rather than functioning primarily as enzymes that directly destroy microbial structures.

Epithelial cells, stromal cells and other responsive populations express receptors through which IL-17 can alter gene expression. One important consequence is enhanced production of chemokines that promote neutrophil recruitment. In some tissues IL-17 also supports the production of antimicrobial peptides and growth factors that influence neutrophil supply. The pathway can therefore connect a rare antigen-specific T-cell population to a much larger group of innate effectors. The neutrophils recruited by this mechanism do not need to possess the same antigen specificity as the initiating Th17 cells. Their own recognition systems determine which microbial structures they engage after arriving at the site.

IL-22, which may be produced by Th17 cells and other immune populations, can influence epithelial barrier functions. It is not a defining product of every Th17 cell, and its biological effects differ from those of IL-17. The combined activity of cytokines may improve barrier defense while also changing cell proliferation, repair and inflammatory gene expression. It is helpful to distinguish the cell that produces a cytokine from the tissue cell that expresses its receptor and from the final effector cell. These three roles may belong to different populations. A question that describes reduced neutrophil recruitment after disruption of epithelial cytokine signaling may therefore be about a defect in the receiving tissue rather than a defect in neutrophil motility.

The formation of Th17 populations depends on the cytokine environment during activation. IL-6, IL-1, TGF-β and IL-23 have important roles in different stages and species-dependent contexts, with RORγt acting as a characteristic transcriptional regulator. It is misleading to memorize all of these cytokines as a single obligatory mixture for every human or mouse Th17 response. IL-23, for example, can contribute strongly to maintenance and pathogenic functional programs without serving as a universal initiating signal for naive T cells. At the examination level, the important relationship is that a population's differentiated cytokine output reflects the context in which antigen recognition occurs, not a direct chemical property of the antigen alone.

An experiment may reveal intact proliferation of antigen-specific Th17 cells but reduced neutrophil accumulation at an infected mucosal site. If the epithelial cells lack functional IL-17 receptor signaling, the deficit can be explained even though T-cell differentiation was normal. If neutrophils lack a receptor required to detect a downstream chemokine, the epithelium may produce the expected recruitment signal but the cells cannot follow it. An investigator can distinguish these two explanations by measuring epithelial chemokine production, the number of circulating neutrophils, and migration toward an appropriate chemoattractant. Different defects can yield a similar tissue phenotype while interrupting different stages of the causal chain.

Th1 and Th17 responses should not be regarded as entirely independent circuits. Both may contribute to resistance against the same organism, and the surrounding cytokines influence the composition of the effector population. A type 1 response may promote activation of macrophages while type 17 signals recruit neutrophils to the infected region. Their relative importance depends on the pathogen's location and the tissue environment. It is possible for a defense mechanism that is advantageous in one location to create tissue injury in another. Extensive neutrophil recruitment can, for example, expose delicate tissue structures to proteases and oxidants during prolonged inflammation.

Delayed-type hypersensitivity provides a classical observable example of cellular effector action. In a sensitized individual, re-exposure to a suitable antigen in tissue can activate previously primed antigen-specific T cells. Cytokines and chemokines then alter the local environment, promote leukocyte recruitment and enhance cellular effector functions. The reaction commonly develops over many hours, with prominent manifestations around one to three days in standard textbook examples. The delay reflects the time required for cellular signaling, gene expression, recruitment and tissue accumulation, rather than a requirement for an antibody to become chemically active after a fixed period.

The delayed local response can be compared with an immediate antibody-dependent reaction. A rapid response mediated by preformed mediators released from sensitized mast cells follows a different causal sequence from the gradual accumulation of activated T cells and recruited leukocytes. Timing is a useful clue but is not by itself sufficient to classify every immune reaction. The molecular recognition system, participating effectors and tissue changes provide stronger evidence. The formal classification of hypersensitivity mechanisms belongs to the next chapter; the present example shows how a cellular immune response can become visible as a tissue-level change.

In an illustrative transfer experiment, a recipient is given antigen-experienced T cells and subsequently exposed to the relevant antigen. Another recipient receives serum instead. A reaction dependent on the transferred viable T-cell population and appropriate antigen would support a cell-mediated component. Controls must establish compatibility of transferred cells, absence of unintended antigen or inflammatory contaminants, and equivalent antigen challenge. The experiment can reveal the type of material that transfers the response, but does not prove that every cell found in the resulting tissue infiltrate is antigen-specific. Many recruited phagocytes may respond to signals initiated by relatively few specific lymphocytes.

9. Cytokine communication: specificity, range and combinations of signals

Cytokines are signaling proteins that allow cells to change the behavior of themselves or other cells. Their production is often regulated by infection, antigen recognition or tissue stress, and their effects depend on receptors expressed by the receiving populations. The term covers diverse molecular families rather than a single structural class. Interleukins, interferons, several members of the tumor necrosis factor family and chemokines are examples. The historical names do not guarantee an exclusive cellular source or a single biological effect. An interleukin may be produced by nonleukocytic cells, and a cytokine initially described through one activity may influence many additional processes.

A cytokine can act in an autocrine manner if it influences the same cell that secretes it. It can act in a paracrine manner if its targets are nearby cells. Some cytokines can reach distant tissues and produce systemic effects when circulating concentrations become sufficient. These categories describe the spatial relationship between source and target, not three different chemical species. A molecule released in a lymphoid organ may contribute to a local proliferative signal and also enter the circulation, but its effects elsewhere depend on concentration, degradation, access and receptor expression. Directional secretion and binding to extracellular structures can help create a local microenvironment whose cytokine concentrations differ from those measured in blood.

Receptor expression provides an important layer of biological specificity. A lymphocyte expressing a high-affinity receptor for a cytokine may respond at a concentration that produces little effect on a different cell expressing fewer receptor chains or a receptor with lower effective affinity. Cells can also alter receptor expression during differentiation. Cytokine exposure can therefore change a population not only by modifying immediate gene expression but also by changing sensitivity to later signals. This relationship is especially relevant to IL-2, which supports expansion of activated lymphocytes and contributes to maintenance of regulatory T cells.

IL-2 illustrates why the same cytokine cannot be classified as simply stimulatory or inhibitory at the organism level. Activated conventional T cells can produce IL-2 and increase expression of the high-affinity IL-2 receptor. The receptor includes the alpha chain CD25, the beta chain CD122 and the common gamma chain CD132. CD25 contributes to efficient ligand capture, whereas the beta and gamma chains participate in intracellular signaling. Regulatory T cells commonly express high levels of CD25 and depend on available IL-2 for survival and function. Increasing or decreasing the amount of IL-2 can consequently affect both effector expansion and regulation, depending on which populations receive the signal.

A receptor's apparent sensitivity cannot be inferred from its alpha-chain abundance alone. A cell may bind ligand through a receptor component without generating the full intracellular response if the signaling chains are absent. Conversely, intermediate-affinity receptor complexes containing beta and gamma chains can respond to sufficient IL-2. This is a useful general principle of multicomponent receptors: ligand binding, receptor assembly and downstream signal transmission are separable events. An experiment reporting abundant surface CD25 does not by itself establish that the cell is a functional regulatory T cell, nor that every cell bearing CD25 will respond identically to the same IL-2 concentration.

Cytokine pleiotropy refers to the capacity of one cytokine to produce several effects in different cellular contexts. IFN-γ can enhance antigen presentation in one cell and alter antimicrobial gene expression in another. Redundancy refers to overlapping biological effects produced by distinct cytokines. Redundancy is rarely complete: two signals that both increase expression of a particular marker may differ in their timing, tissue distribution and other effects. Synergy occurs when two signals cooperate to produce a response greater than would be predicted from their separate contributions under a specified reference model. Antagonism occurs when one influence reduces the response to another. These terms describe relationships that should be defined using a particular endpoint and experimental setting.

Interferons provide a useful comparison between families. Type I interferons signal through the type I interferon receptor and promote antiviral gene programs in many cell types. Type II interferon, IFN-γ, has a different receptor and is strongly associated with type 1 cellular immune functions. Both can increase aspects of antigen presentation, but neither their receptors nor their full biological roles are interchangeable. A knockout of the type I receptor does not directly abolish IFN-γ receptor signaling. A question describing enhanced expression of class I MHC may involve either family in an appropriate context, so additional details are required to assign the upstream signal.

TNF is a representative inflammatory cytokine that can influence endothelial activation, leukocyte recruitment, cellular survival or death depending on receptor and signaling context. Its name originated from a particular biological observation but should not be interpreted as a statement that TNF acts only on tumors or always causes cell death. Some receptor signaling promotes transcriptional responses and survival rather than apoptosis. High or widespread production can contribute to systemic inflammatory disturbances, whereas localized production can support protective responses. The same molecule can therefore be beneficial or harmful depending on amount, distribution, duration and tissue susceptibility.

IL-10 is an important regulatory cytokine. It can reduce production of several inflammatory mediators and restrain aspects of antigen-presenting cell activation. It is produced by regulatory T cells as well as other lymphocyte and myeloid populations in suitable contexts. Its effect may indirectly reduce effector T-cell activity by changing the behavior of the antigen-presenting cell rather than acting directly on the effector alone. TGF-β likewise participates in regulation, tissue repair and differentiation. Its influence depends on the other signals present, and in combination with inflammatory cytokines it can contribute to differentiation patterns that are not simply suppressive.

Chemokines are a family of cytokines specialized in controlling cell positioning through their receptors. Many chemokine receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors. A cell can interpret differences in chemokine concentration across its surface and reorganize its cytoskeleton to migrate. The receptor does not transport the chemokine across the cell membrane as a nutrient; ligand binding triggers signals that alter motility and adhesion. A uniform concentration may activate receptors without providing a directional cue. Consequently, chemokine production, gradient formation and receptor-dependent movement are separate variables in an experiment on leukocyte recruitment.

A cytokine network is not simply a list of arrows. Its behavior emerges from the changing number of source cells, the receptors and signaling states of target cells, the transport and clearance of mediators, and the feedback generated by the responses themselves. An observed increase in extracellular cytokine concentration might reflect increased secretion per cell, expansion of the number of secreting cells, reduced consumption by receptors or slower clearance. Measuring both cell number and cytokine concentration is therefore more informative than measuring either alone. If the concentration changes without a corresponding change in transcript abundance, altered secretion or removal may be plausible explanations.

10. Positive and negative feedback in an expanding immune response

A small number of activated cells can initiate a large immune response because their products stimulate additional cellular activity. During infection, antigen-presenting cells respond to microbial signals and activate lymphocytes. These lymphocytes divide and release mediators that change the activity of phagocytes and other antigen-presenting cells. If the new activity supports further lymphocyte activation, the system contains positive feedback. Positive feedback amplifies a disturbance, but it does not mean that the amplified response is automatically beneficial. It may rapidly improve control of infection, or it may contribute to uncontrolled inflammation when opposing regulatory processes are insufficient.

IL-2 production and receptor expression during T-cell activation illustrate amplification. A newly stimulated conventional T cell can increase its sensitivity to IL-2 and release IL-2 into its environment. Cells receiving growth-promoting signals can proliferate, producing a larger population capable of further responses. The actual outcome is constrained by antigen availability, costimulation, survival factors and consumption of the cytokine. It is therefore incorrect to predict unlimited exponential growth simply because IL-2 participates in a positive feedback loop. Biological resources and feedback inhibition change as the population grows.

A macrophage–Th1 circuit provides a second example with distinct cellular participants. Microbial recognition can support production of IL-12, which promotes type 1 effector responses. IFN-γ produced by the responding cells can enhance macrophage antimicrobial activity and other aspects of the inflammatory environment. If improved killing reduces the microbial stimulus, this introduces an opposing loop: effective defense reduces the original activating input. The same circuit can therefore contain amplification at the cellular level and negative feedback through clearance of the pathogen. Whether the measured response rises or falls depends on the relative rates of these processes.

Negative feedback can also be induced directly by activation. Activated cells can express inhibitory receptors, and inflammation can induce mediators that restrain antigen presentation or inflammatory signaling. Such mechanisms permit substantial early defense while limiting the probability that activation remains high after its biological purpose has been served. A negative regulator that appears after stimulation may not be abundant before activation, so the timing of its expression is essential to its function. If the regulator were active at its highest level from the start, it might prevent an adequate initial response. If it appeared too late, tissue damage might occur before control becomes effective.

A simple quantitative framework helps distinguish population expansion from per-cell activity. If N denotes the number of effector cells and q denotes the average secretion rate per effector cell, the total secretion rate is Nq. If a population doubles while average secretion per cell remains constant, total secretion doubles. But if the population doubles while secretion per cell falls to one-half of its previous value, total secretion does not change. These statements depend on the assumption that other cellular sources are negligible and that the average is measured across the relevant population. In real tissues, changes in cell composition can alter the average even when the secretion program of each individual subtype remains unchanged.

For a soluble mediator in a hypothetical well-mixed compartment of volume V, let C denote concentration and k a first-order removal constant. If the production rate is Nq, the equation dC/dt = Nq/V − kC describes the change in concentration. Production has units of amount per time; division by volume converts it to concentration per time. The term kC has the same units when k is measured in inverse time. The model combines production and removal, and does not require cytokine molecules to remain indefinitely active. Under constant production and constant removal parameters, the steady concentration is Nq/(Vk), obtained by setting the rate of change to zero.

The model is useful precisely because it makes its assumptions visible. Cytokines in tissues are often not well mixed. Receptor-mediated uptake can become saturated, local sources can produce gradients, and cell populations can vary rapidly. The simple equation is therefore not a universal law of immune regulation. It is a tool for asking whether a change in measured concentration could be explained by a change in production, dilution or removal. A student who sees a lower concentration following treatment should not automatically infer that transcription has been inhibited. Faster removal, fewer source cells or changed distribution can lead to the same observation.

A cellular population can be described in a similarly simplified manner. Let p be a per-cell proliferation rate and d a per-cell loss rate. If both rates are constant over a short interval, dN/dt = (p − d)N. The net rate r = p − d determines the direction of change: positive r gives growth, negative r gives contraction and zero r gives a stable total count. A stable count does not establish that no cells are dividing or dying; substantial proliferation and loss can balance each other. Labeling dividing cells and measuring apoptosis independently can distinguish a stable but dynamically turning-over population from a largely quiescent population.

In a negative-feedback model, the net growth rate may fall as the responding population or a regulatory mediator increases. This can produce a peak in the response even before all antigen is eliminated. Conversely, if antigen remains abundant and inhibitory regulation is weakened, expansion may persist longer than usual. The peak cell count is not a sufficient measure of protective effectiveness. A population with a smaller peak but more effective target killing can clear infection sooner than a much larger but functionally impaired population. Quantitative questions should therefore identify the biological endpoint before treating cell abundance as the measure of success.

An experimental approach to feedback uses measurements at multiple times rather than only a single endpoint. If a stimulatory cytokine rises early and an inhibitory mediator rises later, the temporal pattern is consistent with delayed negative feedback but does not demonstrate that the second mediator caused the decline. Selective neutralization or receptor disruption followed by a time-course comparison can test this hypothesis. A rescue that restores the mediator or its functional receptor can strengthen the inference. This experimental reasoning also applies to inhibitory receptors, which alter the response to contact-dependent stimulation rather than necessarily changing the extracellular concentration of a cytokine.

11. CTLA-4 and PD-1: inhibitory regulation at cellular interfaces

A naive T cell encountering its antigen on an antigen-presenting cell generally requires a suitable costimulatory environment. CD28 on the T cell recognizes CD80 and CD86, collectively referred to as B7 molecules, on the presenting cell. These interactions support activation, survival and production of growth-promoting cytokines in conjunction with TCR signaling. The quantity of peptide–MHC on a presenting cell and the availability of B7 molecules can change independently. A cell can display a peptide without delivering sufficient costimulation. This provides a molecular basis for the observation that antigen recognition does not always cause productive immunity.

CTLA-4, also called CD152, recognizes CD80 and CD86, the same ligands used by CD28. CTLA-4 is expressed prominently by regulatory T cells and can be induced in conventional T cells following activation. In contrast to CD28, its overall physiological function is inhibitory. Ligand competition is one important feature of this system. By engaging B7 molecules, CTLA-4 can reduce their availability for CD28-mediated costimulation. The two receptors do not have identical expression patterns, affinity, organization or intracellular behavior. A model based only on the number of each receptor cannot always predict which interaction dominates.

CTLA-4 can also reduce the costimulatory capacity of another cell. In a process demonstrated by Qureshi and colleagues in 2011, CTLA-4-expressing cells can capture B7 ligands from interacting cells and internalize them [15]. This process is termed trans-endocytosis because a receptor in one cell helps remove a molecule from the opposing cell's membrane. The resulting decrease in B7 availability can affect subsequent interactions of that antigen-presenting cell with other lymphocytes. The regulatory effect can therefore extend beyond the immediate CTLA-4-expressing cell. This is distinct from the classical receptor-mediated internalization of a ligand originally present as a soluble extracellular molecule.

The discovery provides an instructive example of how a surface receptor can influence the environment of a cell population. If a regulatory T cell reduces CD80 and CD86 on a dendritic cell, another conventional T cell contacting the same dendritic cell may receive weaker costimulation. The second T cell need not express CTLA-4 at an unusually high level for this consequence to occur. The presenting cell has become a shared point of control. The magnitude of this effect depends on the rate at which B7 molecules are removed and replaced, the frequency of cell contacts, and the activation state of the presenting cell.

PD-1, or programmed cell death protein 1, is a different inhibitory receptor. Its name should not be interpreted as meaning that every cell expressing PD-1 is committed to apoptosis. It can be expressed following activation of T cells and other immune populations. Its ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2, have different patterns of expression. PD-L1 can be expressed by immune cells and many other cell types and may increase during inflammatory signaling. PD-L2 has a more restricted distribution in many physiological contexts. Interaction of PD-1 with a suitable ligand can reduce signaling associated with activation of the lymphocyte.

The cytoplasmic region of PD-1 contains regulatory tyrosine-based motifs. Following ligand engagement in a signaling context, phosphorylation of these motifs can recruit phosphatases such as SHP-2. A phosphatase removes phosphate groups from suitable substrates, counteracting phosphorylation-dependent signaling. Work by Hui and colleagues showed that CD28 signaling was a particularly important target of PD-1-associated inhibition in their experimental systems [16]. For NET/SET purposes, it is sufficient to understand that PD-1 reduces activation-associated signaling through intracellular negative regulation rather than simply preventing a TCR from physically binding peptide–MHC. The relative substrates and degree of dependence on individual phosphatases can vary with biological context.

CTLA-4 and PD-1 are commonly contrasted by stating that CTLA-4 has a prominent role in controlling costimulation during priming, while PD-1 often restrains responses at peripheral sites of ongoing stimulation. This is a useful tendency, not an absolute geographical separation. Both can act in several anatomical and cellular contexts. The more fundamental distinction is that CTLA-4 shares B7 ligands with CD28 and can change their availability, whereas PD-1 engages its own ligands and transmits inhibitory signals within the responding cell. A question asking which checkpoint directly competes with CD28 for CD80 or CD86 is therefore much more precise than a question assigning each receptor to a single tissue.

Interferon-induced changes in PD-L1 expression can illustrate negative feedback. Activated lymphocytes produce IFN-γ, which can change gene expression in nearby tissue cells. If increased PD-L1 on those cells engages PD-1 on activated lymphocytes, the same inflammatory environment that supports cellular defense can generate an opposing influence. This feedback can help protect tissue from sustained activation. In a persistent infection, however, inhibitory signaling may contribute to reduced effector function even while antigen remains present. The physiological value of a checkpoint cannot be judged without considering both infection control and tissue integrity.

PD-1 expression is often associated with T-cell exhaustion during chronic antigen exposure. Exhaustion describes a state of altered differentiation and reduced functional responsiveness that can include changes in cytokine production, proliferative capacity and cytotoxicity. It is not defined solely by the presence of one surface protein. A recently activated functional T cell can also express PD-1. An exhausted population may retain some functions and exhibit heterogeneous degrees of dysfunction. Functional measurements and the history of antigen exposure are required to distinguish ordinary activation-associated inhibitory receptor expression from more persistent changes in cellular state.

In an experimental comparison, antigen-specific T cells can be exposed to otherwise comparable targets with different PD-L1 expression. If the higher-PD-L1 targets stimulate lower cytokine production, the result is consistent with inhibitory signaling, but target differences unrelated to PD-L1 must be considered. A selective ligand or receptor perturbation and an appropriate control can test the proposed interaction. Measuring peptide–MHC abundance and the viability of the T cells helps exclude a reduction in antigen presentation or generalized toxicity as alternative explanations. Restoration of a response after interrupting an inhibitory interaction provides evidence that the pathway contributed to the reduced response under those conditions.

Checkpoint inhibition has a physiological cost-benefit relationship. Increasing cytotoxic activity against an abnormal cell may be helpful if that cell threatens the organism, but broad removal of inhibitory regulation can also permit immune damage to healthy tissues. The severe phenotype of CTLA-4-deficient mice demonstrates that inhibitory pathways are integral components of normal immune organization rather than optional barriers to an otherwise perfect defense. The principle underlies applications of immunomodulation in research and biotechnology, but clinical intervention protocols are not needed to understand the underlying mechanism. The important lesson is that immune activity is optimized for appropriate defense, not maximized independently of its target and consequences.

12. Regulatory T cells and active suppression of immune responses

The observation that mature immune systems contain some lymphocytes capable of recognizing self antigens creates a problem that cannot be solved merely by examining thymic selection. A healthy organism needs a mechanism that controls these cells throughout life. Regulatory T cells provide a particularly important example of active, cell-mediated restraint. The term regulatory T cell, or Treg, commonly refers to a CD4-positive lymphocyte population characterized by expression of the transcription factor FOXP3 and typically high expression of CD25. It is a functional population, not simply a collection of cells bearing a particular surface marker.

FOXP3 contributes to the transcriptional program that supports the development and suppressive function of many regulatory T cells. As a transcription factor, it acts in association with other proteins and regulatory DNA elements to alter patterns of gene expression. Its presence is associated with a distinctive cellular state, including expression of molecules involved in immune regulation and responsiveness to IL-2. A defect that disrupts this program can produce serious failures of immune homeostasis. However, expression of a marker should not be confused with a complete functional assay. Activated conventional T cells can express CD25, and some human conventional T cells can express FOXP3 transiently without acquiring the stable suppressive activity characteristic of a regulatory population.

Many regulatory T cells develop in the thymus, where interactions between a developing T cell and self peptide–MHC can, in an appropriate context, favor differentiation into a regulatory rather than a conventional lineage. Other regulatory T cells arise from conventional precursors outside the thymus under suitable conditions. These are often distinguished as thymic and peripherally generated regulatory T cells. Their relative importance varies among tissues and antigens. The distinction concerns where the regulatory differentiation program arose, not whether a mature cell is capable of acting in the periphery. A thymically generated regulatory cell can migrate to a tissue and exert regulation there.

The relationship between Treg cells and IL-2 is especially informative. Regulatory T cells often produce relatively little IL-2 themselves, yet they depend on IL-2 supplied by other cells for maintenance. Their high-affinity IL-2 receptor helps them respond to available cytokine. When conventional cells become activated and increase IL-2 production, they may indirectly support the maintenance or expansion of a population that restrains the response. Thus the same mediator that supports the growth of responding conventional T cells can also help sustain a negative regulatory mechanism. This is a biological feedback relationship rather than a contradiction in the properties of the molecule.

High expression of CD25 can contribute to competition for available IL-2. In some settings, efficient cytokine capture by regulatory cells can limit the amount available to other populations. Nevertheless, it would be an overstatement to describe all regulatory T-cell suppression as starvation of conventional T cells for IL-2. Regulatory cells can change antigen-presenting cell behavior, produce inhibitory mediators and act through additional contact-dependent or metabolic mechanisms. The contribution of each mechanism depends on the tissue, the type of response and the experimental conditions. A successful explanation must specify which effect has actually been tested.

CTLA-4 provides a clear example of suppression at a shared antigen-presenting cell. A regulatory T cell interacting with a dendritic cell can reduce the availability of B7 costimulatory ligands, thereby limiting activation of conventional T cells that subsequently encounter that dendritic cell. The regulatory T cell may itself have been activated by a different peptide from the conventional cell. Antigen recognition can localize regulatory activity without requiring every cell affected by the regulatory activity to recognize exactly the same antigen. This helps explain how an antigen-specific regulatory population can produce local effects on neighboring immune responses.

Regulatory T cells can also produce IL-10 and, in suitable contexts, TGF-β or influence their activity. IL-10 can change the inflammatory and antigen-presenting functions of myeloid cells. TGF-β can restrain aspects of conventional lymphocyte responses and participate in differentiation of some regulatory populations. Neither molecule belongs exclusively to regulatory T cells. A tissue containing abundant IL-10 could have several sources of this cytokine, and a regulatory cell need not secrete every inhibitory mediator at a high level in all settings. If an experiment attributes suppression to a particular cytokine, source identification and selective perturbation are required.

The importance of tissue context is illustrated by mucosal surfaces. The digestive tract contains food-derived and microbiota-associated molecules that may be recognized by adaptive lymphocytes. A permanent, maximally inflammatory response to every encountered antigen would disrupt barrier function and absorption. Regulatory circuits help establish responses compatible with normal tissue function while preserving the ability to respond to threats. This is not identical to self-tolerance in the strict sense, because food molecules and many microbial antigens are not components of the host. It illustrates the broader principle that antigen recognition and damaging inflammation need not be inseparable.

A loss-of-function experiment can distinguish active suppression from an absence of responders. In an appropriate animal transfer system, one group receives conventional lymphocytes capable of reacting to host antigens, while another receives those cells together with regulatory cells. If the first group develops immune pathology but the second is protected, the regulatory cells can restrain the response under the tested conditions. The result does not establish that regulation occurs through a single molecular pathway. It instead supplies functional evidence for a regulatory population. To identify the mechanism, compare regulatory cells with defined functional defects, examine the activation state of the conventional cells, and determine whether the effect depends on contact or soluble mediators.

An assay that measures conventional T-cell proliferation in the presence of regulatory cells is useful, but a smaller responding population could result from fewer divisions, reduced survival or altered activation. A division-tracking label, a live-cell count and a death marker help distinguish these outcomes. A regulatory cell preparation that fails to suppress proliferation may be defective, but the responder cells might also be unusually resistant to suppression. Reciprocal mixing experiments in which normal and mutant responders are paired with normal and mutant regulatory cells can locate the defect. This distinction is a common foundation of analytical immunology questions: a failed interaction may arise from the sending cell, the receiving cell or the extracellular conditions.

13. Central tolerance: shaping the developing lymphocyte repertoire

The repertoire of conventional lymphocytes must be sufficiently diverse to recognize antigens not previously encountered by the organism. Recombination of antigen receptor gene segments generates extensive diversity before exposure to the particular foreign antigens that will later select responding clones. The molecular processes creating this diversity do not have advance knowledge of the host's complete set of proteins and other molecules. Some newly generated receptors will recognize self structures. Central tolerance comprises developmental mechanisms that restrict the survival or future behavior of such potentially harmful lymphocytes in primary lymphoid organs.

In conventional T-cell development, the thymus provides an environment in which developing cells encounter self peptides presented by MHC molecules. Positive selection and negative selection address related but different problems. Positive selection favors thymocytes whose receptors can interact sufficiently with the host's MHC-associated ligands to make future recognition possible. Negative selection removes many developing T cells whose interactions with self peptide–MHC are sufficiently strong or otherwise signal in a context that promotes deletion. The aim is not to produce T cells that have no interaction whatsoever with self MHC. Without an appropriate relationship to host MHC, conventional T cells would be unable to recognize foreign peptides displayed by host cells efficiently.

It is tempting to draw a single axis of receptor affinity and divide it into three regions corresponding to death by neglect, positive selection and negative selection. Such a diagram can be a useful introductory model, but selection depends on more than equilibrium affinity alone. Ligand density, the identity and developmental state of the presenting cell, the duration and organization of signaling, and the properties of the developing thymocyte can all influence the outcome. Moreover, some self-reactive CD4-lineage precursors differentiate into regulatory T cells rather than being deleted. A high self-reactivity signal therefore does not necessarily have one invariant consequence for every developing T cell.

The thymus faces a practical problem of antigen availability. Many proteins are normally produced predominantly in a particular peripheral organ, whereas developing T cells are located in a different tissue. Medullary thymic epithelial cells can express a broad range of otherwise tissue-restricted antigens, allowing their peptides to participate in the selection process. The autoimmune regulator AIRE contributes to expression of many such antigens. Other regulatory mechanisms, including programs associated with FEZF2, also contribute to the breadth of thymic self-antigen display. AIRE is not an antigen receptor or a molecule that physically binds and kills every autoreactive thymocyte. It influences the set of self antigens available for the selection machinery.

Dendritic cells within the thymus can also contribute to presentation of self antigens, including antigens obtained from other cells. This makes the selection environment a network of antigen sources and antigen-presenting populations. A mutation reducing expression of a particular tissue antigen in the thymus may permit survival of lymphocytes capable of responding to that antigen in the periphery. The resulting risk is antigen-dependent. A general failure of all negative selection is biologically different from a selective failure to display a particular self protein. The pattern of affected tissues can provide clues to which self-antigen representation mechanism is impaired.

Central tolerance in B-cell development operates on a different recognition substrate. A developing B cell can bind a native antigen directly through surface immunoglobulin, unlike a conventional TCR that generally recognizes a peptide–MHC ligand. If a newly formed B-cell receptor interacts with self antigen in a manner that generates an appropriate signal, the cell may undergo additional light-chain gene rearrangement. This process, termed receptor editing, can alter the specificity of the receptor while preserving the developing cell. If editing removes the self-reactive specificity, the cell may continue development. If it fails to remove a problematic specificity, deletion or functional inactivation may occur depending on the conditions.

Receptor editing and class switching must be distinguished. Receptor editing can change the variable-region combination that determines antigen binding, often through additional light-chain rearrangements in immature B cells. Class switching in activated B cells alters the heavy-chain constant region and hence effector properties while retaining the rearranged variable-region specificity in the switching event itself. Somatic hypermutation can subsequently alter the binding properties of antibodies during an activated response. These processes occur at different developmental stages and solve different biological problems. A question describing rescue of an immature autoreactive B cell through further light-chain rearrangement refers to editing, not to a change from IgM to IgG.

Central tolerance cannot be expected to remove every possible dangerous specificity. Some antigens may be insufficiently represented during development, receptor interactions may lie below deletion thresholds in the developmental environment, and the biochemical state of an antigen in peripheral tissue may differ from the form encountered during selection. Somatic hypermutation can also introduce new self-reactivity after a mature B cell has left its primary developmental organ. These considerations explain why the presence of central selection mechanisms does not eliminate the need for peripheral controls. Central and peripheral tolerance are successive and partly overlapping safeguards rather than mutually exclusive theories.

A simple experimental model uses a defined self-reactive receptor together with a controlled source of its cognate antigen. If the antigen is present in the thymus and the number of developing receptor-bearing cells falls, deletion is a plausible explanation. It is necessary to measure developmental stages and viability, however, because a smaller peripheral population could also arise through impaired positive selection, altered migration or developmental arrest. If the antigen is expressed only outside the thymus, and developing cells emerge normally but become unresponsive or disappear later, a peripheral mechanism is implicated. The anatomical location and developmental stage of the first detectable change are therefore central to interpreting the result.

14. Peripheral tolerance, anergy, deletion and the limits of recognition

Peripheral tolerance operates after lymphocytes have reached mature lymphoid compartments and tissues. It addresses self-reactive cells that survived central selection and newly arising potentially harmful responses. Three broad mechanisms are particularly useful for examination reasoning: functional inactivation of the responding cell, elimination of that cell, and suppression by other cells or mediators. They can overlap, and not every inactive lymphocyte has undergone one of these tolerance mechanisms. A cell may simply not have encountered its antigen or may lack physical access to it.

Anergy describes a state of reduced functional responsiveness in a living lymphocyte. A T cell can encounter antigen under conditions that fail to provide the required costimulatory or inflammatory context for productive activation. Rather than expanding and differentiating normally, it may acquire a state in which subsequent stimulation generates less proliferation or cytokine production. The mechanism involves changes in intracellular signaling and gene expression. The important observable distinction from deletion is that an anergic cell remains present and viable but fails to mount the expected response. Anergy is not simply another word for physical absence of antigen.

Anergy may be demonstrated experimentally by exposing a T-cell population to antigen under poorly costimulatory conditions and then comparing its response to a subsequent standardized stimulus with that of an appropriate control population. If the exposed cells remain viable but produce less IL-2 or proliferate less during restimulation, the result supports functional unresponsiveness. It is necessary to control for receptor specificity, cell number, viability and the history of stimulation. A poorly activated antigen-presenting cell may simply have failed to activate the T cells initially; evidence of a subsequent persistent change in responsiveness is needed before concluding that anergy was induced.

Peripheral deletion eliminates potentially harmful lymphocytes by programmed cell death. Repeated stimulation or the withdrawal of survival signals can contribute in different biological circumstances. Fas-mediated death and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways are two relevant examples. The mechanisms are not identical and need not have the same relative importance in every lymphocyte population. In contrast to anergy, deletion reduces the number of cells belonging to the affected clone. If both cell number and response per viable cell decrease, more than one mechanism may have operated. A bulk cytokine measurement cannot distinguish these outcomes because fewer responding cells and reduced output per cell can both lower the total cytokine concentration.

Active regulation provides a third mechanism. A self-reactive conventional cell may retain the potential to respond but remain controlled by regulatory cells, inhibitory ligand interactions or a suppressive tissue environment. If it is removed from that environment and stimulated under suitable conditions, its activity may become detectable. This differs from a permanent loss of the clone. In a mixed-cell experiment, removal of regulatory populations may increase proliferation of conventional cells without requiring any new receptor rearrangement. The increase reflects relief from an extrinsic constraint rather than generation of a novel antigen specificity.

Immune ignorance is distinct from demonstrated active tolerance. A lymphocyte may not meet a particular antigen because the antigen is anatomically inaccessible, is present at too low a concentration or is not adequately displayed in the relevant context. Such a cell may remain responsive if the barrier is removed and the antigen is supplied in an immunogenic form. Certain tissue environments also possess specialized regulatory features that limit harmful inflammation. The term immune privilege refers to relative protection from some immune responses in such sites, not complete absence of immune surveillance. Anatomical separation and local regulation can coexist.

A mature self-reactive B cell may encounter an antigen but receive insufficient help from an antigen-specific helper T cell. The B cell can internalize the antigen and display peptides to T cells, yet the necessary helper population may be absent or tolerant. Without suitable helper signals, the B cell may fail to enter a productive response or may undergo peripheral inactivation or loss. This creates a safeguard that depends on cooperation between two independently selected receptor populations. The safeguard is imperfect, because some responses are T-independent and inflammatory contexts can alter the conditions of activation. It nonetheless illustrates why B-cell tolerance cannot be understood entirely through BCR binding alone.

Self-tolerance is not a state of generalized immune weakness. An organism tolerant of its own proteins can still respond vigorously to a foreign antigen presented in an appropriate inflammatory context. Similarly, a tolerant response to one antigen does not imply tolerance to every unrelated antigen. To test specificity, a researcher can compare responses to the tolerizing antigen and an unrelated immunogenic antigen in the same subject or cell system. Preserved responses to the unrelated antigen argue against nonspecific suppression or widespread loss of immune competence. If both responses decline, broad immunosuppression, loss of cell viability or a shared signaling defect becomes a plausible alternative.

The distinction between tolerance and immunological memory is equally important. Both depend on previous cellular events that influence future responsiveness, but their consequences differ. Memory can accelerate or strengthen a subsequent protective response, whereas tolerance limits a potentially inappropriate response. The absence of an immediate response is insufficient to distinguish these states: a memory cell may be quiescent until restimulation, and a tolerant cell may remain present without responding productively. The comparison requires an appropriate challenge and measurements of the resulting response. Inhibitory receptor expression alone also cannot distinguish tolerance, recent activation and exhaustion without additional functional evidence.

Peripheral tolerance can be challenged by changes in antigen presentation or inflammatory signaling. An infection can increase the expression of costimulatory molecules and alter the cytokine environment in which self antigens are encountered. Such conditions may increase the likelihood of activation of some previously restrained clones, although the existence of inflammation does not imply that autoimmune disease must follow. Several safeguards may remain effective, and individual receptor specificities differ. The later chapter on autoimmunity examines how failures of these safeguards can produce tissue injury. Here the central conclusion is that mature immune cells continually integrate antigen recognition with information about their environment.

15. Contraction, memory and resolution of cellular immune responses

During an effective acute immune response, the population of antigen-specific effector T cells typically increases from a relatively small precursor pool. The expansion provides sufficient cells to reach infected sites and carry out effector functions. Once the activating stimulus declines, maintaining the full expanded population may be unnecessary and energetically costly. Immune contraction describes the reduction in the expanded effector population that commonly follows its peak. It is not synonymous with extinction of the entire antigen-specific repertoire. A smaller population of memory cells may remain and respond to a subsequent encounter.

The disappearance of antigen can influence contraction in several ways. Fewer productive interactions with antigen-presenting cells reduce stimulation and cytokine production. The inflammatory environment changes as microbial products and damaged cellular material are removed. Survival signals that supported the activated population can become less available. Within individual effector cells, the balance between pro-survival and pro-apoptotic proteins may shift toward apoptosis. Bim, a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 protein family, contributes to this process in many experimental systems. The overall response is not a single switch that simultaneously kills every cell at the moment when the last antigen disappears.

Restimulation-induced death provides another regulatory mechanism. Activated T cells can become susceptible to apoptosis in response to repeated receptor engagement under suitable conditions. Fas–FasL interactions contribute to this pathway in several cellular contexts. This is distinguishable from death caused primarily by withdrawal of growth or survival signals. One involves renewed stimulation of a susceptible activated cell; the other follows reduced support. Both can reduce the number of responding lymphocytes, but an experiment that removes antigen and an experiment that repeatedly stimulates the same receptor do not necessarily test the same mechanism.

A memory population survives through cellular differentiation states and homeostatic support that differ from those of many short-lived effector cells. Cytokines such as IL-7 and IL-15 contribute to the maintenance of particular memory T-cell populations. Different memory subsets also occupy distinct anatomical sites and differ in their capacity for circulation or rapid local responses. A tissue-resident memory cell may remain near a previously affected barrier, whereas a circulating memory population can access multiple lymphoid or peripheral compartments. The details of memory differentiation are not uniform across all antigens or infection models. For this chapter, the important principle is that the response can terminate its immediate effector phase while preserving future antigen-specific protection.

Resolution of inflammation requires additional changes beyond reducing effector T-cell numbers. Phagocytes remove apoptotic cells and damaged material; inflammatory recruitment signals decline; tissue cells repair barriers and restore physiological function. If the effector population contracts but macrophage-derived inflammatory mediators persist, tissue dysfunction may continue. Conversely, regulatory changes may reduce the activity of remaining lymphocytes before their numerical decline is complete. Contraction is a population-level phenomenon, whereas resolution refers more broadly to restoration of a controlled tissue environment. A single lymphocyte count is not sufficient to measure both.

A persistent infection presents a different set of conditions. Antigen may remain available, and continuous stimulation may alter the functional state of responding lymphocytes. Inhibitory pathways may restrain excessive tissue damage but also limit pathogen control. Cells with reduced functional capacity can remain detectable in large numbers. This differs from the usual course of an uncomplicated acute infection, in which antigen decreases and the expanded response contracts. Persistent stimulation, exhaustion, contraction and tolerance are distinct concepts. They can coexist in a biological system, but each describes a different feature of the response.

A simple population model provides a useful way to describe one phase of contraction. If a population has negligible new input and each cell has an approximately constant probability of loss per unit time, dN/dt = −kN, with k a positive loss constant. Separation of variables gives dN/N = −k dt. Integration from an initial count N0 at time zero yields ln(N/N0) = −kt, or N = N0 exp(−kt). The half-life is the time needed for N/N0 to equal one-half, so t1/2 = ln(2)/k. The loss constant has units of inverse time. This model can describe a limited interval but should not be treated as a universal description of every phase of the immune response.

For example, a hypothetical effector population declining from 80,000 cells to 20,000 cells over six days has passed through two half-lives if the exponential model applies. Its half-life is therefore three days and the estimated loss constant is approximately 0.231 per day. If the same population later stabilizes near a smaller memory count, extending the same exponential model indefinitely will underestimate the remaining cells. The assumption of a homogeneous population with a constant loss rate becomes inappropriate once surviving memory cells contribute a substantial fraction of the count. The changing slope of a logarithmic plot can reveal such a departure from a one-population model.

The measured number of cells at a particular tissue can decline because of cell death or migration. If a population leaves the infected tissue and enters the circulation or another organ, its local abundance falls even if its total abundance throughout the organism does not change. To interpret contraction correctly, an investigator should compare measurements in relevant compartments or use an approach that tracks the fate of a defined population. Apoptosis markers and cell division measurements can supplement counts. Likewise, an apparent stable count can conceal replacement of dead cells by new divisions. Population curves are powerful descriptions of change, but they do not identify the underlying cellular process without additional information.

16. Experimental and quantitative reasoning across the effector–tolerance system

A cytotoxicity experiment should be interpreted by first identifying its endpoint. Release of a tracer previously retained inside target cells indicates loss of membrane integrity or tracer retention. An apoptosis marker may identify earlier commitment to programmed death. Reduction of viable target-cell count measures a later outcome but can be influenced by target proliferation. These readouts are related without being identical. A treatment that delays membrane disruption might reduce tracer release at an early time point even when the eventual number of killed cells is unchanged. A treatment that suppresses proliferation could reduce target count without producing direct cytotoxicity.

The classical chromium-release assay uses target cells that have incorporated a detectable radioactive tracer. A spontaneous-release control contains labeled targets without cytotoxic effectors, and a maximum-release control determines the signal available when target contents are released under appropriate conditions. If E is experimental release, S is spontaneous release and M is maximum release, the usual normalized specific release is 100(E − S)/(M − S) percent. Subtracting S corrects for background leakage, while dividing by M − S expresses the experimental increment as a fraction of the available signal range. The denominator must be positive and large enough for meaningful interpretation.

Suppose a hypothetical experiment gives E = 450 counts, S = 100 counts and M = 800 counts, using the same measurement interval and target loading conditions. The background-corrected experimental release is 350 counts, while the available signal range is 700 counts. The normalized release is therefore 50 percent. This is a normalized assay result rather than proof that exactly half of the individual target cells have completed apoptosis. Unequal tracer loading, incomplete release from particular targets and variation in the timing of membrane disruption can affect the relationship between signal and cell number. The values are invented for teaching and are not historical measurements.

The effector-to-target ratio is another experimental variable. A ratio of 5:1 means that the initial number of effectors is five times the initial number of target cells; it does not mean that exactly five CTLs contact every target or that each CTL must kill five cells. Contacts depend on movement, access, duration and the fraction of effectors with the relevant specificity. At increasing ratios, killing may initially rise and later approach a plateau when additional effectors contribute little to elimination of susceptible targets during the measured interval. The plateau can reflect exhaustion of accessible targets, a resistant fraction, limitations of the assay time or other constraints.

Consider a hypothetical mixed-target experiment in which two cell populations differ only in a marker that permits them to be counted separately. One displays cognate peptide–MHC and the other does not. If both populations are exposed to the same CTL culture, a selective reduction of the antigen-bearing population supports antigen-directed cytotoxicity. A separate comparison using a perforin-deficient effector can identify a contribution from granule-mediated killing. If the antigen-negative population is also reduced, possible explanations include nonspecific toxicity, additional effector populations, effects of soluble mediators, or imperfect definition of antigen display. Specificity is measured by the relative outcomes and suitable controls, not merely asserted from the name of the effector.

An experiment on cytokine-mediated macrophage activation requires a different endpoint. If colony-forming units of an intracellular bacterium decrease after exposure to a cytokine, the result is consistent with enhanced antimicrobial control. But a lower count could also arise because fewer macrophages survived and released bacteria that were not recovered by the measurement. Normalization to viable host-cell number, measurement of initial uptake and suitable recovery controls help distinguish these explanations. A cytokine may reduce the net replication rate of a microbe without immediately killing it; time-course measurements can differentiate reduced growth from rapid loss of viability.

Perturbations of immune checkpoints require similar care. An increase in culture cytokine after blocking PD-1 may reflect greater output per responding cell, an increased number of cells, or both. Counting viable cells and measuring single-cell activity alongside total concentration can distinguish these contributions. Likewise, a change in B7 expression after interaction with regulatory cells may be due to direct removal of surface ligand, altered synthesis or increased degradation within the presenting cell. Experiments with labeled ligand and appropriate cellular controls can test the fate of the ligand rather than inferring a mechanism solely from its final abundance.

The concepts also connect immunology to general cell biology. Regulated exocytosis explains directed secretion of cytotoxic granules. Receptor signaling explains how cell contact and soluble cytokines change gene expression or survival. Apoptosis explains how a target cell can be committed to death and how expanded lymphocyte populations can contract. Cell adhesion and chemotaxis explain why recognition and killing must occur at the appropriate anatomical location. The next chapter builds upon this integrated framework by examining cases in which the effectors or regulatory mechanisms produce immune-mediated tissue damage rather than maintaining protective homeostasis.

Worked numerical examples

All values in these examples are hypothetical teaching data. They are not measured historical results.

Example 1. Corrected target release

An assay gives experimental release 620 counts, spontaneous release 140 counts and maximum release 940 counts.

The specific increment is 620 − 140 = 480 counts. The usable signal range is 940 − 140 = 800 counts. Normalized release = 100 × 480/800 = 60%. All counts must be measured under comparable conditions. This is normalized tracer release, not an exact direct count of apoptotic cells.

Example 2. Effector-to-target ratio

A well contains 18,000 viable targets. An investigator adds 72,000 viable effector cells.

The initial E:T ratio is 72,000/18,000 = 4:1. To produce an 8:1 ratio with the same targets, use 8 × 18,000 = 144,000 effectors. This ratio gives initial population sizes, not the number of contacts per target. A constant target density and assay time are needed for a meaningful comparison.

Example 3. Secretion and concentration

A population of 50,000 cells secretes 0.004 ng per cell per hour into a well-mixed volume of 4 mL. A first-order removal constant is 0.25 h⁻¹.

Total production = 50,000 × 0.004 = 200 ng/h. The concentration production rate is 200/4 = 50 ng mL⁻¹ h⁻¹. At steady state C* = 50/0.25 = 200 ng/mL. If clearance doubles without changing production or volume, C* becomes 100 ng/mL. The model does not account for spatial gradients or saturable uptake.

Example 4. Population contraction

A hypothetical effector pool falls from 120,000 to 15,000 in 12 days, with negligible influx and a constant fractional loss rate.

15,000/120,000 = 1/8 = (1/2)³, so three half-lives occur in 12 days. Half-life = 4 days. The loss constant is ln(2)/4 = 0.1733 day⁻¹. Extrapolation fails when a stable memory population contributes appreciably to the total.

Example 5. Normalized suppression

In a proliferation assay with identical viable responder input, responders alone yield 48,000 divided-cell equivalents above background and responders plus Tregs yield 18,000.

Using the defined relative inhibition measure, 100 × (48,000 − 18,000)/48,000 = 62.5%. This calculation measures inhibition of the specified proliferation readout. It cannot distinguish reduced cell division from increased death without additional measurements.

Example 6. Microbial control versus absolute killing

At a matched endpoint, macrophage cultures yield 2.4 × 10⁶ CFU without stimulation and 3.0 × 10⁵ CFU after IFN-γ treatment. Equal host viability and recovery efficiency are assumed.

The treated/control ratio = 0.125; the treated endpoint is eightfold lower. The relative reduction compared with control is 100 × (1 − 0.125) = 87.5%. This does not establish that 87.5% of initial bacteria were killed because the starting viable counts and replication dynamics are not specified.

Example 7. Net population growth

An idealized population starts at 2,000 cells and experiences constant per-cell proliferation and loss rates of 0.5 day⁻¹ and 0.2 day⁻¹ over two days.

Net rate r = 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.3 day⁻¹. Solving dN/dt = rN gives N = N0 exp(rt) = 2,000 exp(0.6) ≈ 3,644 cells. This is a deterministic approximation; real cell numbers are discrete and rates may change with antigen, nutrients and regulation.

1. Which ligand is recognized by a conventional antigen-specific CD8 CTL?

  1. Free native microbial protein
  2. An MHC II molecule irrespective of its peptide
  3. A cognate peptide bound to the appropriate MHC I molecule
  4. IgG Fc alone
Answer and explanation

C. A cognate peptide bound to the appropriate MHC I molecule. The conventional CD8 TCR recognizes the composite peptide–MHC I ligand. Native protein can be recognized by antibody or BCR; MHC II is ordinarily associated with CD4, and Fc is recognized by Fc receptors rather than this TCR.

2. Which property distinguishes a differentiated CTL from a naive CD8 T cell of the same specificity?

  1. The effector has acquired machinery for rapid killing after target recognition
  2. The effector has necessarily rearranged a new TCR
  3. The naive cell can only recognize soluble antigen
  4. The effector requires the target to be a professional APC in all cases
Answer and explanation

A. The effector has acquired machinery for rapid killing after target recognition. Differentiation changes the functional state and cytotoxic machinery without requiring a change in the clone's TCR specificity. A differentiated CTL can respond to infected nonprofessional targets; naive cells generally require appropriate priming signals.

3. What is the principal role of perforin during granule-mediated cytotoxicity?

  1. Proteolytically cleaving Bid as a serine protease
  2. Binding peptide–MHC to determine antigen specificity
  3. Recruiting FADD directly to Fas
  4. Facilitating cytosolic delivery of granzymes into a target
Answer and explanation

D. Facilitating cytosolic delivery of granzymes into a target. Perforin is a pore-forming protein that helps deliver granzyme. Granzyme B cleaves suitable substrates such as Bid; TCR controls specificity and the Fas pathway recruits FADD.

4. Which molecule is a serine protease associated with cytotoxic granules?

  1. Perforin
  2. Granzyme B
  3. CD8
  4. CD28
Answer and explanation

B. Granzyme B. Granzyme B acts on protein substrates in the target. Perforin facilitates membrane passage, while CD8 and CD28 are cell-surface recognition or costimulatory components.

5. Which immediate molecular event follows productive Fas engagement in a susceptible cell?

  1. Direct recruitment of TAP into the Fas extracellular domain
  2. Assembly of a death-signaling complex involving FADD and procaspase-8
  3. Rearrangement of the target's TCR genes
  4. Class-switch recombination of immunoglobulin
Answer and explanation

B. Assembly of a death-signaling complex involving FADD and procaspase-8. Fas signals through its cytoplasmic death-domain machinery. FADD supports recruitment of procaspase-8; TAP transports peptides, and receptor gene rearrangement or class switching belongs to lymphocyte development and differentiation.

6. Which signal is characteristically produced by Th1 cells to enhance macrophage antimicrobial activation?

  1. IL-4 alone
  2. IgM
  3. Perforin as a cytokine
  4. IFN-γ
Answer and explanation

D. IFN-γ. IFN-γ acts through its receptor to alter macrophage activity. IL-4 is associated with type 2 programs, IgM is an antibody, and perforin is not a cytokine.

7. Which paired interaction can cooperate with Th1-derived IFN-γ in macrophage activation?

  1. CD40L on the helper T cell with CD40 on the macrophage
  2. CD8 on the macrophage with soluble IgG
  3. CD16 with MHC II
  4. PD-1 with CD28 as its ligand
Answer and explanation

A. CD40L on the helper T cell with CD40 on the macrophage. CD40 engagement provides an additional contact-dependent signal. The alternatives assign receptor partners incorrectly: CD16 recognizes Fc, and PD-1 ligands are PD-L1/PD-L2.

8. Which event is a typical consequence of IL-17 signaling at an epithelial barrier?

  1. Direct enzymatic cleavage of microbial DNA by IL-17
  2. Conversion of all neutrophils into antigen-specific CTLs
  3. Production of signals supporting neutrophil recruitment
  4. Immediate universal apoptosis of epithelium
Answer and explanation

C. Production of signals supporting neutrophil recruitment. IL-17 acts on responsive tissue cells to alter gene expression and support recruitment. It is neither a nuclease nor a mechanism of TCR rearrangement.

9. Which receptor shares CD80 and CD86 ligands with CD28?

  1. CTLA-4
  2. PD-1
  3. IFN-γ receptor
  4. CD16
Answer and explanation

A. CTLA-4. CTLA-4 and CD28 recognize B7 ligands with opposing overall effects. PD-1 recognizes PD-L1 and PD-L2; the remaining receptors bind other ligand classes.

10. What distinguishes PD-1-mediated inhibition from CTLA-4 competition for B7?

  1. PD-1 is itself a secreted granzyme
  2. PD-1 can recruit intracellular phosphatases after engaging PD-L1 or PD-L2
  3. PD-1 binds CD80 and CD86 as its only ligands
  4. PD-1 expression proves that a cell has undergone apoptosis
Answer and explanation

B. PD-1 can recruit intracellular phosphatases after engaging PD-L1 or PD-L2. PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor with distinct ligands and tyrosine-based regulatory motifs. Its expression can accompany activation or exhaustion but does not establish death.

11. Which transcription factor is characteristic of the conventional regulatory T-cell program?

  1. RAG1
  2. AIRE in all Tregs
  3. FOXP3
  4. TAP1
Answer and explanation

C. FOXP3. FOXP3 contributes to regulatory lineage function. RAG1 participates in receptor recombination; AIRE contributes to thymic self-antigen representation; TAP1 supports peptide transport.

12. Which statement about CD25 is accurate?

  1. It uniquely identifies every functional regulatory T cell
  2. It is the cytotoxic granule pore-forming protein
  3. It is the MHC II coreceptor
  4. It is an IL-2 receptor alpha chain also expressed by activated conventional T cells
Answer and explanation

D. It is an IL-2 receptor alpha chain also expressed by activated conventional T cells. CD25 contributes to high-affinity IL-2 binding in the receptor complex. Activated conventional cells can express it, so marker expression alone is insufficient to establish regulatory function.

13. Where does conventional T-cell central tolerance chiefly occur?

  1. Peripheral blood exclusively
  2. Spleen exclusively
  3. Bone marrow exclusively
  4. Thymus
Answer and explanation

D. Thymus. The thymus is the main T-cell developmental selection site. Bone marrow is important for B-cell central tolerance, whereas spleen and other secondary sites support peripheral immune responses and regulation.

14. What is the function of AIRE in central tolerance?

  1. Cleaving perforin into granzymes
  2. Directly editing all TCR variable regions
  3. Promoting thymic expression of many tissue-restricted self antigens
  4. Serving as the principal antibody Fc receptor
Answer and explanation

C. Promoting thymic expression of many tissue-restricted self antigens. AIRE contributes to self-antigen representation by thymic epithelial cells, supporting selection. It is a transcriptional regulator rather than an effector protease, recombination enzyme or Fc receptor.

15. Which outcome best defines anergy?

  1. A cell is physically eliminated by apoptosis
  2. A viable lymphocyte has reduced functional responsiveness
  3. A cell has never encountered its antigen and remains fully responsive
  4. All lymphocytes become nonspecifically activated
Answer and explanation

B. A viable lymphocyte has reduced functional responsiveness. Anergy is functional unresponsiveness, not deletion or simple ignorance. Viability and subsequent standardized stimulation help distinguish these states.

16. What is receptor editing in B-cell central tolerance?

  1. Further receptor gene rearrangement, often involving light chains, that can change specificity
  2. Changing an IgM constant region to IgG without changing the variable region
  3. Inserting perforin into the B-cell receptor
  4. Activating a mature B cell through CD40 alone
Answer and explanation

A. Further receptor gene rearrangement, often involving light chains, that can change specificity. Editing can replace a self-reactive variable-region combination during development. Class switching changes constant-region function and is distinct from receptor editing.

17. Which cytokine illustrates that one molecule can support both effector expansion and regulation?

  1. IgE
  2. Fas
  3. IL-2
  4. C3b
Answer and explanation

C. IL-2. IL-2 supports activated conventional T cells and also helps maintain regulatory T cells. IgE is an immunoglobulin, Fas a receptor, and C3b a complement fragment.

18. What is an example of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity?

  1. A naive CD8 T cell recognizes a free viral capsid through CD8
  2. Complement C3b directly rearranges the NK receptor genes
  3. A Treg removes all antibody from serum
  4. An NK cell recognizes clustered IgG Fc through CD16 on an antibody-coated target
Answer and explanation

D. An NK cell recognizes clustered IgG Fc through CD16 on an antibody-coated target. ADCC links antibody specificity with an Fc receptor-bearing cellular effector. It differs from conventional peptide–MHC recognition and complement activation.

19. Which distinction between contraction and memory is correct?

  1. Many expanded effector cells are lost while a smaller antigen-specific population can persist
  2. Contraction eliminates every antigen-specific lymphocyte
  3. Memory requires permanent maximal inflammation
  4. Contraction and initial clonal expansion mean the same thing
Answer and explanation

A. Many expanded effector cells are lost while a smaller antigen-specific population can persist. Contraction is a reduction of the expanded effector pool. Memory preserves antigen-specific protection in surviving populations rather than requiring continuous effector activity.

20. Which feature distinguishes a chemokine gradient from a uniform chemokine concentration in migration?

  1. A uniform concentration always determines a unique migration direction
  2. A gradient supplies spatial directional information to responsive cells
  3. Gradients can only be detected by antibodies
  4. Chemokines must enter the nucleus intact to guide movement
Answer and explanation

B. A gradient supplies spatial directional information to responsive cells. A spatial concentration difference can orient receptor-dependent migration. Uniform stimulation may alter activity but lacks the same directional information; chemokine receptors transduce signals.

21. A CTL clone is restricted to MHC I allele A and recognizes viral peptide V. Four target groups are A+V, A+unrelated peptide, B+V and B+unrelated peptide. Which pattern most strongly supports the clone's defined specificity?

  1. Equal killing of both V-bearing groups regardless of allele
  2. Preferential killing of A+V only
  3. Equal killing of both allele-A groups
  4. Equal killing of every target group
Answer and explanation

B. Preferential killing of A+V only. The defined TCR ligand requires both the appropriate MHC and cognate peptide. Killing both infected groups would support peptide recognition independent of MHC; killing both allele-A groups would suggest allele-dependent but peptide-independent activity. Mixed populations would need additional controls.

22. A mutant CTL shows normal antigen-induced calcium signaling and CD107a exposure, but target cells show little cytosolic granzyme activity. Granzyme B abundance in the effector is normal. Which defect best explains this pattern?

  1. The TCR cannot recognize cognate peptide–MHC
  2. No granules undergo exocytosis
  3. The target has no MHC I whatsoever
  4. Perforin-dependent delivery is impaired
Answer and explanation

D. Perforin-dependent delivery is impaired. Recognition and degranulation readouts are preserved. Reduced cytosolic granzyme points to delivery or target entry rather than the earlier steps. A perforin defect fits, although target resistance or altered granzyme activity should be independently tested.

23. In a hypothetical assay, specific release is 85% for WT CTLs on Fas-positive targets, 30% for perforin-null CTLs on Fas-positive targets, 70% for WT CTLs on Fas-null targets and 5% for perforin-null CTLs on Fas-null targets. What is the most defensible conclusion?

  1. Perforin contributes exactly 55 percentage points in all conditions
  2. Fas is required for all cytotoxicity
  3. Both pathways contribute; residual activity may reflect other effects
  4. Fas deletion increases CTL recognition
Answer and explanation

C. Both pathways contribute; residual activity may reflect other effects. Each single perturbation reduces killing, with the dual defect producing the lowest value. The effects are not necessarily additive because susceptibility and kinetics overlap. The data do not measure recognition directly.

24. Target release counts are E = 530, S = 130 and M = 930. What is normalized specific release?

  1. 50%
  2. 57%
  3. 43%
  4. 66%

A. 50%. Use 100(E−S)/(M−S). The background-corrected numerator is 400 counts and the range is 800 counts. Their ratio is 0.5. Dividing by total maximum without subtracting spontaneous release gives a different, inappropriate normalization.

25. An assay yields E = 480, S = 470 and M = 500 counts. The calculated specific release is about 33%. What is the primary concern?

  1. Very small usable signal range makes the estimate sensitive to noise
  2. The formula must yield a negative percentage
  3. The assay proves exactly one-third of targets underwent apoptosis
  4. Spontaneous leakage cannot affect interpretation
Answer and explanation

A. Very small usable signal range makes the estimate sensitive to noise. The denominator is only 30 counts and spontaneous release is close to maximum. Small errors can strongly affect the normalized estimate. The result is mathematically computable but poor dynamic range undermines reliability.

26. A mutant infected epithelium has reduced class I display while retaining strong activating NK ligands. What prediction best separates the two responses?

  1. Both CTL and NK recognition must decrease identically
  2. NK attack becomes peptide–MHC I restricted
  3. Conventional CTL recognition may fall while NK attack may increase
  4. Antibody binding necessarily disappears
Answer and explanation

C. Conventional CTL recognition may fall while NK attack may increase. Reduced class I display may remove a cognate CTL ligand and also relieve NK inhibition in a suitable context. NK activity still depends on activating signals and receptor specificity; antibody binding does not necessarily depend on MHC.

27. Two CTL populations have equal TCR recognition and target-killing capacity in direct coculture. Only one population accumulates poorly in infected tissue. Which defect is most consistent?

  1. Loss of granzyme B catalytic activity in that population
  2. Absence of target peptide in both assays
  3. Inability to recognize antigen in vitro
  4. Impaired trafficking or tissue access
Answer and explanation

D. Impaired trafficking or tissue access. Equivalent direct-contact killing argues against a major intrinsic cytotoxic defect under the assay conditions. Altered chemokine or adhesion responses can specifically reduce tissue access. More tests are needed to distinguish migration from local survival.

28. In macrophage cultures, untreated control contains 8 × 10⁵ viable bacteria, Th1-conditioned medium yields 2 × 10⁵, and conditioned medium plus IFN-γ neutralization yields 6 × 10⁵. Host viability and initial uptake match. What is justified?

  1. IFN-γ is proven sufficient for all bacterial killing
  2. IFN-γ contributes to the effect, but other factors may remain
  3. The medium kills exactly 75% of initial bacteria regardless of starting count
  4. CD40L-dependent contact has been proven necessary
Answer and explanation

B. IFN-γ contributes to the effect, but other factors may remain. Neutralization partially reverses the observed reduction, supporting a role for IFN-γ. A recombinant cytokine condition would test sufficiency. No contact occurs in conditioned medium, and endpoint counts do not reveal the initial number killed.

29. Macrophages lack functional IFN-γ receptor, while cognate Th1 cells recognize them and secrete IFN-γ normally. Which readout should be directly impaired?

  1. Recognition of native antigen by every B cell
  2. Th1 secretion of IFN-γ in all circumstances
  3. Perforin synthesis in every CTL
  4. Macrophage response to IFN-γ
Answer and explanation

D. Macrophage response to IFN-γ. The mutation is in the receiving macrophage. It interrupts the cytokine response even though ligand secretion is intact. Secondary effects on antigen presentation and T-cell feedback are possible, but the direct lesion is receptor signaling.

30. An IL-17 receptor-deficient epithelium produces little neutrophil-recruiting chemokine after exposure to Th17 cells. Neutrophils migrate normally toward supplied chemokine. What does this locate?

  1. The defect lies upstream of neutrophil migration in epithelial responsiveness
  2. Neutrophils cannot detect any chemokine
  3. Th17 cells necessarily fail to recognize peptide–MHC
  4. CTL granule fusion is defective
Answer and explanation

A. The defect lies upstream of neutrophil migration in epithelial responsiveness. Normal migration toward the supplied signal shows that the neutrophils can respond. The reduced epithelial production of downstream cues locates the defect in tissue response to IL-17.

31. A cytokine-producing population grows from 1 × 10⁴ to 4 × 10⁴ cells while secretion per cell falls from 2 to 0.5 arbitrary units per hour. With other sources negligible, how does total production change?

  1. It increases fourfold
  2. It is unchanged at 2 × 10⁴ units per hour
  3. It decreases fourfold
  4. It becomes zero
Answer and explanation

B. It is unchanged at 2 × 10⁴ units per hour. Total production is Nq. Initially 10,000 × 2 = 20,000; finally 40,000 × 0.5 = 20,000. A larger cell population does not guarantee larger secretion when the per-cell rate changes.

32. In a well-mixed model, a cytokine is supplied at 120 ng/h into 2 mL and removed at first-order k = 0.5 h⁻¹. What is the steady-state concentration?

  1. 60 ng/mL
  2. 240 ng/mL
  3. 120 ng/mL
  4. 30 ng/mL
Answer and explanation

C. 120 ng/mL. C*=production/(Vk)=120/(2×0.5)=120 ng/mL. The model assumes constant production and first-order removal, with no unresolved spatial gradients.

33. Two activated T-cell populations have equal live counts over two days. One has high division-label incorporation and high apoptosis, the other shows little of either. Which inference is correct?

  1. Both populations must have zero proliferation
  2. The first population necessarily grows faster in net terms
  3. A stable count can arise from matched proliferation and loss
  4. The second population necessarily underwent deletion
Answer and explanation

C. A stable count can arise from matched proliferation and loss. The net rate is the difference between production of new cells and loss. Equal counts can conceal very different turnover. Independent division and death measurements are necessary.

34. APCs cocultured with CTLA-4-expressing cells lose surface CD86; fluorescent CD86 appears inside the CTLA-4-expressing cells. Which mechanism is directly supported?

  1. Increased transcription of CD86 by the APC as the only mechanism
  2. Trans-endocytosis of the ligand from the opposing cell
  3. PD-1 binding to CD86 as its main ligand
  4. Direct degradation of APC genomic DNA
Answer and explanation

B. Trans-endocytosis of the ligand from the opposing cell. Ligand transfer into the CTLA-4-bearing cell supports physical removal from the opposing membrane. Surface loss alone would not discriminate ligand capture from altered synthesis; tracking the ligand adds evidence.

35. A Treg preparation lacking CTLA-4 fails to reduce APC B7 abundance but still secretes IL-10. What should be concluded?

  1. A specific regulatory mechanism is impaired; other regulatory mechanisms may persist
  2. All Treg functions are necessarily absent
  3. IL-10 must be identical to CTLA-4
  4. The APC cannot present any peptide
Answer and explanation

A. A specific regulatory mechanism is impaired; other regulatory mechanisms may persist. CTLA-4 contributes to control of B7 availability, but regulatory cells use multiple mechanisms. Preserved IL-10 secretion does not prove complete suppressive capacity, nor does loss of B7 regulation imply loss of antigen presentation.

36. Identical antigen-specific T cells encounter matched targets that differ in PD-L1. Cytokine output is 30 units with high PD-L1 and 80 with low PD-L1. A selective PD-1 perturbation restores output to 75 with high PD-L1. What is supported?

  1. High PD-L1 eliminated all viable T cells
  2. PD-1 is a receptor for IL-2
  3. The experiment proves every PD-1-positive cell is exhausted
  4. PD-1 engagement contributes to reduced activation under these conditions
Answer and explanation

D. PD-1 engagement contributes to reduced activation under these conditions. The rescue supports a causal contribution of the PD-1 pathway. Viability and antigen density controls remain important. PD-1 expression or a low cytokine endpoint alone cannot define exhaustion.

37. A mature CD4+CD25+ population contains both stable FOXP3-positive cells and recently activated conventional cells. Which additional test best distinguishes functional regulation?

  1. Counting CD25-positive cells only
  2. Suppression of defined responder activation in an appropriately controlled coculture
  3. Measuring serum IgM alone
  4. Confirming all cells express class I MHC
Answer and explanation

B. Suppression of defined responder activation in an appropriately controlled coculture. A functional suppression assay evaluates regulatory activity rather than relying on an ambiguous marker. Live-cell counts and activation measurements distinguish reduced proliferation from nonspecific toxicity.

38. A thymic epithelial mutation selectively reduces display of a tissue-restricted self antigen; thymic selection against unrelated antigens is preserved. Which prediction is most direct?

  1. Every T cell loses its ability to recognize MHC
  2. All mature B cells lose class switching
  3. More potentially reactive clones to that particular self antigen may escape central selection
  4. The mutation necessarily eliminates all peripheral Tregs
Answer and explanation

C. More potentially reactive clones to that particular self antigen may escape central selection. Reduced thymic representation can impair selection of specific clones without eliminating the entire T-cell repertoire. Peripheral control may still restrain escaped cells; autoimmune disease is not inevitable.

39. A mature self-reactive clone remains viable in normal numbers after tolerizing antigen exposure, but produces little IL-2 on standardized restimulation. Unrelated clones respond normally. What fits best?

  1. Deletion of that clone
  2. General failure of all cytokine receptors
  3. Immunological ignorance with normal restimulation
  4. Antigen-specific functional unresponsiveness (anergy)
Answer and explanation

D. Antigen-specific functional unresponsiveness (anergy). The clone is present but less responsive, while unrelated responses are preserved. This supports antigen-specific anergy rather than physical loss, nonspecific suppression or failure to have encountered antigen.

40. A self-reactive population fails to proliferate with regulatory cells present, but responds normally after the regulatory cells are removed. Viability and antigen display are preserved. Which mechanism is favored?

  1. Extrinsic suppression rather than complete deletion
  2. Permanent deletion of every reactive clone
  3. Loss of all TCR expression
  4. Failure of thymic positive selection in every cell
Answer and explanation

A. Extrinsic suppression rather than complete deletion. Recovery after removal of regulatory cells implies a surviving responsive population that had been restrained by its environment. It is inconsistent with complete deletion of that same population.

41. A population of 96,000 effector cells falls to 12,000 over nine days under a constant fractional-loss model. What is its half-life?

  1. 9 days
  2. 1.125 days
  3. 27 days
  4. 3 days
Answer and explanation

D. 3 days. The ratio is 12,000/96,000 = 1/8 = (1/2)³, representing three half-lives in nine days. The model assumes negligible new cell production or migration and homogeneous exponential loss.

42. A local tissue effector count falls sharply, but blood and draining-node counts increase by a similar amount. Apoptosis markers are not increased. What explanation deserves priority?

  1. All missing tissue cells necessarily died
  2. Redistribution or migration may explain the local decline
  3. All blood T cells became anergic
  4. The pathogen must be completely eliminated
Answer and explanation

B. Redistribution or migration may explain the local decline. Local cell counts do not measure organism-wide contraction. Reciprocal changes among compartments suggest redistribution, though tracking defined cells would provide stronger evidence.

43. Tregs rely on available IL-2 while activated conventional T cells also use IL-2. What is the most justified prediction of strong IL-2 deprivation?

  1. It selectively eliminates every CTL without affecting Tregs
  2. It invariably enhances Treg survival
  3. It may impair both conventional expansion and regulatory-cell maintenance
  4. It changes all antigen receptor specificities
Answer and explanation

C. It may impair both conventional expansion and regulatory-cell maintenance. The cytokine supports multiple populations. The net response depends on receptor expression, competing signals and timing, so a simple universal prediction of increased or decreased immunity is not justified.

44. A cytokine inhibitor lowers bulk IFN-γ from 200 to 100 units; live effector count falls from 1,000 to 500 cells, with equal volume and clearance. What conclusion can be made about average output per cell?

  1. The observed bulk output per live cell is unchanged
  2. Average output per cell must be halved
  3. Average output per cell must double
  4. No cells produced IFN-γ after treatment
Answer and explanation

A. The observed bulk output per live cell is unchanged. The ratios are 200/1,000 and 100/500, both 0.2 units per cell. The lower bulk readout can be explained by fewer cells under the stated assumptions; this does not prove identical single-cell distributions.

45. Assertion: Effector CTLs can kill a nonprofessional target displaying cognate peptide–MHC I. Reason: Differentiated CTLs generally require less priming-associated costimulation at the killing stage than naive CD8 T cells.

  1. Both statements are true, but the reason is unrelated
  2. The assertion is true and the reason is false
  3. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  4. The assertion is false and the reason is true
Answer and explanation

C. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion. Priming and execution are different functional stages. A differentiated CTL can use its existing machinery on cognate targets that do not provide the same costimulatory environment needed by naive cells.

46. Assertion: Loss of perforin necessarily abolishes every form of CTL-mediated target apoptosis. Reason: Fas–FasL signaling can activate death pathways independently of perforin.

  1. The assertion is false and the reason is true
  2. Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Both statements are true but the reason is unrelated
  4. The assertion is true and the reason is false
Answer and explanation

A. The assertion is false and the reason is true. Perforin is crucial for normal granule-mediated delivery, but Fas-dependent killing can persist where target and effector molecules are functional. Thus the universal assertion fails.

47. Match I–IV: I. CTLA-4; II. AIRE; III. Granzyme B; IV. IL-17A. a. Tissue-restricted thymic antigen expression; b. Proteolysis of target proteins; c. Control of B7 availability; d. Epithelial recruitment signals.

  1. I–a, II–c, III–d, IV–b
  2. I–c, II–a, III–b, IV–d
  3. I–b, II–d, III–a, IV–c
  4. I–d, II–b, III–c, IV–a
Answer and explanation

B. I–c, II–a, III–b, IV–d. CTLA-4 regulates B7 availability, AIRE supports thymic self-antigen display, granzyme B cleaves target proteins, and IL-17 promotes tissue signals for recruitment. The other mappings exchange distinct molecular roles.

48. Which statements are correct? I. CD25 is a component of the IL-2 receptor. II. PD-1 expression alone proves exhaustion. III. Anergy can retain viable lymphocytes. IV. Receptor editing and class switching are identical.

  1. I and II only
  2. II and IV only
  3. I, III and IV only
  4. I and III only
Answer and explanation

D. I and III only. I and III are correct. CD25 participates in IL-2 capture and anergic cells can remain viable. PD-1 is also expressed during ordinary activation, and editing changes specificity while switching changes constant-region class.

49. In Figure 10, which intervention most directly reduces availability of the shared CD28/CTLA-4 ligands on an APC?

  1. CTLA-4-dependent removal of CD80/CD86
  2. Loss of granzyme B in the target cytosol
  3. Increasing PD-L2 without changing B7
  4. AIRE-dependent transcription in thymic epithelium

A. CTLA-4-dependent removal of CD80/CD86. The upper part of Figure 10 depicts the shared B7 ligand system. CTLA-4 can capture those ligands; PD-L2 belongs to a distinct inhibitory interaction and AIRE is a thymic transcriptional regulator.

50. In Figure 14, a hypothetical curve stabilizes above zero after the peak declines. Which interpretation is compatible with the curve but not proven by it?

  1. All antigen-specific cells have been eliminated
  2. Every surviving cell is an exhausted effector
  3. The remaining cells never divide or die
  4. A smaller memory population persists after effector contraction
Answer and explanation

51. Which comparison best integrates the response to a virus before and after entry into a nucleated host cell?

  1. CTLs neutralize all free virions through antibody Fc receptors; antibodies always enter the infected cytosol
  2. Neutralizing antibody can limit extracellular entry; CTLs can recognize infected cells displaying cognate peptide–MHC I
  3. Th17 cells edit all viral genes directly; NK cells require a rearranged TCR
  4. Class II MHC is the only route for conventional CD8 target recognition
Answer and explanation

B. Neutralizing antibody can limit extracellular entry; CTLs can recognize infected cells displaying cognate peptide–MHC I. The location of the viral material changes the available effector mechanisms. Antibody and CTL activity can cooperate, along with innate interferons and NK responses, rather than being mutually exclusive.

52. A regulatory-cell transfer reduces tissue inflammation without reducing the number of antigen-specific effector cells. Which statement best reconciles the findings?

  1. The experiment necessarily failed because cell numbers did not fall
  2. The regulatory cells must have changed the TCR sequence of every effector
  3. Regulation may reduce activity per cell or alter tissue recruitment without deleting the clone
  4. The antigen is proven absent from every tissue
Answer and explanation

C. Regulation may reduce activity per cell or alter tissue recruitment without deleting the clone. A cellular response has both a numerical and a functional dimension. Reduced inflammation can result from altered activation, cytokine output or localization while the clone remains detectable. Other readouts are needed to identify the specific mechanism.

Examination traps and misconceptions

1. A CTL recognizes a free viral protein.

Correct reasoning: Its conventional TCR recognizes a particular peptide–MHC I complex. Why confusion arises: Students confuse TCR and BCR recognition. Examination strategy: Compare targets carrying a viral peptide on matched and mismatched class I.

2. All CD8 T cells are immediately cytotoxic.

Correct reasoning: Naive and differentiated CD8 populations have distinct functional states. Why confusion arises: Coreceptor identity is mistaken for effector differentiation. Examination strategy: Compare identical TCR specificity before and after priming.

3. Perforin is the protease that cleaves Bid.

Correct reasoning: Perforin promotes granzyme delivery; granzyme B cleaves suitable proteins. Why confusion arises: The two granule contents are learned as an inseparable pair. Examination strategy: Predict the consequence of a catalytic granzyme mutation versus perforin loss.

4. Granzyme must always cross a permanent plasma membrane pore.

Correct reasoning: Perforin-dependent delivery can involve endosomal uptake and release. Why confusion arises: Simple cartoons conceal experimentally supported alternative delivery routes. Examination strategy: Interpret cytosolic versus endosomal granzyme localization.

5. Degranulation proves target killing.

Correct reasoning: Exocytosis, cytosolic delivery and target death are separate steps. Why confusion arises: CD107a is interpreted as a complete cytotoxicity readout. Examination strategy: Compare normal CD107a exposure with impaired target apoptosis.

6. Perforin deficiency removes every death pathway.

Correct reasoning: Fas-mediated apoptosis can remain functional. Why confusion arises: Convergence on apoptosis obscures distinct initiating mechanisms. Examination strategy: Compare single and double pathway perturbations.

7. Loss of MHC I always causes NK killing.

Correct reasoning: NK cells integrate inhibitory and activating cues; missing self can favor, but does not guarantee, attack. Why confusion arises: Missing-self recognition is memorized as an unconditional rule. Examination strategy: Provide MHC-low targets with or without activating ligands.

8. ADCC is a conventional peptide–MHC-restricted CTL response.

Correct reasoning: Antibody specificity can be coupled to an Fc receptor-bearing effector such as NK cells. Why confusion arises: The shared granule machinery obscures the different recognition step. Examination strategy: Test antibody coating and CD16 dependence.

9. IFN-γ directly destroys all intracellular microbes.

Correct reasoning: It changes the functions of responsive cells; antimicrobial outcomes depend on the organism and host context. Why confusion arises: A cytokine is mistaken for a microbicidal enzyme. Examination strategy: Compare normal and IFN-γ receptor-deficient macrophages.

10. Th17 cells are neutrophils or directly kill every extracellular microbe.

Correct reasoning: Th17-derived mediators can induce tissue recruitment signals for innate effectors. Why confusion arises: Cell source and responding population are conflated. Examination strategy: Distinguish epithelial IL-17 response from neutrophil chemotaxis.

11. IL-2 is always purely pro-inflammatory.

Correct reasoning: It supports conventional effector expansion and regulatory-cell maintenance. Why confusion arises: Cytokines are assigned a single fixed function. Examination strategy: Predict effects of IL-2 deprivation on two different receptor-bearing populations.

12. CTLA-4 binds PD-L1.

Correct reasoning: CTLA-4 recognizes CD80/CD86; PD-1 recognizes PD-L1/PD-L2. Why confusion arises: Inhibitory receptors are memorized as one interchangeable family. Examination strategy: Match receptor–ligand pairs and their mechanisms.

13. PD-1 expression proves exhaustion or death.

Correct reasoning: PD-1 can be expressed on activated cells; exhaustion requires functional and contextual evidence. Why confusion arises: The receptor's name and marker association are taken literally. Examination strategy: Compare acute activation and chronic exposure with functional readouts.

14. Every CD25-positive CD4 cell is a Treg.

Correct reasoning: CD25 is also induced on activated conventional cells; regulatory identity requires additional evidence. Why confusion arises: Surface-marker shortcuts replace functional testing. Examination strategy: Use FOXP3 context and controlled suppression assays.

15. Central tolerance means no self-reactive cell survives.

Correct reasoning: Developmental selection is incomplete and peripheral control remains necessary. Why confusion arises: An idealized selection scheme is treated as absolute. Examination strategy: Predict effects of reduced thymic expression of one self antigen.

16. Receptor editing and class switching are the same.

Correct reasoning: Editing changes receptor specificity; class switching changes the heavy-chain constant region. Why confusion arises: Both involve rearrangements of immunoglobulin genes. Examination strategy: Identify the developmental stage and molecular region altered.

17. Anergy is equivalent to deletion.

Correct reasoning: Anergy retains viable but functionally unresponsive cells; deletion removes cells. Why confusion arises: Both reduce measured responses. Examination strategy: Measure clone counts and response per viable cell.

18. A declining local effector count proves organism-wide apoptosis.

Correct reasoning: Migration and redistribution can alter local counts. Why confusion arises: A tissue sample is mistaken for the whole population. Examination strategy: Compare tissue, blood and node counts plus death markers.

19. Tolerance is general immunodeficiency.

Correct reasoning: Tolerance can selectively restrain responses to a particular antigen while preserving others. Why confusion arises: Reduced response is interpreted without specifying antigen specificity. Examination strategy: Challenge with unrelated antigen as a control.

20. The peak number of T cells alone measures protection.

Correct reasoning: Recognition, access, effector capacity and tissue damage also matter. Why confusion arises: Cell number is easy to measure but is not the final biological endpoint. Examination strategy: Compare microbial burden with equal or unequal effector counts.

Revision and student mastery

Cell-mediated immunity converts antigen-specific T-cell recognition into target-cell death, enhanced antimicrobial function and recruitment of additional effectors. CTLs and NK cells may share cytotoxic machinery but differ in recognition. Cytokines and inhibitory receptors control magnitude, location and duration, while developmental selection and peripheral tolerance restrain inappropriate responses. Contraction reduces the expanded effector pool without necessarily eliminating memory.

Essential quantitative relationships

  • Specific release (%) = 100(E − S)/(M − S), with a positive and adequate usable signal range.
  • Total secretion = Nq; under constant production and first-order clearance, C* = Nq/(Vk).
  • For constant net growth, N(t) = N0 exp[(p − d)t]. During simple exponential contraction, t1/2 = ln(2)/k.
  • Relative suppression (%) = 100(control − regulated)/control for a defined background-corrected readout.
