1. Why immune defense must distinguish a target from its surroundings

A virus that has entered a living epithelial cell presents a different defensive problem from a virus that is still moving through extracellular fluid. In the fluid, an antibody may bind a viral surface molecule and prevent attachment to a susceptible cell. Once viral proteins are being synthesized inside a host cell, circulating antibodies generally cannot enter the cytoplasm in sufficient quantities to neutralize that intracellular machinery. The host has to recognize a change in the cell itself, limit the production of new virus particles, and sometimes remove the infected cell altogether. Removing a host cell is a costly action because that cell belongs to the organism. The ability to recognize which cells should be eliminated, while leaving their uninfected neighbors functional, is therefore a central problem of cell-mediated immunity.

Consider a second problem. A macrophage has engulfed a bacterium, but the bacterium survives inside the membrane-bound compartment in which it was captured. The infected macrophage is already a defensive cell. Destroying it is not necessarily the most useful first action. A more efficient solution may be to strengthen the macrophage's own antimicrobial machinery. The lymphocyte that provides this help does not have to enter the phagosome or recognize the bacterium directly. It can receive information about the organism from the macrophage's surface and deliver signals that improve intracellular killing. The two problems illustrate distinct uses of adaptive T cells: removal of an infected host cell and improvement of the defensive capacity of another cell.

An effector is a cell or molecule that actually produces a biological consequence. In an adaptive immune response, a lymphocyte first recognizes an antigen, becomes activated, and differentiates. Its subsequent effector activity is distinct from the recognition and proliferation that produced it. A cytotoxic T lymphocyte, abbreviated CTL, is an activated T cell capable of initiating the death of a suitable target cell. Most conventional CTLs are derived from CD8-positive T cells. Helper T cells derived from conventional CD4-positive T cells instead frequently act through cytokine secretion and membrane-associated signals. The term helper does not mean that these lymphocytes are dispensable. Their ability to coordinate other cells can determine whether recognition of an organism results in successful elimination.

Cell-mediated immunity refers especially to adaptive immune functions that depend on T cells and their coordinated cellular effectors. The expression does not imply that every reaction performed by a cell belongs exclusively to this category. Antibody-coated targets can be ingested by phagocytes or attacked by natural killer cells; those reactions depend on antibody recognition even though a cell carries out the effector step. Conversely, soluble cytokines released by T cells may operate without sustained physical contact. The important distinction is which recognition system directs the response and how that recognition is translated into an effective biological action.

The antigen receptor of a conventional alpha-beta T cell detects a molecular complex rather than a free protein. A fragment of a protein, called a peptide, is displayed by a major histocompatibility complex molecule, abbreviated MHC. Most nucleated mammalian cells display class I MHC molecules with peptides sampled from intracellular protein turnover. Professional antigen-presenting cells also display class II MHC molecules, which frequently carry peptides derived from material acquired into endosomal compartments. The rules are not absolute because cross-presentation and additional processing routes connect compartments. Nevertheless, the distinction provides a reliable starting point for understanding why conventional CD8 T cells survey many tissues and CD4 T cells frequently direct the activities of specialized antigen-presenting cells.

The molecular identity of a displayed peptide is not sufficient by itself to decide whether a lymphocyte should attack. Many self-derived peptides are continually displayed even in healthy tissues. The lymphocyte repertoire contains antigen receptors generated by genetic recombination, so self-reactive receptors can arise as an unavoidable consequence of producing diverse recognition structures. Selection during development removes or redirects many dangerous lymphocytes, but it does not guarantee that no self-reactive lymphocyte ever reaches a peripheral organ. Additional controls must operate after development. These controls explain why recognition, activation and attack are separate biological decisions.

An immune response must also have a termination phase. The disappearance of an organism does not instantly eliminate the many activated cells that arose during its infection. Nor does cessation of an activating signal automatically remove soluble mediators already present in tissues. If activated lymphocytes continue dividing, secreting cytokines or recruiting phagocytes after the threat has been removed, the tissue may suffer more damage from the defense than from the original disturbance. Immune modulation therefore encompasses increases and decreases in immune activity, adjustments in its location and duration, and changes in the relative contributions of different cell populations. Self-tolerance is the condition in which the immune system avoids damaging responses against the organism's own constituents. These properties are interconnected, but they are not interchangeable: a response may be appropriately directed against a foreign antigen yet still be excessive.

To analyze an unfamiliar immune experiment, identify four distinct quantities: the number of antigen-specific lymphocytes present, their activation state, their physical access to the target, and the susceptibility of that target to the effector mechanism. An increase in the first quantity does not necessarily increase the fourth. For example, a tissue may contain many antigen-specific CTLs, yet its infected cells may present very little of the relevant peptide. Another tissue may display abundant peptide but resist one pathway of apoptosis. Measuring lymphocyte number alone cannot discriminate those possibilities. The following mechanisms provide a framework for identifying the relevant missing step.

2. How experiments established antigen-specific cellular defense

Early immune experiments often relied on the transfer of protective activity between animals. If cell-free serum obtained from an immune animal protected another animal, a soluble substance could account for at least part of that protection. If protection instead required viable lymphocytes, a cellular mechanism became a plausible explanation. Transfer experiments do not establish that an immune response is exclusively cellular or exclusively humoral. Rather, they test whether the material being transferred contains a component sufficient to reproduce a particular measured effect under the conditions of the experiment.

The discovery of MHC restriction provided a way to define the target of T-cell recognition. In 1974, Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty reported experiments using lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in mice [1]. Cytotoxic lymphocytes generated during infection were tested against infected target cells from different genetic backgrounds. A central observation was that viral infection alone was not sufficient for efficient recognition: the appropriate relationship between effector and target MHC was also required. These observations helped replace the idea of a T cell responding merely to a viral molecular structure with the modern concept of recognition of a peptide in an appropriate self-MHC context.

An instructive way to reconstruct the logic is to consider four groups of target cells. Let the responding lymphocytes come from an immunized mouse expressing class I allele A. Targets may express allele A or allele B, and each may be infected or uninfected. A model in which the T cell responds to the viral antigen independently of MHC predicts that both infected groups will be susceptible. A model in which the T cell recognizes allele A independently of infection predicts that both allele-A groups will be susceptible. A conventional antigen-specific restricted CTL is instead expected to attack mainly the infected allele-A group. The other three groups test different alternative explanations. This four-group matrix is a simplified teaching reconstruction; it is not a transcription of measured values from the historical paper.

A complication arises if the effector population is not a pure antigen-specific T-cell population. Activated lymphocytes may include cells capable of reacting to foreign MHC molecules, and natural killer cells can attack certain targets without a rearranged peptide-specific T-cell receptor. An experimentally observed attack on an MHC-mismatched target therefore does not automatically invalidate MHC restriction. It may indicate an additional effector population or an alloreactive T-cell clone. The investigator must define the cell population, measure the relevant receptor expression, and include appropriate negative controls before interpreting exceptions.

Genetic disruption experiments subsequently distinguished recognition from the molecular execution of cytotoxicity. In 1994, Kägi and colleagues reported that cytotoxic activity mediated by T cells and natural killer cells was substantially impaired in mice lacking perforin [2]. This provides causal evidence that a cytotoxic granule component is important. It does not imply that every target in every tissue becomes completely resistant when perforin is absent. Alternative killing mechanisms can contribute, and different target populations vary in susceptibility. The contrast between loss of recognition and loss of execution remains useful: a perforin-deficient CTL may still recognize its peptide–MHC ligand, but it cannot execute normal perforin-dependent granule-mediated killing.

The history of immune regulation illustrates a complementary experimental strategy. Instead of asking what is necessary to produce an attack, investigators asked whether a defined cell population was necessary to restrain attack. In 1995, Shimon Sakaguchi and colleagues reported experiments identifying a CD4-positive, CD25-positive population that contributed to maintenance of immunological self-tolerance [3]. Removing this population from transferred T cells permitted pathological immune responses in recipient mice, while the regulatory population could prevent such effects in appropriate transfer settings. This established that self-tolerance is not merely the passive consequence of eliminating every autoreactive cell during development. Some lymphocytes actively restrain other lymphocytes in the periphery.

Two independent reports in 1995 demonstrated the severe immune consequences of disruption of the gene encoding CTLA-4 in mice [4, 5]. This type of observation reverses a common assumption: an immune receptor that inhibits activation is not necessarily an undesirable obstacle to defense. Its absence can produce uncontrolled lymphocyte expansion and destructive tissue inflammation. The relevant comparison is not simply whether knockout lymphocytes proliferate more than normal cells. The decisive organism-level question is whether proliferation remains appropriate in time and location.

The major historical experiments are linked by a common strategy. Each separates a variable that would otherwise change together with many other variables: the genetic identity of a target, the presence of a cytotoxic effector protein, the presence of a regulatory cell population, or the expression of an inhibitory receptor. A convincing conclusion requires both a meaningful perturbation and a readout that measures the predicted consequence. When studying a published figure, ask which variables were held constant and which alternative explanation is excluded by the controls. An experiment showing association may identify a useful candidate mechanism, but a selective perturbation followed by a suitable rescue or independent measurement usually gives stronger causal evidence.

3. From primed CD8 T cells to recognition of peripheral targets

A naive CD8 T cell and a differentiated CTL can express the same antigen-specific T-cell receptor but behave very differently after binding antigen. The naive cell generally needs signals from an appropriately activated antigen-presenting cell. Antigen presentation, costimulation and cytokines cooperate to initiate proliferation and differentiation. A differentiated CTL already contains much of the machinery needed for immediate effector activity. It can therefore respond to peptide–MHC on a target that would not be effective at priming naive T cells. This distinction resolves the apparent paradox that infected epithelial cells, which usually do not provide the full costimulatory context required for priming, can nonetheless be killed by antigen-specific effector T cells.

Dendritic cells help bridge the anatomical separation between priming and attack. They can acquire antigen from infected tissues and activate T cells in draining lymphoid organs. If the dendritic cell itself synthesizes microbial protein, the conventional intracellular route can supply peptides to class I MHC. If the antigen is acquired from another infected cell, cross-presentation may instead deliver suitable peptides to class I molecules. Cross-presentation is a mechanism of peptide display; cross-priming is the resulting activation of naive CD8 T cells when the other necessary conditions are satisfied. A dendritic cell may cross-present antigen without producing effective immunity if costimulation is insufficient or the surrounding environment is tolerogenic.

Once differentiation has occurred, effector cells leave lymphoid sites and circulate or migrate into tissues. A chemokine gradient can help direct their movement, and adhesion molecules facilitate passage through endothelium and contacts within tissue. These mechanisms determine access, whereas the T-cell receptor determines molecular specificity. A chemokine receptor defect can reduce the number of CTLs reaching an infected tissue without preventing those same cells from killing appropriate targets when the cells are brought together in a culture assay. This type of comparison distinguishes a trafficking defect from a defect intrinsic to effector function.

At the target interface, a conventional T-cell receptor recognizes features contributed by both peptide and MHC. CD8 acts as a coreceptor by interacting with a relatively conserved portion of class I MHC and by helping position signaling machinery close to the engaged receptor complex. CD8 does not provide peptide-specific recognition by itself. An isolated CD8 molecule cannot replace a TCR with an unrelated binding specificity. Conversely, the contribution of CD8 to a particular response varies with the properties of the TCR–peptide–MHC interaction. It is therefore incorrect to assume that loss of CD8 causes precisely the same quantitative reduction for every clone under all assay conditions.

A target cell displays many different peptide–MHC complexes at the same time. Only a fraction may contain the cognate antigen, meaning the peptide–MHC ligand recognized by the particular CTL. The abundance of this ligand depends on antigen synthesis and degradation, processing, peptide transport, binding to class I MHC and surface persistence. A cell that produces abundant viral protein need not display a correspondingly large amount of every viral peptide. Different epitopes can be produced with different efficiencies. This makes the density of cognate peptide–MHC a more direct recognition variable than the total amount of viral protein inside a cell.

Recognition has a physical as well as chemical component. Protein–protein binding depends on complementarity of molecular surfaces and the sum of reversible noncovalent interactions. Contact between two moving cells also depends on adhesion and membrane organization. Integrins such as LFA-1 on the lymphocyte can bind ICAM molecules and help stabilize productive contact. This interaction is not a substitute for the specific antigen receptor, but it gives that receptor time and spatial opportunity to generate signals. If a mutation reduces adhesion, short contacts may become insufficient for normal degranulation even though the TCR still binds its ligand in a purified binding assay.

The organized interface between a lymphocyte and its interacting cell is called an immunological synapse. Its components include antigen receptors, coreceptors, adhesion proteins and the cytoskeleton that supports localized secretion. The arrangement is dynamic; an idealized pattern of a central receptor region surrounded by an adhesion ring is a useful representation of some mature contacts, not a universal architecture that every successful killing event must display. The essential functional point is the coupling of recognition at the membrane to directed delivery of effector molecules. The next stage of the response depends on that spatial coupling.

4. The perforin–granzyme pathway: delivering a death signal

A cytotoxic granule is a specialized membrane-bound compartment within an effector lymphocyte. It combines features of a secretory organelle and a lysosome. Unlike an ordinary lysosome whose contents are primarily used within its own cell, the cytotoxic granule stores molecules that can be exported during interaction with an appropriate target. Perforin is a pore-forming protein and granzymes are proteolytic enzymes. Their functions complement one another: perforin helps overcome a membrane barrier, whereas granzymes alter proteins inside the target cell. The destructive molecules must be stored and delivered in a way that avoids premature activity against the lymphocyte producing them.

The granule environment is acidic, and its contents are organized in association with negatively charged matrix components such as the proteoglycan serglycin. Granule packaging, processing of proteases and membrane separation all contribute to safe storage. The extracellular medium differs chemically from the granule lumen. When secreted, perforin encounters calcium-containing fluid at a more nearly neutral pH. These conditions favor its association with target membranes and formation of functional assemblies. Thus the change in compartment is a functional part of secretion: molecules that are safely sequestered in one environment become active in another. A defect that changes storage or processing might reduce killing even when synthesis of the relevant protein remains detectable.

Recognition of antigen stimulates rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton and helps orient the secretory apparatus toward the contact. Microtubules contribute to the transport of cytotoxic granules, which accumulate in the region facing the target. The response also depends on docking, priming and membrane fusion. These events are regulated rather than being a simple rupture of the granule membrane. A rise in intracellular calcium in the effector cell contributes to regulated exocytosis. The membranes of secretory granules fuse with the plasma membrane of the lymphocyte, exposing their luminal contents to the space between the two cells. The fusion event does not directly mix the cytoplasm of the lymphocyte with the cytoplasm of the target.

Polarized secretion raises the local concentration of cytotoxic molecules while limiting the total amount that escapes into the surrounding tissue. It also creates a mechanistic relationship between cell contact and delivery. A lymphocyte may contain normal amounts of perforin and granzyme B but be unable to release them at the contact because its trafficking or fusion machinery is defective. Such a cell differs fundamentally from one lacking the TCR ligand or from one unable to synthesize the effector proteins. In a degranulation assay, surface exposure of a normally granule-associated membrane protein such as CD107a can provide evidence that fusion occurred. This readout does not establish that perforin was functional or that the recipient cell received cytosolic granzymes.

Perforin contains a membrane-interacting domain that is important for calcium-dependent membrane association and a pore-forming region related to the membrane attack complex/perforin family. Multiple molecules can associate within a membrane to form a pore. The pore changes the permeability of the membrane, which can allow ions to move according to their electrochemical gradients. Perforin must not be confused with the terminal complement membrane attack complex. Both involve pore-forming proteins, but complement assembly takes place through its extracellular activation cascade, whereas perforin is supplied by a cytotoxic lymphocyte. Eliminating a complement protein does not directly remove the perforin stored in a CTL.

A simplified textbook diagram often shows granzyme moving through a perforin-generated opening in the target membrane. The central idea is that perforin promotes delivery of granzyme to the target cytosol, not that the granzyme freely crosses an intact lipid bilayer. Experimental work has also demonstrated a pathway involving transient plasma membrane damage, uptake of perforin and granzyme into vesicles, and perforin-dependent escape of granzyme from the endosomal compartment [12, 13]. The exact route can depend on the experimental system. For examination reasoning, avoid claiming that all physiological granzyme delivery necessarily occurs by direct passage through a large permanent plasma membrane hole.

Once a granzyme reaches the target cytoplasm it can cleave suitable protein substrates. Granzyme B is a serine protease with a preference for cleavage after particular aspartate residues in its substrates. Its actions can activate executioner caspases and can promote mitochondrial amplification through cleavage of Bid, a proapoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family. Caspases are themselves proteolytic enzymes that dismantle selected cellular components. The target cell, rather than the lymphocyte, contains much of the machinery that executes the final programmed death. Not every granzyme acts on the same substrates or requires exactly the same death pathway, and the details can differ between species.

A cell undergoing apoptosis commonly exhibits chromatin condensation, cell shrinkage and membrane blebbing, followed by removal of cellular material by phagocytes. The membrane often remains sufficiently intact during early apoptosis to prevent immediate uncontrolled release of cytosolic contents. This contrasts with rapid lytic injury. The distinction is important at tissue scale: a targeted death that can be followed by efficient clearance may disrupt fewer neighboring cells than indiscriminate destruction. However, apoptosis is not a guarantee that inflammation cannot occur. The response depends on the nature of the infection, clearance of dying cells and accompanying cytokine secretion.

After delivering a sufficient stimulus, a CTL can detach from the target before the target completes its death program. It can then engage additional susceptible cells. This property is sometimes described as serial killing. The capacity is not unlimited: the lymphocyte requires adequate effector stores, metabolic resources and access to additional targets, and its responses may change during prolonged antigen exposure. A single CTL is not consumed in every successful attack, so a one-to-one numerical correspondence between lymphocyte number and killed target number is not a general law. Conversely, not every contact with a target leads to commitment to death.

There are several experimentally distinguishable points of failure in this pathway. If antigen recognition fails, antigen-triggered signaling and degranulation can both be reduced. If membrane fusion fails, upstream activation may remain normal while delivery is impaired. If perforin is defective, degranulation may be detectable without efficient cytosolic granzyme action. If an inhibitory protein in the target prevents the action of a particular granzyme, recognition and delivery may proceed normally but target death may be reduced. The stages form a causal sequence, but a measurement at any one stage should not be treated as a complete measure of all the others.

Suppose an inhibitor reduces calcium availability outside the two interacting cells. A reduced level of killing cannot be interpreted immediately as specific evidence that perforin no longer forms pores. Calcium can also influence lymphocyte activation, secretion, target membrane repair and adhesion under certain conditions. An investigator could separate the possibilities by measuring effector degranulation independently of target apoptosis and by testing a condition in which already secreted effector molecules are examined. The significance of a reagent follows from its effects on the relevant system, not simply from the name of the molecule to which it is usually associated.

5. Fas–FasL signaling and alternative routes to target-cell death

The second major contact-dependent strategy used by CTLs is to activate a death receptor on the surface of the target. Fas, also named CD95 or APO-1, belongs to the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily. It possesses an extracellular region that recognizes its ligand and a cytoplasmic region that can participate in death-signaling complexes. Fas ligand, abbreviated FasL or CD95L, is a corresponding member of the TNF ligand family that can be expressed by activated T cells. Recognition of cognate antigen helps focus the action of the T cell, but the actual activation of Fas requires the presence of a suitable Fas molecule on the target.

Fas engagement promotes the assembly of a signaling complex that recruits the adaptor FADD through death-domain interactions. FADD in turn supports recruitment of initiator procaspase-8 through complementary protein-interaction domains. Concentrating appropriate procaspase molecules within such a complex favors their activation. Active initiator caspases can then activate downstream executioner caspases and, in some cell types, engage mitochondrial amplification. It is not necessary for Fas ligand to cross the plasma membrane of the target or enter its nucleus. The extracellular ligand changes the assembly of signaling proteins on the cytoplasmic side of the receptor.

The logic of Fas signaling differs from that of the perforin–granzyme pathway. In granule-mediated killing, the effector cell exports proteins that must ultimately influence intracellular substrates in the target. In Fas-mediated killing, the effector cell engages a surface receptor that transmits a death signal through the target's own machinery. Both can converge on apoptosis, but convergence at a late stage does not make their upstream events identical. A caspase measurement, for example, may show that the target is dying without establishing which of the two routes initiated death.

Fas expression is an important limiting condition. A target with very little functional Fas can remain susceptible to granzyme-mediated killing if it displays the cognate antigen and supports granzyme entry. Conversely, a target lacking susceptibility to perforin-mediated granule delivery may still die when an activated CTL engages Fas, provided the receptor and downstream signaling remain functional. Resistance to one pathway therefore cannot be generalized automatically to complete resistance to the effector cell. Some targets resist both pathways, and cytokines secreted by the effector may still change their behavior in the absence of direct cytotoxicity.

Fas ligand is not exclusively a weapon used against infected cells. Fas-mediated apoptosis also contributes to regulation of some activated lymphocyte populations. Repeated stimulation can increase susceptibility to death-receptor-mediated elimination under suitable conditions. This illustrates how a molecular system used to execute an immune response can also help restrict its duration and amplitude. The biological consequence depends on which cell expresses the receptor, which expresses the ligand, whether they meet, and how the intracellular death machinery is regulated. The same named receptor can thus participate in both defense and homeostasis.

A useful experimental design compares wild-type effector cells with perforin-deficient cells against targets that either express functional Fas or lack it. A reduction when only perforin is absent supports a contribution from the granule pathway. A further reduction when Fas is also absent from the target supports a contribution from the death-receptor route. The informative result is the pattern across conditions, not a claim that the percentages contributed by the two mechanisms must sum arithmetically to one hundred. The same cell may be susceptible to either route, and the rates and probabilities of the two pathways can interact.

The interpretation also depends on the time at which cytotoxicity is measured. Granule-mediated killing can be initiated rapidly because differentiated CTLs have preformed effector contents. Fas-dependent killing may have different kinetics depending on ligand expression, receptor density and the target's intracellular machinery. A comparison at an early time point may underestimate a slower pathway. At a late time point, spontaneous target death may become substantial, reducing the ability to distinguish specific killing. Appropriate spontaneous-death controls and time courses are therefore integral to interpreting such an experiment.

The death receptor family illustrates an important exception to simplistic interpretations of ligand binding. Expression of a receptor does not prove that its signal will cause cell death. The downstream proteins required to transmit the signal may be absent, inhibited or subject to alternative regulation. Similarly, expression of FasL on an activated lymphocyte does not demonstrate that a particular target died through Fas. Functional perturbations are required to assign the actual causal contribution. For an examination question, distinguish a necessary component of a proposed pathway from a marker merely associated with the cells involved.

6. Comparing adaptive CTL killing, NK cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent defense

Cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells share the ability to secrete perforin and granzymes, but their recognition systems are different. A conventional CTL responds to a particular peptide–MHC complex using its rearranged T-cell receptor. Natural killer cells integrate signals from multiple germline-encoded activating and inhibitory receptors. In many settings, recognition of normal class I MHC by inhibitory NK receptors reduces the likelihood of attack. Reduced expression of relevant class I ligands can remove this inhibition, but loss of an inhibitory ligand is not always sufficient to produce cytotoxicity because activating signals and cellular context also matter.

The comparison reveals a complementarity between the two mechanisms. A virus-infected cell that reduces surface MHC I may become less visible to a conventional CD8 T cell. At the same time, it may become more susceptible to an NK response if it has lost inhibitory ligands and retains sufficient activating signals. It would be misleading to state that every class-I-low cell is automatically killed by every NK cell. NK receptors differ in specificity, normal tissues can vary in their expression of ligands, and mechanisms of NK education influence responsiveness. The key prediction is a possible shift in the balance of recognition between the adaptive and innate cytotoxic compartments.

A different route to NK-cell cytotoxicity uses antibodies. An IgG antibody can recognize an antigen on a cell surface through its Fab regions, leaving its Fc region available for binding by Fc receptors. CD16, an Fc receptor expressed by many NK cells, can respond to appropriately clustered IgG on a target. This phenomenon is called antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity or ADCC. The antibody determines antigen specificity, while the NK cell supplies cytotoxic effector machinery. No conventional peptide–MHC recognition by a rearranged NK-cell antigen receptor is required. It is therefore an example of cooperation between humoral recognition and an innate cellular effector.

Antibody-mediated neutralization is mechanistically different from ADCC. In neutralization, binding prevents a pathogen or its product from performing a necessary biological interaction. For example, an antibody may obstruct a viral surface protein required for cell entry. Neutralization does not necessarily require direct death of the cell displaying an antigen. Opsonization instead makes a target easier for a phagocyte to recognize and ingest. Complement-dependent cytotoxicity uses a soluble cascade that can damage susceptible membranes after suitable activation. Although all of these outcomes may reduce the number of viable pathogens, their intermediate steps and genetic dependencies differ.

Imagine a pathogen that remains mainly extracellular. Specific antibodies may reduce access to susceptible cells, while complement and phagocytes help remove microbial material. A deficiency of perforin need not directly abolish antibody binding or complement opsonization. Now imagine an organism that replicates in the cytosol of a nucleated cell. The adaptive CD8 response gains particular importance because class I presentation samples intracellular proteins. But humoral immunity can still limit the spread of extracellular infectious particles, and innate interferons can suppress replication without killing the infected cell. The immune system normally uses cooperating functions rather than selecting one exclusively.

The biological compartment also changes over an infection cycle. A virus moves between extracellular and intracellular states, and a bacterium may pass between an extracellular site and an intracellular phagosome. The preferred effector mechanism can therefore change as infection proceeds. A question that asks which immune mechanism is relevant should specify the stage or location being considered. The blanket claim that viruses are controlled only by CTLs and bacteria only by antibodies overlooks these transitions and the important activities of CD4 T cells, innate leukocytes and soluble mediators.

Some CD4 T cells can acquire direct cytotoxic capacity, and not every CD8 T cell is actively cytotoxic. Coreceptor expression predicts a conventional recognition relationship more reliably than it predicts the entire functional state of an individual cell. In addition, unconventional T-cell populations can recognize ligands other than typical classical peptide–MHC complexes. These exceptions do not eliminate the usefulness of the conventional CD8/class I and CD4/class II framework. They establish its domain: it describes the predominant organization of conventional alpha-beta T-cell responses rather than every lymphocyte in every vertebrate tissue.

When interpreting an experiment in which a mixed lymphocyte population destroys a target, use the recognition mechanism to identify the effector. Target lysis alone cannot distinguish NK killing from CTL killing. Dependence on cognate peptide–MHC supports the conventional CTL mechanism; dependence on antibody coating and CD16 supports ADCC; sensitivity to the balance of NK activating and inhibitory ligands supports innate recognition. Measuring the effector population and perturbing one of these recognition steps is more informative than relying on an endpoint shared by all three pathways.

7. Th1 cells and the activation of macrophage antimicrobial functions

Macrophages are capable of engulfing particles, digesting cellular debris and responding to microbial molecular patterns. Nevertheless, the basal antimicrobial capacity of a macrophage may be inadequate when a pathogen survives after uptake. Some organisms interfere with maturation of the phagosome, resist antimicrobial chemicals or tolerate the conditions within a membrane-bound compartment. An adaptive immune response can strengthen the defenses of the macrophage rather than requiring an antibody to penetrate that compartment. This is a characteristic function of type 1 helper T cells, conventionally abbreviated Th1.

A macrophage that has ingested microbial material can process proteins and display suitable peptides using class II MHC. A differentiated Th1 cell with the appropriate TCR can then recognize the peptide–MHC complex. CD4 contributes to conventional class II recognition. The antigen therefore serves as a molecular link between the infection and the helper cell's response. The cytokine released by the helper cell is not itself specific for the microbial peptide. The specificity lies in the TCR-dependent decision to produce or direct the cytokine signal. Nearby cells that express the corresponding cytokine receptor may respond even if they do not display the cognate peptide.

Interferon gamma, commonly written IFN-γ, is a central mediator of classical macrophage activation. It is a type II interferon, distinct from the type I interferons IFN-α and IFN-β that participate prominently in antiviral states. The receptor for IFN-γ is expressed by many cells. Binding activates receptor-associated signaling proteins, leading through JAK and STAT1 to altered gene expression. In macrophages, this can promote programs that improve antimicrobial functions and antigen presentation. The pathway is an example of how extracellular protein binding modifies nuclear transcription without the cytokine itself traveling to the nucleus as a transcription factor.

A second important contact-dependent input is delivered through CD40 ligand on an activated T cell and CD40 on the macrophage. CD40 belongs to the TNF receptor superfamily and transmits signals through associated intracellular proteins. It is not an antigen receptor and does not determine which microbial peptide the T cell recognizes. The contact provides an additional activation cue whose effect can cooperate with IFN-γ and signals generated by innate microbial recognition. The relative requirement for these inputs varies with the macrophage state and the microorganism involved. The full response is better understood as integration of multiple cues than as a single universal molecular switch.

Among the functions enhanced in an activated macrophage are mechanisms associated with antimicrobial reactive oxygen species, changes in phagosome handling, production of antimicrobial enzymes and other transcriptionally regulated effector molecules. Nitric oxide generated by inducible nitric oxide synthase is an important antimicrobial mechanism in many experimental mouse macrophage systems. Human macrophages do not always reproduce the same magnitude or pattern of nitric oxide production, so results from a particular murine model should not be treated as a quantitative universal mammalian rule. This comparative limitation does not weaken the central concept that T-cell signals can increase the intracellular microbicidal capacity of a phagocyte.

Enhanced antimicrobial function does not mean that all microbes are killed simply because IFN-γ is present. A microorganism may resist particular effector molecules, occupy a compartment inaccessible to them, or alter the responsiveness of the host cell. The outcome depends on both the macrophage and the pathogen. Equally, increased antigen presentation alone is not a direct demonstration of antimicrobial killing. A macrophage may display more MHC molecules yet fail to eliminate a pathogen. An experiment should measure viable organisms or a validated measure of replication alongside the cellular activation markers.

There is reciprocal communication between macrophages, dendritic cells and Th1 cells. Innate microbial recognition can promote production of interleukin-12 by antigen-presenting cells. IL-12 helps support type 1 responses and IFN-γ production by appropriate T cells and natural killer cells. In turn, IFN-γ can strengthen macrophage activity and promote a cellular environment supportive of further type 1 responses. Such cooperation helps maintain the response while microbial cues persist. It also creates a risk of amplification beyond the original need, especially when recruitment of additional cells expands the number of cytokine-producing sources.

The term classically activated macrophage is useful for describing a pattern of inflammatory antimicrobial activation. It should not be read as the claim that every macrophage in a living tissue belongs permanently to one of two sharply separated categories. Tissue origin, metabolic state, microbial products and multiple cytokines shape macrophage behavior. Cells can adopt mixed or changing functional programs. For the NET/SET examination, the most reliable distinction is between a type 1 response that promotes antimicrobial activity through IFN-γ and other cooperating signals, and regulatory or repair-associated influences that can restrain inflammation or support restoration of tissue integrity.

The activated macrophage faces a trade-off. Chemical products that damage microbes may also damage host molecules if they escape into neighboring tissue. Cytokines released to recruit help can contribute to swelling and local dysfunction. The aim is not simply to maximize antimicrobial output under all circumstances but to produce adequate control of infection while limiting harm. Persistent stimulation by an intracellular organism can keep macrophages and T cells interacting for extended periods. The resulting chronic inflammatory organization and its pathogen-specific consequences are developed in later chapters. Here the essential mechanism is the conversion of adaptive antigen recognition into enhanced innate cellular activity.

Consider two macrophage cultures infected with the same number of organisms. One culture is exposed to conditioned medium from stimulated Th1 cells; the other receives otherwise identical medium from unstimulated cells. If microbial survival is lower in the first culture, a soluble product is a plausible contributor. Neutralizing IFN-γ and comparing the result with an appropriate control antibody can test whether this cytokine is necessary for the observed effect. Adding recombinant IFN-γ to a separate culture can test sufficiency under the defined conditions. The results are not automatically equivalent: a molecule can be necessary for the full effect without being sufficient to reproduce it alone.

A contact-dependent contribution can be investigated by comparing direct coculture with a separation system that allows diffusible molecules to pass but prevents the cells from touching. A reduction in activation in the separated condition supports the importance of contact or short-range delivery, but it may also reflect changes in local cytokine concentration. Blocking CD40–CD40L interactions supplies a more focused test of that particular pathway. The strongest interpretation combines contact, cytokine and receptor perturbations with a functional microbial readout rather than relying on a single expression measurement.
