Cellular homeostasis under challenge

An animal can maintain a nearly constant body temperature and blood glucose concentration while individual cells experience substantial changes in their immediate environments. During muscular activity, for example, ATP consumption rises as myosin cycles and ion pumps restore electrochemical gradients. A secretory cell faces a different problem when the rate of synthesis of proteins entering its endoplasmic reticulum increases faster than the rate at which those proteins can fold. A third cell may receive inflammatory signals that stimulate the production of reactive oxygen species. These examples share a principle: a demand has temporarily exceeded, or threatens to exceed, the capacity of a cellular process. The cellular response is not simply an alarm that reports the presence of a harmful agent. It is an adjustment of production, repair, removal and resource allocation designed to preserve function within the limits imposed by that challenge.

It is useful to distinguish the stressor, the change that the stressor produces, and the response to that change. Heat is a stressor; destabilization of protein structure is one possible consequence; increased chaperone synthesis is a protective response. A shortage of substrate or oxygen may reduce ATP production; a rise in the cellular AMP to ATP ratio is a signal of altered energy availability; activation of AMP-activated protein kinase is a regulatory response. These distinctions matter because measuring a stress-response protein does not directly measure the severity of injury. A cell with a well-developed protective response may express more of that protein yet sustain less damage than another cell exposed to the same challenge.

Consider two otherwise identical populations of cells. Both experience a brief heat exposure, but one population has previously undergone a mild, nonlethal thermal challenge and recovered. The earlier exposure may have increased folding capacity, allowing this population to survive the second challenge more effectively. This acquired tolerance is a physiological adjustment of the cells, not evidence that a heritable evolutionary adaptation has occurred. Whether tolerance develops depends on the nature, magnitude and duration of the initial challenge and the interval before the second challenge. Excessive initial exposure can instead leave residual damage and reduce subsequent tolerance.

Whole-animal neural and endocrine responses coordinate the supply and use of resources across tissues. Cellular responses operate within the cells of those tissues. The two scales influence each other, but a culture of isolated animal cells can activate antioxidant genes or the heat-shock response even in the absence of an adrenal gland. Conversely, a circulating hormone can change cellular metabolism without directly causing damage to protein structure. In this chapter the relevant question is how a cell recognizes molecular disturbances and adjusts its internal machinery, rather than how the hypothalamus initiates a systemic stress response.

Capacity, reserve and the time dimension

The word homeostasis sometimes suggests that every intracellular variable must remain perfectly fixed. In fact, a healthy cell exhibits fluctuations in ATP turnover, calcium concentration, redox state and rates of synthesis. A regulated fluctuation is often part of normal function. For example, a contractile cell cannot use calcium as a signal if cytosolic calcium never changes. What must be maintained is the capacity to terminate the signal and restore the conditions required for future activity. A transient increase in cytosolic calcium can be physiological, whereas continued elevation can activate destructive enzymes and disrupt mitochondrial function. Therefore duration and compartment are essential when deciding whether a change constitutes a damaging stress. A measurement taken at the peak of normal activity cannot be interpreted using the same expectation as a measurement made after a long recovery interval.

One useful way to organize cellular protection is to consider the difference between demand and available capacity. Imagine that a tissue produces ten arbitrary units of damaged protein per hour and can restore or remove twelve. Under these conditions it has a small reserve. If damage production increases to fifteen while removal remains twelve, the damaged pool increases by three units per hour. If subsequent induction of chaperones and degradation systems raises capacity above fifteen, accumulation can stop and previously damaged molecules can be cleared. The model assumes constant rates during each interval and treats all damaged protein as a single pool, which is not literally true. Nevertheless, it illustrates why a small additional challenge can have disproportionately large consequences when reserve capacity is almost exhausted. It also explains why measuring only the abundance of damaged protein does not distinguish increased production from reduced removal.

The same reasoning reveals why recovery cannot be defined merely as disappearance of a stressor. After heat exposure ends, aggregated proteins can persist, damaged membranes may need replacement and altered gene expression can continue. A cell that survives immediately may fail to divide or perform its specialized function later. For a proliferating cell, formation of a colony after recovery can be more informative than immediate membrane integrity. For a nondividing neuron, an assay of electrical signaling or another relevant functional output may be more appropriate. The biological endpoint must match the particular tissue. Survival, restoration of performance and restoration of the original molecular state are not interchangeable outcomes.

The response can be divided into overlapping temporal layers. Within seconds, existing enzymes and channels change activity through modifications or changes in substrate availability. Within minutes, regulatory proteins redistribute or change their interactions. Over longer periods, transcription, translation, degradation and organelle turnover alter the composition of the cell. These layers should not be treated as a rigid clock: an existing chaperone begins work before new chaperones are synthesized, and a transcription factor can be activated before repair is completed. A useful question for every mechanism is whether it changes the activity of a preexisting component or changes the quantity of that component. In an experiment, an inhibitor of protein synthesis may abolish a delayed protective response while leaving an early response intact. That finding alone does not identify the newly synthesized protein responsible for protection.

The molecular responses described here are best understood as networks rather than as mutually exclusive categories. An oxidant may modify proteins and impair mitochondria, simultaneously increasing the need for chaperones and reducing the ATP available for chaperone activity. If the cell reduces biosynthesis, energy becomes available for essential transport and repair, but continued suppression of biosynthesis can impair renewal of proteins. The successful response is therefore not necessarily the maximum activation of each protective pathway. It is a coordinated response whose cost remains compatible with physiological function. A highly secretory cell and a resting muscle fiber may respond differently because their normal allocation of energy and their demand for protein folding are different.

Reactive oxygen species: chemistry, origin and consequences

Oxygen is essential to aerobic metabolism because it can accept electrons at the end of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Complete reduction of molecular oxygen yields water, but transfer of a single electron to oxygen produces the superoxide radical anion, O₂•−. A radical possesses an unpaired electron; not all reactive oxygen species are radicals. Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, is a nonradical reactive oxygen species, and the hydroxyl radical, •OH, is a radical with particularly high reactivity toward nearby molecules. Distinguishing these chemical species explains why the site of production and the identity of the species are more informative than a single undifferentiated quantity called oxidative stress.

The mitochondrial respiratory chain can transfer electrons prematurely to oxygen, notably at certain sites associated with complexes I and III. The rate and location of this process depend on substrate availability, respiratory-chain reduction state, membrane potential and other conditions; it does not follow that more oxygen consumption always causes more superoxide production. NADPH oxidases are another important source. Unlike accidental diversion of electrons from respiration, their physiological role can be the regulated transfer of electrons from NADPH to oxygen. Phagocytes exploit an oxidative burst to help kill engulfed microorganisms. In other cells, regulated oxidant production participates in communication and changes in cellular behavior.

Hydrogen peroxide can influence the state of selected protein cysteine residues, directly or through redox relay proteins. Oxidation of a susceptible thiol can change enzyme activity or protein interactions. A regulated and reversible chemical change is therefore a plausible signal. The same species at an inappropriate concentration, in a vulnerable compartment, or for a prolonged period may disrupt the normal signaling network. Hydroxyl radicals formed when peroxide reacts with accessible ferrous iron react so rapidly that removing them after formation is difficult; limiting accessible redox-active metal and the peroxide supply is more effective than expecting a distant scavenger to intercept each radical.

Membranes containing polyunsaturated fatty acids are vulnerable to lipid peroxidation. Abstraction of a hydrogen atom can produce a lipid radical that reacts with oxygen. The resulting lipid peroxyl radical can attack another lipid, propagating a chain reaction. The biological importance is not limited to the disappearance of individual lipid molecules. The membrane environment may change, and reactive lipid-breakdown products may modify proteins. Oxidation of proteins can disturb catalytic activity, interactions or stability. Oxidative modifications of DNA bases can change pairing behavior during replication if the lesions remain unrepaired. Thus one perturbation can affect membrane transport, protein function and genome integrity through different chemical routes.

A common conceptual error is to treat oxidative stress as synonymous with any detectable ROS. Basal oxidant production occurs in healthy cells, and some ROS support regulated signaling and host defense. A useful distinction is between controlled redox signaling and an oxidant-associated disturbance of redox control or molecular integrity. A further distinction separates exposure from outcome: the quantity of oxidant generated, the quantity removed, the chemical changes accumulated in targets and the residual physiological function are separate measurements. These relationships explain why an antioxidant enzyme assay is not itself a direct assay of lipid or DNA damage.

Compartmental chemistry and the origin of redox selectivity

The term oxidation describes loss of electrons, and reduction describes gain of electrons. In biological electron transfer, hydrogen atoms or hydride equivalents often carry reducing power between molecules, so the language of oxidation does not require that molecular oxygen participate in every reaction. Reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, NADH, transfers reducing equivalents from metabolism into respiratory pathways, while NADPH supplies reducing equivalents for biosynthetic and antioxidant reactions. Their chemically similar names should not obscure their different physiological roles. The ratios of oxidized and reduced forms vary among cellular compartments. Increasing NADH availability cannot be assumed to substitute directly for a shortage of NADPH at a particular antioxidant enzyme, because enzyme specificity and intracellular transport constrain that substitution.

A molecule of oxygen accepts four electrons and four protons during its complete conversion to two molecules of water. Respiratory complex IV carries out this reduction in a controlled catalytic environment. One-electron transfer outside that coordinated reaction instead gives superoxide. Superoxide can undergo spontaneous dismutation or enzyme-catalyzed dismutation to peroxide and oxygen. Because the superoxide anion has a charge at physiological pH, it does not readily pass through an ordinary lipid bilayer in the same manner as a small uncharged molecule. Hydrogen peroxide is neutral and has different transport properties; certain aquaporins can facilitate its movement. Nevertheless, its local concentration is strongly affected by removal enzymes, so production in one compartment does not mean that the entire cell experiences the same concentration.

The mitochondrial inner membrane separates the matrix from the intermembrane space, while the outer membrane provides another boundary between mitochondria and cytosol. A superoxide-generating site on one side of the inner membrane does not necessarily expose both sides to an equal initial flux. A matrix-localized antioxidant enzyme may protect enzymes of intermediary metabolism but cannot automatically replace a cytosolic defense. A useful experimental approach is to compare targeted probes or targeted restoration of antioxidant proteins in the two compartments. Interpretation requires checking that the targeted protein really reaches the intended compartment and that its expression does not itself perturb cellular physiology. An absence of a signal in the cytosol is not proof that oxidant production is absent in mitochondria.

Peroxisomes offer a different example of the relationship between metabolism and oxidation. Some oxidative reactions in these organelles generate hydrogen peroxide as a product rather than transferring all reducing equivalents to a respiratory chain. Catalase provides substantial peroxide-removal capacity in many peroxisomes. The proximity of source and removal is a form of protection: a reactive product can be consumed near its site of production before it affects sensitive targets elsewhere. This does not mean that peroxisomes never contribute to cellular redox signaling. The balance between production, local removal and movement across organellar membranes determines the net influence. Therefore an experiment that increases a peroxisomal oxidase should not automatically be interpreted as a specific mitochondrial perturbation.

Some oxidants interact with nitrogen-containing signaling molecules. Nitric oxide is a radical involved in normal physiology, including vascular relaxation. Its reaction with superoxide can generate peroxynitrite, a reactive nitrogen species. This provides an instructive link between redox disturbances in a cell and whole-animal vascular function: greater superoxide production can reduce the availability of nitric oxide for normal signaling while also giving rise to additional chemical modifications. A decrease in a nitric-oxide-dependent response therefore need not arise from reduced synthesis alone. The detailed mechanisms controlling vascular tone belong to earlier physiology chapters, but this example shows why a local oxidative disturbance can change an integrated physiological outcome.

Why chemical reactivity is not equivalent to biological importance

Students often predict that the most reactive species will travel farthest and affect the most targets. The reverse can be true. An extremely reactive species encounters and reacts with a nearby molecule before it can diffuse through much of the cell. A species of lower intrinsic reactivity may travel farther or be regulated through selective reactions. Reaction rate depends not only on intrinsic reactivity but also on the concentrations of the reacting species. Abundant antioxidant enzymes can intercept much of the peroxide before it encounters a particular signaling protein. Some signaling targets acquire redox modifications through intermediary proteins. This is one reason why a large chemical list of possible reactions cannot establish which reactions dominate in a living cell. Physiological relevance requires information about location, concentration, competing reactions and time.

The Fenton reaction provides a useful example of this selectivity problem. Ferrous iron reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce ferric iron, hydroxide and a hydroxyl radical. If the iron is located near a vulnerable DNA molecule, the radical may react before a soluble scavenger can intercept it. Proteins that bind, transport and store iron contribute to preventing inappropriate chemistry by restricting the accessible metal pool. Total cellular iron is consequently not the same quantity as the redox-active iron available to a peroxide molecule. An iron-binding compound may reduce one class of oxidative damage without decreasing the production of peroxide. Conversely, removing too much biologically available iron can impair iron-dependent enzymes and oxygen-related functions. Protective interventions have limits because the materials that support damaging reactions also serve essential biological roles.

The propagation phase of membrane lipid peroxidation differs from a single modification of a protein. A lipid-derived radical can initiate further radical formation in neighboring lipids, amplifying damage within a membrane. Polyunsaturated lipids are particularly susceptible because abstraction of certain hydrogens yields resonance-stabilized radicals. A lipid-soluble chain-breaking antioxidant can interrupt propagation, and enzymes that reduce lipid hydroperoxides help prevent accumulation of oxidized phospholipids. Neither intervention is equivalent to superoxide dismutation in the aqueous matrix. The distinction matters when comparing tissues that differ in fatty acid composition, membrane turnover or antioxidant capacity. Equal rates of oxidant production need not result in equal rates of membrane injury.

An important downstream consequence of membrane oxidation is loss of the controlled boundary that makes cellular physiology possible. Altered bilayer properties may change the function of channels and transporters, and damaged mitochondrial membranes may reduce the capacity to sustain the electrochemical gradient used for ATP synthesis. Reactive products formed during lipid breakdown can modify proteins beyond the original site of injury. Thus membrane injury may connect an initially local oxidative event with an energetic disturbance elsewhere. A decline in mitochondrial ATP production after oxidant exposure, however, does not by itself identify membrane peroxidation as the cause. Direct inhibition of a respiratory enzyme, altered substrate delivery or other lesions remain plausible explanations until independently assessed.

DNA is susceptible to several chemically distinct lesions, including oxidized bases and strand breaks. Oxidation of guanine can produce 8-oxoguanine, which may pair incorrectly during DNA synthesis if not removed. A damaged base is a chemical lesion, whereas a mutation is a persistent change in sequence. A nondividing cell can accumulate lesions that affect transcription without immediately fixing new mutations through replication. Conversely, a previously exposed dividing cell may acquire a mutation that persists after the original lesion is no longer present. This temporal distinction is essential for interpreting assays of DNA damage versus mutation frequency. It also motivates the repair mechanisms discussed later in the chapter.

A simple quantitative model of oxidant balance

Let P denote the production rate of a particular peroxide species in concentration units per minute and let C denote its concentration. In an illustrative cell compartment with first-order clearance, removal rate equals k times C, where k is a positive rate constant in inverse minutes. The rate of concentration change is P minus kC. At a steady concentration the change is zero, so C equals P divided by k. Suppose production is 0.6 micromoles per litre per minute and the clearance constant is 0.3 per minute. The predicted concentration is 2 micromoles per litre. If production doubles to 1.2 with unchanged clearance, the predicted concentration is 4. If clearance also doubles, the predicted concentration returns to 2. These are hypothetical values used to understand proportionality, not estimates of normal intracellular peroxide levels.

The model highlights a common experimental ambiguity. Equal measured concentrations in two cell populations do not guarantee equal rates of production. A population with rapid production and rapid clearance can have the same concentration as one with slow production and slow clearance. Conversely, a measured increase in concentration might result from impaired clearance without any increase in production. The first-order approximation is useful only when the effective removal machinery behaves approximately linearly over the relevant concentration range. Saturation, enzyme inactivation, multiple compartments and time-dependent expression can make the real behavior nonlinear. At high challenge the proportionality may fail precisely because the protective system is overwhelmed. This is why a small dose series around the normal operating range can be more informative than extrapolation from a very large toxic dose.

Enzymatic antioxidant defense and redox balance

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalyzes dismutation of two superoxide anions in the presence of two protons, producing hydrogen peroxide and molecular oxygen. The equation is 2 O₂•− + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. One superoxide species undergoes oxidation and the other reduction. Mammalian SOD1 is predominantly cytosolic, whereas SOD2 is located in the mitochondrial matrix; extracellular SOD3 protects extracellular environments. Their localization is important because superoxide is charged and crosses ordinary lipid bilayers poorly. A change in mitochondrial SOD2 cannot be assumed to have the same effect as an equal change in extracellular SOD3.

SOD is not an endpoint for the entire antioxidant pathway. It changes superoxide into peroxide, which must itself be managed. Catalase, abundant in peroxisomes in many animal cells, catalyzes the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide according to the net equation 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. Its protective role is especially important when peroxide is produced at relatively high rates. Other peroxide-removing systems operate in compartments where catalase is less abundant. An experiment that increases SOD activity without measuring peroxide removal may therefore fail to detect a redistribution of oxidative burden rather than an overall improvement.

Glutathione is a tripeptide whose cysteinyl group supplies a redox-active thiol. Reduced glutathione is abbreviated GSH; the disulfide made by linking two glutathione molecules is GSSG. Glutathione peroxidases use reducing equivalents from GSH to reduce peroxide substrates. In a simplified reaction with hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂ + 2 GSH → 2 H₂O + GSSG. Glutathione reductase then uses NADPH to regenerate reduced glutathione: GSSG + NADPH + H⁺ → 2 GSH + NADP⁺. Combining the two reactions reveals that glutathione is recycled, while NADPH supplies the net reducing power. In cells that rely on the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway for NADPH, disturbances in glucose metabolism can therefore affect antioxidant defense even when the amount of glutathione peroxidase protein does not change.

The ratio of reduced to oxidized glutathione is a useful descriptive measure, but it should not be confused with a complete measurement of redox potential. At fixed pH, the Nernst expression for the GSSG/2GSH couple depends on the ratio of GSSG activity to the square of GSH activity, not merely the simple GSH:GSSG ratio. Compartmental concentrations, pH and sample preparation affect interpretation. A large change in total glutathione pool size can alter the thermodynamic tendency for thiol-disulfide exchange even when a simple reported ratio remains unchanged. Moreover, redox couples in living cells need not be at equilibrium with one another.

Peroxiredoxins, supported by thioredoxin and associated reductive systems, also remove peroxides and contribute to controlled signaling. The diversity of enzymes should be understood in terms of local substrate, regeneration and competition rather than a contest to identify a single best antioxidant. An oxidative disturbance may be limited by the rate of oxidant production, by the availability of NADPH, by enzyme activity, or by the accessibility of substrates. Restoring an enzyme alone cannot necessarily restore every limiting process.

The chemistry of coordinated antioxidant defenses

Superoxide dismutases accelerate a reaction that converts two superoxide species into one molecule each of peroxide and oxygen, with consumption of two protons. Their function depends on redox cycling of metal cofactors in their active sites. One catalytic half-reaction accepts an electron from superoxide, and the other transfers an electron to a second superoxide molecule. The overall reaction removes superoxide without consuming a new molecule of reducing substrate for every pair of radicals. The metal is part of the catalyst, not a reagent that disappears in the balanced reaction. This helps explain why deficiencies in metal incorporation or enzyme maturation can reduce activity even when immunoblotting shows a normal quantity of SOD protein.

Different mammalian SOD isoforms are adapted to different locations. The major cytosolic Cu/Zn enzyme is SOD1, although some is also present in the mitochondrial intermembrane space. Manganese-containing SOD2 functions primarily in the mitochondrial matrix. Extracellular SOD3 acts outside cells in relevant tissues. The reaction is fundamentally the same, but the biological consequences of loss of each isoform can differ because the substrates are produced in different places. Overexpressing the cytosolic enzyme is not a reliable test of whether the matrix enzyme is dispensable. An experiment seeking to compare the protective contributions should match expression, verify activity and localization, and measure an outcome related to the compartment in question.

Catalase removes two molecules of hydrogen peroxide by converting them into two molecules of water and one molecule of oxygen. Unlike a reaction that uses a separate stoichiometric reducing substrate, this reaction uses peroxide in both oxidation and reduction steps of its catalytic cycle. Catalase can provide substantial capacity when peroxide supply is high. Other peroxide-removal enzymes differ in substrate preference, kinetics and compartmental distribution. Their importance cannot be inferred from a ranking of maximal activities measured in cell extracts, because the concentrations and accessibility of substrates in an intact cell may differ markedly from those in the assay. Distinguish catalytic capacity under assay conditions from actual flux through a reaction inside a cell.

Glutathione is a tripeptide whose cysteine residue contains a reactive sulfhydryl group. The reduced form is denoted GSH; two glutathione molecules can be joined through a disulfide to produce the oxidized form, GSSG. A glutathione peroxidase can couple peroxide reduction to oxidation of glutathione. Glutathione reductase then uses NADPH to reduce GSSG back to two GSH molecules. The two reactions form a cycle in which the immediate glutathione substrate is regenerated and the ultimate reducing power is supplied by metabolism. If regeneration slows, peroxide-removal capacity can decline despite the presence of a normal amount of peroxidase. Accordingly the antioxidant system is not a set of independent enzymes: each component depends on chemical and metabolic conditions that support the rest.

Glutathione-dependent protection also relies on synthesis of the glutathione pool and on transport or compartmental exchange. A decrease in total glutathione differs from a change in the fraction of the pool that is oxidized. In the first case there is less available buffering material; in the second case the existing pool is more oxidized. The two phenomena can coexist but should be measured separately. Expressing total glutathione in reducing equivalents requires counting two GSH equivalents for each GSSG molecule. For example, 8 millimolar GSH and 0.5 millimolar GSSG correspond to 9 millimolar total equivalents, of which 1 millimolar equivalent is in the oxidized form. The oxidized equivalent fraction is one ninth, approximately 11.1 percent. This is distinct from dividing GSSG molecules by the sum of GSH and GSSG molecules. Always state the convention when comparing reported glutathione fractions.

The glutathione redox couple depends on both the amount of GSH and the amount of GSSG. Its reduction half-reaction consumes two electrons and two protons. At fixed pH the Nernst relationship contains a term proportional to the natural logarithm of the ratio of GSSG activity to the square of GSH activity, divided by two because two electrons are transferred. More positive potential indicates a more oxidizing state of that couple under the stated conditions. Therefore an identical GSH-to-GSSG ratio at two different total concentrations does not necessarily imply an identical redox potential. The square arises from the two reduced glutathione molecules produced per oxidized molecule. In examinations, distinguish this equilibrium-based chemical expression from the rate at which an enzyme removes a peroxide. A calculated potential is not a measurement of reaction flux.

Thioredoxin and peroxiredoxin systems provide another major route of peroxide reduction. A peroxiredoxin uses a reactive cysteine to reduce a peroxide and subsequently requires regeneration of its reduced catalytic state. Thioredoxin provides reducing equivalents in many such cycles and thioredoxin reductase ultimately uses NADPH. These pathways are relevant for understanding why peroxide signaling is spatially and temporally regulated despite substantial endogenous peroxide-removal capacity. At sufficiently high challenge, some peroxiredoxins can undergo further oxidation that changes their catalytic behavior. This is not a universal description of every isoform or every exposure. The lesson at exam level is that the available catalytic state, regeneration and substrate accessibility together determine protection, not simply how many enzyme molecules are detected.

The pentose phosphate pathway supplies cytosolic NADPH through its oxidative reactions, connecting carbon metabolism with antioxidant defense. In a cell experiencing increased oxidant burden, diverting glucose-derived intermediates toward reducing-power production can support regeneration of glutathione and thioredoxin. This diversion has a cost in terms of alternative uses of the carbon substrate and depends on the metabolic state of the cell. Other NADPH-generating enzymes contribute in different compartments, so the pentose phosphate pathway is not the only source. A red blood cell provides a useful physiological illustration because its mature form lacks mitochondria and depends strongly on glycolysis for ATP and on the pentose phosphate pathway for NADPH. Impaired NADPH supply leaves it especially vulnerable to certain oxidant challenges. The biochemical details of glucose metabolism belong in the biomolecule and metabolism units; here the point is that a metabolic defect can impair a defense without directly changing the corresponding antioxidant gene.

A coordinated defense must also prevent the product of one protective reaction from becoming a new burden. Increased SOD can accelerate conversion of superoxide into peroxide, while limited downstream clearance can permit peroxide accumulation. The overall effect depends on the relative sources and sinks, as well as on competing reactions involving superoxide. A measured fall in superoxide-sensitive signal cannot establish that oxidative injury has declined in every molecular target. A useful comparison would measure peroxide-related responses, lipid or protein damage, and relevant cellular function in cells with altered SOD alone and cells with both SOD and peroxide-removal capacity altered. Such a design tests interaction between components rather than assuming that any single antioxidant increase must improve survival.

Inducible antioxidant responses: KEAP1 and NRF2

Cells benefit from maintaining a basal defense rather than waiting for injury before producing antioxidant proteins. They also benefit from increasing defense when conditions change, because maintaining maximal defense continuously consumes resources and may interfere with signaling. The transcription factor NRF2 illustrates an inducible regulatory system. Under many basal conditions, the adaptor protein KEAP1 participates with a CUL3-containing ubiquitin ligase complex in targeting NRF2 for proteasomal turnover. NRF2 can therefore be synthesized continuously while remaining relatively short-lived. This is a useful regulatory architecture because altering degradation permits a rapid change in protein abundance without requiring an initial increase in NRF2 transcription.

Electrophilic and oxidative perturbations can alter the function of susceptible cysteines within KEAP1. The efficiency of NRF2 turnover decreases, allowing newly synthesized NRF2 to accumulate and enter the nucleus, where it forms complexes with small Maf proteins and regulates genes containing appropriate antioxidant response elements. Examples of downstream functions include glutathione synthesis, detoxification and reducing-equivalent metabolism. The particular target genes expressed differ between cell types and experimental conditions. It is therefore inaccurate to treat NRF2 as a single enzyme that directly consumes peroxide or to require that every antioxidant enzyme must increase after NRF2 activation.

The temporal order is important in evaluating evidence. A decrease in NRF2 turnover may produce an early increase in protein abundance. A later increase in target-gene RNA may precede a measurable rise in enzyme capacity. A still later reduction in oxidative damage would be consistent with protection, but by itself would not prove that NRF2 caused the protection. An intervention that prevents NRF2 function and abolishes the target-gene response provides stronger evidence of necessity. If a separate restoration of functional NRF2 rescues the response, the causal interpretation is strengthened. The intervention still requires appropriate controls, since cells with altered basal NRF2 activity may begin with a different physiological state.

The direction of regulation offers a useful contrast with kinase pathways. Some regulatory systems activate an existing protein by phosphorylation; this pathway prominently changes the lifetime and availability of a transcription factor. Negative feedback and removal of the initiating disturbance help restore the system to an appropriate basal state. Persistent NRF2 activation is not automatically beneficial: it may alter normal resource allocation and in some pathological contexts favor survival of abnormal cells. Protective signaling must be evaluated in relation to the cell's role within the organism.

The logic of regulated protein turnover

A cell can increase the concentration of a regulator in two fundamentally different ways: it can synthesize more of it, or it can remove less of it. NRF2 illustrates the second mechanism particularly well. Under basal conditions, a protein complex involving KEAP1 and a Cullin 3-containing ubiquitin ligase facilitates the attachment of ubiquitin to NRF2, targeting it for degradation by the proteasome. KEAP1 is thus an adaptor that couples substrate recognition to the machinery of ubiquitination. It is not itself the proteasome and does not digest NRF2 directly. Continuous synthesis and rapid destruction keep basal NRF2 abundance relatively low, while preserving the possibility of rapid accumulation when the destruction rate falls.

This regulatory arrangement is analogous to filling a container through a continuously open inlet while water leaves through a controllable outlet. Closing the outlet raises the water level without necessarily increasing inflow. The analogy is limited because protein synthesis, degradation and intracellular localization are themselves regulated and are not simply mechanical flow through fixed pipes. Yet it explains why a rise in NRF2 protein can precede or occur without a comparable rise in its messenger RNA. If the experimental question is whether stress changes NRF2 synthesis or stability, measuring messenger RNA alone is inadequate. Measuring protein turnover over time provides complementary information. A rise in nuclear NRF2 also needs to be distinguished from a rise in total cellular NRF2, since transcriptional activity depends on access to nuclear targets.

KEAP1 contains cysteine residues whose chemical environments make some of them responsive to electrophiles or changes in redox conditions. Electrophiles are chemical species that accept electron density from another molecule; reaction with a cysteine thiol can alter the protein that bears it. Under suitable stress conditions, changes in KEAP1 interfere with efficient NRF2 ubiquitination. Newly synthesized NRF2 then accumulates and can participate in transcriptional regulation. The essential concept is interruption of effective targeting to degradation, rather than a universal requirement that all previously associated NRF2 molecules must be abruptly released from KEAP1. Different chemical inducers act through different sensor residues and regulatory details. Treating one simplified molecular cartoon as a complete description of every inducer would exceed what is supported experimentally.

In the nucleus, NRF2 associates with small Maf proteins and recognizes regulatory DNA sequences commonly called antioxidant response elements. These regulatory elements occur in the control regions of a number of cytoprotective genes. Binding alone is not a guarantee of increased transcription; chromatin accessibility and interactions with the transcription machinery also matter. Expression changes can increase capacity for glutathione synthesis, reducing-power metabolism, conjugation reactions or handling of reactive chemical products. Examples include genes encoding enzymes involved in glutathione synthesis and NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1. The outcome is a coordinated change in several pathways rather than merely synthesis of one molecule that directly neutralizes every oxidant.

A particularly important distinction is that NRF2 is a transcription factor, while its downstream targets are diverse effector proteins. Increasing NRF2 abundance does not directly consume a molecule of peroxide in the way that catalase does. The transcriptional program requires time to change RNA abundance and, subsequently, protein abundance and activity. The immediate protection afforded by existing enzymes can therefore be separated experimentally from delayed protection afforded by induction. A short oxidant pulse may terminate before a full transcriptional response develops. That does not make the response irrelevant: the induced machinery may help repair damage during recovery or reduce injury from a later exposure. Conversely, a cell with severely compromised translation or depleted substrates may activate NRF2 without developing the expected functional benefit.

How genetic perturbations reveal the pathway

Consider a set of matched cells containing either functional NRF2, a loss-of-function NRF2 allele, or a restored functional allele introduced into the deficient background. Expose all groups to the same modest electrophilic challenge, alongside unexposed controls. An increase in expression of an antioxidant response element-driven reporter in the first group, failure of induction in the deficient group and restoration in the rescued group would support a role for NRF2 in that response. A mutant reporter lacking the relevant binding sequence would further help test whether the DNA regulatory element contributes to the induction. This is an illustrative experimental design, not a report of numerical results from a particular paper. Its strength is that genetic loss and restoration address causality more directly than an observation that two quantities rise together.

The same design cannot by itself prove that every induced gene is a direct NRF2 target. Some changes may be secondary to newly expressed proteins or altered metabolism. Direct transcriptional regulation would be better supported by evidence that the regulator occupies the relevant DNA region together with appropriate dependence of transcription on that region. Likewise, a reporter construct may not reproduce the full chromatin environment of an endogenous gene. To establish biological protection, measure a damage endpoint and physiological function in addition to reporter activity. An induction signal is evidence of response activation; resistance to injury is evidence of a different, downstream outcome. The two may diverge under very severe exposure.

The KEAP1-NRF2 pathway offers a useful way to think about negative control. Loss of a positive transcriptional regulator tends to reduce its dependent expression. Loss of a negative regulator may increase the abundance or activity of the positive regulator. Thus a KEAP1-deficient cell can display increased basal NRF2-dependent transcription rather than failure to detect all stress. However, its behavior depends on the precise perturbation and on other regulatory routes. For examination questions, determine the sign of each causal connection: KEAP1 promotes NRF2 removal, NRF2 promotes transcription of many defensive genes, and those genes may reduce a relevant burden. Removing a negative connection can increase the next quantity. This reasoning is more reliable than memorizing that every stress-sensing protein must increase protective transcription when activated.

Long-term activation is not synonymous with universal benefit. A persistently elevated defensive program can change the availability of metabolic intermediates and alter responses to physiological signals. In some cancer contexts, constitutive NRF2 activity can help abnormal cells survive stressful conditions. This is not a contradiction of its protective function; survival of a damaged or transformed cell and welfare of the organism are different endpoints. The pathway evolved in a context where regulation and termination matter. A normal animal tissue benefits when protection is sufficient and appropriately timed, not necessarily when every defense gene remains maximally active. The distinction prepares for later consideration of chronic physiological stress, where sustained compensations may impose costs.

Protein misfolding and the cytosolic heat-shock response

A protein folds because interactions among its constituent amino acids and surrounding solvent favor particular conformations under suitable conditions. Heating, oxidation, unfavorable pH or ionic changes may destabilize those conformations. Unfolding does not necessarily mean cleavage of the polypeptide chain. A polypeptide may retain its primary structure while losing the organization needed for function. Exposed hydrophobic regions can interact with similar regions on other molecules, producing aggregates and reducing the pool of functional protein. Such interactions are particularly consequential inside cells because the cytoplasm contains a high concentration of macromolecules.

Molecular chaperones assist proteins in achieving or maintaining functional conformations, while generally not specifying the final amino-acid sequence or being permanent parts of their clients. HSP70-family chaperones bind exposed regions of nonnative proteins and undergo ATP-dependent cycles of binding and release. Co-chaperones regulate nucleotide exchange and client interactions. These cycles can decrease inappropriate aggregation and give clients further opportunities to fold. Some small heat-shock proteins act as ATP-independent holding chaperones, limiting aggregation until other components can help restore or remove the clients. HSP90-family systems assist particular classes of clients, including numerous regulatory proteins. A stressed cell therefore uses a network with distinct functions rather than a universal folding enzyme.

The heat-shock response in animal cells is strongly associated with heat shock factor 1 (HSF1). Under suitable stress, HSF1 becomes transcriptionally competent, typically involving trimerization and binding to heat-shock elements in target genes. The increased chaperone synthesis raises the cell's capacity to deal with nonnative proteins. Chaperone availability and additional regulatory processes help modulate HSF1 activity, contributing to termination of the response during recovery. The induction of heat-shock proteins by stresses other than heat shows that the name describes a historically recognized response rather than an absolute requirement for temperature elevation.

Refolding and degradation are complementary. Attempting to recover an intact but misfolded enzyme can conserve the energy needed to replace it. A severely damaged or persistently aggregation-prone protein may instead need removal. The ubiquitin-proteasome system removes many soluble cellular proteins, while larger aggregates and damaged organelles may be handled through lysosome-dependent processes. Neither route is universally suitable for all clients. The response to protein damage depends on the probability that function can be recovered, the burden of a persistent damaged molecule and the available energy and degradation capacity.

Why folding capacity becomes a limiting resource

A protein's amino acid sequence constrains the structures it can adopt, but the process of reaching a functional conformation takes place in a crowded cellular environment. Hydrophobic groups that would normally become buried within a folded protein can be exposed during synthesis or partial unfolding. Exposed surfaces can bind other proteins inappropriately, leading to aggregation. Temperature, pH, ionic conditions, oxidation and mutations can influence the relative stability of conformations. Moderate heat need not break peptide bonds to impair a protein; it can shift the balance of interactions that maintain higher-order structure. A polypeptide may therefore remain chemically intact while its enzymatic activity disappears. A useful experimental distinction is between detecting a protein by antibody and measuring whether that protein still performs its biological function.

The relationship between temperature and function is not uniform across proteins. A protein that normally operates in a cold-adapted animal may have different structural flexibility from one functioning at higher temperatures. Even within one cell, a modest increase in temperature may destabilize some proteins earlier than others. The resulting stress is not defined by a single universal temperature at which all proteins unfold simultaneously. A sharp rise in aggregation can emerge from the combined behavior of several vulnerable proteins and their competition for finite chaperone capacity. This is another example of how a smoothly increasing challenge can yield an abrupt functional decline near a capacity limit. In a comparative experiment, equal absolute temperatures are not necessarily equal physiological challenges for species with different thermal histories.

Molecular chaperones help other proteins acquire or maintain appropriate structures without becoming permanent structural components of most of their clients. They can bind transiently exposed regions, limit inappropriate intermolecular interactions, and participate in cycles of binding and release. Some facilitate folding of newly synthesized proteins, while others assist in quality control after damage. A chaperone cannot make an arbitrary amino acid sequence adopt any desired structure, nor can it reverse every chemical modification. If the client protein is irreversibly damaged or lacks the sequence information needed for a stable fold, removal and replacement may be necessary. The term proteostasis describes the combined processes that maintain a functional collection of proteins, encompassing synthesis, folding, localization and degradation.

HSP70 family proteins are useful for examining how ATP hydrolysis can drive a regulated binding cycle. In a simplified representation, the ATP-bound state exchanges clients relatively readily, whereas the ADP-bound state retains certain clients more stably. J-domain co-chaperones promote ATP hydrolysis and influence substrate handling. Nucleotide exchange factors facilitate the exchange of ADP for ATP, allowing release and another cycle. Repeated cycles may provide opportunities for a nonnative client to find an appropriate structure or to be transferred to another quality-control pathway. This description is a functional model of the cycle, not a claim that one complete round guarantees successful refolding. The energetic requirement explains why a chaperone system can become less effective in a cell whose ATP production has been severely compromised.

Small heat shock proteins illustrate a contrasting strategy. Many of them bind vulnerable proteins and help keep them from forming harmful aggregates without using ATP hydrolysis directly for that binding function. Other chaperones can subsequently participate in recovery of the held substrates. They do not replace ATP-dependent folding machinery in every situation. A cell can therefore combine an early holding response with later ATP-dependent recovery when energy availability improves. The distinction between holding and refolding also matters in assays: a reduction in formation of large insoluble aggregates may reflect sequestration into an alternative state rather than restoration of enzyme activity. Assessing solubility and measuring function answer related but different questions.

Chaperonins provide another strategy by creating a protected environment that reduces inappropriate contact between a folding protein and the surrounding molecules. The mitochondrial HSP60 system illustrates this principle in animal cells. Its substrate handling involves regulated conformational changes and ATP-dependent cycling, in association with a co-chaperonin. The central idea is spatial isolation of certain folding intermediates. HSP90, in contrast, participates in maturation and regulation of selected clients, including some signaling proteins. It is not simply interchangeable with every other HSP. This variety explains why an experiment that increases one chaperone may protect against one class of injury but have limited effect on another. Students should identify what process the chaperone assists before predicting its phenotype.

From the heat-shock observation to transcriptional control

Historical studies of heat-treated Drosophila salivary gland chromosomes revealed changes in the appearance of particular chromosome regions. The polytene chromosomes in these tissues provide a visible system for observing regions of altered transcriptional activity. Subsequent research connected heat-induced chromosome puffing with expression of heat-shock genes and identified regulatory proteins that recognize particular DNA elements. The significance of this historical sequence is not simply that a heated animal cell makes extra protein. It established that a physical environmental disturbance can elicit selective gene expression, even when other cellular processes are impaired. The observation, the identification of transcripts and proteins, and the characterization of the regulatory factor represent different levels of evidence.

In mammalian cells, HSF1 is a major transcriptional regulator of the heat-shock response. Stress-associated changes in protein folding and interactions with regulatory chaperones influence HSF1 activity. Active HSF1 forms oligomeric complexes, commonly represented as trimers, that can bind heat shock elements in target DNA. These elements contain arrangements of sequence motifs recognized by the transcription factor. The resulting transcriptional response can increase the production of chaperones and other protective components. The pathway also contains mechanisms that restrict activity when the perturbation is resolved. It is inaccurate to interpret HSF1 as a simple physical thermometer whose only input is the absolute temperature. Other proteotoxic disturbances can activate the response, and the regulatory state of the cell influences its threshold.

The feedback logic is especially instructive. During a folding challenge, chaperones can become increasingly engaged with nonnative clients. In appropriate systems, changes in the availability or interactions of chaperones relieve inhibition of the heat-shock transcription factor. Increased synthesis of chaperones then expands the available capacity, helping reduce the burden and attenuate the transcriptional response. This titration model captures an important regulatory principle, although additional modifications and interactions influence HSF1 in real mammalian cells. It does not follow that every chaperone directly inhibits HSF1 in the same way. A response that diminishes after successful repair may represent normal negative feedback rather than progressive loss of the cell's ability to detect stress.

An experimental way to separate the roles of HSF1 and a particular target chaperone is to compare several perturbations. Loss of HSF1 may reduce induction of multiple heat-shock genes. Loss of a single chaperone can impair a particular aspect of folding capacity while leaving other transcriptional responses intact. Restoring that chaperone in HSF1-deficient cells may partially rescue one endpoint but not the full program. If a student sees partial rescue, the proper conclusion is that the restored component contributes to the measured outcome under the tested conditions. The remaining deficit may involve other genes or functions. It would be an overstatement to conclude from partial rescue that the transcription factor works exclusively through the one restored component.

Thermotolerance and recovery are experimentally separable

A mild heat treatment followed by a recovery period can increase resistance to a later, stronger treatment in some experimental systems. This induced thermotolerance provides an opportunity to distinguish immediate protection by preexisting machinery from delayed induction of protective proteins. Four groups are particularly informative: untreated cells, mildly treated cells, strongly treated cells and cells receiving the mild treatment followed by the stronger one. Match the time spent in culture and the conditions of recovery across groups. The mild treatment alone establishes whether the conditioning exposure is itself harmful. The strong treatment alone provides the baseline for evaluating added resistance. Measuring both soluble protein function and later viability helps distinguish transient stabilization from meaningful recovery.

An additional group treated with an inhibitor of protein synthesis during conditioning or recovery can help test the requirement for new protein production, provided toxicity and effects on baseline survival are controlled. Reduced acquired tolerance in this group is consistent with a role for new synthesis but does not identify the required gene. Specific genetic disruption and restoration of an inducible chaperone can refine the causal interpretation. The interval between conditioning and challenge also matters. If challenge arrives before enough protective machinery accumulates, conditioning may offer little benefit. If it arrives long after protective proteins turn over, the benefit may have declined. A time course is therefore more informative than comparing only one conditioning schedule.

The physiological role of chaperones extends beyond acute heat exposure. Muscle cells must maintain contractile proteins under repeated mechanical and metabolic demands. Neurons maintain long-lived proteins and cannot simply rely on frequent cell replacement. Secretory and immune cells may change protein production rapidly during functional activation. Each tissue has different vulnerable clients and different opportunities for renewal. Heat shock proteins participate in broader cellular quality control in these settings. However, an increased abundance of a heat shock protein in tissue does not prove that the animal recently experienced environmental heat. Many disturbances converge on protein folding, and some chaperones are expressed constitutively. Identify the exposure and the functional changes separately before interpreting the marker.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a major site of synthesis and maturation of proteins destined for secretion or membranes. The ER lumen provides a specialized environment for processes such as formation of disulfide bonds and glycoprotein quality control. Changes in calcium homeostasis, redox conditions or the quantity of newly synthesized clients can disturb folding. The resulting imbalance between demand and capacity is termed ER stress. The unfolded protein response (UPR) is the set of signaling adjustments that responds to that imbalance. It should not be equated with the unfolded proteins themselves.

Three major signaling branches in mammalian cells are named for their ER membrane-associated sensors: IRE1, PERK and ATF6. They communicate the folding state of the organelle to cytosolic or nuclear processes through different mechanisms. IRE1 possesses a cytosolic kinase and ribonuclease domain. Activated IRE1 promotes unconventional splicing of XBP1 messenger RNA, generating a transcript encoding the active transcription factor XBP1s. XBP1s supports expression of components involved in folding, secretion and ER-associated degradation. This is an example in which regulated processing of an RNA, rather than simply transcription of more RNA, changes the functional product.

PERK phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2α). This reduces the efficiency of initiation for much general translation, thereby decreasing the arrival of new clients at the stressed ER. However, translation of certain transcripts, notably ATF4 under relevant conditions, is favored because of their regulatory upstream open reading frames. Therefore the statement that phosphorylation of eIF2α shuts down all protein synthesis is incorrect. ATF4 contributes to adaptive changes in amino acid metabolism, stress response and other processes. Other kinases also phosphorylate eIF2α in response to different disturbances, so detecting phosphorylated eIF2α alone does not uniquely establish PERK activation.

ATF6 provides a third form of regulation. During ER stress it can travel from the ER to the Golgi, where proteolytic processing releases its cytosolic transcription factor domain. This active fragment enters the nucleus and contributes to the induction of genes involved in ER folding and quality control. The route illustrates how controlled trafficking and cleavage can convert a membrane-associated protein into a nuclear regulator. It is not the same molecular operation as the splicing of XBP1 RNA or phosphorylation of eIF2α.

The branches are often active together. Lowering the rate of new protein entry buys time, while increasing the machinery for folding and removal helps resolve the problem. If the burden is prolonged and severe, the signaling context can shift toward cell death, with contributions from stress-associated transcriptional programs including CHOP under some conditions. A transient increase in CHOP expression alone is not proof that every cell has irreversibly committed to apoptosis. Duration, cell type, accompanying injury and direct measurements of survival or death matter. A complete interpretation follows both the adaptive phase and the later outcome.

Folding within the secretory pathway

The endoplasmic reticulum performs a special role in the production of proteins destined for secretion, insertion into membranes or certain organelles of the endomembrane system. Nascent polypeptides entering the ER encounter a lumen whose chemical conditions differ from those of the cytosol. For many secretory proteins, formation of disulfide bonds and glycoprotein processing are important steps toward functional structure. Resident folding factors include BiP, a member of the HSP70 family, and enzymes that assist appropriate disulfide formation. The lumen also maintains a calcium environment important for various ER functions. Changes in calcium availability, redox conditions or the load of newly synthesized clients can disturb this folding environment. Therefore an ER-specific stress response may be elicited without a general increase in temperature.

Quality control in the ER helps prevent defective proteins from progressing through the secretory pathway. Some nonnative proteins are retained and given further opportunities to fold. Others are selected for ER-associated degradation, which commonly involves movement of a substrate or relevant portion toward the cytosolic degradation machinery, ubiquitination and proteasomal destruction. These processes require coordinated recognition and energy expenditure. If delivery of new clients exceeds the combined capacity for folding and removal, nonnative proteins accumulate. In a cell that normally secretes a large amount of protein, reducing incoming load can be a rapid protective intervention, but doing so for too long may compromise the cell's physiological role. The unfolded protein response addresses this conflict through multiple outputs rather than a single switch.

The three canonical mammalian ER stress sensors are IRE1, PERK and ATF6. Their activities respond to disturbances in the ER and are communicated across its membrane. BiP interactions and, in certain systems, direct interactions involving nonnative proteins contribute to regulation. A simple model in which BiP is recruited to clients and changes its association with sensors is useful as a starting point, but the molecular mechanisms are not identical for all three sensors. The model should not be treated as an exclusive universal mechanism. The sensors have different downstream activities and can be activated with different temporal patterns. A particular assay may report activation of one branch without establishing activity of the others.

IRE1 and selective processing of an RNA

IRE1 is an ER membrane protein with cytosolic kinase and ribonuclease functions. When activated in mammalian cells, an important action of its ribonuclease is the unconventional processing of XBP1 messenger RNA. A short intron is excised and the RNA is ligated, changing the reading frame used to produce the transcriptionally active XBP1s protein. This should not be confused with ordinary nuclear spliceosome-dependent splicing of many precursor messenger RNAs. The regulatory event is especially informative because a change in protein output results from processing of a specific RNA, not solely from increasing transcription of the gene. XBP1s contributes to induction of genes that support the secretory pathway and ER quality control.

A student can interpret an assay that distinguishes the unspliced and spliced XBP1 forms without assuming that both encode equivalent proteins. If the spliced form increases while total XBP1 RNA remains similar, the proportion processed has changed. For illustration, suppose an assay with equal amplification efficiency estimates 80 arbitrary molecules of unspliced RNA and 20 of spliced RNA before a challenge. The spliced fraction is 20 divided by 100, or 20 percent. After a challenge the values are 35 and 65, yielding 65 percent. This does not demonstrate a 3.25-fold increase in the transcription rate of the XBP1 gene. It indicates a change in the measured RNA populations and is consistent with activation of the processing pathway. In a real assay, amplification efficiency, normalization and the nature of the RNA measurement must be considered.

IRE1 can also affect other RNAs under certain conditions. The net effect of such activities varies with stress intensity, persistence and cell context. The pathway is not exclusively adaptive in every situation. This complexity need not be expanded into specialist details for the examination, but it prevents an overly simple prediction that greater IRE1 activation must always mean better survival. The relevant protective outcome is restoration of ER function. If the disturbance persists, altering RNA populations may not suffice to resolve it. Therefore a time course measuring XBP1 processing, folding outcomes and cell function gives a fuller account than one endpoint measuring the abundance of a spliced transcript.

PERK and the regulation of protein input

PERK is an ER-associated kinase that phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2. During normal translation initiation, eIF2 participates in delivery of initiator transfer RNA to the ribosomal machinery and cycles between nucleotide-bound states. Phosphorylation of eIF2α affects the nucleotide exchange process involving eIF2B and reduces initiation of translation for many messenger RNAs. Reduced initiation can lower the flow of newly synthesized proteins requiring folding and can reduce energetic demand. This is analogous to reducing incoming traffic when a processing system is overloaded, not to eliminating the machinery already responsible for processing clients. Translation decreases selectively and incompletely, and the degree of reduction depends on the regulatory conditions.

Some transcripts respond differently to the changed availability of initiation complexes. ATF4 is a well-known example whose translation is favored under appropriate conditions of eIF2α phosphorylation because regulatory upstream reading frames influence the initiation pattern. Thus a reduction in general translation can coexist with an increase in production of a particular regulatory protein. ATF4 participates in transcriptional responses involving metabolism and stress adaptation. Persistent signaling may also contribute to expression of factors associated with maladaptive responses, including CHOP in relevant contexts. Neither ATF4 nor CHOP abundance alone establishes the fate of every cell. The outcome depends on the severity and duration of the initial challenge and on the ability of the cell to restore functional balance.

A central examination distinction is that eIF2α phosphorylation can arise from pathways other than PERK. Other kinases respond to different cellular perturbations, collectively contributing to what is often called the integrated stress response. Therefore detection of phosphorylated eIF2α alone does not establish that ER stress specifically activated PERK. Evidence from XBP1 processing or ATF6 activation may support an ER disturbance, while a PERK-specific genetic perturbation or suitable measurement can help attribute the phosphorylation to PERK. Even a selective loss of a signal after inhibitor treatment should be interpreted with awareness of drug specificity and toxicity. A shared downstream marker is often insufficient to identify its upstream cause.

The response must eventually permit restoration of protein synthesis if the cell is to maintain function. Regulatory dephosphorylation of eIF2α can contribute to this restoration. Premature recovery of translation while folding capacity is still inadequate may worsen the burden, whereas indefinitely preventing translation can impede production of essential proteins. The balance between restraining the load and rebuilding capacity is therefore dynamic. In an illustrative experiment, a brief translational reduction followed by recovery may accompany successful adaptation. A more persistent reduction may accompany failure of recovery, but causality requires further testing. Measuring only protein incorporation at one time point cannot distinguish a deliberate temporary reduction from permanent damage to translational machinery.

ATF6 and trafficking-dependent activation

ATF6 represents a third solution to communication across the ER membrane. It is synthesized as a membrane-associated protein that, during appropriate ER stress, can travel to the Golgi apparatus. Proteolytic processing there releases a cytosolic fragment capable of functioning as a transcription factor in the nucleus. Thus activation involves regulated trafficking and cleavage rather than the specific RNA processing used by IRE1 or the substrate phosphorylation used by PERK. The released factor contributes to transcription of genes that support folding and quality control. Its pathway provides an excellent exercise in tracing compartments: the original protein is associated with the ER, cleavage occurs in the Golgi, and the active transcriptional fragment acts in the nucleus.

An immunoblot designed to detect both full-length and cleaved ATF6 can support interpretation of the activation mechanism. If the full-length band decreases and a smaller fragment appears during a controlled stress treatment, processing is a plausible explanation. If both bands disappear, protein degradation or another effect may need consideration. A change in nuclear localization of the relevant fragment would provide complementary evidence. An intervention that prevents the required trafficking or cleavage should reduce generation of that fragment, but any broader disturbance to organelle function may complicate the experiment. The proper conclusion must match the measured process rather than assigning all gene expression changes to ATF6 simply because the cell was exposed to an ER stressor.

The three branches share the broad purpose of responding to an imbalance in the secretory pathway. Their differences are useful because multiple outputs can operate together: reduce the incoming protein load, increase capacity for folding, improve removal of defective clients, and change cellular metabolism. One branch can partially compensate for another under certain conditions, while a particular cell type may depend more strongly on one output. A conceptual diagram should therefore show parallel branches rather than a linear sequence in which IRE1 always activates PERK and PERK always activates ATF6. Their activities can overlap and influence shared genes, but they are distinct sensors with distinct immediate molecular actions.

Secretory physiology illustrates the cost of protection

Consider a cell producing a secreted digestive enzyme or an antibody. Successful physiological activation requires increased production of a functional protein, not simply increased abundance of its messenger RNA. If folding capacity is insufficient, increasing translation alone may increase degradation of defective clients rather than secretion of active protein. Conversely, an adaptive expansion of the secretory machinery can increase effective output after an adjustment period. In a controlled experiment, measuring both intracellular protein and functional secretion would distinguish retention or degradation from productive processing. The physiological endpoint might be enzyme activity in the extracellular medium or another measure suited to the secreted product.

The adaptive unfolded protein response is not restricted to obvious pathological injury. Changes in normal physiological demand can require adjustment of folding capacity. A response marker should therefore be interpreted against the baseline appropriate to the differentiated cell. A professional secretory cell may possess a large ER and high constitutive folding capacity. Another cell may express a smaller amount of the same machinery under normal conditions yet induce it substantially under stress. Comparing absolute marker abundance across unrelated tissues without accounting for their functions can be misleading. Relative changes, measures of client load and functional outcomes together provide a better basis for comparison.

When ER stress remains unresolved, the adaptive response can be accompanied by processes leading to loss of viability. The transition is not determined by a universal molecular clock or a single fixed concentration of misfolded protein. Cellular energy availability, the nature of the defective clients, the efficiency of degradation and the broader physiological environment all matter. Persistent stress may also disturb communication between organelles. At the present scope, the key distinction is between activation of a protective program and successful restoration of function. Detailed execution mechanisms of apoptosis belong to the relevant cell organization and developmental biology chapters. Here cell death is considered as a possible outcome when protective capacity cannot meet the challenge.

Energy sensing, damage removal and cellular recovery

ATP supplies free energy for molecular work, including membrane pumps, protein synthesis, chaperone cycles and repair. The cellular concentration of ATP may initially be buffered by reactions that redistribute adenine nucleotides, such as the adenylate kinase reaction 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP. Consequently a modest fall in ATP can accompany a substantial relative rise in AMP. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) responds to changes in adenine nucleotide availability and phosphorylation state. It is a heterotrimeric protein kinase, not an ATP-generating enzyme. Its regulation helps align ATP-consuming processes with the ability of metabolic pathways to replenish ATP.

AMPK activation commonly favors ATP-generating pathways and restrains selected ATP-consuming biosynthetic activities. For example, phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase can decrease malonyl-CoA production; in appropriate tissues this reduces inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, helping permit mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. AMPK can also restrain mTORC1-dependent anabolic signaling. These effects do not guarantee recovery under every condition: if oxygen is unavailable, increasing fatty acid delivery cannot by itself make oxidative ATP production proceed normally. The useful response depends on available fuel, oxygen, mitochondrial competence and the demand for ATP.

Macroautophagy sequesters cytoplasmic material within a double-membrane autophagosome, which subsequently delivers cargo to lysosomal compartments for degradation. The process may supply reusable substrates and remove harmful material. Selective removal of damaged mitochondria, called mitophagy, is relevant to stress adaptation because defective mitochondria may impair energy metabolism and contribute to disturbed redox control. Autophagy is distinct from apoptosis and can support survival, but neither activation nor inhibition of autophagy has a single inevitable consequence in every stressed tissue.

Interpreting autophagy requires attention to flow. If the quantity of a particular type of autophagosome increases, either production has accelerated or removal has slowed. An increase in LC3-II at one time point cannot settle this distinction. An appropriate comparison with and without inhibition of lysosomal degradation, interpreted alongside additional markers and controls, can help determine whether cargo delivery through the pathway has increased. This is an example of why an accumulated intermediate is not a direct measure of throughput.

DNA damage creates a separate problem: a chemically altered base is a lesion, while a mutation is a change in genetic sequence that can become fixed through replication or erroneous repair. Oxidation of guanine can produce 8-oxoguanine, which may pair incorrectly during DNA synthesis. Base excision repair removes specific damaged bases using DNA glycosylases such as OGG1, followed by processing of the resulting abasic site, replacement of nucleotides and sealing of the backbone. Repair pathways differ in their substrate and lesion recognition, and the detailed mechanisms belong in Unit 3. The physiological principle here is that damage prevention and damage removal complement each other; even an effective antioxidant network cannot eliminate every lesion.

A cell recovering from stress must re-establish ion gradients, restore the functional protein pool and balance energy expenditure with supply. A reversible arrest of growth or reduced translation can be beneficial if it prevents new demands from competing with repair. When damage exceeds the capacity for correction, prolonged dysfunction or regulated cell death may result. At tissue level, elimination of a severely damaged cell may preserve the function of the organism, whereas inappropriate elimination of many cells may cause organ dysfunction. Cellular survival and whole-animal fitness are therefore related but not identical outcomes.

Adenine nucleotides and the energetic priority of maintenance

ATP links the energy released by metabolism to cellular processes that require energy. Its hydrolysis supports such activities as ion transport, contraction and regulated changes in protein structure. A cell continuously resynthesizes ATP, so a measured concentration represents the balance of consumption and production rather than the accumulated energy produced over a day. If consumption and production both double, ATP concentration can remain approximately stable even though metabolic flux has changed markedly. By contrast, failure to match consumption with production can alter nucleotide ratios and signal a need for adjustment. An ATP measurement therefore needs to be interpreted together with the physiological workload of the cell and the time at which it was obtained.

Adenylate kinase catalyzes the reversible interconversion of two ADP molecules with ATP and AMP. This reaction connects changes in ADP with the relative abundance of AMP. Under conditions in which the reaction is close to equilibrium, an increase in ADP relative to ATP can produce a proportionately greater increase in AMP relative to ATP. For instance, if the ADP-to-ATP ratio doubles while the relevant equilibrium relationship remains approximately unchanged, the AMP-to-ATP ratio can rise approximately fourfold. This is an illustrative proportionality, not a universal prediction for every cellular compartment. It helps explain why AMP can serve as a sensitive indicator of altered nucleotide balance. Measurements of whole-cell nucleotide pools may conceal changes within particular locations.

A second useful description of adenine nucleotide status is the adenylate energy charge, defined as the concentration of ATP plus one-half the concentration of ADP, divided by the sum of ATP, ADP and AMP concentrations. The numerator weights ATP as one, ADP as one-half and AMP as zero; the denominator normalizes the total pool. The resulting quantity is dimensionless and lies between zero and one when concentrations are nonnegative and the total pool is nonzero. It is not the fraction of ATP molecules hydrolyzed per minute and does not directly measure the energy released by ATP hydrolysis, which also depends on phosphate concentration and other conditions. It is useful as a compact description of the relative distribution of the three nucleotides.

For a hypothetical example, suppose an extract contains 4 millimolar ATP, 0.8 millimolar ADP and 0.2 millimolar AMP. The total is 5 millimolar and the weighted numerator is 4.4 millimolar, yielding an energy charge of 0.88. After a challenge, suppose ATP is 3, ADP is 1.2 and AMP is 0.8 millimolar. The total remains 5 and the weighted numerator is 3.6, yielding 0.72. The AMP-to-ATP ratio has increased from 0.05 to about 0.267, more than fivefold. The two summaries describe the same underlying pool in different ways. Neither calculation establishes which enzyme changed activity or identifies the cause of the imbalance. Measurements of nucleotide turnover, substrate availability or mitochondrial function would be needed for those additional conclusions.

AMPK is a heterotrimeric protein kinase whose regulatory subunits participate in sensing adenine nucleotides and whose catalytic subunit phosphorylates protein targets. In a common mammalian model, AMP and ADP binding influence phosphorylation status of the activating residue in the catalytic subunit; AMP can additionally stimulate activity allosterically. ATP competes with these nucleotides at relevant regulatory sites. Upstream kinases, including LKB1 in many contexts, contribute to activating phosphorylation. Calcium-dependent signaling can also activate AMPK through an upstream kinase under some conditions. Therefore phosphorylation and activity need not be determined by one nucleotide ratio alone. This is an important exception to describing AMPK as an exclusive direct meter of ATP concentration.

During energetic stress, a useful response is to increase ATP-producing capacity where feasible while restraining ATP-consuming processes that are not immediately essential. AMPK participates in both sides of that adjustment. In appropriate tissues it can stimulate glucose uptake or favor fatty acid oxidation and inhibit aspects of fatty acid synthesis and other biosynthetic processes. One well-known target is acetyl-CoA carboxylase. Inhibition of this enzyme can reduce malonyl-CoA production, relieving inhibition of carnitine-dependent entry of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria in appropriate metabolic contexts. The overall effect depends on substrate supply, oxygen availability and the functional state of mitochondria. Activating a regulatory signal cannot make an unavailable substrate appear or restore a terminal electron acceptor that is absent.

Growth-associated signaling provides a complementary example. When nutrients and energy are adequate, mTOR complex 1 contributes to promotion of biosynthesis and growth. Under certain energy-limited conditions, AMPK can restrain this signaling and favor pathways that support recycling. The pathways should not be interpreted as permanently antagonistic master switches with no other inputs. Growth-factor signals, amino acid availability and other physiological variables also influence mTOR complex 1. In a cell exposed to nutrient scarcity, inhibiting growth may free resources for maintenance. In a cell that must repair damaged proteins, however, indefinite suppression of synthesis is not a complete solution. Successful adaptation includes appropriate restoration of biosynthetic activity when conditions permit.

Consider an isolated muscle preparation that receives repeated stimulation. Early during stimulation, increased ATP demand can be matched by mobilization of existing energy stores and metabolic responses. If substrate supply becomes inadequate, sustaining contraction at the same rate may threaten ion balance and cell integrity. A response that limits some nonessential expenditure can support survival at the cost of reduced maximal performance. This illustrates why an adaptation judged beneficial by a survival assay may not maximize force production during the challenge. Cellular priorities depend on the time horizon of the endpoint. A physiological response can temporarily reduce one function in order to preserve capacity for future function.

Selective removal, recycling and the importance of flux

The proteasome and lysosome provide complementary routes for the destruction of cellular components. Many short-lived or damaged soluble proteins can be recognized through ubiquitin-dependent pathways and degraded by the proteasome. Larger structures, portions of cytoplasm or organelles can be delivered to lysosomes through forms of autophagy. These descriptions identify common functional roles, not an absolute rule that every substrate of one type uses only one route. Proteins in insoluble assemblies may be poorly accessible to proteasomal degradation, and selective autophagy can help remove some of these assemblies. In either case, removal is useful only if essential cellular components can subsequently be maintained or replaced.

Macroautophagy involves the formation of a membrane compartment that encloses material from the cytoplasm. The resulting autophagosome can fuse with a lysosomal compartment, permitting degradation by lysosomal hydrolases. Some breakdown products become available for further cellular metabolism. The membranes, transport steps and lysosomal environment needed for this process must themselves be functional. Consequently, increased initiation of autophagy does not ensure increased delivery of useful breakdown products. If fusion or degradation fails, intermediate compartments can accumulate even while completion of recycling decreases. This makes autophagy a particularly useful example of the difference between the abundance of an intermediate and the rate of flow through a pathway.

In selective autophagy, recognition of a cargo helps direct the removal machinery toward particular cellular components. Mitophagy refers to selective autophagic removal of mitochondria. Damaged mitochondria may have impaired energetic function and can contribute to inappropriate oxidant production. Removing them may reduce further injury, but loss of too much mitochondrial capacity before replacements are available can create another energetic problem. Mitochondrial maintenance therefore involves coordinated removal and renewal. A reduced mitochondrial mass may signify successful quality control or failure to maintain the organelle population, depending on which mitochondria were lost and whether remaining capacity meets demand. A measurement of respiration normalized to cell number provides different information from one normalized to mitochondrial protein.

A common experimental marker associated with autophagosome formation is the lipidated form of LC3, known as LC3-II in standard mammalian assays. Its abundance reflects formation and degradation processes. Suppose untreated cells have one arbitrary unit of LC3-II and stressed cells have three units. This observation alone cannot determine the direction of change in autophagic flux. In a second illustrative comparison, blocking lysosomal degradation for a matched short interval increases LC3-II from one to four in untreated cells and from three to eight in stressed cells. The larger increment in stressed cells is consistent with greater turnover during that interval, assuming that the block is effective and that other relevant conditions remain comparable. If stressed cells instead remain near three after the block, impaired downstream turnover becomes a plausible interpretation, but additional controls are still required.

The numerical example demonstrates the logic of comparing the change during an intervention, rather than regarding a high abundance as evidence of increased flux. A static photograph of a road filled with vehicles cannot distinguish rapid traffic flow from a traffic jam. The analogy is limited because synthesis and degradation of cellular markers involve biochemical transformations rather than physical transport alone, but it captures the distinction between stock and flow. Independent assessments of cargo turnover, lysosomal function and cell health strengthen an autophagy interpretation. Different experimental markers may respond differently to a disturbance, and no single assay is sufficient for every cell type or experimental purpose.

Repair of oxidative DNA damage and decisions about cell fate

Cells reduce exposure of DNA to reactive species through antioxidant defenses, but prevention is not perfect. Base excision repair removes certain modified bases that distort the DNA helix only modestly. A DNA glycosylase recognizes an appropriate lesion and removes the damaged base, leaving an abasic site. Further processing of that site allows a DNA polymerase to replace missing nucleotide information and a ligase to restore continuity of the DNA strand. The exact sequence of enzymes depends on the lesion and repair route. The complementary strand often provides information needed to restore the original sequence. The protective value of repair lies in recovering the information and function of the DNA, not merely eliminating a measurable oxidized chemical group.

The oxidized base 8-oxoguanine illustrates the importance of timing. A guanine originally paired with cytosine can become oxidized and later pair with adenine during replication. If the resulting mismatch is not corrected, another round of replication may fix a G:C to T:A transversion. OGG1 can remove 8-oxoguanine from an appropriate pairing context, and another glycosylase, MUTYH, contributes to removal of adenine mispaired with 8-oxoguanine. The two activities address different steps in avoiding mutation fixation. This is why deletion of one repair enzyme may alter a specific mutation spectrum without causing an immediate large decrease in viability. The endpoint of genomic fidelity can be more sensitive than an assay of short-term survival.

A cell can sometimes reduce the risk of copying damaged DNA by restraining cell-cycle progression until appropriate repair has occurred. Cell-cycle checkpoints connect genome integrity with progression through division. A checkpoint is not itself a DNA-repair enzyme and does not guarantee successful repair. It changes the timing of events so that replication or division does not proceed under unsuitable conditions. A prolonged arrest may prevent propagation of damage but also prevent tissue renewal. Nondividing cells have a different relationship to replication-associated mutation, yet still require intact DNA for transcription and long-term function. The broader details of repair and checkpoints are covered in the corresponding fundamental-processes and cell-organization chapters.

If protective responses do not restore essential functions, the outcomes can include persistent dysfunction, arrest, senescence or death. Programmed cell death can limit the persistence of severely damaged cells in some tissues, while loss of many cells may itself compromise an organ. A membrane-impermeant dye identifies loss of membrane integrity at the sampling time; it does not by itself identify the molecular route of cell death. Likewise, the presence of a stress-response protein or an autophagy marker does not establish that the cell is dying by a particular mechanism. The proper inference requires appropriate multiple measures and consideration of the order in which changes occur. At animal physiology level, the main point is that protection of an individual cell and preservation of tissue-level function are related but not always identical goals.

Interpreting experimental evidence

Suppose an investigator exposes cultured animal cells to a brief oxidant challenge and measures relative NRF2 abundance, glutathione peroxidase activity and lipid oxidation at several intervals. The appropriate untreated controls must be sampled at the same intervals, because time in culture can independently influence the measurements. Protein abundance should be normalized using a suitable validated loading strategy, whereas enzyme activity should be expressed per defined amount of sample protein or another justified denominator. A high enzyme activity after exposure does not establish that the cells were never injured; a subsequent decline in damage markers or recovery of function supplies different information.

Consider an illustrative dataset, not published experimental results. Relative NRF2 abundance at zero, one, four and eight hours is 1.0, 2.8, 2.1 and 1.2; relative glutathione peroxidase activity is 1.0, 1.0, 1.5 and 1.7; relative lipid oxidation is 1.0, 1.9, 1.6 and 1.1. The early increase in the regulator precedes the rise in enzyme activity, and the damage marker subsequently declines. This order is consistent with an adaptive response, but an alternative explanation is that the initiating oxidant was removed or metabolized independently of NRF2. To test whether the response depends on NRF2, compare matched cells with impaired NRF2 function, and assess whether restoration of NRF2 reverses the altered phenotype. To distinguish recovery from selective death of the most damaged cells, measure viability and account for cell number.

A second illustrative calculation concerns an energy-stressed cell. Let ATP, ADP and AMP concentrations be 4.0, 0.8 and 0.2 mmol/L initially, then 3.0, 1.3 and 0.7 mmol/L after stress. The total adenine nucleotide concentration is 5.0 mmol/L in each condition. The AMP:ATP ratio changes from 0.05 to approximately 0.233, a 4.67-fold increase. The adenylate energy charge, defined as ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP]+[ADP]+[AMP]), changes from 0.88 to 0.73. Both metrics indicate altered energy status, but neither directly measures AMPK activity. Phosphorylation and substrate-based assays provide additional evidence about the kinase. The example assumes comparable sampling and a single effective pool of nucleotides; real cells have compartment-specific pools.

A third experiment compares protein synthesis and viability after a reversible ER folding challenge. A transient reduction in bulk translation, increased XBP1 splicing and later restoration of secretion are consistent with adaptation. Persistent suppression of translation, increasing markers of damage and declining viability are consistent with failed recovery. Neither pattern alone identifies the branch necessary for survival. Genetic or other selective perturbations of the relevant branch, plus restoration and appropriate controls, help separate correlation from causal dependence. The central examination skill is to specify what each measurement establishes, what it does not establish, and which additional comparison could distinguish competing explanations.

A systematic strategy for reading a stress experiment

Start with the experimental variable that was changed deliberately. This may be temperature, substrate supply, exposure to an oxidant, or a genetic alteration affecting a particular defense. Then separate proximal measurements of the molecular disturbance from markers of response activation and final functional endpoints. For example, oxidized protein abundance is a damage-related measurement, HSF1-dependent reporter expression is a response measurement, and recovery of enzyme activity is a functional measurement. A convincing mechanistic explanation predicts relationships among these quantities across time and across perturbations. An association between any two measures at a single time point is not sufficient to establish the entire sequence.

Protein abundance measurements require normalization appropriate to the comparison. Suppose two lanes of an immunoblot contain different total quantities of protein because one population lost many cells during treatment. A darker band in the second lane might result from more protein being loaded rather than greater expression per cell. Conversely, equal total protein loaded does not necessarily represent equal numbers of viable cells if treatment changes cellular protein content. An appropriate loading or total-protein control helps interpret the analytical signal, while measurements of cell count, viability and sample handling address the biological comparison. Both classes of control matter. A ratio of band intensities is meaningful only within the response range of the measurement system.

An enzyme activity assay supplies information not provided by an immunoblot. Two cell extracts can contain equal amounts of catalase but have different activity if one preparation contains inactivated enzyme. On the other hand, measuring activity in a cell extract with excess substrate can reveal potential capacity without reproducing the flux in an intact cell. In living cells the accessible substrate and other competing enzymes affect the actual rate. Therefore combining measurements of protein amount, catalytic capacity and cellular damage helps identify where the response fails. An increased messenger RNA level alone is even further removed from functional activity: translation, turnover, localization and cofactors intervene between transcript abundance and catalytic function.

Consider a hypothetical recovery time course with control and genetically deficient cells. Both show similar initial protein aggregation immediately after a standardized heat challenge. Two hours later, the control has cleared half its initial aggregate burden while the deficient population has cleared very little. This pattern suggests a defect in recovery rather than an obvious difference in initial susceptibility. If the deficient cells also had less ATP throughout the recovery period, insufficient energy could contribute to the phenotype. Restoring the suspected missing chaperone and assessing whether ATP status and aggregation change independently would strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. The experiment illustrates why the earliest available measurement is valuable: it helps distinguish altered formation of damage from altered removal.

Some fluorescent probes used in redox experiments react with more than one chemical species or require intermediate reactions before generating a signal. Probe uptake, localization, light exposure and the number of viable cells can also influence fluorescence. Thus a rise in a general oxidant-sensitive fluorescence measurement should not automatically be described as a direct quantitative increase in mitochondrial superoxide. More specific information requires an appropriately validated measurement, compartmental localization or complementary chemical and genetic evidence. In examinations, evaluate the exact claim supported by the given probe rather than assuming the label assigned to it by an experimental cartoon is fully specific.

Another recurrent issue is survivor bias. After a severe challenge, the most damaged cells may detach or die and be absent from the analyzed sample. A later measurement might then indicate less average damage in the remaining cells even if no individual cell repaired its damage. Tracking the number and state of cells and reporting how the sample was collected are important for interpretation. An apparent increase in average chaperone expression could likewise reflect enrichment of a resistant subpopulation. The distinction between changes within a cell and changes in the composition of the sampled population is crucial when interpreting tissue or culture data. Single endpoint averages can conceal several biologically distinct processes.

Quantitative comparisons should state their denominators. If a marker rises from 20 to 30 units, its absolute increase is 10 units, its relative increase is 50 percent and its final value is 1.5-fold the original. A decrease from 30 to 20 is also 10 units in magnitude but is a 33.3 percent decrease relative to 30. The same absolute difference does not imply symmetric percentage changes. When comparing a treatment and rescue condition, the fraction of the lost function restored may be a useful summary, but it requires defining the control baseline. If normal activity is 100, deficient activity is 40 and rescued activity is 70, the lost activity is 60 and the restored activity is 30, corresponding to half the deficit. The rescued value is also 70 percent of the normal activity; these two percentages answer different questions.

Integrating multiple molecular disturbances

A single physiological challenge can produce several disturbances at once. During reduced oxygen delivery, the ability to sustain oxidative phosphorylation can decline, and changes in the respiratory chain can affect oxidant generation. When oxygen supply is restored after a period of inadequate delivery, the changing metabolic conditions can also contribute to oxidant production. Energy sensing, antioxidant regulation and quality control may therefore all be relevant. Nonetheless, the specific relationship between oxygen availability and oxidant production depends on the state of the tissue and is not a simple monotonic rule. The following node examines environmental hypoxia and comparative adaptation more fully; the cellular principle here is that energy and redox status interact without being interchangeable variables.

The same integration can be seen during increased secretory demand. Synthesis and processing of proteins require energy, while alterations in folding conditions may lead to ER stress. An adaptive decrease in general translation can reduce the immediate burden, and transcriptional adjustments can improve quality control. At the same time, altered energy availability may activate metabolic regulation that restrains growth. If these responses reduce damage and allow recovery, their combination has served the cell. If the burden persists and replacement of essential components remains inadequate, the same markers may remain high while function declines. The biological question is not which pathway has the largest response, but whether the combined changes restore a state compatible with the cell's role in the organism.

Finally, a cell's capacity for adaptation should not be confused with an animal's heritable adaptation to an environment. A transient improvement in antioxidant capacity after exposure demonstrates a form of physiological plasticity if appropriate controls establish the effect. Demonstrating evolutionary adaptation requires evidence about heritable variation and fitness across generations. Similarly, a culture experiment establishes cell-autonomous capability under the tested conditions, not necessarily the dominant response in an intact animal whose endocrine, circulatory and immune systems also influence exposure and resource supply. Connecting these levels of explanation without treating them as synonyms is essential to the study of animal stress physiology.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All ROS are radicals
Correct concept: Hydrogen peroxide is a nonradical ROS. Why confusing: The term reactive is mistaken for unpaired-electron chemistry. Exam reasoning: Classify peroxide separately from superoxide and hydroxyl radical.
All ROS are harmful
Correct concept: Regulated peroxide and oxidant production can participate in signaling. Why confusing: Textbook injury examples overshadow normal functions. Exam reasoning: Ask whether the species, compartment, magnitude and duration imply controlled signaling or damage.
SOD eliminates all oxidative burden
Correct concept: It converts superoxide to peroxide; downstream disposal still matters. Why confusing: The word antioxidant is interpreted as complete detoxification. Exam reasoning: Predict superoxide and peroxide separately in a SOD gain-of-function experiment.
Catalase consumes GSH
Correct concept: Catalase dismutates peroxide; GPx uses GSH in peroxide reduction. Why confusing: Both enzymes remove peroxide and are easily conflated. Exam reasoning: Trace reactants and products rather than matching the shared substrate alone.
GSSG is reduced glutathione
Correct concept: GSSG is an oxidized disulfide containing two glutathione equivalents. Why confusing: The two abbreviations are similar. Exam reasoning: Count two GSH equivalents for each GSSG when calculating pool size.
NRF2 directly neutralizes peroxide
Correct concept: NRF2 regulates expression of cytoprotective genes. Why confusing: The pathway is named antioxidant response. Exam reasoning: Distinguish a transcription factor from its catalytic effector proteins.
Heat-shock proteins are induced only by heat
Correct concept: Many proteotoxic disturbances can activate the response. Why confusing: The historical name sounds exposure-specific. Exam reasoning: Infer a folding challenge, not a unique environmental temperature, from HSP induction alone.
Unfolding always breaks peptide bonds
Correct concept: Loss of noncovalent structural organization can impair function without backbone cleavage. Why confusing: Denaturation is confused with chemical hydrolysis. Exam reasoning: Separate molecular mass or immunoreactivity from functional enzyme activity.
Every chaperone consumes ATP directly
Correct concept: Small HSP holding functions need not directly hydrolyze ATP. Why confusing: HSP70 is often taught as the prototype for all chaperones. Exam reasoning: Compare ATP-dependent folding cycles with ATP-independent client holding.
ER stress and UPR are synonyms
Correct concept: ER stress is a disturbance; UPR is the response. Why confusing: Both terms often appear together in figures. Exam reasoning: High UPR markers need not mean greater injury if capacity has improved.
IRE1 splices XBP1 protein
Correct concept: IRE1 processes XBP1 RNA, changing the translated product. Why confusing: The active output is described as XBP1s protein. Exam reasoning: Locate the regulated substrate at the RNA level, unlike ATF6 proteolysis.
PERK stops all translation
Correct concept: General initiation declines while selected transcripts can be favored. Why confusing: Global translation measures are mistaken for every individual transcript. Exam reasoning: Expect ATF4 protein to increase in an appropriate phosphorylated eIF2α context.
LC3-II abundance equals autophagic flux
Correct concept: Abundance depends on formation and removal. Why confusing: A marker of an intermediate is mistaken for the rate of pathway completion. Exam reasoning: Compare changes during a suitable turnover block with independent cargo assays.
An oxidized base is already a fixed mutation
Correct concept: A chemical lesion can be repaired before incorrect copying fixes a sequence change. Why confusing: Both are described as DNA damage in informal language. Exam reasoning: Distinguish lesion measurements from mutation-frequency measurements and consider replication timing.
AMPK directly makes ATP
Correct concept: AMPK phosphorylates regulatory targets to alter metabolic allocation. Why confusing: Its name is associated with energy homeostasis. Exam reasoning: Predict that activation may fail to restore ATP when substrate or oxygen is limiting.
Phosphorylated eIF2α identifies PERK uniquely
Correct concept: Several stress-responsive kinases converge on eIF2α. Why confusing: PERK is the relevant ER kinase but not the only eIF2α kinase. Exam reasoning: Require branch-specific evidence before attributing the phosphorylation to PERK.
Higher stress marker means worse damage
Correct concept: Induction and injury are separate measurements. Why confusing: Both often rise after exposure in uncontrolled observations. Exam reasoning: Compare matched exposure and recovery outcomes rather than ranking stress by a single marker.

Original examination practice

These original questions are designed for examination preparation, not reproduced from past papers. Part B tests conceptual distinctions; Part C requires quantitative or experimental reasoning.

1. Part B

Which compound is a reactive oxygen species but not a free radical?

  1. Superoxide
  2. Hydroxyl radical
  3. Hydrogen peroxide
  4. Nitric oxide
Answer and reasoning

C. Hydrogen peroxide has no unpaired electron but participates in oxidative reactions. Superoxide, hydroxyl radical and nitric oxide possess unpaired electrons; nitric oxide is also classified as a reactive nitrogen species.

2. Part B

Which reaction is catalyzed by superoxide dismutase?

  1. 2 O₂•− + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂
  2. 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂
  3. GSSG + NADPH → 2 GSH + NADP⁺
  4. H₂O₂ + Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH
Answer and reasoning

A. SOD catalyzes dismutation of superoxide. The other reactions describe catalase, glutathione reductase (with proton balance omitted) and Fenton chemistry, respectively.

3. Part B

Which isoform acts primarily in the mammalian mitochondrial matrix?

  1. SOD3
  2. SOD1
  3. Catalase
  4. SOD2
Answer and reasoning

D. Manganese SOD2 is principally matrix-localized. SOD1 is predominantly cytosolic with additional localization, SOD3 is extracellular, and catalase removes peroxide rather than superoxide.

4. Part B

Which enzyme directly regenerates GSH from GSSG using NADPH?

  1. Glutathione peroxidase
  2. Glutathione reductase
  3. Superoxide dismutase
  4. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase
Answer and reasoning

B. Glutathione reductase supplies reduced glutathione. Peroxidase consumes GSH in peroxide reduction; the other enzymes have different substrates.

5. Part B

The oxidative pentose phosphate pathway helps antioxidant protection mainly by providing which molecule?

  1. NADPH
  2. NADH exclusively
  3. GSSG
  4. Molecular oxygen
Answer and reasoning

A. NADPH supplies reducing power for glutathione and thioredoxin regeneration. NADH is important in other metabolic reactions; GSSG is oxidized glutathione, and oxygen is not the reducing currency.

6. Part B

Which is the most appropriate immediate interpretation of increased NRF2 protein without increased NRF2 mRNA?

  1. Increased transcription is proven
  2. All protein synthesis ceased
  3. Reduced turnover is a plausible explanation
  4. The DNA sequence mutated
Answer and reasoning

C. NRF2 abundance depends on synthesis and degradation. Reduced KEAP1-dependent ubiquitination can stabilize it. Neither transcription, total translational arrest nor mutation follows from the data.

7. Part B

Under basal conditions KEAP1 primarily contributes to what process?

  1. Cleavage of XBP1 mRNA
  2. Targeting NRF2 for ubiquitination
  3. Direct conversion of H₂O₂ to water
  4. Phosphorylation of AMPK
Answer and reasoning

B. KEAP1 serves as an adaptor for a Cullin 3 ubiquitin ligase. It is neither an RNA-processing enzyme, peroxide-removal catalyst nor the kinase responsible for AMPK activation.

8. Part B

What is the function of an antioxidant response element?

  1. A lysosomal membrane channel
  2. An ATP-binding site of SOD
  3. An oxidant generated in mitochondria
  4. A regulatory DNA sequence
Answer and reasoning

D. AREs are cis-regulatory DNA regions recognized by NRF2-containing transcriptional complexes. The other options confuse a regulatory sequence with an organelle component, enzyme site or chemical species.

9. Part B

Which event can occur when a protein unfolds during moderate heat stress?

  1. Loss of activity with peptide bonds intact
  2. Guaranteed cleavage of every peptide bond
  3. Conversion into a nucleic acid
  4. Obligatory ubiquitination of all copies
Answer and reasoning

A. Noncovalent interactions supporting conformation can be disrupted without backbone cleavage. Ubiquitination is one possible later quality-control event, not a necessary result for every protein.

10. Part B

Which factor is a major transcriptional regulator of mammalian cytosolic heat-shock genes?

  1. ATF6 only
  2. HSF1
  3. OGG1
  4. SOD2
Answer and reasoning

B. HSF1 regulates heat-shock transcription; ATF6 acts in the ER response, OGG1 is a DNA glycosylase and SOD2 is an antioxidant enzyme.

11. Part B

Which is most characteristic of HSP70 client cycling?

  1. Irreversible covalent addition to every client
  2. Direct DNA repair by glycosylase activity
  3. Nucleotide-dependent binding and release
  4. Cleavage in Golgi to release NRF2
Answer and reasoning

C. HSP70 cycles between nucleotide-dependent client-binding states, assisted by co-chaperones. The remaining options assign unrelated or incorrect mechanisms.

12. Part B

Small heat-shock proteins commonly help protect vulnerable substrates through which strategy?

  1. ATP synthesis from ADP
  2. RNA splicing
  3. DNA ligation
  4. Holding nonnative clients and limiting aggregation
Answer and reasoning

D. Small HSPs can hold exposed client surfaces without direct ATP hydrolysis for the holding step. They do not catalyze the unrelated reactions listed.

13. Part B

Which mammalian ER stress sensor directly processes XBP1 RNA?

  1. IRE1
  2. PERK
  3. ATF6
  4. HSF1
Answer and reasoning

A. IRE1 has RNase activity that generates the spliced XBP1 transcript. PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, ATF6 undergoes proteolytic activation, and HSF1 regulates heat-shock genes.

14. Part B

What is the principal immediate translation-related target of PERK?

  1. NRF2
  2. eIF2α
  3. GSSG
  4. LC3-II
Answer and reasoning

B. PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, reducing general initiation while permitting selective responses. The other options are respectively a transcription factor, redox metabolite and autophagy-associated marker.

15. Part B

Where does regulated ATF6 cleavage normally occur in the canonical ER stress pathway?

  1. Mitochondrial matrix
  2. Nucleolus
  3. Golgi apparatus
  4. Extracellular space
Answer and reasoning

C. ATF6 traffics from ER to Golgi for proteolytic activation, after which its active fragment acts in the nucleus. The other compartments do not describe this pathway.

16. Part B

Which statement about eIF2α phosphorylation is correct?

  1. It proves PERK activation uniquely
  2. It requires NRF2 binding to DNA
  3. It stops all translation absolutely
  4. It can favor translation of selected transcripts such as ATF4
Answer and reasoning

D. Phosphorylation changes initiation availability and can favor ATF4 translation through upstream reading-frame regulation. Other kinases also phosphorylate eIF2α, and translation is not abolished universally.

17. Part B

Which is a common result of AMPK activation during energy shortage?

  1. Restraint of energy-consuming biosynthesis
  2. Direct catalysis of ATP synthesis
  3. Oxidation of GSH as its only substrate
  4. Obligatory acceleration of all anabolic pathways
Answer and reasoning

A. AMPK coordinates resource allocation, often restraining costly synthesis and favoring energy-producing pathways. It does not itself make ATP or function as a glutathione peroxidase.

18. Part B

Which reaction is catalyzed by adenylate kinase?

  1. ATP + AMP → 2 AMP
  2. 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP
  3. GSH + GSSG → ATP
  4. NADH + O₂ → GSSG
Answer and reasoning

B. Adenylate kinase interconverts two ADP with ATP and AMP. This equilibrium can amplify relative changes in AMP during energetic disturbance; the other reactions are not its reaction.

19. Part B

Which component completes much of macroautophagic degradation?

  1. Ribosome
  2. Centrosome
  3. Lysosomal compartment
  4. Nucleolus
Answer and reasoning

C. Autophagosomes deliver cargo to lysosomal compartments for breakdown. Formation of the carrier alone is not completion of degradation.

20. Part B

Which observation alone cannot establish increased autophagic flux?

  1. Increased turnover during a validated matched block
  2. Increased lysosomal cargo degradation
  3. A validated increase in delivered and degraded cargo
  4. Higher steady-state LC3-II abundance
Answer and reasoning

D. Higher LC3-II can result from more formation or less clearance. The other observations concern turnover or degradation and are more informative when properly controlled.

21. Part B

Which enzyme can initiate base excision repair of 8-oxoguanine in an appropriate pairing context?

  1. OGG1
  2. Catalase
  3. HSF1
  4. PERK
Answer and reasoning

A. OGG1 is a DNA glycosylase that removes the oxidized base. Catalase prevents peroxide accumulation, while HSF1 and PERK regulate protein stress responses.

22. Part B

Which distinction between lesion and mutation is valid?

  1. Every lesion is already a heritable sequence change
  2. A lesion can be repaired before a mutation is fixed
  3. A mutation is always a transient protein modification
  4. Oxidized DNA cannot be repaired
Answer and reasoning

B. Lesions are chemical or structural damage; a mutation is a persistent sequence alteration. Repair can prevent incorrect copying and mutation fixation.

23. Part B

Which is the best marker of completed adaptive recovery in a secretory cell?

  1. Maximum NRF2 band intensity alone
  2. Maximum LC3-II alone
  3. Restoration of functional secretion with maintained viability
  4. Permanent absence of all translation
Answer and reasoning

C. Recovery requires a biologically relevant endpoint. High response marker abundance may reflect continued stress, while lasting translational arrest would impair secretion.

24. Part B

Why can an isolated cultured animal cell mount a heat-shock response?

  1. An adrenal gland is always present in culture
  2. Circulating cortisol is required for HSF1 activation
  3. All cultured cells lack stress sensors
  4. Cell-autonomous mechanisms can detect proteotoxic disturbance
Answer and reasoning

D. Protein quality-control networks exist within cells independently of intact endocrine axes. Hormones can modulate the response but are not a universal prerequisite.

25. Part B

Which statement correctly describes a mild-conditioning response?

  1. It can produce reversible physiological tolerance
  2. It always proves heritable adaptation
  3. It eliminates all subsequent stress responses
  4. It guarantees protection regardless of dose
Answer and reasoning

A. Conditioning may alter protective capacity temporarily. Heritable adaptation requires evolutionary evidence, and excessive or mistimed conditioning can be ineffective or damaging.

26. Part C

A mitochondrial SOD2 transgene reduces superoxide-sensitive signal by 40%, but peroxide-sensitive signal rises and respiratory capacity falls. Explain why these observations are compatible.

Answer and reasoning

SOD2 changes superoxide into peroxide. If peroxide disposal or reducing-power supply is inadequate, peroxide-related injury can remain or rise despite lower superoxide signal. Confirm probe specificity, quantify mitochondrial peroxide-removing capacity, and compare respiratory function with a matched combined intervention. Lower superoxide alone does not prove net protection.

27. Part C

An illustrative compartment produces peroxide at 0.8 µmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ with first-order clearance k = 0.4 min⁻¹. Stress doubles production while halving k. Calculate the two steady concentrations and their ratio.

Answer and reasoning

Initially C = P/k = 0.8/0.4 = 2 µmol L⁻¹. Under stress C = 1.6/0.2 = 8 µmol L⁻¹, fourfold greater. This assumes first-order clearance, constant rates and a true steady state; a transient sample or enzyme saturation could violate the model.

28. Part C

Two populations have identical steady peroxide concentration. Population A has production 1 and clearance constant 0.5; population B has production 4 and clearance constant 2 in compatible units. Is their turnover identical?

Answer and reasoning

Both have C = 2, but B produces and removes four times as much peroxide per unit time at steady state. A concentration is a stock, not a flux. Comparing clearance following a controlled perturbation or independent production estimates could distinguish the systems.

29. Part C

GSH is 6 mM and GSSG is 1 mM. After challenge, GSH is 4 mM and GSSG is 2 mM. Compare total glutathione equivalents and oxidized fraction.

Answer and reasoning

Before: 6 + 2(1) = 8 mM equivalents; oxidized fraction 2/8 = 25%. After: 4 + 2(2) = 8 mM equivalents; oxidized fraction 4/8 = 50%. The pool has become more oxidized without a decline in total equivalents. A loss of total glutathione is not supported by these values.

30. Part C

At constant pH, compare GSSG/GSH² for samples with GSH/GSSG values 10 mM/1 mM and 1 mM/0.1 mM. What happens to redox potential?

Answer and reasoning

Using the same concentration units, the quotients are 0.01 and 0.1 mM⁻¹, respectively. The second has a tenfold higher quotient and hence a more positive potential under the fixed-pH Nernst model. Equal simple GSH:GSSG ratios do not ensure equal redox potentials.

31. Part C

An extract has unchanged GPx protein but reduced NADPH and increased GSSG after challenge. Predict the limitation in intact-cell defense.

Answer and reasoning

Glutathione reductase requires NADPH to restore GSH. A shortage can impair regeneration of the substrate used by GPx even when GPx abundance is unchanged. Measure enzyme activity, GSH regeneration and NADPH supply to distinguish this mechanism from direct GPx inhibition.

32. Part C

A wild-type ARE reporter doubles after electrophile exposure. A reporter lacking its ARE does not respond, and NRF2-null cells do not induce the intact reporter. What is supported, and what is not?

Answer and reasoning

The results support dependence of this reporter response on the ARE and NRF2 under the tested conditions. They do not prove direct binding at every endogenous gene, nor show that cells survived better. Rescue and direct occupancy or functional outcome measurements would strengthen those distinct conclusions.

33. Part C

KEAP1 loss causes high basal NRF2 and high basal target gene expression, while NRF2 loss produces weak induction. Explain the opposite effects.

Answer and reasoning

KEAP1 normally promotes NRF2 turnover, so removing it removes a negative control. NRF2 acts positively on many target genes, so its loss reduces induction. This is a sign-of-regulation problem; neither perturbation demonstrates that all stress genes belong to the same pathway.

34. Part C

A short challenge increases NRF2 protein at 15 min, target RNA at 1 h and enzyme activity at 5 h. What causal order is consistent with the data, and which experiment would strengthen it?

Answer and reasoning

Stabilization or activation of NRF2 can precede transcription and subsequent enzyme accumulation. Timing supports but does not prove causality. Compare a matched NRF2 loss-and-rescue series and test target regulatory sequences; monitor exposure and viability to avoid confounding.

35. Part C

Two cells exposed to equal heat have similar immediate aggregate loads. After recovery, cell A clears 75% and B clears 25%. What can you infer?

Answer and reasoning

The later divergence supports a difference in clearance or recovery rather than an obvious difference in initial aggregation. It does not identify the responsible protein. Compare ATP, chaperone activity and degradation capacity, and use a specific restoration experiment.

36. Part C

A mild pre-treatment raises HSP70, and survival after a second challenge rises. Why is correlation insufficient to establish that HSP70 mediates the effect?

Answer and reasoning

Conditioning can change many pathways at once. Use matched untreated, conditioning-only, challenge-only and conditioning-plus-challenge groups, with a specific HSP70 perturbation and rescue if possible. Measure relevant function and ensure the perturbation is not independently toxic.

37. Part C

A mutant HSP70 binds clients but cannot release them efficiently. Predict a likely effect on recovery.

Answer and reasoning

Client sequestration may reduce some aggregation initially but impede repeated folding cycles and restoration of active protein. Measure both aggregation and enzyme function; soluble or held protein is not necessarily native or active.

38. Part C

A small HSP increases after energy depletion while HSP70-dependent recovery slows. Why is this not inconsistent?

Answer and reasoning

Small HSPs can hold nonnative proteins without direct ATP hydrolysis, whereas HSP70 cycling requires nucleotide-dependent steps. Energy shortage may favor temporary holding but limit active refolding. Restoration of ATP and client function would distinguish successful recovery from persistent sequestration.

39. Part C

Total XBP1 RNA is 200 units before and after exposure. Spliced RNA rises from 30 to 120 units. Calculate the spliced fraction and interpret it.

Answer and reasoning

The spliced fraction rises from 30/200 = 15% to 120/200 = 60%, a fourfold increase in the fraction. Total RNA is unchanged, so the result supports altered processing rather than a necessary increase in total transcription. Verify equal detection efficiency and test IRE1 dependence.

40. Part C

A PERK inhibitor lowers eIF2α phosphorylation only partly, while XBP1 splicing remains elevated. Explain both results.

Answer and reasoning

Other eIF2α kinases may contribute to phosphorylation, or inhibition may be incomplete. XBP1 splicing reports IRE1 RNase activity, not the immediate PERK output. Verify inhibitor specificity and use genetic or additional activity measurements.

41. Part C

A stressed cell shows reduced global protein synthesis but increased ATF4 protein. Explain the apparent contradiction.

Answer and reasoning

Phosphorylation of eIF2α reduces initiation-complex availability for many RNAs, but the upstream reading-frame arrangement of ATF4 can favor its translation under those conditions. Global output is an average, not a universal rule for each transcript. Measure the relevant RNA and protein to distinguish translation from transcription.

42. Part C

A trafficking perturbation prevents formation of the cleaved ATF6 fragment but leaves XBP1 splicing detectable. Which branch is directly affected?

Answer and reasoning

The ATF6 branch requires movement to the Golgi and proteolytic processing, so the result is consistent with impairment of this route. IRE1 can remain active in parallel. A broad trafficking inhibitor may cause additional stress; demonstrate specificity before attributing all effects to ATF6.

43. Part C

A secretory cell has twice the intracellular enzyme protein but only half the extracellular enzyme activity. Give two plausible mechanisms and a discriminating measurement.

Answer and reasoning

The intracellular material may be nonnative and retained, or functional protein may fail to exit the secretory pathway. Measure intracellular enzymatic activity, secretion of correctly folded protein, and ER stress markers over time. Protein abundance alone cannot distinguish folding and transport defects.

44. Part C

ATP, ADP and AMP are 5, 1 and 0.2 mM before challenge and 3, 2 and 1.2 mM after. Compute energy charge.

Answer and reasoning

Before: (5 + 0.5)/6.2 = 0.887. After: (3 + 1)/6.2 = 0.645. The decline indicates altered nucleotide distribution. It is not direct proof of AMPK phosphorylation or of the primary cause of energy stress.

45. Part C

AMPK is strongly activated in oxygen-deprived cells, but ATP does not recover. Why does this not disprove its role?

Answer and reasoning

AMPK can redistribute expenditure and favor energy-producing pathways, but cannot supply oxygen for respiratory electron transfer. Severe substrate or organelle limitations can prevent recovery despite activation. Compare metabolic flux, oxygen availability and essential ATP consumption.

46. Part C

LC3-II is 2 units in controls and 5 in stressed cells. A matched lysosomal block yields 6 and 6 units, respectively. Compare increments and interpret cautiously.

Answer and reasoning

The control increment is 4 and the stressed increment is 1. The smaller stress-associated increment is consistent with reduced turnover during the block interval despite high basal LC3-II. Verify effective blockade, comparable sampling and additional cargo-degradation markers before making a definitive conclusion.

47. Part C

Mitochondrial mass decreases after stress while respiration per unit mitochondrial protein rises. Can total cellular respiratory capacity be inferred?

Answer and reasoning

No. Total capacity depends on both mitochondrial mass and activity per unit mass. For example, a 50% mass loss and 20% rise in specific capacity yields 0.5 × 1.2 = 0.6 of original capacity. Assess respiration per cell and the physiological workload.

48. Part C

An OGG1-deficient cell population shows normal short-term viability but greater G:C to T:A mutations after repeated oxidant challenge. Explain the relationship.

Answer and reasoning

Oxidized guanine can pair with adenine during replication, and failure to repair it can promote mutation fixation. Short-term survival may remain normal while genome fidelity is impaired. Compare baseline mutation rates, exposure and proliferation and rescue repair capacity to strengthen causality.

49. Part C

A damage marker declines from 100 to 50 units in a culture, but viable cell count also falls by half. Is repair demonstrated?

Answer and reasoning

No. Selective loss of highly damaged cells, altered sample composition or normalization can produce a lower average or total marker without repair in survivors. Track individual or appropriately normalized cell measurements and viability, and document which cells were sampled.

50. Part C

A stressed population initially reduces translation, increases chaperone expression and subsequently recovers secretion. Another maintains high stress markers but loses secretion. Which population adapted more effectively?

Answer and reasoning

The first shows restoration of a relevant physiological output after a temporary response. High markers in the second may indicate unresolved burden rather than superior protection. Compare baseline function, stress intensity, viability and recovery duration; distinguish response magnitude from success.

Revision and active recall

Stress responses coordinate prevention, repair, resource allocation and removal. Interpret the molecular response separately from accumulated damage and physiological recovery.

  5. Explain why KEAP1-dependent turnover controls NRF2 abundance.
  6. Compare HSF1 with the three ER stress sensors.
  7. Explain why eIF2α phosphorylation does not stop all translation.
  8. Derive the AMP:ATP fold change in the worked example.
