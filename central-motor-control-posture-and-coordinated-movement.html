1. The physiological problem: purposeful movement and stable posture

A person standing at a bench reaches for a flask. The hand must travel to a particular position, the fingers must open by an appropriate amount, and the arm must decelerate without knocking the flask over. At the same time, the forward movement of the arm changes the distribution of body mass. Unless the trunk and legs develop compensating forces, the person will sway or step unexpectedly. An apparently simple voluntary movement therefore consists of at least two interacting tasks: directing the part of the body that performs the action and maintaining the conditions under which the action can succeed. Neither task can be explained by knowing the structure of a single neuron or the properties of a single skeletal muscle.

The earlier chapters established that a skeletal muscle fibre contracts when its motor neuron delivers an appropriate electrical signal. The present question concerns how the nervous system determines which motor neurons should fire, when they should fire, and how rapidly they should discharge. An alpha motor neuron supplies extrafusal muscle fibres, which develop force. Gamma motor neurons regulate intrafusal fibre tension and thereby influence spindle sensitivity. In the central nervous system, neurons that influence these motor pools can combine information about the external world, the body's current state and the intended action. The output is not a picture of the desired movement transmitted directly to a muscle. It is a changing pattern of excitation and inhibition distributed over motor pools.

A distinction between movement and posture is useful but not absolute. Movement is a change in the configuration or location of a body part. Posture describes the arrangement of body segments and their orientation with respect to one another and the environment. Even quiet standing contains small movements, because the body is mechanically compliant and sensory signals and muscle forces fluctuate. Conversely, movement requires a sequence of postures. A bird landing on a branch must control the position of its feet relative to the branch while altering the orientation of its trunk and wings. Thus, an apparently static task may require continuous neural regulation, and a dynamic task may require temporary stabilisation of selected segments.

The motor system faces a problem of redundancy, sometimes called the problem of multiple degrees of freedom. A degree of freedom is an independent variable needed to specify a mechanical configuration. There are many ways to bring a hand to a location using different combinations of shoulder, elbow and wrist angles. Even when the joint angles are specified, different combinations of muscle activity can produce the same net torque. An animal need not use every mechanically possible solution. It can exploit regular combinations of muscle activity, constrain irrelevant variation and select a movement suited to the task. The existence of many solutions is advantageous when an obstacle, fatigue or a changed load makes the previously chosen solution less effective.

Force and motion must also be distinguished. An isometric contraction can develop tension without appreciable change in muscle length; a limb can move because an external force acts upon it without a voluntary contraction of the moving muscle. Antagonistic muscles can be activated together and generate substantial force while producing little net joint torque. Central motor commands are therefore interpreted through the biomechanics of the body. A neural signal that is useful in one posture may produce a different movement in another because joint geometry and muscle moment arms have changed. This is one reason why monitoring the current configuration of the body is indispensable.

It is convenient to describe four cooperating classes of central motor circuits. Local spinal and brainstem circuits organise motor neuron output and provide reflex interactions. Descending pathways from the cortex and brainstem change the activity of those circuits and, in selected cases, act more directly on motor neurons. Basal ganglia circuits influence action selection, initiation and the scaling of motor behaviour through loops involving the thalamus and other targets. Cerebellar circuits contribute to coordination, timing, predictive control and adaptation. The four are not successive obligatory stages in a single chain. Each can receive different information while influencing the others, and many processes operate in parallel during the same movement.

A command for a movement must be distinguished from an estimate of its outcome. Sensory information can reveal a difference between the intended and actual state. However, sensory transmission, processing and muscle activation take time. If a correction were always initiated only after a substantial error appeared, rapid movements could overshoot. A controller can use learned predictions to initiate a suitable pattern before the error develops and then use sensory feedback to refine it. In biological systems, feedforward and feedback processes coexist rather than replacing one another. The more predictable the task, the more useful prior experience becomes; when the environment changes unexpectedly, rapid feedback and subsequent learning become especially important.

A useful example is lifting two visually identical containers, one empty and the other filled with water. Their appearance does not disclose their precise mass. A force chosen from past experience can be too small for the heavier container or too large for the lighter one. Sensory events after contact, including cutaneous deformation and changes in muscle tension and limb acceleration, provide information that can alter subsequent muscle activity. When the same heavy container is lifted repeatedly, preparatory forces are likely to become better matched to the expected load. The change in performance need not mean that muscle fibres have become stronger during those few repetitions; it may arise from an improved estimate of the required force.

Motor performance can be described through measurable variables. Kinematics refers to positions, velocities and accelerations without specifying the forces producing them. Kinetics concerns forces and torques. Electromyography measures electrical activity associated with active muscle fibres; it is not a direct measurement of external force. A trajectory recorded by a camera, a force measured by a transducer and an electrical signal recorded from a muscle answer different questions. The value of combining such measurements is that apparently similar failures of performance can be distinguished: an inappropriate neural recruitment pattern, altered muscle force production and an external mechanical disturbance are not equivalent.

The central objective of this chapter is to connect neural organisation with these observable consequences. The cortex contributes to choosing and implementing skilled actions, descending pathways organise access to spinal and cranial motor pools, subcortical loops modify the selection and accuracy of actions, and sensory information makes those actions appropriate to changing circumstances. The resulting framework explains why isolated alterations in different parts of the motor system can lead to markedly different patterns of movement, coordination and muscle tone.

2. Motor cortex: representation, preparation and execution

The cerebral cortex is not an undifferentiated motor centre. The primary motor cortex, commonly designated M1 or Brodmann area 4, occupies much of the precentral gyrus and adjacent medial frontal surface. Areas immediately anterior to it include premotor cortex on the lateral surface and supplementary motor areas on the medial surface, often associated with area 6. These regions are connected with sensory association areas, each other, subcortical structures and descending motor systems. Their overlapping activities permit the transformation of sensory information and goals into appropriately timed motor output.

Historical electrical stimulation experiments helped establish that movement could be related to particular cortical regions. In 1870 Fritsch and Hitzig reported that stimulation of selected sites in the canine cerebral cortex could evoke movements, frequently on the side opposite the stimulated hemisphere. Later mapping studies, including work by Penfield and colleagues in humans, established a rough correspondence between cortical location and the body parts influenced by stimulation. The important conceptual change was from treating the cortex as a largely unlocalised organ to recognising that different cortical regions make specialised contributions to behaviour. Electrical stimulation provides evidence of a functional link between a stimulated neural region and an evoked motor response, not proof that each individual neuron is assigned exclusively to one muscle.

The motor homunculus is a schematic depiction of somatotopy, the orderly relation of body regions to cortical territories. Lower limb representations tend to lie medially, while upper limb and facial representations occupy progressively more lateral regions. The relative size of a representation does not correspond simply to the physical size of the body part. Hands and face require particularly differentiated patterns of activity and occupy substantial cortical territory. A drawing of a large hand on the cortical map indicates representational emphasis, not a larger physical hand or necessarily a larger average motor neuron.

Real cortical maps contain overlap and variability. A stimulation site can influence several muscles, and neurons with related effects may be distributed across a region. The current amplitude, duration and pattern of experimental stimulation affect the response. Current can spread beyond the intended location, and the state of the subject can influence excitability. Consequently, a map should be interpreted as a useful broad organisation rather than a precise map in which each square millimetre controls exactly one muscle. A student examining a cortical diagram should first identify the medial and lateral regions and then distinguish the principle of somatotopy from the stronger and incorrect claim of a rigid one-muscle map.

Layer V pyramidal neurons are important sources of long descending projections from the cortex. Their axons can reach the brainstem or spinal cord. Large Betz cells occur among the pyramidal neurons of primary motor cortex, but they account for only a minority of corticospinal neurons. Not every pyramidal neuron participates in the corticospinal tract, and corticospinal fibres arise outside M1 as well. The anatomical shape of a neuron, the route of its axon and the functional consequences of its activity are related descriptions but should not be treated as identical classifications.

Motor cortical activity can reflect several features of a movement, including direction, force, muscle activation and task context. These variables frequently covary in natural behaviour. During reaching, for instance, a particular direction may require changes in joint angle and muscle recruitment. A neuron whose discharge correlates with movement direction might respond to some combination of these underlying quantities. To determine whether it relates more closely to direction, force or muscle activity, an experiment must vary those quantities independently where possible. A correlation discovered during a single restricted task cannot automatically be generalised to all movements.

Population activity offers an explanation of how broad neural representations can participate in precise behaviour. Individual neurons may respond over a range of movement directions, with stronger discharge for some directions than others. The combined pattern across many neurons can contain information about a selected direction more reliably than the discharge of one cell alone. This does not require that the brain literally calculate the textbook population-vector formula in every situation. It demonstrates that a distributed representation can carry information with greater specificity than any one of its component responses. Such distributed patterns also help explain why a limited injury can impair precision without completely eliminating a movement.

Premotor cortex is especially important for preparing actions in relation to sensory cues and task demands. Consider an animal trained to reach to the left after a light appears on the left and to the right after a light appears on the right. In a delayed-response task, the cue can be presented several seconds before permission to move. Activity recorded during the delay cannot be explained simply by contraction of the reaching muscles if electromyography confirms that those muscles are not yet active. It may reflect the preparation or selection of the movement. To interpret such a signal, however, it is necessary to distinguish preparation from attention, cue identity and expectation of reward.

Supplementary motor regions are associated with internally organised movement sequences, coordination between limbs and aspects of preparation. The distinction between lateral premotor cortex and supplementary motor areas is relative rather than a strict separation of externally cued and internally generated behaviour. Both contain diverse neural populations, receive extensive connections and can participate in both categories of action. A complex task such as playing a short learned sequence on a keyboard combines visual guidance, memory of the sequence, bilateral coordination and execution. These functions recruit a distributed network rather than one exclusive cortical region.

The posterior parietal cortex provides information about the relationship between sensory stimuli and the body that can guide movement. A target located at a fixed point in the room requires different joint configurations depending on the position of the trunk and shoulder. Visual coordinates must be related to the body and to its current configuration. The general concept of a sensorimotor transformation describes this conversion between descriptions useful for detecting a target and descriptions useful for acting on it. Parietal and frontal interactions provide an important basis for such transformations. The detailed visual transduction mechanisms responsible for the initial sensory signal belong to the following subunit.

One way to analyse cortical contributions is to compare preparation and execution while keeping movement direction constant. In an illustrative experiment, a monkey learns to move a handle toward a target only after a separate go signal. Recording begins when the target appears and continues through the waiting interval and movement. If a neuron changes its activity after target presentation but before muscle activity, the result is consistent with a preparatory function. If another neuron responds mainly when force develops and its activity changes with independently altered resistance, it is consistent with a contribution to execution. Neither observation alone establishes necessity. Reversible disruption of the relevant region, combined with behavioural measurement and an appropriate control condition, can provide complementary evidence.

The motor cortex therefore has more than a single role as a source of commands. It is part of a network that prepares movement, translates task information, integrates feedback and influences descending output. The corticospinal pathway is one major anatomical route by which these processes affect spinal motor circuits. Understanding that route makes it possible to predict what happens when communication is interrupted above or below an important anatomical crossing.

3. Corticospinal and corticobulbar pathways

The word tract refers to a bundle of axons within the central nervous system that travel together for part of their course. A descending tract carries signals toward lower levels of the brainstem or spinal cord. Descending does not mean that an individual signal must excite its final target: axons can activate inhibitory interneurons, and the net effect on a muscle can be inhibition. Similarly, a pathway may affect several muscles through divergent connections even if its own fibres form a relatively compact anatomical bundle.

The corticospinal tract originates from neurons in several cortical regions, including primary motor, premotor and somatosensory areas. Fibres converge through the subcortical white matter and pass through the internal capsule, then the cerebral peduncles of the midbrain and ventral regions of the pons. In the caudal medulla, many collect into visible longitudinal bundles known as the pyramids. Most corticospinal fibres cross in the pyramidal decussation and descend in the lateral corticospinal tract. A smaller contingent continues initially on the same side in the anterior corticospinal tract; many of its fibres exert bilateral effects or cross near their spinal termination levels. Precise proportions vary between individuals, so a broad majority rather than an invariant percentage is the important anatomical fact.

A simple tracing exercise clarifies laterality. Suppose a neuron in the left primary motor cortex sends an axon through the left internal capsule and left medullary pyramid. If the axon crosses in the caudal medulla, it enters the right lateral corticospinal tract. A lesion in the left internal capsule interrupts this axon before crossing and can impair movement on the right side of the body. A lesion of the right lateral corticospinal tract in the spinal cord interrupts the same pathway after crossing and can impair functions on the right side below the affected level. These predictions follow from the anatomical course rather than from memorising a rule that all central nervous system lesions have contralateral effects.

The lateral corticospinal system is particularly important for skilful control of distal limb musculature. Fine independent finger movements require activity to be differentiated among motor pools that might otherwise be recruited together. In primates, some corticospinal neurons form direct connections with spinal alpha motor neurons, especially those related to the hand. Many corticospinal connections, however, terminate on interneurons. The degree of direct cortical access varies between species and among muscles. The existence of direct connections helps explain certain aspects of dexterity but does not mean that voluntary movement bypasses spinal circuitry in general.

Corticospinal axons can influence spinal inhibitory and excitatory interneurons, altering the gain and distribution of responses to sensory input. They can also influence processing in sensory regions of the spinal cord. This matters because the movement that is appropriate during a particular task depends on which sensory response is useful. A powerful stretch reflex that resists all changes in muscle length would oppose intended shortening or lengthening under some circumstances. Descending commands can alter the operating conditions of reflex circuits so that sensory feedback supports rather than obstructs the desired action.

The anterior corticospinal pathway belongs to a broader set of descending systems that influence axial and proximal muscles. Bilateral innervation is useful for controlling muscles whose actions contribute to trunk stabilisation and symmetrical postural tasks. The distinction between lateral and medial descending systems is a functional generalisation: lateral pathways tend to be associated with more independent distal control, while medial pathways have important roles in axial and proximal coordination. It is not a complete partition because many movements involve both and their spinal terminations can overlap.

Corticobulbar fibres are cortical projections that influence motor nuclei or associated local circuits in the brainstem. The term bulbar refers historically to the brainstem, especially the medulla, but corticobulbar projections affect functions at multiple brainstem levels. They contribute to voluntary control of muscles involved in facial expression, jaw movement, tongue movement, swallowing and other cranial motor functions. As with spinal motor control, an upper motor pathway should not be confused with the lower motor neurons whose axons leave the brainstem to innervate skeletal muscle.

Many cranial motor nuclei receive cortical influences from both hemispheres. Important exceptions include the predominantly contralateral cortical influence over lower facial musculature and aspects of tongue control. For example, a unilateral supranuclear injury may impair voluntary movement of the opposite lower face while relatively sparing the forehead, whose motor organisation receives more bilateral cortical input. This is a physiological illustration of converging control, not an absolute diagnostic rule for every facial weakness. A lesion of the peripheral facial nerve can disrupt the final lower motor pathway to both upper and lower facial muscles on its own side. The difference arises from where the signal is interrupted relative to the pattern of convergence.

The expression pyramidal system traditionally refers mainly to the corticospinal and corticobulbar pathways associated with voluntary motor control. Extrapyramidal has historically been used for motor systems outside that designation, including basal ganglia and certain brainstem descending tracts. The terminology is useful for recognising older textbook classifications, but it can be misleading when used to describe two independent motor systems. Basal ganglia output influences cortical motor activity, and cortical signals influence brainstem systems. Consequently, a basal ganglia disorder is not a failure of a single extrapyramidal spinal tract. It is a change in the activity of interacting central circuits.

Selective interruption of corticospinal projections can disproportionately impair fractionated voluntary movements even when broad postural adjustments remain possible. Such preservation is expected because brainstem and spinal systems remain available to organise some muscle activity. Conversely, a disturbance of medial descending pathways can impair balance and proximal stabilisation even if some isolated distal movements remain accessible. These observations show that a large motor action usually combines functions supplied by several pathways.

To distinguish pathway-specific effects experimentally, one can compare tasks that require fine independent digit movements with tasks that require gross limb support and locomotion. Performance should be assessed before and after a controlled manipulation and interpreted in relation to sensory and muscular function. A reduction in grip force alone would be ambiguous: weakness might arise from altered motor recruitment, peripheral damage, discomfort or a failure of coordination. Evidence from neural recordings, anatomically verified disruptions and several behavioural tasks is stronger than a single behavioural score. The key principle is that the corticospinal system contributes importantly to voluntary skill, but it acts within a network that also controls posture, tone and rhythmic movement.

4. Brainstem motor systems and spinal organisation

The brainstem is a major source of descending signals that coordinate the head, trunk and limbs. It receives input from higher cortical areas, the cerebellum, basal ganglia-related systems and sensory structures. Many of its motor effects reach the spinal cord through pathways that terminate on interneurons and motor pools associated with broad movements and postural support. The brainstem should therefore not be described merely as a cable connecting the brain to the spinal cord. Its nuclei contain neural circuits capable of organising context-sensitive patterns of activity.

The vestibular nuclei receive signals related to head orientation and movement, as well as additional information from the cerebellum and other sensory systems. They influence eye movements, head stabilisation and spinal muscle activity. The lateral vestibulospinal tract arises principally from the lateral vestibular nucleus and descends mainly ipsilaterally through much of the spinal cord. It is classically associated with facilitation of extensor or antigravity activity and the organisation of postural responses. In an upright animal, such activity helps oppose the tendency of gravity to flex supporting joints. The word antigravity refers to a functional role; which muscle is used to oppose gravity depends on the body configuration.

The medial vestibulospinal tract originates largely from medial vestibular nuclear regions and travels bilaterally through the medial longitudinal fasciculus toward cervical and upper thoracic levels. It has an important influence on neck and upper trunk circuits. If the head unexpectedly moves relative to the trunk, changes in the activity of neck muscles can help stabilise it. Coordination of head and eye movements permits visual information to remain useful during changes in body orientation. The receptor mechanisms that transduce angular acceleration and gravito-inertial acceleration need not be repeated here; the central motor issue is how the nervous system uses the resulting information to adjust muscle activity.

Reticulospinal pathways originate in the reticular formation of the brainstem, a distributed collection of neuronal populations rather than a sharply bounded single nucleus. They contribute to anticipatory postural adjustments, locomotor coordination, axial and proximal muscle recruitment and the modulation of reflex excitability. Pontine and medullary reticular formations are conventionally discussed as sources of descending influences that may favour or reduce extensor activity. This traditional contrast is helpful when first learning the pathways, but their effects are more flexible than a fixed division into universally excitatory and inhibitory tracts. Different reticulospinal neurons contact different circuits, and effects change with the behavioural task.

A person who prepares to pull a heavy object may increase the activity of leg and trunk muscles before the arm movement begins. Cortical signals can engage brainstem systems that help prepare this support. If the posture were adjusted only after the trunk began to rotate, the correction would arrive after the disturbance had already affected the body. The reticulospinal system is therefore one route by which preparation of movement can be coupled with preparation of posture. A consequence is that signals descending from the cortex need not all travel in the corticospinal tract to influence spinal muscles.

The red nucleus is another midbrain motor structure. Its rubrospinal projections are important in several nonhuman mammals, especially in relation to limb control; in humans the rubrospinal tract is relatively reduced compared with the corticospinal system. The superior colliculus contributes to the orientation of the head and eyes toward significant stimuli and can influence spinal circuits through tectospinal projections, primarily toward cervical levels. These pathways illustrate the broader principle that movements selected in response to sensory stimuli need not begin in primary motor cortex. Their relevance and relative prominence differ across species.

Local spinal motor circuits convert descending influences into coordinated muscle output. The spinal ventral horn contains motor neurons, while interneurons in several regions integrate sensory input and descending signals. A simple reciprocal inhibitory circuit is a familiar example. Activation of an agonist can be accompanied by reduced activity of its antagonist, limiting unnecessary opposing torque. But reciprocal inhibition is not an obligatory rule for all tasks: simultaneous activation of antagonists can increase joint stiffness when stability is more important than minimising muscular effort.

Motor neurons supplying axial and proximal muscles are generally located more medially in the ventral horn than motor neurons supplying distal limb muscles. The spatial arrangement of motor pools relates to the pattern of descending projections. Medial systems often influence interconnected motor pools over several segments, which is appropriate for muscles that cooperate to maintain the position of the trunk and girdles. Lateral systems can provide more differentiated control over individual limb movements. This anatomical organisation is consistent with the mechanical requirements of whole-body support and fine manipulation.

An experimental observation of an extensor response after stimulation of a brainstem site demonstrates that the stimulation can influence a pathway capable of producing that response. It does not establish that the site serves only that one function during natural behaviour. Electrical stimulation may activate fibres passing through a region, and different behavioural conditions change the background excitability of spinal neurons. More informative studies can compare responses during rest, movement preparation and different phases of locomotion while measuring activity in several muscles. A task-dependent change in the response can reveal that descending regulation is conditional on the current state of the motor network.

Brainstem pathways complement, rather than oppose in every situation, corticospinal pathways. When a person reaches while standing, cortical commands can contribute to the precise trajectory of the hand, reticulospinal influences can prepare supporting musculature, and vestibulospinal influences can adjust head and body orientation as the movement proceeds. The spinal cord integrates these descending signals with sensory feedback. The relative contributions change if the person sits down, uses external support or reaches from an unstable surface. This flexibility is central to understanding neural control of posture.

5. Basal ganglia: the circuitry of action selection

Movement control involves choosing which action should occur, not simply activating every muscle capable of moving. When several actions are available, an appropriate motor system must favour one pattern while limiting competing patterns. The basal ganglia are interconnected subcortical structures that contribute to this regulation. They influence movement through loops with the cerebral cortex, thalamus and brainstem, but do not form a direct final motor pathway to skeletal muscle. Their contribution is particularly evident when actions must be initiated, selected, scaled or modified in relation to previous experience and context.

The main input structure of the basal ganglia is the striatum, which in the dorsal motor system comprises the caudate nucleus and putamen. The globus pallidus has external and internal segments, designated GPe and GPi. The subthalamic nucleus is a distinct excitatory structure. The substantia nigra includes a pars compacta, containing dopamine-producing neurons that project strongly to the striatum, and a pars reticulata, which resembles GPi in many aspects of its output function. GPi and substantia nigra pars reticulata provide major inhibitory output to thalamic and brainstem targets. These names identify different positions in a circuit and should not be learned as an isolated anatomical list.

The classical motor loop begins with excitatory cortical inputs to the striatum. Principal striatal projection neurons release GABA, a neurotransmitter whose usual effect at their major adult basal ganglia targets is inhibitory. Pallidal and nigral output neurons are also GABAergic and generally discharge tonically, meaning that they remain active even without an immediately preceding voluntary movement. Their activity restrains selected targets. A reduction in this inhibitory output can permit the target to become more active, provided that appropriate excitatory inputs are present. This mechanism is called disinhibition.

Disinhibition is often difficult at first because two inhibitory effects can produce a net facilitatory consequence. Imagine a thalamic neuron receiving continuous inhibitory input from GPi. If a striatal neuron suppresses GPi activity, the thalamic neuron experiences less inhibition. Its firing need not increase under every condition because it also receives other inputs, but it has been released from part of its restraint. The striatal neuron has not become excitatory; its inhibitory synapse has changed the inhibition exerted by another neuron. Examination problems commonly exploit confusion between the transmitter at a particular synapse and the net effect along a chain of synapses.

The direct pathway of the classical model runs from cortex to striatum, then from striatum to GPi or SNr. The cortex excites a population of striatal neurons, these neurons inhibit basal ganglia output neurons, and output inhibition of thalamic targets is reduced. Thalamocortical projections can then provide increased support to the relevant cortical motor network. This pathway is often summarised as facilitating selected actions. Its function depends on which functional channel is engaged and on the other inputs to the relevant neurons. The description does not imply that the direct pathway by itself specifies the direction, force and timing of every muscle contraction.

The indirect pathway includes two additional major relays. Striatal neurons inhibit GPe. Because GPe normally inhibits the subthalamic nucleus, decreasing GPe activity releases the subthalamic nucleus from part of that inhibition. The subthalamic nucleus sends excitatory projections to GPi and SNr, tending to increase output inhibition of downstream targets. Thus, in the standard teaching model, recruitment of the indirect pathway can increase the restraint of a motor channel. Counting the signs of the individual connections helps students derive the result, but the tonic activity and other inputs of the intermediate neurons explain why sign counting is physiologically meaningful.

The hyperdirect pathway provides an additional route from the cortex to the subthalamic nucleus and from there to the output nuclei. Its early excitatory influence on GPi or SNr can increase inhibition of targets without first passing through the striatum. This relatively direct route is useful in conceptual accounts of rapidly constraining or interrupting actions. It does not mean that every stop signal is implemented solely by this pathway or that every indirect-pathway response has an identical timing. The three routes interact within larger circuits, and all are influenced by the current context.

Classical descriptions often compare direct and indirect pathways to an accelerator and a brake. The analogy can help a beginner remember their broad net effects on a selected channel. It fails if it suggests that all direct-pathway neurons are active only during movement and all indirect-pathway neurons are silent at that time. Neural recordings and manipulation experiments demonstrate that both classes of striatal neurons can be recruited around the onset of actions. Coordinated selection can require the facilitation of useful actions and restraint of incompatible actions at the same time. The relevant physiological output is spatially and temporally patterned activity rather than a single measure of whether the entire motor system is globally activated.

The basal ganglia are arranged in several interacting loops that include motor, associative and limbic territories. In this chapter the focus is on the motor loop. Cortical input conveys aspects of the behavioural context and intended action, while basal ganglia output shapes the conditions under which cortical and brainstem motor circuits operate. The thalamus is not a passive switch: its neurons integrate multiple signals and participate in reciprocal cortical interactions. A simple diagram of the loop therefore identifies the principal direction and signs of selected connections, not the complete set of synapses participating in behaviour.

One can test the logic of the direct and indirect pathways with a hypothetical selective perturbation. If a striatal input to GPi becomes more active while other influences are held fixed, GPi activity tends to decrease and inhibition of its target tends to fall. If instead the subthalamic excitatory input to GPi increases, GPi activity tends to rise and its target experiences greater inhibition. This type of local prediction is appropriate for a circuit question. It is not always sufficient to predict whole-animal behaviour, because compensatory processes and activation of neighbouring channels may alter the response. A well designed experiment therefore measures both the intermediate circuit activity and the motor outcome.

The basal ganglia have a distinctive role relative to spinal motor circuits. Damage to a lower motor neuron removes a route by which muscle fibres receive excitation. Dysfunction of basal ganglia circuits may leave that final route intact while altering when and how strongly it is recruited. An animal may retain the ability to generate force during a task but show difficulty initiating or scaling a self-generated action. This distinction becomes especially clear when considering how dopamine changes the operation of the basal ganglia and what happens when the dopaminergic input is reduced.

6. Dopamine modulation and the regulation of movement

Dopamine produced by neurons of substantia nigra pars compacta is released into the striatum, where it changes the responses of striatal neurons to other synaptic inputs. Dopamine is a modulator rather than the main fast excitatory transmitter linking the cortex to the striatum. Its effects depend on receptor type, intracellular signalling mechanisms, synaptic context and the state of the receiving cell. The distinction is important because a single neurotransmitter can produce different consequences at different target neurons.

In the simplified classical motor model, direct-pathway striatal projection neurons predominantly express D1 dopamine receptors, whereas indirect-pathway projection neurons predominantly express D2 receptors. D1 receptor signalling generally favours the excitability and function of the direct-pathway population under suitable conditions; D2 receptor signalling tends to reduce the responsiveness of indirect-pathway populations. D1 receptors commonly couple to stimulatory G proteins that promote cyclic AMP signalling, while D2 receptors commonly couple to inhibitory G proteins that reduce it. These intracellular actions are more complex than a fixed assignment of excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials. Modulation changes how neurons integrate their inputs, and a change in a signalling molecule cannot be equated directly with a fixed number of action potentials.

The broad motor consequence of intact nigrostriatal dopamine in the standard model is a reduction of excessive restraint on selected actions. Direct-pathway facilitation can reduce output inhibition, and reduced recruitment of indirect-pathway restraint can also tend to reduce that output inhibition. This is an example of different cellular effects that converge on a similar circuit-level direction. A student who remembers only that dopamine is excitatory will incorrectly predict its effect on D2-expressing neurons. A student who remembers only that D2 signalling is inhibitory may incorrectly assume that the final consequence must be suppression of movement. The correct approach is to distinguish the immediate receptor action from the resulting changes in each downstream relay.

Parkinsonian movement abnormalities provide a biological illustration of the importance of nigrostriatal modulation. Loss of dopamine-producing neurons in substantia nigra pars compacta can reduce dopamine availability in the striatum. In the classical rate model, reduced D1-mediated facilitation weakens direct-pathway effects, and reduced D2-mediated restraint allows greater indirect-pathway influence. Both changes can favour excessive inhibitory basal ganglia output to particular thalamic targets, making voluntary movement initiation and scaling more difficult. Bradykinesia describes slowness of movement, and akinesia refers to marked difficulty in initiating or producing movement. Neither term is synonymous with loss of all muscle force.

Parkinsonian rigidity refers to increased resistance to passive movement and is not identical to the velocity-dependent spasticity often associated with upper motor pathway injury. Resting tremor can occur but cannot be explained adequately by a simple increase in the average firing rate of basal ganglia output neurons. Abnormal synchrony, temporal firing patterns and interactions among basal ganglia, thalamic and cortical circuits contribute to motor phenomena. Thus, the direct and indirect pathway model provides a useful starting point for understanding bradykinesia, but it should not be extended into a complete explanation of every sign.

In Huntington disease, degeneration of striatal neurons, including populations associated with the indirect pathway in early stages, can reduce the restraint of inappropriate movements. The resulting chorea illustrates the fact that excessive motor activity can arise when inhibitory control at particular levels is impaired. Following the classical chain, less inhibition of GPe may increase GPe activity, suppress the subthalamic nucleus and reduce excitatory support to GPi. The inhibitory output of GPi can consequently decrease. The disease affects multiple neural populations as it progresses; later motor abnormalities and nonmotor features cannot be inferred from selective loss of a single pathway. The example is useful for learning the circuit logic, not for reducing the disease to one synapse.

Consider a hypothetical comparison of two manipulations of the same motor channel. In preparation A, a researcher increases the activity of D1-expressing striatal projection neurons. In preparation B, the researcher increases the activity of D2-expressing indirect-pathway projection neurons independently of dopamine receptors. It is essential to distinguish activating a D2-expressing cell from activating its D2 receptor. The first increases that cell's firing by assumption; the second commonly reduces its responsiveness. Increasing the firing of indirect-pathway cells tends to increase inhibitory basal ganglia output in the simplified circuit, whereas stimulating inhibitory D2 receptor signalling can tend to produce the opposite net effect.

The selective circuit manipulations that underlie such reasoning are more precise than broad electrical stimulation. Cell-type-targeted activation or inhibition allows investigators to ask whether a particular population contributes causally to an aspect of behaviour. An informative design would compare matched control animals, measure the relevant neural responses and verify that the altered behaviour is not explained by a failure to detect the cue or maintain balance. Observing reduced movement after a manipulation may reflect impaired initiation, changed motivation or disrupted execution. More than one behavioural measure is needed to distinguish these possibilities.

The basal ganglia also participate in learning which actions are effective. If a movement leads to an unexpected beneficial outcome, subsequent selection of that action may change. Dopamine signals can contribute to plasticity at corticostriatal synapses, allowing past outcomes to influence future behaviour. This general principle connects motor selection with learning without requiring a detailed treatment of reinforcement-learning algorithms. The basal ganglia are not the only site of motor learning, and the activity of dopamine neurons is not reducible to a universal signal for reward or pleasure. The central point is that selection of action can depend on experience as well as the immediate sensory stimulus.

The ability to choose an action differs from the ability to perform that action smoothly and accurately. A chosen reach may be initiated at the appropriate time yet still overshoot its target. The basal ganglia can contribute to scaling and sequencing, but a different set of subcortical circuits is particularly important for correcting timing and coordination errors. Understanding the cerebellum requires a shift from the logic of action gating to the logic of prediction, adjustment and comparison of motor outcomes.

7. Cerebellar organisation: inputs, circuitry and motor output

The cerebellum lies posterior to the brainstem and is connected to it through three pairs of cerebellar peduncles. Its surface is folded into narrow folia, which increase the area of cerebellar cortex. Beneath that cortex lie white matter and deep cerebellar nuclei. Although the cerebellar cortex has a characteristic layered architecture across much of its surface, different regions receive different combinations of sensory and motor information and influence different targets. This permits related cellular mechanisms to participate in balance, ongoing limb coordination and the planning of skilled actions.

One useful functional classification distinguishes vestibulocerebellum, spinocerebellum and cerebrocerebellum. The vestibulocerebellum includes the flocculonodular lobe and is closely related to vestibular functions, balance and the coordination of eye movements with head movement. The spinocerebellum includes the vermis and intermediate zones and receives information about body movements, proprioception and descending motor activity. The cerebrocerebellum includes much of the lateral cerebellar hemispheres and interacts extensively with cerebral cortical areas through pontine and thalamic relays. It contributes to the preparation, timing and coordination of complex actions. These are functional groupings with overlapping influences, not independent organs.

The cerebellar peduncles provide major routes for information exchange. The middle cerebellar peduncle contains a very large contingent of fibres from pontine nuclei carrying information derived from the cerebral cortex. The inferior peduncle carries important inputs from the spinal cord, vestibular structures and inferior olive, among other pathways. The superior peduncle carries much of the output from the deep cerebellar nuclei to rostral brainstem structures and thalamus, although it also contains some afferent fibres. Students should learn the principal direction of information flow without treating every peduncle as exclusively input or output.

The deep cerebellar nuclei include the fastigial nucleus, interposed nuclei and dentate nucleus. Broadly, the fastigial nucleus is related to midline cerebellar regions, the interposed nuclei to intermediate regions and the dentate nucleus to lateral cerebellar regions. Output from these nuclei influences upper motor systems through brainstem and thalamocortical pathways. Some Purkinje cells, especially in vestibulocerebellar regions, project directly to vestibular nuclei, which in that context perform functions analogous to cerebellar output nuclei. This is an important exception to the simplified claim that every signal leaving cerebellar cortex must pass through a deep nucleus.

The cerebellar cortex has three main layers: an outer molecular layer, a Purkinje cell layer and an inner granular layer. Purkinje cells possess extensive dendritic trees extending into the molecular layer. Their axons provide the principal output from the cerebellar cortex and release GABA. Granule cells, in contrast, receive excitatory input within the granular layer and give rise to axons that ascend into the molecular layer, where they bifurcate into parallel fibres. Parallel fibres provide excitatory input to Purkinje cells and other cortical neurons. The arrangement allows many different combinations of incoming signals to influence Purkinje cell activity.

Two major classes of afferents reach the cerebellar cortex. Mossy fibres arise from numerous sources, including pathways carrying spinal, vestibular and pontine information. They excite granule cells through specialised synaptic arrangements and can send excitatory collaterals to deep nuclei. Climbing fibres originate in the inferior olivary complex and make powerful excitatory connections with Purkinje cells. A mature Purkinje cell typically receives one climbing fibre, which branches extensively on its dendrites, while it receives input from many parallel fibres. Climbing fibre activation can evoke a characteristic complex spike. The differing convergence patterns of the two inputs are important because they allow a distinction between widely distributed contextual information and strong signals associated with particular events.

Purkinje cells also produce simple spikes, whose rate depends on their intrinsic activity and integration of synaptic input. Local inhibitory interneurons, including basket and stellate cells in the molecular layer and Golgi cells in the granular layer, shape the processing of signals. A complete circuit contains both excitation and inhibition, so an increase in one afferent pathway cannot be translated into a unique output change without specifying the relevant synaptic routes and timing. For introductory examination questions, it is often appropriate to isolate the effect of an increased Purkinje cell firing rate on its deep nuclear target, while holding other inputs constant.

Deep nuclear neurons receive both excitatory afferent collaterals and inhibitory Purkinje cell signals. A reduction in Purkinje cell activity can release nuclear neurons from some inhibition, whereas increased Purkinje cell activity tends to suppress the same target if excitatory inputs are unchanged. Much cerebellar nuclear output to upper motor centres is excitatory, although inhibitory nuclear populations and projections also exist. The circuit therefore cannot be accurately described by saying simply that the cerebellum is inhibitory because its cortical output cells release GABA. The sign of the cortical synapse is one element of a larger input-output system.

The distinction between a sensory signal and a motor-related signal is particularly significant in the cerebellum. During a reaching movement, ascending proprioceptive signals convey information about the configuration and movement of the limb. Pontine inputs can convey information related to cortical motor activity. A signal related to the command can become available before the movement has produced its full sensory consequences. Access to both types of information permits a circuit to relate an intended or predicted state to evidence about the actual state. The cerebellum is not a camera watching the body, nor is it a separate motor cortex that directly excites every muscle; it contributes to the control of upper motor systems through its characteristic processing.

Cerebellar control commonly produces effects on the ipsilateral side of the body. For example, a lateral cerebellar hemisphere communicates through crossed cerebellothalamic projections with the opposite cerebral motor cortex, whose major lateral corticospinal output crosses again in the medulla. Two crossings can therefore link one cerebellar hemisphere to limb muscles on the same side of the body. Many proprioceptive pathways also convey information to cerebellar regions associated with the corresponding body side. This simple tracing rule helps explain why cerebellar limb coordination abnormalities are often ipsilateral, while remembering that midline cerebellar structures contribute strongly to axial and bilateral functions.

The cerebellar architecture allows a relatively small change in the weighting or timing of synaptic inputs to influence a complex sequence of actions. It can adjust upper motor neuron systems without replacing their role in selection and execution. To understand the functional significance of that circuitry, the next question is how an animal improves its movements when a familiar motor command produces an unfamiliar outcome.

8. Cerebellar prediction, timing and error-based adaptation

Suppose a person repeatedly reaches for a target while an experimental device displaces the visual feedback of the moving hand. On the first trials the person may direct the hand to an incorrect location, because the previous relationship between the motor command and perceived trajectory no longer applies. Over subsequent trials, the direction of movement can gradually change so that performance improves. If the displacement is suddenly removed, the compensatory movement may persist briefly and produce an error in the opposite direction. This aftereffect provides evidence that some aspect of the sensorimotor relationship has been recalibrated, rather than every successful movement being generated solely by a correction after the error is observed.

The general term motor adaptation describes adjustments to a motor action in response to a changed relationship between commands and their consequences. Adaptation may occur alongside explicit strategies, changes in attention and longer-term skill learning. An improvement in endpoint accuracy does not establish which mechanism is responsible. The presence of aftereffects, the time course of improvement and the transfer of learning to other movements provide complementary information. The cerebellum is particularly important for many forms of error-based motor adaptation, though other neural structures also contribute to performance and learning.

A sensory prediction error is a difference between the expected sensory consequence of an action and the sensory consequence that is observed. It should be distinguished from a task error, which is a difference between the desired goal and the achieved outcome. These errors can coincide, but they need not. An individual might deliberately aim away from a target to compensate for a visual displacement and hit the target successfully, while still experiencing an unexpected discrepancy between the predicted and observed hand movement. The possibility that task success and sensory prediction error can be dissociated is useful in understanding why different types of learning may operate in parallel.

The concept of an internal forward model describes a neural computation that uses information about the current state and a motor command to predict a future state or sensory consequence. It is a conceptual model, not a miniature physical copy of the limb inside the brain. If sensory feedback is delayed, a prediction can provide useful information before direct measurement of the result becomes available. A prediction that is systematically inaccurate can be updated through experience. The cerebellum is widely implicated in predictive sensorimotor control, but its functions cannot be identified with one unique mathematical algorithm in all tasks.

An efference copy is an internal signal related to an outgoing motor command. It can be used by other circuits to anticipate the effects of self-generated movement. For example, the sensory consequences of a head movement initiated by the animal may be interpreted differently from those of an unexpected displacement caused by the environment. Access to motor-related information helps distinguish expected reafference, meaning sensory signals produced by the animal's own actions, from unexpected changes. The cerebellum receives information through pathways suitable for integrating motor-related and sensory signals, although the exact content of each neural signal must be established experimentally rather than inferred solely from its anatomical origin.

In the classical cerebellar learning framework, parallel fibre activity provides information about the context in which an action takes place, and climbing fibre signals can indicate unexpected outcomes or errors. Coincident patterns of parallel and climbing fibre activity can alter synaptic transmission at parallel fibre–Purkinje cell synapses. Long-term depression at these synapses is an important example of such plasticity. Reduced effectiveness of a subset of excitatory parallel fibre inputs changes Purkinje cell output and therefore can modify the response of downstream nuclear circuits. Other forms and sites of plasticity also exist. The evidence does not justify claiming that every cerebellar memory is stored exclusively as long-term depression at one synapse.

Climbing fibres can signal more than a simple scalar measure of error. Their activity may reflect task events, unexpected sensory outcomes, movement context and other variables, depending on the region and behavioural paradigm. For NET-level reasoning, the standard instructional distinction remains useful: mossy and parallel fibre pathways provide a distributed set of signals that can describe the context, while climbing fibre input can supply powerful information relevant to teaching or updating a response. The limitation of the distinction is essential, because neural activity is not assigned a universal meaning independently of the circuit and experiment.

Classical eyeblink conditioning provides an example of learning in a cerebellar-dependent motor system. A neutral cue can be presented before an air puff directed at the eye. Initially the puff evokes a protective blink, while the cue alone evokes little or no appropriately timed conditioned blink. With repeated pairing, a blink can develop in response to the cue before the puff arrives. Comparing paired training with explicitly unpaired cue and puff presentations helps distinguish associative learning from a general increase in responsiveness. The response is valuable not simply because a blink occurs, but because its timing becomes appropriate to the expected stimulus.

A suitable experimental analysis would record eyelid movement and the timing of each cue and puff. One could distinguish the original unconditioned response after the puff from the learned anticipatory response before it. If a cerebellar manipulation prevents acquisition of the anticipatory response while preserving the basic unconditioned blink and the ability to detect the cue, the result supports a role in learning the timed association. If an intervention is applied after learning and disrupts expression, the interpretation differs: the affected structure may be needed for performance, storage or retrieval. Carefully designed acquisition and expression tests help avoid equating loss of behaviour with loss of the memory itself.

Cerebellar dysfunction can lead to ataxia, a disturbance of coordination, and dysmetria, in which the extent of movement is poorly matched to the goal. A reach may overshoot or undershoot; rapid alternating movements may become irregular; an intention tremor may become more apparent as the limb approaches a target. These effects do not require loss of the final lower motor pathway. The person may be able to generate substantial force but apply it at inappropriate times or in inappropriate combinations. Hypotonia can accompany some cerebellar disorders, but it is not an invariant consequence of every cerebellar lesion.

One simple measure of reaching accuracy is the signed endpoint error. If the target is at 20 cm along a measurement axis and the hand reaches 23 cm, the signed error defined as actual minus target is +3 cm. If the hand reaches 17 cm, the error is −3 cm. Both movements have an absolute error of 3 cm, but they fail in opposite directions. Averaging signed errors across trials may conceal poor accuracy if overshoots and undershoots cancel. The mean absolute error or root mean square error can supplement the signed average. These measures capture different aspects of performance: bias describes a systematic directional deviation, while variability describes inconsistency among trials.

A cerebellar contribution is particularly evident when a motor task requires appropriate timing among muscles. In a fast single-joint movement, activation of an agonist can accelerate the limb and subsequent antagonist activity can help decelerate it. If braking occurs too late, the limb may overshoot even though its initial acceleration was adequate. If braking occurs too early, it may undershoot. The same net amount of muscle activity distributed differently in time can produce a different trajectory. This explains why measuring average muscle activation over an entire trial cannot substitute for a time-resolved analysis of muscle recruitment.

Motor prediction and feedback are also essential when the goal is to hold the body upright. An adjustment made during standing must account not only for the expected effect of a muscle contraction but also for information about body orientation in relation to gravity and the support surface. The cerebellum interacts closely with brainstem and vestibular systems in this task. Balance provides an especially clear example of how the nervous system integrates signals that are individually incomplete.

9. Vestibular, visual and proprioceptive integration

The nervous system cannot directly measure every aspect of the body's mechanical state with a single receptor. Proprioceptive signals provide information about muscle length, changes in length, tension and joint configuration. Visual signals describe features of the surrounding scene and changes in its image on the retina. Vestibular signals provide information related to head rotation and linear acceleration, including the effects of gravity. Each source has particular strengths, delays and ambiguities. Central integration is required to estimate the variables relevant to posture and movement.

The vestibular apparatus in the inner ear has semicircular canals associated with angular head motion and otolith organs associated with gravito-inertial acceleration. The semicircular canals are especially useful for detecting changes in head rotation, while the otolith organs respond to the combined effects of linear acceleration and gravity. A sustained head tilt changes the gravitational component acting on the otolith system. A linear acceleration without a tilt can also change the gravito-inertial vector. Consequently, the otolith signal alone does not always distinguish head tilt from translation. Information from semicircular canals, vision and other sources helps resolve this ambiguity. Detailed hair-cell transduction is treated with the sensory mechanisms of the subsequent subunit.

Vestibular afferents convey information to vestibular nuclei and cerebellar structures. Central vestibular circuits combine signals from the two sides of the head and from nonvestibular sources. The outputs can affect ocular motor nuclei, neck muscle circuits and spinal postural systems. These multiple output routes are necessary because the response to head movement has more than one objective. Maintaining visual fixation requires appropriate eye movement, whereas preventing a fall may require altered activation of trunk and limb muscles. The direction and magnitude of these responses depend on the movement and the current orientation of the body.

The vestibulo-ocular reflex provides a familiar illustration of sensorimotor compensation. When the head turns to one side while the eyes maintain fixation on a stationary distant target, the eyes move in the opposite direction relative to the head. This reduces retinal image motion. The reflex must adjust when the relationship between head motion and visual image motion changes, such as when optical conditions alter the required compensation. Cerebellar and vestibular circuitry contribute to this adjustment. Eye and head movements are coordinated, but their precise roles depend on the task: during an intentional gaze shift, the organism may want the eyes to move with rather than oppose an aspect of the head movement.

A proprioceptive signal usually describes a relationship within the body, such as the length of a muscle or movement of a joint, rather than directly specifying the body's orientation to the gravitational vertical. If a person stands on a rigid horizontal floor, ankle joint configuration can provide useful information about body sway. If the support surface rotates beneath the feet, the same ankle angle can arise from a different combination of body orientation and surface orientation. The central nervous system must therefore interpret proprioceptive information in relation to other cues. The proprioceptive reflexes studied in the preceding node supply important local information, but their contribution to whole-body balance depends on how central circuits combine and regulate those signals.

Visual information has similar limitations. When the surrounding visual scene moves, the observer may initially interpret some of that motion as movement of the self. A person standing near a large moving visual surface may show postural responses even when the support surface remains stationary. Visual motion is informative when the environment is stable but can become misleading when the scene moves independently of the body. Closing the eyes removes this source of information and often increases sway, particularly if other sensory cues are degraded. The magnitude of the effect is not universal because the reliability of the remaining sources and the mechanical demands of the task differ.

Sensory reweighting describes the adjustment of the relative influence of different sensory cues when their reliability or relevance changes. Suppose a stable floor supplies dependable information about ankle movement. The controller can use this signal together with vision and vestibular information. If the floor begins to tilt unpredictably, ankle-related signals become less reliable as an indicator of body orientation in space. Greater reliance on vestibular or visual information can improve the estimate of vertical orientation. Reweighting does not mean that proprioceptive receptors stop firing. It refers to a change in the contribution of their signals to the resulting central estimate or motor response.

One simplified quantitative representation combines three estimates of an orientation angle. Let θV be the estimate based on visual information, θP the estimate based on proprioception and θB the vestibular estimate. A model may define θ̂ = wVθV + wPθP + wBθB, with nonnegative dimensionless weights that sum to one. If the three signals indicate 2°, 6° and 1°, and the weights are 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25, the combined estimate is 0.50° + 3.00° + 0.25° = 3.75°. If the support becomes unreliable and the weights change to 0.35, 0.15 and 0.50, the estimate becomes 0.70° + 0.90° + 0.50° = 2.10°. The change in estimate reflects the changed weighting of the same inputs, not a necessary change in receptor activity.

This arithmetic is an illustrative teaching model. It assumes that the estimates represent the same underlying variable in compatible units, that the response can be approximated as linear within the range considered and that weights can be represented by fixed values during the chosen interval. Real postural control includes sensory delays, different coordinate frames, feedback loops and nonlinearity. A system may also use sensory signals differently for estimating position and estimating velocity. The equation is valuable for interpreting a simple experiment, but it should not be mistaken for a literal description of all central vestibular computation.

Peterka's 2002 human postural-control study provides evidence for context-dependent integration of sensory information. The investigators used controlled perturbations of the visual surroundings or support surface to evoke body sway and compared responses under different stimulus conditions, including participants with vestibular impairment. Changes in responses were analysed using a feedback model of posture. The results were consistent with adjustment of the relative contributions of sensory orientation cues as conditions changed. The inference depended on the measured response and the assumptions of the model; sensory weights were not directly measured as electrical currents inside an individual neuron. This is an important distinction between a measured behavioural variable and an inferred computational parameter.

A comparable teaching experiment could compare standing under four conditions: firm support with eyes open, firm support with eyes closed, compliant support with eyes open, and compliant support with eyes closed. A compliant surface can make ankle-related information less reliable for inferring orientation. If sway rises especially when both reliable visual information and stable support are absent, the pattern supports the importance of combining sensory sources. Such an experiment should also control foot position, duration, task instructions and surface properties. The result alone does not reveal the precise neural locus of the reweighting, and compliant support changes mechanics as well as sensory information.

The ability to estimate orientation has value only if appropriate muscles produce corrective forces. Central circuits use the sensory information to engage descending pathways, modulate reflexes and coordinate motor pools. The mechanical consequences of those forces can be understood with a simplified model of upright posture. That model also clarifies why excessive feedback gain or delayed corrections can make an apparently responsive system less stable.

10. The mechanics of posture and the control of balance

Standing is mechanically demanding because gravity exerts a downward force through the centre of mass. The centre of mass is the position at which the mass of a body can be treated as concentrated for certain calculations of translational motion and gravitational force in a uniform field. Its position changes when body segments move. The base of support is the area bounded by the parts of the body or other supports through which external support forces are applied. During two-footed standing it includes the region associated with the two feet and the space between their supporting contact regions.

A broad static rule is that the vertical projection of the centre of mass should lie within the base of support for unsupported stationary equilibrium on a horizontal surface. The rule does not fully describe dynamic balance. When the body has appreciable momentum, the projection may lie inside the support area even though current motion would carry it outside unless corrective action is taken. During walking, the projection may lie outside a currently supporting foot for part of the step, and the next foot placement provides a new base of support. Therefore, the position of the centre of mass is important but cannot be used alone to determine whether a moving body will remain balanced.

The centre of pressure is the location of the resultant vertical ground reaction force on the support surface under typical force-platform measurement conventions. It is not identical to the centre of mass. A standing person changes the distribution of forces beneath the feet by altering muscle activity and joint torque. The centre of pressure shifts in response. Its displacement relative to the centre of mass is associated with the horizontal acceleration of the body in simplified standing models. A force-platform record of the centre of pressure therefore contains information about corrective actions as well as about the movement of the body.

An inverted pendulum provides a useful first approximation for small forward and backward sways during quiet standing. The body is represented as a relatively rigid segment pivoting about the ankle. The centre of mass lies above the pivot, so a small lean creates a gravitational torque that tends to increase the lean rather than restore the body to vertical. This is unlike a hanging pendulum, whose centre of mass lies below the pivot and whose gravitational torque tends to restore equilibrium. Active muscle torque and passive mechanical properties are needed to oppose the destabilising gravitational torque of the standing body.

If the body's mass is m in kilograms, gravitational acceleration is g in metres per second squared, and the centre of mass lies a distance h in metres from the ankle pivot, then the magnitude of gravitational torque in this simple model is τg = mgh sin θ, where θ is the forward lean measured from vertical. Torque has units newton metres. With a chosen sign convention in which forward lean is positive, gravity acts in the direction of increasing θ. For sufficiently small angles expressed in radians, sin θ is approximately θ, giving τg ≈ mghθ. The approximation becomes less accurate as the angle increases and does not account for changes in body shape or multiple moving joints.

For an illustrative numerical example, take m = 60 kg, h = 0.90 m, g = 9.81 m/s² and θ = 5°. Since 5° × π/180 is approximately 0.08727 rad, sin 5° is approximately 0.08716. The gravitational torque is 60 × 9.81 × 0.90 × 0.08716, which is approximately 46.2 N m. Under the ideal static assumptions, an equal and opposite net torque is required to balance it. If the effective perpendicular moment arm of the relevant muscle-tendon force were 0.05 m, an isolated force of approximately 924 N would generate that torque. This is an equivalent-force calculation, not a measurement of the force of any individual muscle during human standing.

The fact that an equivalent muscle force can exceed body weight should not be surprising. A relatively short muscle moment arm can require a large force to produce the torque generated by an external force acting at a longer moment arm. Muscles usually act at short distances from joint centres compared with the distances of external loads. The nervous system regulates combinations of muscles, and the force required depends on body geometry and mechanical support. A common numerical trap is to multiply mass by distance without including gravitational acceleration or to substitute an angle in degrees directly into the small-angle formula.

A simplified rotational equation of motion is Iθ̈ = mgh sin θ − τc, where I is moment of inertia about the pivot in kg m², θ̈ is angular acceleration in rad/s², and τc is the total corrective torque expressed as a positive value opposing a forward lean. If τc equals the gravitational torque, angular acceleration is zero in this model. Zero acceleration does not guarantee zero angular velocity. A person already rotating forward may require a larger corrective torque for a time to slow the rotation and return to a desired posture. This distinction between force balance and motion is fundamental to understanding corrective movement.

One possible feedback law for small deviations is τc = Kpθ + Kdθ̇. The proportional gain Kp relates angular displacement to corrective torque and has units N m/rad. The derivative gain Kd relates angular velocity to torque and has units N m s/rad. For a forward lean of 0.05 rad and forward angular velocity of 0.10 rad/s, let Kp be 600 N m/rad and Kd be 80 N m s/rad. The model predicts a corrective torque of 30 + 8 = 38 N m. For the 60 kg and 0.90 m body considered above, the small-angle gravitational torque is 60 × 9.81 × 0.90 × 0.05 = 26.49 N m. The net torque is approximately −11.51 N m, so the angular acceleration tends to oppose the forward motion.

This teaching equation illustrates why both displacement and velocity can be useful for maintaining posture. If the body is already returning rapidly toward vertical, a large additional corrective torque may produce overshoot. Velocity-related feedback can help regulate this transition. In the delay-free linearised model, the effective stiffness opposing displacement is Kp − mgh, and positive effective stiffness together with positive damping favours stability. Real neural control has unavoidable delays. Very large feedback gains can produce oscillations when corrective actions arrive after the relevant state has changed. The model therefore predicts neither that larger gain is always better nor that low gain is always sufficient.

The single-pivot approximation works best for small sways in conditions where the ankle strategy predominates. Humans also use hip movements, knee adjustments, trunk motion, arm movements and changes in foot placement. On a narrow or rapidly moving support surface, the assumptions of a rigid single segment may fail. A person can also use a handrail, adding another region of support and changing the external forces. The pendulum model is useful because it exposes the basic instability caused by gravity, not because it reproduces every movement used in balance.

Co-contraction of antagonist muscles is one way of increasing the mechanical resistance of a joint to perturbations. If the flexor and extensor each increase their activity by amounts producing equal opposing torque, the net torque may remain approximately unchanged while joint stiffness increases. This can improve stability in some circumstances but also increases energy expenditure and may reduce the ease of producing rapid flexible movements. A motor system must therefore regulate stiffness according to the task. It is not optimal to activate every available muscle as strongly as possible whenever balance is challenged.

The mechanics of posture show why the nervous system must combine prediction, ongoing sensory measurement and coordinated muscle activity. A perturbation can alter position, velocity, support geometry or load, and different responses are useful for each. In the next section, these mechanical principles will be used to distinguish anticipatory responses that prepare for expected disturbances from reactive responses triggered by unexpected events.
