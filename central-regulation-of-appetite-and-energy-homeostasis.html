1. The physiological problem: why meals are intermittent

A mammal requires a continuous supply of usable energy even though food enters its digestive tract in separate episodes. The nervous system must coordinate the search for food, its consumption and the cessation of a meal with the changing availability of metabolic fuels. The challenge is not simply to maintain glucose concentration. A recently fed animal can have a normal blood glucose concentration and nevertheless begin eating when a learned food cue appears. An animal with depleted energy reserves can maintain its blood glucose by mobilising hepatic substrates and still show powerful motivation to seek food. These observations distinguish the regulation of circulating fuels from the regulation of behaviour and stored energy.

Energy balance describes the difference between metabolizable energy acquired and energy expended over a specified interval. Energy homeostasis describes the coordinated physiological processes that tend to constrain changes in energy stores. The latter is a regulatory concept rather than a mathematical identity. An animal can be in negative energy balance for several hours without a failure of homeostasis: mobilising stored triglycerides during fasting is a normal response. Over longer periods, persistent changes in intake and expenditure influence the quantity and composition of stored tissue. The brain does not measure all of these energy flows directly. It receives information about the availability of food, the state of the digestive organs, circulating hormones and the conditions under which tissues are using fuel.

A useful starting distinction is between hunger, appetite, satiation and satiety. Hunger denotes the internal motivation associated with obtaining food, while appetite encompasses the desire to eat, including learned preferences and external influences. Satiation describes processes that limit the amount consumed during a meal. Satiety refers to the inhibition of further eating between meals. These categories overlap because a meal-related signal can influence both meal termination and subsequent meal timing. Neither a self-reported sensation nor an observed feeding response in a laboratory animal is a direct measurement of all the underlying physiological processes.

The separation between behavioural and physiological variables is central to experimental interpretation. Suppose an animal stops approaching food after receiving an experimental compound. The compound might increase satiation, decrease motivation to approach food, impair movement or produce visceral discomfort. All four possibilities could decrease measured food intake. A sound experiment must distinguish between them before assigning a physiological function to the compound. Conversely, an intervention that changes heat production without changing meal size may still have a substantial effect on energy homeostasis. The chapter therefore treats food intake and energy expenditure as separate, interacting outputs rather than interchangeable measurements.

A useful way to understand regulation is to distinguish a stock from a flow. The energy held in triglyceride molecules within adipocytes is a stock, whereas the rate at which a meal delivers absorbed nutrients is a flow. The number of kilojoules in the stock can be large even when the immediate flow of nutrients from the intestine is zero. Conversely, after a meal the rate of glucose absorption may be high even in an individual whose adipose stores are relatively small. A signal that reliably conveys the second quantity need not convey the first. This is why a single measurement of blood glucose cannot serve as a universal gauge of the need to eat. The rate of hepatic glucose release, the demand for glucose in tissues and the action of insulin all contribute to that measurement. The nervous system must therefore combine evidence about several variables and must sometimes respond to their rates of change rather than their absolute values.

The term feedback has a particular meaning in this context. When a change in stored energy produces a signal which changes behaviour and expenditure in a direction that opposes the original change, a negative feedback loop is present. The decrease in adipose-derived leptin during energy deprivation can participate in such a loop: the fall in hormonal input alters the activity of relevant neural circuits, increases motivation to eat and can restrain expenditure. The resulting restoration of energy availability can ultimately increase the hormonal signal again. A meal, however, can be initiated before the stored energy decreases detectably. If an animal learns that a particular sound predicts the appearance of food, the sound may initiate approach behaviour in anticipation of a future nutrient supply. This is feedforward regulation. It reduces delays inherent in regulation by feedback alone. It is not a contradiction of homeostasis; it is an additional strategy for managing an uncertain environment.

Students often imagine a thermostat with an exact body-fat set point, a comparator and a single output. This analogy clarifies the direction of negative feedback but has limited explanatory power. A thermostat has a directly measured temperature, whereas stored chemical energy is represented in the brain by multiple imperfect signals. A mammal may conserve energy while defending its body temperature, or delay food acquisition while escaping a predator. Thus, the outputs of feeding circuits are constrained by competing physiological and behavioural requirements. The concept of a defended range is useful when discussing compensatory responses to deprivation, but it does not imply that every individual possesses an immutable numerical target or that body mass must return to exactly its initial value after every perturbation. Food accessibility, composition, environmental temperature, developmental history and sustained changes in activity can alter the conditions under which the feedback operates.

Consider the period between the last meal of the day and the next meal. Intestinal delivery of nutrients declines while hepatic metabolism maintains circulating glucose and adipose tissue can release fatty acids. Changes in endocrine signals occur at the same time as fluctuations in the sensory and circadian influences on the brain. Neither glucose nor adipose triglycerides must become completely exhausted for food seeking to begin. Indeed, a system that waited for complete depletion before initiating a search would be poorly suited to survival. Neural circuits can respond to a state of declining availability while reserves are still sufficient to support locomotion and other essential functions. When food is obtained, sensory detection provides rapid evidence of an opportunity; mechanical and chemical signals from the digestive tract then confirm ingestion; subsequent absorption and metabolism provide still later evidence of restoration. These successive observations explain why behavioural regulation involves several overlapping time scales rather than a single instantaneous correction.

A regulatory response can also be appropriate without being successful. An animal in an environment without accessible food may show increased searching and reduced nonessential expenditure yet remain in negative energy balance. In this case the direction of the physiological response is compatible with negative feedback, even though its behavioural output cannot restore the missing energy. This distinction is important in experimental animals maintained on restricted feeding schedules. A low body mass or continuing decline in stored energy does not by itself demonstrate that the central energy-sensing pathway has failed. It may demonstrate that the regulated animal cannot obtain the necessary input. To distinguish these possibilities the investigator must establish whether food is available, whether the animal can detect and consume it, and whether the measured endocrine response has the expected direction. The same logic explains why the absence of a feeding response during illness cannot automatically be interpreted as complete loss of hunger neurons.

2. Anatomical organisation of central energy sensing

The hypothalamus occupies a relatively small region at the base of the forebrain. It surrounds part of the third ventricle and communicates with the pituitary, limbic structures, brainstem and autonomic pathways. Within it are several anatomically defined nuclei, each containing diverse neuronal populations. A nucleus is a grouping of neuronal cell bodies in the central nervous system, not a single neuron or a single type of transmitter. The arcuate nucleus is situated near the ventral portion of the third ventricle and close to the median eminence. This position is relevant because the median eminence is a neurovascular interface where access to circulating molecules differs from that in many other brain regions. It does not mean that the entire hypothalamus lacks a blood–brain barrier.

The paraventricular nucleus, abbreviated PVN or PVH in different textbooks, contains neurons that influence endocrine secretion and autonomic pathways. Some populations express melanocortin receptors and receive signals from arcuate neurons. The lateral hypothalamic area contains heterogeneous neurons implicated in arousal, feeding, motivation and other behaviours. It includes populations producing orexin, also called hypocretin, and melanin-concentrating hormone. These peptides must not be confused with melanocortins simply because their names refer to pigmentation or related biological terminology. Other hypothalamic regions, including the dorsomedial and ventromedial nuclei, contribute to the coordination of autonomic responses and energy use.

The caudal brainstem is equally important. The nucleus of the solitary tract, commonly called the NTS, lies in the medulla and receives visceral sensory input. Many vagal afferents have their cell bodies in the nodose ganglia. Their peripheral endings respond to conditions in internal organs and their central processes terminate in brainstem circuits. Neurons in the NTS communicate with neighbouring brainstem regions and ascending pathways. The area postrema lies nearby and has comparatively permeable capillaries, permitting access to certain circulating chemical signals. Its special vascular properties should not be generalised to every medullary neuron.

The traditional description of a lateral hypothalamic hunger centre and a ventromedial satiety centre arose from lesion and stimulation experiments. These studies demonstrated that damaging selected regions could alter feeding, but they could not establish that the damaged region contained a single exclusive controller. A lesion destroys several cell types and may disrupt axons passing through an area. Changes in arousal, motor activity, endocrine function and expenditure may accompany changes in feeding. Modern cell-type-specific experiments refine rather than erase the historical observation that the hypothalamus is important. Their major advance is to identify particular populations and connections that contribute to a measurable response.

A distributed control network allows signals of different duration and origin to influence behaviour. A sensory cue may change neural activity within seconds, gastric distension develops during a meal, and adiposity-related endocrine information acts across longer intervals. These are overlapping timescales, not mutually exclusive categories. A major challenge in studying the system is to determine which signal reaches which cells under which physiological conditions.

A brain nucleus is a collection of neuronal cell bodies and associated cells, not a membrane-bound organelle of a single cell. The term is anatomical. Neurons within one nucleus can differ in transmitter identity, receptor expression, projection targets and physiological function. Consequently a drug delivered to a nucleus or a lesion of that nucleus does not necessarily isolate one molecularly defined circuit. This distinction explains a major transition in the history of appetite physiology: anatomically defined stimulation and lesion experiments identified regions of importance, whereas molecular markers later allowed investigators to examine smaller populations and their connections. Neither approach alone completely describes the network. Anatomical identification establishes where a population is located, while circuit tracing and physiological measurements help establish where its signals go and what the signals accomplish.

The arcuate nucleus is positioned close to the floor of the third ventricle and to the median eminence. This location makes it especially relevant to communication between the circulation and the brain. The median eminence has specialized vascular properties associated with neuroendocrine exchange, but the neighbouring arcuate nucleus must not be described as if all of its neurons were freely exposed to every circulating macromolecule. Endothelial barriers, local glial interfaces, transport processes and receptor expression influence which signals reach particular cells. A hormone concentration measured in peripheral plasma does not establish an identical concentration at a neuronal receptor. When comparing two experimental groups with similar circulating leptin, for example, differences in access to the central nervous system could contribute to differences in neural responses without requiring different plasma concentrations.

The paraventricular nucleus lies near the third ventricle and contains several populations of neurons with different outputs. Some contribute to endocrine control through signals to the pituitary system, while others project towards regions involved in autonomic regulation or feeding. When a melanocortin-responsive neuronal population changes its activity, the consequences depend on its projection and transmitter rather than on the name of the nucleus alone. An experiment that activates all cells in the paraventricular region might engage neuroendocrine and autonomic responses simultaneously. Selective activation of a melanocortin-responsive subset could instead reveal a more specific effect on intake. This difference is useful for examination questions that compare region-wide manipulations with receptor-specific or cell-type-specific manipulations.

The lateral hypothalamic area is similarly heterogeneous. Orexin-producing neurons are relevant to arousal and motivated behaviour; melanin-concentrating hormone-producing neurons and other populations participate in feeding and state regulation. These are examples of functional specialization, not exhaustive classifications of the region. The ability of an animal to eat requires wakefulness, movement, orientation towards food and coordination of ingestion. A lesion that interferes with these enabling functions can reduce measured intake without directly eliminating physiological sensing of an energy deficit. Conversely, increasing arousal can make a food-directed response more likely without proving that stored energy has declined. Distinguishing the control of behavioural opportunity from the control of metabolic demand prevents the oversimplification of the lateral hypothalamus as a single hunger switch.

The brainstem contains neural circuitry capable of organizing many components of ingestion and receiving visceral sensory information. The nucleus tractus solitarius, or NTS, receives input from visceral sensory neurons; neighbouring nuclei contribute to autonomic integration. In a simplified pathway, a sensory ending detects gastrointestinal distension, action potentials travel through an afferent fibre and the signal reaches brainstem synapses. The receiving neuron can then influence local reflex circuits or send information to higher regions, sometimes through intervening relays. A projection from the hypothalamus can also influence how a brainstem circuit responds to incoming sensory information. The anatomical distinction between ascending and descending pathways is therefore important: one conveys information towards higher brain regions and the other can alter the responsiveness of lower circuits.

Historical lesion results become more informative when the expected alternative explanations are made explicit. Suppose a rat with damage to a hypothalamic region stops eating. One hypothesis is loss of a population that promotes food acquisition. Another is that fibres connecting separate populations were damaged. A third is that the operation impaired sensory perception or movement. Measurements of water intake, exploration, ability to approach a food source and responses to other stimuli can help separate these explanations. Later, selective interference with defined neurons can test the first hypothesis with greater precision. Even then, a population may influence multiple behaviours, and an abrupt intervention may produce effects that differ from gradual physiological modulation. Therefore the most useful interpretation of classical lesion studies is that particular regions are important for integrated behaviour, not that those studies identified a unique anatomical site where hunger resides.

3. Arcuate neurons and the melanocortin pathway

The arcuate nucleus contains two much-studied populations with opposing average effects on feeding. One expresses agouti-related peptide (AgRP) and neuropeptide Y (NPY); many of these neurons also release gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). The second expresses pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), a precursor polypeptide from which several biologically active peptides are generated. Some POMC neurons also express cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART). These names identify molecular features of neurons; they do not imply that every member of a population has identical connections, receptor expression or electrophysiological responses.

In the melanocortin pathway, enzymatic processing of POMC produces alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), among other products. Peptide processing matters because synthesis of a precursor and secretion of a biologically active product are different events. The complement of processing enzymes in a cell determines which products accumulate. Thus, expression of POMC messenger RNA alone cannot establish the quantity of α-MSH released at a particular synapse.

α-MSH activates melanocortin-4 receptors (MC4R) on target neurons, including populations in the paraventricular hypothalamus. MC4R is a G-protein-coupled receptor whose canonical signalling includes stimulation of adenylyl cyclase through Gs and increased intracellular cyclic AMP. The relationship between this molecular signal and neuronal electrical activity depends on the target cell and its signalling machinery. Increased melanocortin signalling generally constrains feeding and participates in expenditure regulation. MC4R is not a receptor for leptin: leptin can alter the activity of cells upstream of melanocortin-responsive neurons.

AgRP opposes melanocortin signalling, acting as an antagonist and, in appropriate receptor assays, an inverse agonist. An antagonist limits the response to an agonist. An inverse agonist can also reduce constitutive receptor activity, meaning receptor signalling present even without added agonist. This distinction provides a molecular reason why an increase in AgRP and a reduction in α-MSH are not necessarily equivalent changes. Both can reduce melanocortin output, but the receptor state and the duration of peptide action can differ.

NPY and GABA introduce additional pathways. GABA can rapidly inhibit target neurons through inhibitory receptors; NPY acts through its own family of G-protein-coupled receptors. AgRP neurons therefore need not stimulate feeding exclusively by antagonising MC4R. An intervention that blocks one transmitter may alter the duration or magnitude of a response without eliminating it. This is a useful illustration of parallel signalling from a single neuronal population: cotransmitters can act on different targets and timescales.

A fasting animal commonly shows enhanced activity of AgRP neurons. The resulting pattern promotes food seeking and food intake, but the precise response depends on the animal's environment and the availability of food. The presence of a food cue can rapidly modify the activity of this population, even before nutrient absorption. Consequently, a recording showing reduced AgRP activity does not, by itself, establish that energy stores have been replenished. Neural activity is influenced by predicted as well as current physiological state.

The words orexigenic and anorexigenic describe tendencies to increase and reduce food intake, respectively. They do not indicate that every action of the corresponding neuron is directed exclusively towards intake. An orexigenic neuron can influence the willingness to search for food, modify the responses of a target circuit and affect hormone release. Its apparent effect will depend on the endpoint measured. The arcuate AgRP population provides a particularly instructive example because a single identified neuron can release chemically different transmitters. GABA is a small-molecule transmitter released at synapses, whereas NPY and AgRP are peptides processed from larger precursors. Their release, receptors and persistence of effect need not be identical. For this reason the activity of AgRP-expressing neurons is not equivalent to the concentration of extracellular AgRP peptide at all of their target synapses.

Neuropeptide production links gene expression to the physiological response. Translation of the POMC transcript produces a precursor polypeptide that enters the secretory pathway. Proteolytic cleavage by processing enzymes produces biologically active peptide products; the repertoire of products depends on the cell type and its complement of processing machinery. POMC expressed in a pituitary endocrine cell is therefore not necessarily processed in exactly the same manner as POMC expressed in a hypothalamic neuron. The melanocortin peptide relevant to the simplified appetite pathway is alpha-MSH. When studying a mutation that reduces a peptide-processing enzyme, one should consider whether the defect alters the production of active peptides despite preserved expression of the precursor gene. A measurement of POMC messenger RNA alone would not resolve that question.

At a target neuron, MC4R belongs to the family of seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors. In its canonical signalling pathway, activation by melanocortin agonists can increase signalling through Gs and adenylyl cyclase, raising intracellular cyclic AMP. This is a familiar second-messenger mechanism from cell signalling. The outcome in an intact neuron also depends on ion channels, other receptor inputs and the identity of the cell. Hence the biochemical statement that a receptor can stimulate cyclic AMP does not imply that the firing rate of every receptor-bearing neuron must change by the same amount under every condition. In an examination experiment based on a receptor-expressing cell line, cyclic AMP can serve as a readout of receptor activation. In an intact animal, feeding and energy expenditure are more distant readouts that reflect the combined influence of many intervening cells and tissues.

An antagonist and an inverse agonist can be distinguished using basal receptor activity. If a receptor has measurable spontaneous signalling in the absence of an added agonist, a neutral antagonist prevents an agonist from increasing that activity but need not reduce the basal signal itself. An inverse agonist can reduce the basal activity under appropriate assay conditions. AgRP can oppose the effects of melanocortin agonists at MC4R, and evidence from receptor assays also supports inverse agonism. The distinction matters when predicting the result of adding AgRP to receptor-expressing cells without exogenous alpha-MSH. Reduced signalling in that condition may be compatible with inverse agonism, provided endogenous agonists and other sources of cyclic AMP have been considered. One should not infer inverse agonism solely from the observation that AgRP reduces the effect of added alpha-MSH.

The inhibitory transmission mediated by GABA provides a pathway that does not require AgRP peptide to compete for melanocortin receptors. When an AgRP neuron releases GABA at an appropriate target synapse, opening of GABA-A receptor channels can reduce postsynaptic excitability under the usual mature-neuron chloride gradient. The exact effect of chloride conductance depends on the relationship between the chloride equilibrium potential, the resting membrane potential and the action-potential threshold; inhibition need not always take the form of a large hyperpolarization. NPY operates through its own G protein-coupled receptors and can modulate synaptic or cellular function on a different time scale. Consequently, an experiment deleting MC4R does not necessarily remove all feeding-related effects of activating AgRP neurons. Parallel transmitter actions provide a mechanistic basis for residual responses.

A distinction between upstream and downstream perturbations helps explain genetic predictions. Suppose a mutation prevents synthesis of a melanocortin agonist while leaving MC4R functional. Supplying an effective agonist to the relevant receptor may bypass the missing endogenous signal, assuming it reaches the receptor. If the receptor itself is absent, increasing agonist availability cannot restore signalling through that missing receptor. This reasoning resembles the ligand-receptor analysis used for leptin but operates at a different level of the circuit. In real animals, the feeding phenotype may not be fully rescued by manipulating one receptor because developmental adaptations, other POMC products and parallel neurotransmitters contribute to regulation. A successful rescue therefore supports the particular causal link that was restored; it does not establish that the pathway is the only regulator of appetite.

The pathway can be treated as a signed network for qualitative predictions. If a stimulus decreases activity in a population that normally inhibits a feeding-suppressing target, removing that inhibition tends to increase the target's activity. The direction of the overall effect is therefore opposite to what might be inferred by considering only the word inhibitory at the first synapse. In the arcuate system, reducing an inhibitory AgRP input can disinhibit a downstream population associated with limiting feeding. At the same time, increased melanocortin agonist signalling can favour related outputs by a separate mechanism. This simplified sign analysis is valuable for solving circuit questions, but the predicted output assumes the specified connection is functionally important under the experimental conditions and that other inputs are unchanged. It should not be confused with a quantitative model of firing frequencies or food intake.

The physiological significance of MC4R is supported by genetic studies. Targeted disruption of the mouse receptor produces an obesity phenotype, establishing that normal receptor function contributes to long-term energy regulation. However, body mass is an integrated outcome. To determine whether a particular increase in mass resulted from altered intake, altered expenditure or both, measurements must be made before and during the development of the phenotype. Comparing only adult body mass would omit information about the direction and time course of the underlying energy flows. The interpretation is especially important when a receptor is expressed in different neuronal populations that influence different physiological outputs. The gene-disruption experiment provides evidence of physiological importance, while cell-type-specific manipulations help localize the contribution of particular neural pathways.

4. Leptin and the distinction between a signal and its receptor

White adipose tissue stores triglycerides and also functions as an endocrine organ. Its adipocytes secrete leptin, a protein hormone whose circulating concentration generally relates to adiposity but also responds to nutritional state and other regulatory influences. During energy deprivation, circulating leptin can fall before there has been a proportionate reduction in fat mass. This makes leptin particularly useful as a signal of inadequate energy availability, rather than merely a static indicator of fat quantity.

The long signalling isoform of the leptin receptor belongs to the cytokine receptor family. Receptor activation engages the associated tyrosine kinase JAK2 and signalling pathways including STAT3. Phosphorylated STAT3 can influence gene expression. Other intracellular pathways contribute to changes in neuronal activity and metabolism. The receptor does not itself have an intrinsic tyrosine kinase domain comparable to that of the insulin receptor. This distinction is important when mapping a physiological outcome to the correct signalling mechanism.

In the simplified arcuate model, leptin tends to restrain AgRP neuronal activity and promote melanocortin-associated anorexigenic signalling. These changes reduce the drive to acquire food when the signal indicates energy sufficiency. Leptin also influences endocrine and autonomic responses through additional neural populations. During fasting, a decline in leptin contributes to a coordinated adjustment that favours energy conservation and food acquisition. These responses should not be interpreted as a guarantee of constant body mass under all environmental conditions.

Classical mouse genetics clarified the difference between production of a circulating signal and responsiveness to it. In parabiosis, two animals share a circulation after surgical joining. Douglas Coleman's experiments with genetically obese mice indicated that the ob/ob animal lacked an effective circulating factor, whereas the db/db animal was unable to respond normally to the factor. The direction of the response in the partner animal was critical: sharing a circulation with an animal that produced the signal but could not respond could strongly suppress feeding in a responsive partner. Later identification of the ob gene by Zhang and colleagues in 1994 established the molecular identity of leptin. Mutations in the leptin receptor explain the classical db phenotype.

The reasoning generalises to endocrine systems. Supplying a missing ligand can restore signalling if the responding machinery remains functional. Supplying more ligand cannot ordinarily restore the absent function of a receptor that has lost its capacity to transmit that signal. However, a partial loss of function can differ from a complete loss, and an endocrine intervention may act through additional tissues or pathways. The expected response must be derived from the actual defect, not simply from the phenotype of the animal.

In many individuals with high adiposity, leptin is not deficient. High hormone concentration can coexist with reduced responsiveness of portions of the system, commonly described as leptin resistance. This term covers several potential mechanisms rather than a single universally demonstrated molecular defect. It is incorrect to infer that every person with obesity has a mutation in LEP or LEPR, or that a measurement of plasma leptin alone reveals the sensitivity of all target neurons.

The relationship between adiposity and leptin concentration is best understood as an endocrine relationship rather than a direct weighing mechanism. The triglyceride content of adipose tissue cannot itself travel as a neural impulse. Adipocytes produce leptin, and the amount released into the circulation depends in part on the size and metabolic state of the adipose compartment. The hormone is transported through the circulation and can influence cells expressing an appropriate leptin receptor. In a fasting animal the amount of stored fat may have changed only modestly, but leptin concentration can already have fallen substantially. Thus the signal contains information not only about the long-term size of the energy reserve but also about the recent conditions under which it is being maintained. The fact that leptin often correlates with adipose mass does not make it a fixed conversion factor between plasma concentration and kilograms of body fat.

The structure of the long leptin receptor isoform illustrates how an extracellular signal is converted into a nuclear response. Unlike a receptor tyrosine kinase, the leptin receptor does not contain its own intrinsic tyrosine kinase catalytic domain. It associates with an intracellular Janus kinase, JAK2. Ligand-dependent activation of the receptor complex permits phosphorylation of intracellular residues that serve as docking sites for signalling proteins. STAT3 is recruited and phosphorylated, and activated STAT3 can participate in transcriptional regulation in the nucleus. The transcriptional response may influence the expression of proteins involved in subsequent signalling and neuronal function. Additional pathways, including pathways involving phosphoinositide signalling, can contribute to effects of leptin in certain neuronal contexts. The consequence is a combination of changes that cannot be described adequately by one transcription factor or a single membrane current.

Negative feedback at the receptor signalling level is distinct from negative feedback at the whole-body level. A cell exposed to a cytokine-like signal may increase expression of an intracellular inhibitor such as SOCS3, which can restrain aspects of the receptor-associated signalling pathway. At the whole-body level, a change in energy stores may alter leptin production and the resulting neural response may affect intake and expenditure. These feedback loops operate at different levels of organization. Increasing the strength of intracellular inhibitory feedback can reduce the neural response to the same extracellular hormone concentration. In a model experiment, measuring plasma leptin alone would not reveal such an alteration. A complementary readout, for example a leptin-induced increase in phosphorylated STAT3 in receptor-bearing neurons, can test whether a particular intracellular response remains detectable. This readout still samples only one branch of the receptor signalling system.

The classic mutant mice provide a natural experiment that distinguishes two steps in an endocrine feedback loop. In the obese or ob phenotype, the primary genetic defect affects the production of functional leptin. In the diabetic or db phenotype, the classic mutation affects leptin receptor function. Both mutations can produce a large increase in adiposity, but the circulating signals differ because the two defects occur at different positions in the regulatory pathway. When the receptor is defective, the excess adipose tissue may continue producing abundant leptin without generating the normal central response. When the ligand is missing, receptor-bearing tissues receive insufficient hormone even though the tissue producing the hormone may be abundant. Phenotypic resemblance between the two strains therefore does not demonstrate identical biochemical defects.

Parabiosis links the circulation of two animals, allowing some blood-borne factors to be exchanged. Its conceptual strength lies in the fact that the nervous systems of the two individuals are not directly joined into a common circuit, while circulating substances can pass between the partners. If a phenotype is changed in one animal by a factor produced by another, the result supports a circulating component of the regulatory system. In Coleman's experiments, pairing mutant and normal mice in different combinations provided evidence consistent with the absence of an inhibitory circulating factor in one mutant and the failure to respond to the factor in the other. The interpretation did not require that the hormone had already been chemically identified. Positional cloning of the ob gene and subsequent identification of the receptor supplied molecular evidence for the proposed model. The sequence illustrates the difference between inferring the existence of a factor from physiological experiments and establishing its molecular identity.

The very severe reduction of intake reported in some classical parabiosis combinations must not be converted into a general rule that high leptin concentrations alone always cause starvation. Parabiosis changes the physiological circumstances of both animals and entails surgical and other stresses. Later experiments have raised questions about how much the procedure contributes to the severity of the response. The durable lesson from the mutant combinations is the distinction between signal production and responsiveness, supported by later molecular evidence. The extreme outcome is a feature of particular experimental conditions, not a dose-response law that can be applied directly to ordinary physiology. This qualification is important when a question asks which conclusion is justified by historical results rather than which mechanistic explanation sounds plausible in retrospect.

A complementary experiment uses hormone replacement rather than shared circulation. If an animal lacks a functional circulating ligand but retains receptor signalling, supplying the ligand under conditions that permit access to its targets should restore at least some downstream responses. If the relevant receptor cannot function, administering more ligand cannot restore signalling through that receptor. The interpretation requires appropriate controls: the same vehicle, comparable treatment conditions, measurements before and after treatment, and evidence that the preparation contains biologically active hormone. A negative result could reflect a defective receptor, inadequate delivery, an inactive preparation or a failure at another point in the pathway. Thus even the apparently simple comparison between ob and db mice illustrates why a rescue experiment is informative only when its biological assumptions are tested.

High leptin in an individual with increased adiposity can coexist with continuing food intake and weight gain. The phrase leptin resistance refers broadly to a reduced physiological response to a given leptin exposure relative to a suitable comparison; it does not identify a single molecular lesion. Altered access to responsive neurons, changes in signalling efficiency and network-level adaptation are possible contributors. Some leptin actions may be retained even if another response is attenuated. A laboratory investigator should define the outcome of interest, such as acute intake suppression or a biochemical response, and specify the dose and physiological state. A large circulating concentration is compatible with reduced sensitivity, but it is insufficient on its own to diagnose the mechanism. The contrast between common high-adiposity states and rare complete loss of functional leptin helps prevent a mistaken assumption that every obesity phenotype represents hormone deficiency.

A fall in leptin during sustained energy deprivation can influence functions outside the immediate control of meal size. The endocrine systems involved in growth, reproduction and thyroid function are energetically important, and the availability of fuel is relevant to their regulation. Hypothalamic neurons integrate this information with other inputs, so the reproductive or thyroid consequences of deprivation cannot be attributed to leptin alone. In an experiment restoring leptin to a food-restricted animal, some neuroendocrine changes may be modified even though the animal remains in negative energy balance. This dissociation is useful because it demonstrates that a signal associated with the energy state can contribute causally to particular adaptations without being identical to the energy state itself. The direction of the response and the extent of rescue depend on the physiological conditions and endpoint selected.

5. Insulin and ghrelin: distinct information from pancreas and stomach

Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to nutrient-related stimulation. Its peripheral actions coordinate uptake and storage of nutrients, while insulin signalling in the nervous system can influence feeding and the autonomic regulation of metabolism. The receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, and its downstream pathways include insulin receptor substrates and phosphoinositide 3-kinase signalling. Central and peripheral actions must be distinguished experimentally because changing circulating insulin also changes glucose and other metabolic variables.

In contrast to a stable long-term measure of adiposity, insulin concentration can change substantially during a meal. Basal insulin levels also reflect insulin sensitivity and nutritional state. It can therefore convey overlapping information about recent food intake and metabolic status. A high concentration does not necessarily mean that every tissue is responding strongly to insulin. The distinction between signal concentration and responsiveness, introduced for leptin, applies here as well.

Ghrelin is produced predominantly by endocrine cells of the stomach. Its acylated form activates the growth hormone secretagogue receptor, GHSR1a. Acylation by ghrelin O-acyltransferase is essential for this canonical receptor activation. Plasma ghrelin commonly increases before meals and declines after eating, although the pattern is influenced by meal composition, timing, metabolic state and individual variation. Ghrelin can stimulate AgRP-associated pathways and promote feeding. Its name reflects its discovery in relation to growth hormone release, not a restriction to pituitary regulation.

A pre-meal rise in a hormone may be a contributing cause of meal initiation, a response to an anticipated meal, or both. Measuring the hormone before a meal does not distinguish these possibilities. To test causality, investigators may change receptor signalling while controlling meal availability, or assess whether the hormonal response persists when scheduled feeding cues are altered. Ghrelin's action on feeding should not be described as the only mechanism of hunger. Food seeking can occur when ghrelin signalling is reduced because other neural, metabolic and sensory pathways remain.

The three signals can be contrasted without assigning each a rigid time category. Leptin often provides information about stored energy and recent energy deprivation; insulin responds strongly to nutrient exposure and metabolic status; ghrelin can provide information associated with anticipation of a meal and fasting. All are interpreted by cells within a distributed network. Their combined effect depends on receptor availability, access to the brain, prior state and the concurrent inputs arriving at those cells.

Insulin and leptin are both endocrine signals of peripheral origin that influence central circuits, but their receptor mechanisms provide an instructive contrast. The insulin receptor possesses intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. Binding of insulin promotes receptor phosphorylation and recruitment of intracellular adaptor proteins including insulin receptor substrates. These proteins can recruit phosphoinositide 3-kinase, which contributes to the activation of Akt. The pathway has diverse downstream effects in different tissues. In a neuron, it can participate in regulation of excitability and transcription rather than merely stimulating the movement of a glucose transporter to the membrane as taught for skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. It is therefore essential to distinguish the general molecular pathway from a tissue-specific physiological output. A student should not infer that every central action of insulin is equivalent to its peripheral metabolic action.

The route of administration is especially important when interpreting insulin experiments. An intervention that increases circulating insulin can lower blood glucose by altering metabolism in peripheral tissues. A sufficiently large fall in glucose may activate counterregulatory responses and increase motivation to obtain food, even though insulin can participate in feeding-suppressing signalling in central circuits under other circumstances. These observations do not require insulin to have contradictory actions at the same receptor. They arise because an intervention changes several variables in an integrated organism. An experiment designed to investigate a central effect can attempt to control blood glucose and systemic insulin exposure or use a central delivery method with suitable controls. Even central delivery does not prove that insulin acts exclusively at one neuronal population. Receptor-specific manipulations and measurement of peripheral variables can provide additional separation of the possible routes.

Insulin secretion after a meal is influenced by the amount and type of nutrient entering the circulation, as well as incretin signalling and other regulatory factors. In many physiological conditions insulin also carries information related to the longer-term nutritional state, although its concentration can vary substantially across meals and with insulin sensitivity. Two animals with the same amount of adipose tissue can have different plasma insulin concentrations if their recent nutrient exposures differ or if their tissues differ in sensitivity to the hormone. This makes insulin complementary to, rather than interchangeable with, leptin. In a simplified feeding experiment, the plasma concentration of either hormone should be interpreted in relation to the time since the previous meal and the metabolic state of the animal. Without such information a difference between groups may reflect the sampling schedule rather than a fundamental change in the central feedback system.

Ghrelin offers an example of post-translational modification determining the effectiveness of an endocrine signal. Its precursor is processed to produce the mature peptide, and ghrelin O-acyltransferase can attach a fatty acyl group to a specific serine residue. Acylated ghrelin is the canonical endogenous agonist of the growth hormone secretagogue receptor, GHSR1a. The receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor, and signalling in an appropriate cellular context can involve phospholipase C, inositol phosphate production and calcium-related mechanisms. The receptor is named for its role in growth hormone secretion, but its expression and actions are not confined to pituitary regulation. The capacity of circulating ghrelin to influence feeding circuits links a signal from the gastrointestinal system to hypothalamic activity before ingestion. Des-acyl ghrelin should not simply be described as a second equivalent agonist of GHSR1a; it does not activate the canonical receptor in the same manner.

An assay that measures total ghrelin may not distinguish the acylated hormone from forms lacking the acyl modification. Suppose two hypothetical plasma samples both contain 100 arbitrary concentration units of total ghrelin. In sample A, 20 units represent acylated hormone, whereas in sample B, 50 units do so. If the assay sums both forms, it will report identical values. Nevertheless, the amount of canonical receptor agonist is greater in sample B. The actual neuronal response would also depend on receptor access and sensitivity, so a two-and-a-half-fold difference in the active fraction is not equivalent to a two-and-a-half-fold difference in food intake. This example illustrates why measuring the chemically relevant form of a hormone is a prerequisite for a mechanistic interpretation. The numerical values are chosen to illustrate assay logic, not to represent measured mammalian concentrations.

The preprandial increase of ghrelin has a different logical significance from the rise of a hormone during nutrient absorption. A preprandial increase is compatible with a role in anticipation or initiation of a meal, whereas a postprandial increase is compatible with a response to ingestion. Neither temporal sequence alone proves a causal role. If meals are regularly available at a predictable time, internal timing mechanisms and learned cues can influence endocrine secretion as well as behavioural preparation. To test whether ghrelin contributes to meal initiation, it is useful to compare controlled ghrelin administration or disruption of receptor function with matched controls, while measuring latency to eat and the amount consumed. A manipulation might change the latency without substantially changing daily intake if compensatory changes occur later. This is one reason to record multiple feeding variables rather than a single food-consumption value.

The effects of fasting on these three signals can be used to reason through the direction of neural regulation. Under typical fasting conditions, the decline in leptin and insulin input removes some feeding-inhibitory influence, and ghrelin may provide a feeding-promoting signal. The convergence can favour activation of appetite-promoting pathways. It is not necessary for each hormone to change by the same percentage or on the same time scale. Moreover, an animal with a receptor defect may respond abnormally to one signal while retaining responses to others. If ghrelin increases during fasting but its receptor is experimentally absent, fasting may still produce feeding-related changes through reduced leptin or insulin input, neural nutrient sensing and learned influences. The persistence of a response under such conditions is not evidence that ghrelin normally has no function; it indicates that the system has more than one route capable of influencing the output.

6. Gastrointestinal signals and meal termination

During ingestion, the stomach expands and its contents begin to enter the small intestine. Neural receptors and enteroendocrine cells detect mechanical and chemical changes. Afferent signals report these changes to central circuits, permitting regulation before all nutrients are absorbed. This early feedback is essential because waiting for complete absorption would delay information about meal size.

Cholecystokinin (CCK) is released by intestinal I cells in response to nutrients, particularly products of fat and protein digestion. It participates in digestive coordination and can reduce meal size through mechanisms involving CCK1 receptors on vagal afferents. The pathway illustrates how a hormone released in one organ can influence a distant neural circuit indirectly: the hormone acts locally on a nerve ending whose axon transmits information to the brainstem. An experiment that interrupts vagal signalling may attenuate some effects of CCK, but the result must be interpreted in relation to the type and completeness of the interruption.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is released by intestinal enteroendocrine L cells in response to nutrient exposure. It supports glucose-dependent insulin secretion and also participates in regulation of gastric emptying and food intake. There are central GLP-1-producing neurons as well, so the location of hormone synthesis and the location of receptor activation must not be conflated. Endogenous intestinal GLP-1 is rapidly degraded in circulation; the effects of pharmacologically prolonged receptor activation cannot automatically be used to infer the normal reach or duration of endogenous signals.

Peptide YY (PYY) is another nutrient-responsive product of enteroendocrine cells, including L cells. Its processed form PYY3–36 can activate Y2 receptors and influence feeding-related pathways. The receptor subtype matters: members of the NPY receptor family can have different locations and consequences. A peptide belonging to the same broad signalling family as NPY does not necessarily increase intake in the same physiological setting.

Meal-related signals usually combine with mechanical feedback rather than replacing it. A stomach containing a large volume of nonnutritive material can activate distension-sensitive pathways without producing the same endocrine response as a nutrient-rich meal. A small-volume energy-dense meal may create a different combination of endocrine feedback and gastric pressure. Thus, both volume and nutrient content are potential explanatory variables in experiments comparing feeding responses. A meaningful comparison requires explicit control of whichever variable is not the primary object of study.

The terms satiation and satiety help organise these mechanisms. Reduced meal size following a nutrient-related signal suggests a change in satiation if the animal begins eating normally but terminates sooner. A longer interval before the next meal suggests enhanced post-meal satiety. Both observations can occur together, and neither alone demonstrates a persistent reduction in total energy intake because later compensatory changes may occur.

Enteroendocrine cells are specialized epithelial cells dispersed through the intestinal lining. They provide a bridge between conditions within the lumen and signals that can influence other organs. The luminal contents of the intestine are not in direct contact with most brain neurons. A nutrient therefore has to be detected and its presence communicated, either by a change in the activity of a sensory nerve, secretion of a chemical messenger, transport of the nutrient into the circulation or a combination of these routes. Nutrient composition matters because digestive products interact with distinct sensing mechanisms. A meal rich in lipid and protein can engage hormonal and mechanical pathways in a pattern different from that of an equal mass of water or dilute carbohydrate solution. Measuring meal volume alone cannot capture these differences. This is the reason why gastrointestinal sensing complements the central detection of longer-term energy status.

CCK provides an example of a signal that coordinates digestion with feeding behaviour. Nutrient-related release of CCK can contribute to gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme secretion, helping the gastrointestinal tract process incoming food. In addition, activation of CCK1 receptors on relevant vagal afferent pathways can contribute to signalling that limits the size of an ongoing meal. These functions are physiologically compatible: the presence of digestible nutrients requires digestive responses and also provides evidence that further rapid ingestion may be unnecessary. CCK is therefore not simply an appetite-suppressing substance unrelated to digestion. Its role depends on the tissue expressing the receptor and on the time at which the signal is released. A reduction in food intake following CCK administration can reflect physiological meal-limiting pathways at suitable exposures; at inappropriate doses, unpleasant visceral effects may contribute and should be considered.

The duration of a feeding experiment determines what can be concluded about CCK and related meal signals. An animal given a meal-limiting stimulus may terminate its first meal earlier, but it could compensate by consuming another meal sooner. The reduction in first-meal size would be a measurable effect on satiation, while the change in total daily energy intake might be smaller or absent. To distinguish the possibilities, an investigator needs the timing and size of successive meals, not just their combined mass. Conversely, a treatment that prolongs the time until the next meal can affect satiety without substantially altering the amount consumed in the meal that preceded it. A signal may affect both variables in certain conditions. The classification depends on the observations, not solely on the name of the hormone being studied.

GLP-1 has several actions that connect nutrient exposure with metabolic regulation. Incretin action refers to the enhancement of glucose-dependent insulin secretion following nutrient delivery through the gastrointestinal tract. GLP-1 participates in this effect through receptors on pancreatic beta cells and also influences gastrointestinal motility and feeding-related signalling. Endogenous GLP-1 is subject to enzymatic degradation, including cleavage by dipeptidyl peptidase-4, which limits the persistence of intact circulating peptide. This creates an important distinction between a brief physiological signal released from the gut and a pharmacological receptor agonist designed to remain active for much longer. A prolonged effect of such an agonist cannot automatically be taken as a measure of the duration or anatomical route of natural intestinal GLP-1 action. The next node develops its pancreatic and hepatic significance in greater detail.

The brain also contains neurons capable of producing GLP-1-related peptides through processing of proglucagon. These neurons are relevant to understanding the distinction between peripheral and central signalling systems. An intestinal L cell and a neuron in the caudal brainstem can produce a related peptide while participating in different patterns of communication. Blocking a GLP-1 receptor in a particular brain region may alter a response without revealing whether the active ligand originated from the intestine or from a central neuron. The answer requires experiments that distinguish the sources of the ligand or the routes by which the relevant signal reaches the target. An observation that a receptor is present in the brain is not proof that a circulating gut peptide reaches that receptor in effective amounts during an ordinary meal. Neural relays and accessible brain regions may contribute without requiring unrestricted entry throughout the brain.

PYY illustrates how peptide processing can change receptor preference. A form of the peptide released after nutrient ingestion can be cleaved to produce PYY3-36, which has a preference for the Y2 receptor among the neuropeptide Y receptor family. In relevant neural circuits, Y2 receptor activation can restrain NPY-related signalling and contribute to feeding inhibition. The presence of the shared letter Y in PYY and NPY does not imply identical physiological functions. Different ligands can engage different receptor subtypes and different neural populations even when they belong to related peptide families. A question involving receptor-specific antagonism should therefore be solved by tracing the actual receptor and the sign of its action. If an effect of PYY3-36 depends on Y2 receptors under the stated experimental conditions, blocking those receptors is expected to weaken that component of the response, not necessarily all of the feeding effects of a mixed meal.

Mechanical distension and chemical nutrient sensing may convey different information about a meal. A fixed volume of an inert material can increase gastric stretch and evoke sensory responses without delivering metabolizable energy. A smaller volume of a nutrient-dense substance may provide more chemical energy with less distension. In a hypothetical experiment, two treatments can be matched for volume but differ in nutrient composition; a larger response to the nutrient treatment would then support an additional chemical or post-ingestive influence. The design is stronger if osmolarity, texture, temperature and the rate of gastric emptying are considered, since these variables can also affect gastrointestinal responses. Conversely, matching the total energy of two meals does not ensure matching distension. The experimental distinction between volume and energy is central to understanding why the physiological system requires more than one type of gastrointestinal input.

The rate of gastric emptying influences the timing of nutrient delivery to the intestine. A signal that slows the passage of stomach contents can prolong gastric distension and modify the time course of intestinal stimulation. Thus a treatment that reduces food intake may act partly by changing the conditions under which mechanical and hormonal signals are generated rather than by sending a direct command to stop eating. The same principle applies when interpreting a hormone's effect on blood glucose after a meal: slower nutrient delivery can alter glucose appearance independently of a direct effect on a metabolic enzyme. In appetite experiments it is helpful to distinguish a change in the generation of a gastrointestinal signal from a change in the central interpretation of that signal. Both can produce a smaller meal, but they represent different sites of action and require different evidence.

A single meal generally activates overlapping pathways. Imagine that a receptor antagonist eliminates the response to one isolated intestinal peptide, while leaving mechanical afferent signalling intact. The effect of that antagonist on intake of a complete meal may be much smaller than its effect on intake after administration of the isolated peptide. Other nutrients and signals can continue contributing to meal termination. This result demonstrates redundancy in the physiological system, not necessarily a failure of the antagonist. A complementary experiment that examines combinations of perturbations could establish whether the signals act independently, interact or converge on a shared pathway. Such a study must interpret combined effects with care because feeding has a finite range: once a meal is very small, there is little room for an additional reduction. A non-additive response does not automatically demonstrate that two hormones act on the same receptor.

7. The vagus, NTS and ascending communication

The vagus nerve carries sensory and motor fibres. Afferent fibres transmit information towards the central nervous system; efferent fibres transmit commands towards peripheral targets. Direction is defined by the relationship to the CNS, not by whether a fibre is anatomically above or below an organ. The sensory component provides information about gastric distension, intestinal nutrient-related conditions and selected chemical signals.

Vagal afferent endings convert local stimuli into altered patterns of action potentials. Their central processes communicate with neurons in the nucleus of the solitary tract. Glutamatergic transmission is important at many of these sensory synapses. Brainstem processing can influence local autonomic reflexes, feeding movements and ascending signals to forebrain regions. The NTS is therefore neither merely a passive relay nor a separate controller operating independently of the hypothalamus.

Afferent activity is interpreted in context. For example, gastric expansion may be expected during a meal, while unusual visceral signals may produce avoidance or discomfort. Both situations can decrease food consumption. Circuit studies therefore need behavioural measurements beyond food weight alone. Latency to initiate eating, meal duration, movement and avoidance of a previously associated flavour are informative complementary variables. The goal is not to assign every reduction of intake to a normal satiety signal.

Some routes of communication use the bloodstream rather than a vagal synapse. Circulating compounds may act at specialised neurovascular regions or reach receptors by other routes. Distinguishing a direct central action from a vagal-mediated one requires appropriately designed interventions. Systemic administration demonstrates a whole-organism effect but does not identify the receptor-bearing cell or the communication route. A comparison of systemic and local interventions can narrow the possibilities, especially if peripheral changes in glucose, gastric emptying or movement are also measured.

The relationship between hindbrain and hypothalamic circuits is reciprocal. Information about recent nutrient exposure can alter forebrain responses, while descending signals can modify autonomic output and the interpretation of incoming sensory information. This organisation enables feeding behaviour to adapt to both immediate meal conditions and the longer-term state of energy reserves.

A visceral afferent neuron is a sensory neuron whose peripheral ending detects conditions in an internal organ. Many vagal sensory neurons have their cell bodies in the nodose ganglion and send central processes towards the NTS. The fact that the ganglion contains cell bodies does not mean that every signal must cross a conventional synapse within that ganglion. The peripheral and central processes belong to the same primary sensory neuron. A stimulus at an ending can change electrical activity that is conveyed along its processes to a central synapse. The NTS neuron receiving this input is part of a subsequent stage of integration. Keeping the cell bodies, peripheral endings and central synapses in their correct locations helps to interpret diagrams in which a line connecting the gut and brain may otherwise be mistaken for a single endocrine pathway.

A mechanosensory ending responds to the physical conditions of the tissue in which it is embedded. Gastric distension can therefore affect vagal sensory discharge without requiring a peptide first to reach the circulation. Chemical signalling can interact with this mechanical input. A gastrointestinal hormone may act on a receptor expressed by a sensory neuron and change its responsiveness to a mechanical stimulus, or it may influence the motility of the organ and thereby change the stimulus itself. The observed neural response can consequently depend on both the local hormone exposure and the mechanical state of the organ. To investigate an interaction, an experimental design could independently vary distension and hormone exposure while measuring afferent activity. A change in the slope of the response to distension would suggest modulation of sensitivity rather than simply the addition of an identical number of impulses at every stimulus intensity.

Vagal efferent fibres transmit motor and regulatory signals from the central nervous system towards visceral targets. A manipulation of the entire vagus nerve can affect both directions of signalling, making causal interpretation difficult. If such a manipulation reduces the meal-limiting effect of an intestinal stimulus, the result may be consistent with interruption of sensory information, but altered gastrointestinal motility or other efferent consequences could also have changed the stimulus. Comparing selective sensory interference with broader nerve interruption can help separate the alternatives. In addition, the completeness of the interference must be established before a residual response is attributed to an alternative pathway. A reduction, rather than abolition, of the response may indicate partial interruption or parallel signalling. These considerations are especially important when a question uses the words necessary and sufficient.

The area postrema, a brainstem structure with specialized vascular accessibility, provides another example of how peripheral chemical information can influence neural networks. It is anatomically close to the NTS but should not be treated as the same nucleus. Circulating substances can influence accessible sensory structures, which can then communicate with other neurons by synaptic pathways. Some visceral chemical signals are associated with protective responses to potentially harmful ingested material. A manipulation that strongly activates these circuits can suppress feeding because ingestion is temporarily inappropriate, not because energy stores have been restored. An intake measurement should therefore be accompanied by observations of general behaviour and, where appropriate, indices of aversive responses. Appetite regulation and the avoidance of noxious stimuli can converge on the same behavioural endpoint through different mechanisms.

A brainstem circuit can also produce a rapid behavioural or autonomic response without waiting for a slow change in an adipose-derived hormone. During a meal, gastric stretch and intestinal stimulation can occur within the time required to ingest food, whereas changes in adipose energy reserves are comparatively gradual. Descending signals from hypothalamic regions can modify the response to this sensory input according to the animal's current state. It follows that an identical degree of gastric distension need not have an identical effect in a food-deprived and a recently fed animal. This is an example of state-dependent gain: the magnitude of the output produced by a given sensory input depends on the conditions of the receiving network. It does not require a change in the physical size of the stomach. Experimentally, one can vary the nutritional state while maintaining a comparable distension stimulus and examine whether the behavioural or neural response differs.

8. Homeostatic regulation, reward and anticipation

Food has sensory properties that can influence its acquisition independently of an immediate physiological deficit. The term hedonic regulation refers to the contributions of reward-related and motivational processes, rather than an alternative system wholly disconnected from homeostatic control. Hunger changes how attractive food cues are, and the cues themselves can influence circuits that respond to nutritional state.

Dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area and their projections to the nucleus accumbens participate in learning, motivation and the evaluation of rewarding stimuli. Dopamine signalling should not be equated directly with the subjective pleasure of eating. An animal may work to acquire a food without displaying a proportionately increased hedonic reaction to its taste. These behavioural variables measure different components of reward, and experimentally manipulating dopamine can affect learning and action initiation as well as feeding.

Learned associations are particularly important when food availability follows a regular pattern. A cue that previously predicted food can evoke anticipatory behaviour. Chen and colleagues reported in 2015 that food-associated sensory information rapidly changed activity in arcuate feeding circuits in mice before food consumption. This demonstrates that neural responses associated with energy deficit can be modified by the prospect of obtaining food. It does not demonstrate immediate restoration of energy stores by seeing food. The distinction between predictive and restorative signals is therefore essential.

One can design a teaching experiment in which a fasting mouse is presented with visible food behind a barrier, then the barrier is removed in a second trial. If neuronal activity changes when food becomes visible, before ingestion, the early response cannot be attributed to absorption of the newly presented nutrients. If the activity returns towards its fasting pattern when the cue is removed and no meal occurs, the pattern supports the interpretation that access to food was predicted rather than achieved. Control conditions should account for handling, unrelated sensory stimulation and the animal's prior experience.

The homeostatic and reward-related contributions to feeding converge on behaviour, but one should not divide a measured meal into a fixed percentage of biological hunger and a fixed percentage of reward. The relative contributions change with context. Sensory properties, learning, arousal and physiological state influence the same organism at the same time.

Homeostatic and hedonic regulation are sometimes represented as separate systems competing for control over eating. This is a useful introductory model if it is understood as a distinction between kinds of information. Homeostatic signals provide evidence of physiological need, while reward-related processes assign motivational value to objects and actions in the environment. The same food can acquire greater motivational significance when the animal is deprived, and its value can decline after a meal. In that case a change in physiological state modifies reward-related behaviour without requiring a completely separate command to eat. Conversely, the accessibility and learned value of a food can influence the probability of ingestion even when the animal has no large energy deficit. The two categories describe interacting processes, not mutually exclusive anatomical compartments.

Reward is not a synonym for pleasure. In behavioural neuroscience, the willingness to perform an action to obtain a stimulus, the pleasurable responses to its consumption and the learning that associates the stimulus with preceding cues are distinguishable aspects of reward. Dopaminergic signalling in mesolimbic pathways contributes importantly to motivation and learning, but the observation that a dopaminergic neuron changes activity during food presentation does not by itself measure subjective pleasure. A rodent trained to perform an increasing number of responses to obtain a food item can reveal a change in willingness to work for it. That measurement must still be interpreted in relation to motor ability, arousal and prior learning. A treatment that reduces the number of responses may have affected any of these variables. It is not sufficient simply to label the treatment as having reduced the rewarding nature of food.

Sensory cues provide information about future events before nutrients have entered the body. Chen and colleagues recorded activity associated with identified arcuate neuronal populations in awake mice and found rapid responses to food presentation. The timing of the response is important because it precedes the slower consequences of digestion and absorption. This observation supports a role for sensory information in modulating the activity of these neurons. It does not mean that the presentation of food has replenished the animal's energy reserve. Inaccessible food can produce a response that differs from the response to food that can actually be consumed, and post-ingestive nutrient signals can help sustain the change. An experiment separating the moment of food presentation from the moment of ingestion therefore provides a way to distinguish anticipatory and consummatory influences. The animal's previous experience with the cue is another variable that may affect the response.

The apparently paradoxical observation that a feeding-promoting neuronal population can decrease its activity before the animal begins eating becomes easier to understand when behaviour is divided into stages. An animal must first locate food, approach it, obtain access and then ingest it. A neural signal that motivates searching need not remain at its maximum once a reliable indication of available food appears. Effects of previously released neuropeptides and activity in downstream circuits can persist after the activity of the initiating population changes. This provides one explanation for the observation that feeding continues after a rapid sensory response in arcuate neurons. The explanation should not be turned into a claim that these neurons never contribute during ingestion. Their physiological activity is dynamic and depends on whether predicted food is actually obtained, what is consumed and the state of the animal.

An informative behavioural design can contrast a familiar cue associated with a nutritious food and an equally detectable novel cue. If the animal responds strongly to the familiar cue but weakly to the novel cue before either delivers nutrients, learned prediction is a plausible contributor. If the novel cue acquires influence after repeated pairing with the nutrient, the change provides additional evidence for learning. Appropriate controls include exposure to the cues without nutrient delivery and counterbalancing of sensory identities, since the physical properties of one cue could be intrinsically more attractive. A recording of neural activity during the task can reveal the timing of responses relative to cue presentation and delivery of food. Such temporal information does not automatically establish that the recorded activity is necessary for learning; a separate manipulation during the relevant stage of the task would be needed to address that causal question.

Circadian influences help organize food seeking in relation to recurring environmental conditions. The mammalian central circadian system is closely associated with the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and feeding patterns interact with clocks in other neural and peripheral tissues. A nocturnal laboratory mouse commonly consumes much of its food during the dark phase when maintained under conventional light-dark conditions. Measuring intake or plasma hormone concentration in two groups at different phases of the daily cycle can therefore introduce a systematic difference unrelated to the experimental treatment. To compare the groups, the time of testing and feeding history should be controlled. An animal trained to anticipate a meal at a particular time may show preparatory behaviour even before food is delivered. Such observations illustrate how temporal information and metabolic signals can influence overlapping neural circuits. They do not require the assumption that circadian control is the only cause of meal timing.

9. Autonomic output and adaptive energy expenditure

The brain regulates more than the decision to eat. Descending pathways influence sympathetic and parasympathetic outputs, endocrine secretion and behaviour that determines how much energy is expended. The hypothalamus can therefore coordinate the availability of incoming nutrients with the distribution and use of existing reserves.

Sympathetic nerves participate in regulating brown adipose thermogenesis. Norepinephrine released from sympathetic endings activates adrenergic signalling in brown adipocytes, promoting mobilisation and oxidation of fuels. Uncoupling protein 1 permits proton conductance across the inner mitochondrial membrane, reducing the proportion of the proton-motive force used for ATP synthesis and increasing heat production. The mechanism is especially important in the thermoregulatory physiology of small mammals. Its quantitative contribution to total human energy expenditure varies with environmental and physiological conditions.

A fall in energy availability can produce adaptive adjustments in expenditure as well as increased feeding motivation. Some responses reflect a reduction in the metabolically active tissue mass of the body after prolonged energy deficit, whereas others reflect changes in regulation that are not explained solely by tissue mass. Lower leptin contributes to changes in neuroendocrine signalling associated with conserving energy. Activity and thermogenesis can change in context-dependent ways, so a reduction in expenditure should not automatically be attributed to a single hormone.

Autonomic effects are tissue-specific. A change in sympathetic outflow to brown adipose tissue does not necessarily imply an identical change in sympathetic outflow to the heart or liver. The nervous system uses different efferent pathways, and the physiological responses of tissues depend on their receptors and metabolic state. Measurements of heart rate cannot establish the magnitude of thermogenic sympathetic output without further evidence.

Central regulation of glucose production can also interact with feeding circuits. This is a link to the next node, which examines the gut–pancreas–liver axis. The present focus is on the principle that a neural population can affect several physiological outputs and that feeding behaviour alone cannot reveal the total metabolic effect of manipulating that population.

The output of a central energy-regulatory circuit can be divided conceptually into behavioural, autonomic and neuroendocrine components. Behavioural outputs include food acquisition and spontaneous physical activity. Autonomic outputs alter the functions of peripheral organs, including adipose tissue and the gastrointestinal tract. Neuroendocrine outputs can change the secretion of hormones that regulate metabolism and other energy-requiring processes. These categories are not independent energy terms that can simply be added together: a hormonal change can modify metabolism and thereby contribute to expenditure, and behavioural activity can evoke autonomic responses. Their value is in locating the routes by which a neural change is transmitted to the rest of the body. If two treatments produce similar reductions in body mass but differ in activity and thermogenesis, they need not have acted through the same central pathway.

Brown adipose tissue provides a concrete example of expenditure under neural regulation. Sympathetic input can promote adrenergic signalling in brown adipocytes, which can stimulate mobilization of fuel and mitochondrial thermogenic activity. UCP1 facilitates proton conductance across the inner mitochondrial membrane in the thermogenic context, reducing the fraction of energy from the proton gradient conserved as ATP and allowing more energy to be released as heat. The process still depends on mitochondrial substrate oxidation and oxygen delivery; UCP1 does not create energy from nothing. The sympathetic signal changes the conditions under which chemical energy is used. This mechanism connects the central control of energy expenditure to the thermoregulation mechanisms already introduced in the earlier physiology subunit. It also illustrates why a change in food intake alone cannot describe all the possible consequences of hypothalamic activity.

Ambient temperature influences the baseline demand for thermogenesis. Small mammals maintained below their thermoneutral range may require appreciable additional heat production even when they are at rest. A treatment that modifies sympathetic thermogenesis under those conditions can have a different apparent effect on energy expenditure than the same treatment at thermoneutrality. A laboratory comparison must therefore specify the temperature of the environment and consider whether the groups have comparable insulation, body size and activity. A fall in oxygen consumption could reflect lower thermogenic demand if one group has been housed in warmer conditions. Conversely, a thermogenic response that is readily detectable in a cold environment may not be equally prominent under all other conditions. These considerations are relevant to interpreting rodent data and are among the reasons that the magnitude of an experimental effect should not be transferred directly to human energy metabolism.

Reduced expenditure during a period of energy restriction has more than one possible explanation. A smaller body may require less energy for maintenance and locomotion, and changes in body composition can influence resting metabolic demand. A reduction in the amount of food eaten also changes the energetic cost of digestion and nutrient processing. Adaptive thermogenesis describes an alteration in energy expenditure relative to what would be expected from such changes under an appropriate comparison model. Establishing it requires more than observing that total expenditure is lower after weight loss. The investigator must estimate or account for the expenditure expected at the new body composition and conditions. The interpretation is sensitive to how this expected value is calculated. In an examination question, the term adaptive response should be associated with a specified comparison rather than used as a synonym for any decrease in expenditure.

The balance between conserving energy and acquiring it involves trade-offs. During a shortage of food, an animal may increase exploratory behaviour even while suppressing certain other energy-consuming processes. The total change in expenditure therefore need not be uniformly downward at every moment. Foraging itself has an energetic cost but can improve the likelihood of obtaining a much larger energy input. Thermal demands, reproduction and protection from danger can impose additional constraints on how energy is allocated. The central nervous system integrates signals related to these requirements rather than implementing a universal rule that every energy-requiring process should stop during fasting. A measured increase in locomotion can coexist with a decrease in resting thermogenesis, and a decrease in total expenditure can coexist with intense food-directed motivation. Identifying the component being measured is therefore essential.

The autonomic nervous system also provides routes through which central nutritional information can affect organ function even when ingestion has not yet occurred. Changes in neural output can influence gastrointestinal activity, pancreatic secretion and metabolic processes in other organs. These effects may be anticipatory, as when a food-related cue alters physiological preparation for ingestion, or compensatory, as when declining nutrient availability calls for mobilization of reserves. It is not appropriate to assume that sympathetic activity increases or decreases simultaneously in all organs. Different sympathetic pathways can be regulated in different directions according to the physiological demand. Nor should parasympathetic activity be equated with a universal inhibition of expenditure. In a specific experiment the organ, branch of the autonomic system and physiological endpoint must be named. The downstream organ mechanisms will be developed more fully in the subsequent interorgan communication chapters.

10. Quantifying energy balance and interpreting intake

Let E denote the chemical energy stored in the body, measured in kilojoules, I the metabolizable energy acquired per unit time and X total energy expenditure per unit time. Over a sufficiently short interval in which average rates are meaningful, the change in stored energy is given by ΔE = (I − X)Δt. More generally, the difference in rates must be integrated over the interval. This accounting relationship does not specify how intake or expenditure is regulated.

Consider an illustrative animal with mean metabolizable energy intake of 80 kJ per day and mean energy expenditure of 74 kJ per day over three days. Its energy balance is positive by 6 kJ per day. Multiplying by three gives a gain of 18 kJ in stored energy under the assumptions of the model. If a neuronal intervention reduces daily intake to 70 kJ but also reduces expenditure to 64 kJ, the net balance remains positive by 6 kJ per day. These hypothetical values show why reduced consumption alone does not establish reduced energy storage.

Food mass and energy intake are distinct measurements. Suppose two diets provide 12 and 20 kJ of metabolizable energy per gram respectively. Consumption of 5 g of the first diet provides 60 kJ; consumption of 4 g of the second provides 80 kJ. The animal consuming less mass receives more energy. Experimental food-intake records should therefore specify diet composition and, when appropriate, metabolizable energy density. Spillage and evaporation can also affect measured disappearance of food.

For meal-pattern analysis, total intake over a day can be expressed as the sum of the energy acquired at each meal. A signal that decreases average meal size may provoke a compensatory increase in meal frequency. For example, four meals of 15 kJ each provide the same daily intake as six meals of 10 kJ each. If meal frequency is not measured, a decrease in meal size can be incorrectly interpreted as a sustained decrease in daily energy intake.

Energy expenditure can be estimated using indirect calorimetry, which measures respiratory gas exchange and applies relationships between substrate oxidation and energy release. Oxygen consumption is informative, but it is not an identical measure of energy expenditure regardless of substrate. Comparisons across animals of different body composition also require care: simple division by body mass may create artefacts. Detailed interpretation of gas exchange and metabolic flexibility belongs to a later node. Here the essential principle is to distinguish a measured quantity, a derived estimate and the physiological process being inferred.

The energy balance equation can be derived by considering the body as a system with a boundary. Over a chosen time interval, the energy entering in a form available for metabolism must be accounted for by energy transferred to the environment, external work and the change in stored chemical energy, provided the accounting definitions are consistent. In an integrated whole-body model these losses and work terms are represented in total energy expenditure. If the rate of metabolizable energy acquisition is I and expenditure is X, then the instantaneous rate of change in the energy stock is I minus X. When both rates vary with time, the change over an interval is the integral of their difference; for a simple calculation using average daily rates, it is the mean difference multiplied by the number of days. The sign is meaningful only when both rates refer to the same time interval and use the same energy units.

A worked example demonstrates why food mass is an inadequate substitute for energy intake. In a hypothetical experiment, group A consumes 16 grams of a diet providing 12 kilojoules of metabolizable energy per gram, while group B consumes 12 grams of a diet providing 18 kilojoules per gram. The energy intakes are 192 and 216 kilojoules respectively. Group B consumes four grams less material yet acquires 24 kilojoules more energy, a 12.5 percent increase relative to group A. If both groups expend 200 kilojoules over the measurement interval, group A has a negative balance of 8 kilojoules and group B has a positive balance of 16 kilojoules. This inference assumes the stated energy densities are metabolizable values and that no important losses have been omitted. The example demonstrates why the direction of a change in food mass may differ from the direction of a change in stored energy.

The distinction between satiation and satiety can also be examined numerically. Suppose a control animal consumes four meals per observation period, each providing 25 kilojoules. Its total is 100 kilojoules. After a hypothetical treatment that reduces meal size to 20 kilojoules, the animal consumes five meals, yielding the same total. The observed meal pattern is consistent with a stronger meal-terminating influence combined with compensatory changes in meal frequency. It is not evidence for a reduction in total energy intake. Another treatment could leave meal size at 25 kilojoules but reduce the number of meals from four to three, lowering total intake to 75 kilojoules. The numerical examples are simplified because real meals vary in size and interval, but they demonstrate why recording the distribution of eating events reveals mechanisms that are hidden when only totals are available.

In indirect calorimetry, oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production provide information about whole-body oxidative metabolism. The respiratory exchange ratio is the measured carbon dioxide production rate divided by the oxygen consumption rate, with both expressed in compatible volume units. Under suitable steady-state conditions the ratio can provide information about the relative oxidation of carbohydrate and fat. Complete oxidation of glucose consumes six molecules of oxygen and produces six molecules of carbon dioxide, giving a ratio of one. Fatty acid oxidation has a lower ratio because the molecule is more chemically reduced and requires more oxygen relative to its carbon dioxide output. A decrease in the ratio may therefore be compatible with increased reliance on lipid oxidation under the relevant assumptions. However, changing ventilation, acid-base buffering and other non-steady-state conditions can alter the measured ratio, and the ratio alone is not a measure of the number of kilojoules expended.

Interpreting expenditure across animals of different size requires additional care. A larger animal may expend more energy in absolute terms simply because it contains more metabolically active tissue. Dividing expenditure by total body mass can also be misleading because fat and lean tissues do not have identical metabolic rates and the relationship between size and expenditure need not pass through the origin. If one animal has 30 grams of mass and expends 60 kilojoules while another has 40 grams and expends 72 kilojoules, simple division gives 2.0 and 1.8 kilojoules per gram. The second animal has higher absolute expenditure but lower expenditure per gram. Neither comparison alone establishes that its central nervous system suppresses metabolism. A stronger analysis considers body composition and other relevant covariates and reports the actual expenditure alongside the method of adjustment. 11. Experimental evidence and causal inference

An important advance in appetite physiology came from identifying neurons by molecular characteristics instead of destroying entire regions of the brain. Aponte, Atasoy and Sternson reported in 2011 that optogenetic activation of AgRP neurons in mice could rapidly evoke feeding. Optogenetics uses a genetically introduced light-responsive protein to influence selected cells with light. The result demonstrates sufficiency under the conditions of the experiment: stimulating the targeted population can produce a feeding response. It does not establish that those cells are the only possible source of feeding motivation.

Necessity is a different causal question. A population is necessary for a specified response under specified conditions if suppressing its function prevents or substantially impairs that response. A population can be sufficient but not necessary if parallel circuits can produce the same output. Conversely, a cell population may be necessary for a complex behaviour without being sufficient to initiate it in isolation. Examination questions frequently confuse the two forms of evidence.

Targeted gene disruption provides a second approach. Huszar and colleagues reported in 1997 that disruption of MC4R in mice produced a syndrome that included excessive feeding and increased body mass. This supports an important role for MC4R in normal energy regulation. A whole-body germline mutation acts throughout development, however, and its resulting phenotype can reflect changes in several populations or compensatory responses. Cell-specific and adult-onset perturbations can help separate these possibilities.

Interpretation of recordings also requires attention to what is actually measured. A calcium-dependent fluorescence signal reflects changes in intracellular calcium and is often used as an indirect measure of neural activity. It does not report every action potential with perfect fidelity. Movement, changes in fluorescence unrelated to calcium and differences in reporter expression can influence the measurement. Appropriate controls and independent validation strengthen the link between observed fluorescence and neuronal activity.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which mice expressing a light-sensitive excitatory protein in AgRP neurons eat more during light delivery than mice expressing an inactive fluorescent control protein. All groups receive identical light delivery and access to the same food. This contrast supports an effect of the specific neural manipulation rather than light exposure alone. If the intervention also increases general movement, food-directed behaviour should be examined to determine whether the increased intake reflects targeted feeding motivation or merely altered access to food.

Combining genetic, endocrine and behavioural evidence produces a stronger explanation than relying on any one technique. The goal is to trace the chain from peripheral signal to receptor, neuronal response and physiological outcome, while stating which links were measured and which remain inferred. Rodent experiments establish powerful mechanistic principles, but the magnitude and context of behavioural responses in humans cannot be assumed to be identical.

An optogenetic activation experiment addresses whether an artificial change in the activity of identified neurons can produce a particular response under stated conditions. A light-sensitive protein expressed in a selected neuronal population allows illumination to alter its activity, assuming the expression and illumination are effective. In the classic experiment of Aponte and colleagues, selective activation of AgRP neurons demonstrated the capacity of this population to rapidly promote feeding in mice. The result supplies evidence of sufficiency: artificially increasing activity can be enough to produce the response in the experimental circumstances. It does not demonstrate that the same pattern of activation normally occurs at the beginning of every meal. The distinction is a general one in physiology. A stimulus can reveal what a circuit is capable of doing without reproducing the ordinary inputs and timing that determine its natural operation.

Necessity is tested by interfering with a component during a condition in which the output would normally occur. If selective inhibition of a population reduces food intake in a deprived animal, the result supports a contribution of the population to that response. The strength of the inference depends on the selectivity, degree and timing of the inhibition. Acute inhibition and long-term genetic loss are not interchangeable. A chronic change permits developmental or physiological adaptations that may compensate for the missing component, whereas abrupt inhibition may leave little time for compensation. A normal response after chronic loss of a component does not necessarily mean the component had no function in the original system. Conversely, a severe response to abrupt disruption may reflect the lack of time for other pathways to adapt. Comparing acute and chronic manipulations can help clarify both the normal contribution and the capacity for compensation.

Recording neuronal calcium-related fluorescence is an indirect measure of aspects of neuronal activity. Changes in intracellular calcium can accompany electrical activity, and a genetically encoded fluorescent indicator can permit changes in fluorescence to be measured in identified cell populations. The signal is influenced by indicator kinetics, expression and the activity of the sampled cells. If the measurement combines signals from a population, it can obscure differences among individual neurons. An increase in fluorescence does not by itself measure the release of a particular neurotransmitter at a particular projection. Therefore the method is particularly useful for examining the timing of activity changes relative to a stimulus, while additional evidence is needed to identify the transmitter responsible for a behavioural effect. Controls for movement-related optical changes are relevant when animals are freely behaving. The interpretation should distinguish the recorded signal from the cellular event and from the behavioural consequence inferred from it.

A pathway-specific experiment can be designed to distinguish receptor dependence from a general effect of neuronal activation. Consider four hypothetical groups: a receptor-intact control group with inactive stimulation, a receptor-intact group with active stimulation, a receptor-deficient control group and a receptor-deficient stimulated group. The comparison of the two stimulation conditions within each genotype estimates the effect of stimulation in that genotype. Comparing those effects tests whether the effect differs with receptor status. Suppose stimulation increases food intake by 30 kilojoules in receptor-intact animals and by 10 kilojoules in receptor-deficient animals. The difference between the stimulation effects is 20 kilojoules. This pattern is compatible with a contribution of receptor-dependent transmission and a remaining effect through other routes. It is not proof of the exact fraction of normal physiological signalling mediated by the receptor. Baseline intake, ceiling effects, variability and developmental changes must be considered before assigning a mechanistic meaning to the difference.

Pair-feeding is a useful design when a manipulation changes energy intake and also changes a metabolic measurement. A comparison group can be supplied with the same amount of food consumed by the treated group, helping distinguish effects attributable to altered intake from those that persist at matched intake. The design has limitations. Restricting the comparison group can create a different pattern of hunger, stress or meal timing, even if total energy provided is the same. If the treatment also changes nutrient absorption, matching food mass may not match metabolizable energy. Nonetheless, pair-feeding can provide an informative control when its assumptions and limitations are considered. A difference in thermogenesis between groups with comparable metabolizable energy intake may suggest that a treatment affects expenditure independently of its effect on intake, but the interpretation requires direct or indirect measurement of expenditure and attention to other confounders.

The strongest interpretation of central appetite regulation emerges when findings from different levels agree. An endocrine mutation can reveal the importance of a circulating signal, a biochemical assay can identify the receptor response, anatomical studies can locate responsive cells, recordings can establish the timing of activity and selective perturbations can test the behavioural consequences. These lines of evidence answer distinct questions. A correlation between leptin and body fat is not the same as evidence that leptin acts through a defined neuronal population; a receptor expression map is not the same as proof that the receptor is functionally engaged during an ordinary meal. A clear examination answer therefore traces the proposed causal chain and identifies which link is established by the particular experiment described. It also recognizes the limitations of translating a controlled mouse experiment into a universal account of human behaviour. This approach prepares the student for the next chapter, where related signals coordinate pancreatic secretion and hepatic glucose handling.

Common misconceptions

Incorrect assumption: Hunger equals hypoglycaemia. Correction: Hunger is influenced by several neural and endocrine inputs; normal circulating glucose does not exclude feeding motivation. Examination reasoning: A test may hold glucose constant and alter leptin or cues; predict behaviour from all changed inputs. Why the confusion occurs: Glucose is often taught as the principal immediate fuel, which encourages confusing a substrate concentration with total energy status.

Incorrect assumption: Satiety and satiation are identical. Correction: Satiation limits an ongoing meal; satiety delays subsequent intake. Examination reasoning: Distinguish meal size from intermeal interval. Why the confusion occurs: The everyday word fullness is used for both the ending of a meal and the period following it.

Incorrect assumption: All hypothalamic neurons share one function. Correction: Nuclei contain diverse cells and circuits. Examination reasoning: Do not infer cell-specific function from a lesion. Why the confusion occurs: Anatomical diagrams give each nucleus a single label even though its cells are heterogeneous.

Incorrect assumption: POMC is a mature peptide. Correction: POMC is a precursor processed into several peptides. Examination reasoning: Precursor abundance cannot substitute for secretion measurements. Why the confusion occurs: Precursor names and mature peptide names are often listed together without explaining processing.

Incorrect assumption: Leptin directly binds MC4R. Correction: Leptin acts through LEPR and modulates upstream pathways. Examination reasoning: Identify ligand and receptor separately. Why the confusion occurs: Both hormones and melanocortin peptides can suppress feeding, but a shared outcome does not imply a shared receptor.

Incorrect assumption: AgRP neurons use only AgRP. Correction: NPY and GABA are important cotransmitters. Examination reasoning: Blocking MC4R interaction need not abolish every AgRP-neuron effect. Why the confusion occurs: The cell population is named after one peptide, obscuring its cotransmitters.

Incorrect assumption: All obesity implies low leptin. Correction: High adiposity commonly accompanies elevated leptin. Examination reasoning: Distinguish deficiency from response impairment. Why the confusion occurs: The classic ob/ob phenotype is memorable but is not the usual mechanism of high adiposity.

Incorrect assumption: LEPR is an intrinsic tyrosine kinase. Correction: The long receptor isoform recruits JAK2. Examination reasoning: Compare receptor classes with insulin signalling. Why the confusion occurs: The phosphorylation downstream of LEPR resembles the output of a receptor tyrosine kinase.

Incorrect assumption: Ghrelin is always the sole initiator of eating. Correction: Meal timing, sensory cues and other physiological signals contribute. Examination reasoning: A ghrelin perturbation need not abolish all intake. Why the confusion occurs: The common nickname hunger hormone obscures overlapping signals and learned behaviour.

Incorrect assumption: All GLP-1 comes from gut. Correction: Central neurons can also produce GLP-1. Examination reasoning: Localise the source before inferring the route. Why the confusion occurs: Endocrine descriptions emphasize intestinal L cells and can omit the neural source.

Incorrect assumption: The vagus is exclusively motor. Correction: It includes sensory afferents and motor efferents. Examination reasoning: Identify signal direction before interpreting interruption experiments. Why the confusion occurs: Descriptions of autonomic pathways often emphasize motor control rather than visceral sensation.

Incorrect assumption: Any reduced intake proves satiation. Correction: Malaise and locomotor impairment can reduce intake. Examination reasoning: Evaluate alternative behavioural outcomes. Why the confusion occurs: A behavioural endpoint can look identical despite different underlying causes.

Incorrect assumption: Dopamine is simply pleasure. Correction: Dopaminergic systems participate in motivation and learning as well. Examination reasoning: Separate approach from taste-reactivity measures. Why the confusion occurs: Popular descriptions compress several dimensions of reward into a single chemical signal.

Incorrect assumption: Less food mass always means less energy. Correction: Energy density can differ. Examination reasoning: Multiply mass by metabolizable energy density. Why the confusion occurs: Food intake is often reported in grams even when energy density varies.

Incorrect assumption: Reduced intake guarantees negative balance. Correction: Expenditure may decrease at the same time. Examination reasoning: Calculate intake minus expenditure. Why the confusion occurs: The intake term is frequently discussed in isolation from expenditure.

Incorrect assumption: Sufficiency proves necessity. Correction: A selectively activated pathway may be redundant in natural behaviour. Examination reasoning: Distinguish activation and inhibition experiments. Why the confusion occurs: A dramatic activation result is tempting to interpret as an exclusive explanation of natural behaviour.

Incorrect assumption: A calcium signal equals an exact spike count. Correction: Fluorescence is an indirect activity measure. Examination reasoning: Check reporter calibration and movement artefacts. Why the confusion occurs: The measured fluorescence trace is often drawn like an electrical recording although its underlying signal is different.

Original examination practice

Part B — core concepts

1. Which variable is an accounting measure rather than a regulatory process?

  1. Satiation
  2. Hunger
  3. Energy balance
  4. Satiety
Answer and explanation

C. Energy balance — Energy balance is the difference between metabolizable intake and expenditure; the other terms describe behavioural regulation. The other terms refer to the regulation of eating rather than a signed difference of energy flows.

2. Which nucleus contains well-characterised AgRP/NPY neurons?

  1. Arcuate nucleus
  2. Suprachiasmatic nucleus
  3. Cochlear nucleus
  4. Red nucleus
Answer and explanation

A. Arcuate nucleus — The arcuate nucleus includes AgRP/NPY and POMC populations. Other nuclei have distinct primary roles. The suprachiasmatic nucleus primarily coordinates circadian timing; the cochlear and red nuclei are not the arcuate feeding populations.

3. What is POMC?

  1. An adrenergic receptor
  2. A monosaccharide
  3. A leptin receptor
  4. A peptide precursor
Answer and explanation

D. A peptide precursor — POMC is proteolytically processed into bioactive peptides, including α-MSH in relevant neurons. A precursor must be processed before the active melanocortin ligand is available; it is neither a receptor nor carbohydrate.

4. Which peptide directly activates MC4R in the classical model?

  1. CCK
  2. Leptin
  3. α-MSH
  4. Insulin
Answer and explanation

C. α-MSH — α-MSH is a melanocortin agonist; the other hormones signal through their own receptors. CCK, leptin and insulin have distinct cognate receptors and may act upstream or in parallel.

5. Which peptide counteracts MC4R signalling?

  1. GLP-1
  2. AgRP
  3. Orexin
  4. CCK
Answer and explanation

B. AgRP — AgRP is an endogenous melanocortin antagonist/inverse agonist, unlike the other listed peptides. GLP-1 and CCK signal through their own receptors; orexin is not the endogenous MC4R antagonist.

6. Which transmitter is also released by many AgRP neurons?

  1. Adrenaline exclusively
  2. Acetylcholine exclusively
  3. Thyroxine
  4. GABA
Answer and explanation

D. GABA — Many AgRP neurons are GABAergic. Thyroxine is an endocrine hormone, not their conventional transmitter. The other choices do not describe the characteristic inhibitory transmitter of these cells.

7. The canonical MC4R pathway involves which second messenger?

  1. cAMP
  2. IP3 exclusively
  3. ATP secretion as sole messenger
  4. cGMP exclusively
Answer and explanation

A. cAMP — MC4R canonically couples to Gs and adenylyl cyclase; additional context-dependent mechanisms do not negate this. An exclusive IP3 or cGMP mechanism would misidentify the canonical Gs-linked signalling pathway.

8. What is the principal source of circulating leptin?

  1. Gastric parietal cells
  2. Adipocytes
  3. Erythrocytes
  4. Pancreatic alpha cells
Answer and explanation

B. Adipocytes — White adipose tissue is a major endocrine source of leptin, not just a fuel store. The other tissues are not the main endocrine source of leptin.

9. Which kinase is associated with the long leptin receptor isoform?

  1. Intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase of LEPR
  2. JAK2
  3. Pyruvate kinase
  4. DNA polymerase
Answer and explanation

B. JAK2 — LEPR is a cytokine-family receptor that engages JAK2; it does not possess intrinsic receptor kinase activity. A receptor-associated kinase phosphorylates signalling components; a metabolic enzyme or DNA polymerase does not perform that role.

10. The insulin receptor differs from LEPR because it has what feature?

  1. A ligand-binding domain only in nuclei
  2. A catalytic RNA domain
  3. Intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity
  4. A GABA channel pore
Answer and explanation

C. Intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity — Insulin binds a receptor tyrosine kinase; LEPR instead recruits associated kinases. The receptor is a membrane protein kinase, not an RNA catalyst or a ligand-gated chloride channel.

11. Which modification enables canonical ghrelin activation of GHSR1a?

  1. Acylation
  2. Glycogen branching
  3. Deacetylation of histones
  4. DNA methylation
Answer and explanation

A. Acylation — Ghrelin O-acyltransferase modifies ghrelin; the acylated peptide activates the classical receptor. Acylation modifies the peptide itself, unlike DNA methylation or histone modification.

12. Which organ is the major source of circulating ghrelin?

  1. Adrenal medulla
  2. Spleen
  3. Thyroid
  4. Stomach
Answer and explanation

D. Stomach — Ghrelin is produced predominantly by gastric endocrine cells, with additional sources. The major gastric endocrine source does not imply that the stomach is the only site of expression.

13. Which intestinal endocrine cell class produces CCK?

  1. Pancreatic beta cells
  2. I cells
  3. Gastric chief cells
  4. Erythrocytes
Answer and explanation

B. I cells — I cells release CCK in response to nutrients including lipid and protein digestion products. Beta cells produce insulin, whereas chief cells produce digestive precursors; erythrocytes are not intestinal endocrine cells.

14. Which receptor mediates an important vagal action of CCK?

  1. CCK1 receptor
  2. MC4R
  3. LEPR
  4. Insulin receptor
Answer and explanation

A. CCK1 receptor — CCK1 receptors on vagal sensory pathways participate in meal-related feedback. MC4R, LEPR and insulin receptors are important elsewhere in the network but do not replace CCK1 in this route.

15. Which hormone is produced by intestinal L cells and has an incretin action?

  1. Cortisol
  2. NPY
  3. GLP-1
  4. AgRP
Answer and explanation

C. GLP-1 — GLP-1 potentiates glucose-dependent insulin secretion and participates in feeding regulation. Cortisol is a steroid and NPY and AgRP are neural peptides; they are not the intestinal incretin in this question.

16. PYY3–36 preferentially acts through which receptor in a commonly taught feeding pathway?

  1. CCK1
  2. MC4
  3. GHSR1a
  4. Y2
Answer and explanation

D. Y2 — The processed PYY peptide acts through Y2 receptors; receptor subtype determines physiological effect. Y2 is the relevant preference of the processed form; CCK1, MC4R and GHSR1a bind other signals.

17. Where do many vagal visceral afferents terminate centrally?

  1. Primary visual cortex
  2. Cerebellar vermis exclusively
  3. NTS
  4. Spinal ventral horn exclusively
Answer and explanation

C. NTS — The NTS in the medulla receives major visceral afferent input. The other locations are not the principal central target of vagal visceral sensory fibres.

18. A reduction in the size of an ongoing meal most directly reflects which process, other causes excluded?

  1. Post-meal satiety only
  2. Satiation
  3. Thermogenesis
  4. Glycogenolysis
Answer and explanation

B. Satiation — Satiation terminates a meal; satiety concerns inhibition of subsequent eating. Thermogenesis and glycogenolysis describe metabolic processes, not termination of ingestion.

19. Which protein directly facilitates proton leak for brown-fat thermogenesis?

  1. GLUT4
  2. STAT3
  3. MC4R
  4. UCP1
Answer and explanation

D. UCP1 — UCP1 is an inner mitochondrial membrane protein that enables regulated uncoupling. GLUT4 transports glucose; STAT3 is a signalling protein and MC4R a GPCR. Neither is the mitochondrial proton conductance pathway.

20. Which term describes the direction of a vagal signal travelling from stomach to medulla?

  1. Afferent
  2. Paracrine only
  3. Efferent
  4. Autocrine
Answer and explanation

A. Afferent — Afferent signals travel towards the CNS; efferent signals travel towards peripheral targets. Paracrine and autocrine refer to chemical communication distances, not the direction of a nerve impulse.

Part C — analytical reasoning

1. A leptin-deficient mouse has an intact LEPR. A second mouse lacks functional LEPR. Which is the most direct predicted response to supplying leptin?

  1. Both respond identically
  2. Only the receptor-null mouse responds
  3. Neither responds because both are obese
  4. The deficient mouse can respond; the receptor-null mouse cannot respond through LEPR
Answer and explanation

D. The deficient mouse can respond; the receptor-null mouse cannot respond through LEPR — The first has a missing ligand with intact downstream machinery; the second cannot transduce the ligand signal. The phenotype does not identify the molecular defect. Replacement cannot bypass the missing receptor; similar body mass does not make the mutations mechanistically equivalent.

2. Food appearance reduces AgRP calcium activity before a fasting animal eats. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Adipose stores have been restored
  2. Sensory information can alter the activity before nutrient absorption
  3. Leptin concentration must have doubled
  4. AgRP neurons cannot regulate feeding
Answer and explanation

B. Sensory information can alter the activity before nutrient absorption — No nutrient from the presented food has yet entered the body, so anticipation can explain the early response. The result does not quantify hormone levels. Neither fat restoration nor a specific leptin change follows from the timing; the observation does not negate earlier causal activation experiments.

3. Selective AgRP activation elicits eating, but suppression of one projection does not abolish natural feeding. Which inference is justified?

  1. Activation can be sufficient while parallel pathways support feeding
  2. The suppression proves AgRP is never involved
  3. The outcomes are logically incompatible
  4. The activation proves exclusive necessity
Answer and explanation

A. Activation can be sufficient while parallel pathways support feeding — Sufficiency and necessity are distinct causal properties. Redundant output pathways can preserve behaviour after one is suppressed. A negative intervention in one projection cannot establish the absence of all activity in the parent cell population.

4. MC4R-null neurons receive more α-MSH. What is the expected receptor-specific consequence?

  1. Ghrelin is necessarily eliminated
  2. Normal MC4R activity resumes
  3. No restoration of MC4R-dependent signalling in null cells
  4. Leptin receptor becomes MC4R
Answer and explanation

C. No restoration of MC4R-dependent signalling in null cells — The agonist requires functional receptor; other melanocortin receptors may still signal but do not restore the missing MC4R. Increasing agonist cannot supply a missing receptor protein; unrelated endocrine receptors do not become MC4R.

5. An intervention changes daily intake from 80 to 70 kJ and expenditure from 74 to 64 kJ. What happens to net daily storage in the simplified accounting model?

  1. It falls to −10 kJ
  2. It becomes zero
  3. It remains +6 kJ
  4. It increases to +14 kJ
Answer and explanation

C. It remains +6 kJ — Subtract expenditure from intake in both conditions: 80−74=6 and 70−64=6 kJ. Both rates change equally. The difference of two rates is unchanged even though both individual rates decrease. The other answers confuse rate changes with storage.

6. Diet A provides 12 kJ/g and diet B 20 kJ/g. Animals consume 5 g and 4 g respectively. Which consumes more energy?

  1. B, by 20 kJ
  2. A, by 12 kJ
  3. Both equally
  4. B, by 8 kJ
Answer and explanation

A. B, by 20 kJ — A receives 60 kJ and B receives 80 kJ. Grams of food cannot substitute for metabolizable energy. Multiplying density by mass is required before comparing the two diets; the absolute mass difference has the opposite sign.

7. A hormone shortens meals from 15 to 10 kJ, but meal number rises from four to six daily. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Satiety must increase
  2. Expenditure necessarily decreases
  3. Daily intake falls by one-third
  4. Satiation may increase without changing daily energy intake
Answer and explanation

D. Satiation may increase without changing daily energy intake — Both meal patterns deliver 60 kJ per day. Meal frequency compensates for reduced meal size. A smaller meal suggests a meal-termination effect, but a shorter intermeal interval may counteract it. No expenditure measurement was given.

8. A systemic insulin infusion reduces feeding while producing hypoglycaemia. Why is the experiment difficult to interpret?

  1. Hypoglycaemia proves MC4R activation
  2. Central receptor effects and peripheral glucose changes are confounded
  3. Insulin cannot affect peripheral tissues
  4. Blood glucose is irrelevant to the brain
Answer and explanation

B. Central receptor effects and peripheral glucose changes are confounded — The intervention alters multiple signals. A glucose-matched control can help distinguish insulin-associated from glycaemic effects. Hypoglycaemia may trigger counterregulation and feeding independently of the central insulin action. A glucose clamp or matched comparison would strengthen inference.

9. A peptide decreases food intake but also produces conditioned flavour avoidance. What is the key interpretation problem?

  1. Visceral malaise may contribute to the reduced intake
  2. Flavour avoidance proves normal satiety
  3. Food intake necessarily measures energy expenditure
  4. The peptide must activate POMC
Answer and explanation

A. Visceral malaise may contribute to the reduced intake — Avoidance is evidence that an aversive state may be involved, so reduced intake is not enough to establish physiological satiation. Avoidance does not prove the nutrient status has changed; alternative explanations should be evaluated using suitable behavioural controls.

10. A nonnutritive gastric load reduces meal size while plasma gut peptide levels barely change. Which pathway could explain the response?

  1. Increased nutrient absorption
  2. Ob gene deletion
  3. Loss of UCP1
  4. Distension-sensitive afferents
Answer and explanation

D. Distension-sensitive afferents — Mechanical feedback can inform the brainstem without requiring absorbed nutrients. The experiment does not establish that other pathways are absent. Absorption is not required for stretch-induced signalling; neither a gene deletion nor thermogenic protein loss is established.

11. A vagal intervention attenuates CCK-induced reduction in meal size but does not eliminate it. What is the best conclusion?

  1. CCK has no feeding effect
  2. All remaining effects prove direct MC4R binding
  3. The vagus contributes; incomplete intervention or other pathways remain possible
  4. CCK acts only through the vagus in all circumstances
Answer and explanation

C. The vagus contributes; incomplete intervention or other pathways remain possible — Partial attenuation does not establish exclusive mediation. Intervention completeness and alternative mechanisms must be assessed. The experiment distinguishes a contribution from exclusive necessity; it does not establish an MC4R ligand interaction.

12. Two groups have the same fat mass but different leptin after acute fasting. What does this show?

  1. LEPR necessarily differs between groups
  2. Leptin can reflect recent energy status as well as fat mass
  3. Leptin is produced only in stomach
  4. Fat mass measurements must be impossible
Answer and explanation

B. Leptin can reflect recent energy status as well as fat mass — Nutritional state can modify leptin secretion without equivalent changes in adiposity. The result does not identify receptor function. The groups can differ in hormonal response to recent energy supply without a difference in receptor sequence or adiposity.

13. An animal shows high plasma leptin and weak response to injected leptin. Which is the cautious interpretation?

  1. Leptin is absent
  2. LEPR gene is certainly deleted
  3. All neurons are resistant
  4. Reduced responsiveness is plausible but mechanism remains undetermined
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced responsiveness is plausible but mechanism remains undetermined — High ligand and a weak response are compatible with resistance; receptor loss is only one of many possible explanations. Neither a specific gene deletion nor uniform resistance across cell populations can be diagnosed from two whole-body observations.

14. A POMC neuron has increased precursor mRNA but unchanged α-MSH secretion. Which mechanism can reconcile these findings?

  1. POMC is identical to α-MSH
  2. MC4R synthesises POMC
  3. Peptide processing, translation or release may be independently regulated
  4. mRNA directly activates MC4R
Answer and explanation

C. Peptide processing, translation or release may be independently regulated — Precursor expression is not a direct measure of mature peptide production and release. The transcript, the processed peptide and its release are separate steps; the receptor does not synthesize the precursor.

15. A drug prevents AgRP binding to MC4R but leaves NPY and GABA release intact. What is a defensible prediction?

  1. GABA cannot inhibit any target
  2. Some feeding effects of AgRP neurons may remain
  3. NPY becomes an MC4R agonist
  4. All feeding responses must vanish
Answer and explanation

B. Some feeding effects of AgRP neurons may remain — AgRP neurons use parallel transmitters, so blocking one mechanism need not eliminate all responses. Cotransmitters can act at their respective receptors even while the peptide interaction is blocked.

16. Light is delivered to mice expressing a neuronal opsin and to mice expressing a fluorophore only. Why use the second group?

  1. To control for light delivery and associated procedures
  2. To eliminate all developmental effects
  3. To prove that every neuron is necessary
  4. To measure leptin directly
Answer and explanation

A. To control for light delivery and associated procedures — A matched control helps attribute differences to opsin-mediated perturbation rather than light or handling. The comparison does not eliminate developmental differences; it controls aspects of the stimulation procedure and supports attribution of the acute effect.

17. A mouse with lifelong MC4R disruption is obese. Which follow-up best isolates adult function?

  1. Another germline mutation without controls
  2. Adult-onset receptor perturbation with suitable controls
  3. A single measurement of food mass
  4. An unrelated gastric lesion
Answer and explanation

B. Adult-onset receptor perturbation with suitable controls — An adult-onset manipulation reduces ambiguity due to developmental compensation, although it still needs careful control. An adult manipulation should be accompanied by confirmation of receptor change and a matched control, rather than another isolated endpoint.

18. Brown adipose sympathetic activity rises with no detectable change in daily food intake. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. Energy balance cannot change
  2. All sympathetic organs must respond equally
  3. Central output can alter a component of expenditure independently of intake
  4. Food intake measures heat directly
Answer and explanation

C. Central output can alter a component of expenditure independently of intake — Autonomic outputs can regulate tissue-specific metabolism. Net storage depends on the actual change in expenditure. A tissue-specific sympathetic increase does not determine the activity of other sympathetic nerves or actual net energy storage.

19. A food cue increases approach behaviour but does not increase measured taste reactions. Which distinction is illustrated?

  1. Incentive motivation and hedonic response can diverge
  2. Homeostasis never influences reward
  3. The cue must increase leptin
  4. Dopamine is identical to subjective pleasure
Answer and explanation

A. Incentive motivation and hedonic response can diverge — Approach and taste reactivity are different behavioural measures; neither alone characterises all reward processes. Incentive and hedonic measures may be affected differently; no specific hormonal change follows from the behavioural observation alone.

20. A recording shows larger calcium fluorescence during a behavioural task. Which additional evidence most directly strengthens interpretation as neuronal activity?

  1. Assuming fluorescence counts action potentials exactly
  2. Measurement of body mass only
  3. Replacement of the recording with a meal-size estimate
  4. Validation of reporter response with electrophysiology and motion controls
Answer and explanation

D. Validation of reporter response with electrophysiology and motion controls — Calcium signals are indirect and can be affected by artefacts; independent validation links them more securely to electrical activity. An independent activity measure and motion controls address the interpretation of the recording rather than substituting an unrelated endpoint.

SET and mixed applications

1. Define the difference between energy homeostasis and energy balance.

Answer and explanation

Energy balance is quantitative energy accounting. Homeostasis is the interacting control processes that regulate energy acquisition and use; transient imbalance can be normal.

2. Explain why the traditional hunger-centre and satiety-centre model is insufficient.

Answer and explanation

Lesions affect mixed cell populations, axons and multiple functions. Distributed hypothalamic, hindbrain and reward circuits jointly influence feeding.

3. Predict the change in energy stores over five days if mean intake is 55 kJ/day and expenditure is 61 kJ/day.

Answer and explanation

ΔE = (55−61)×5 = −30 kJ, assuming mean rates represent the interval. This is energy, not a direct estimate of body mass.

4. Assertion: A hormone that lowers meal size necessarily lowers daily energy intake. Reason: Later meals can compensate for smaller initial meals.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true. Daily intake depends on both meal size and frequency.

5. Assertion: AgRP and α-MSH can oppose one another at MC4R. Reason: α-MSH activates MC4R while AgRP can oppose its signalling.

Answer and explanation

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. AgRP neurons also use other transmitters.

6. Why is systemic GLP-1 receptor agonism not identical to the physiology of endogenous gut GLP-1?

Answer and explanation

Exposure time, concentration and access to different receptors may differ; endogenous peptide degradation and local routes matter.

7. Describe a control for distinguishing food-associated cues from general sensory arousal.

Answer and explanation

Present a matched non-food stimulus and control prior experience, timing and handling; compare neuronal activity before consumption.

8. Why does a high leptin measurement not diagnose a particular molecular defect?

Answer and explanation

Ligand concentration is distinct from receptor access, signalling and network response; each requires separate evidence.

9. Differentiate vagal afferent and efferent functions in a meal response.

Answer and explanation

Afferents convey visceral state to central circuits, while efferents convey motor/autonomic commands to peripheral organs.

10. What is the central limitation of extrapolating a mouse optogenetic experiment to human appetite?

Answer and explanation

Species, behavioural context and the artificial nature of the stimulation constrain extrapolation; the experiment supports a mechanism under its tested conditions.

Revision and mastery

Feeding emerges from distributed hypothalamic, brainstem and reward networks. Energy-status hormones and meal-related signals alter these networks, which coordinate behaviour and autonomic output. Use the molecular identity of each ligand and receptor to predict the consequences of a specific perturbation. Distinguish current state from predicted food availability, and physiological satiation from nonspecific avoidance.

Key relationship: ΔE = (I − X)Δt for constant mean rates, with intake and expenditure in matching energy-per-time units.
