1. The physiological problem: why meals are intermittent

A mammal requires a continuous supply of usable energy even though food enters its digestive tract in separate episodes. The nervous system must coordinate the search for food, its consumption and the cessation of a meal with the changing availability of metabolic fuels. The challenge is not simply to maintain glucose concentration. A recently fed animal can have a normal blood glucose concentration and nevertheless begin eating when a learned food cue appears. An animal with depleted energy reserves can maintain its blood glucose by mobilising hepatic substrates and still show powerful motivation to seek food. These observations distinguish the regulation of circulating fuels from the regulation of behaviour and stored energy.

Energy balance describes the difference between metabolizable energy acquired and energy expended over a specified interval. Energy homeostasis describes the coordinated physiological processes that tend to constrain changes in energy stores. The latter is a regulatory concept rather than a mathematical identity. An animal can be in negative energy balance for several hours without a failure of homeostasis: mobilising stored triglycerides during fasting is a normal response. Over longer periods, persistent changes in intake and expenditure influence the quantity and composition of stored tissue. The brain does not measure all of these energy flows directly. It receives information about the availability of food, the state of the digestive organs, circulating hormones and the conditions under which tissues are using fuel.

A useful starting distinction is between hunger, appetite, satiation and satiety. Hunger denotes the internal motivation associated with obtaining food, while appetite encompasses the desire to eat, including learned preferences and external influences. Satiation describes processes that limit the amount consumed during a meal. Satiety refers to the inhibition of further eating between meals. These categories overlap because a meal-related signal can influence both meal termination and subsequent meal timing. Neither a self-reported sensation nor an observed feeding response in a laboratory animal is a direct measurement of all the underlying physiological processes.

The separation between behavioural and physiological variables is central to experimental interpretation. Suppose an animal stops approaching food after receiving an experimental compound. The compound might increase satiation, decrease motivation to approach food, impair movement or produce visceral discomfort. All four possibilities could decrease measured food intake. A sound experiment must distinguish between them before assigning a physiological function to the compound. Conversely, an intervention that changes heat production without changing meal size may still have a substantial effect on energy homeostasis. The chapter therefore treats food intake and energy expenditure as separate, interacting outputs rather than interchangeable measurements.

The continuity of cellular metabolism does not require continuity of feeding because the organism has mechanisms for buffering the intervals between meals. Glycogen can supply glucose, and triglyceride stores can supply fatty acids. These processes are regulated at the level of the liver, muscle and adipose tissue, but their coordination creates a problem for the nervous system. A temporary fall in the amount of recently absorbed glucose is not equivalent to exhaustion of all stored energy. Responding to every fluctuation by immediately seeking food would be costly, especially in an environment where food acquisition requires locomotion or exposure to danger. A useful controller must therefore weigh the present supply of fuel against the future benefit of obtaining more.

The controlled quantity in energy homeostasis is not adequately described by a single concentration in blood. Body energy is stored in several compartments, with very different capacities and rates of mobilisation. Even when circulating glucose remains within a narrow range, liver glycogen content may decline and adipose triglyceride mobilisation may increase. Such an organism can have adequate current plasma glucose but still be experiencing a net loss of chemical energy. For this reason a student should avoid treating a glucose measurement as a direct measurement of the animal's total energy reserves. A glucose-sensing pathway answers a narrower question about the availability or use of a specific substrate. Signals associated with adiposity add information on a different timescale.

A second distinction is between the mechanisms that maintain a variable and the level at which the variable is observed. The word homeostasis sometimes suggests that a fixed value is maintained with perfect precision. Energy stores instead vary with age, reproduction, seasonal conditions, illness and changes in the environment. The nervous system may defend some ranges more strongly than others, and the intensity of compensatory responses depends on the preceding state of the animal. A sustained reduction in available food can change not only the energy content of the body but also subsequent energy expenditure. Thus a model with a single unchanging reference value is a useful starting point for understanding negative feedback, not a full description of mammalian physiology.

A signal that corrects an existing departure from a regulated condition participates in feedback. For example, decreased availability of energy can stimulate food acquisition, which subsequently increases availability and reduces the original stimulus. A feedforward signal instead initiates a response in anticipation of a future change. The smell of familiar food can provide information about an impending meal while the nutrients are still outside the body. Such anticipatory control is advantageous because digestive and behavioural changes need time to develop. Feedforward control is not always accurate: a familiar odour may appear without accessible food. Feedback from the stomach, intestine and absorbed nutrients helps correct the prediction. Observing a neural response before a nutrient enters the bloodstream therefore does not contradict homeostatic regulation.

The animal also needs to decide which behaviour has priority. Eating is not the only activity that contributes to survival. Foraging competes with sleep, defensive behaviour, thermoregulation and reproduction. A laboratory animal exposed to a stressful novel environment may delay eating despite a substantial energy deficit. A measurement of the time before food is approached can therefore reveal conflict between behavioural systems, rather than the absence of a biological signal of hunger. One should distinguish the motivation to obtain food, the execution of food-directed behaviour and the amount consumed after food is obtained. These variables are related but are not identical and may be affected at different points in the network.

Consider two animals with identical total intake over twenty-four hours. In the first, ingestion occurs in several relatively small meals; the second consumes fewer large meals. Their patterns of gastric filling, nutrient absorption and hormone release will differ even if their metabolizable energy intake is identical. A manipulation that changes meal pattern without changing daily intake might still alter digestive workload or the timing of endocrine responses. Conversely a change in total daily intake might originate entirely from a reduction in the number of meals, even if the size of each meal is unaffected. These distinctions motivate the use of meal-by-meal measurements when identifying the role of a neural or hormonal pathway. They also prevent a common examination error: inferring a particular process of appetite regulation from total food intake alone.

2. Anatomical organisation of central energy sensing

The hypothalamus occupies a relatively small region at the base of the forebrain. It surrounds part of the third ventricle and communicates with the pituitary, limbic structures, brainstem and autonomic pathways. Within it are several anatomically defined nuclei, each containing diverse neuronal populations. A nucleus is a grouping of neuronal cell bodies in the central nervous system, not a single neuron or a single type of transmitter. The arcuate nucleus is situated near the ventral portion of the third ventricle and close to the median eminence. This position is relevant because the median eminence is a neurovascular interface where access to circulating molecules differs from that in many other brain regions. It does not mean that the entire hypothalamus lacks a blood–brain barrier.

The paraventricular nucleus, abbreviated PVN or PVH in different textbooks, contains neurons that influence endocrine secretion and autonomic pathways. Some populations express melanocortin receptors and receive signals from arcuate neurons. The lateral hypothalamic area contains heterogeneous neurons implicated in arousal, feeding, motivation and other behaviours. It includes populations producing orexin, also called hypocretin, and melanin-concentrating hormone. These peptides must not be confused with melanocortins simply because their names refer to pigmentation or related biological terminology. Other hypothalamic regions, including the dorsomedial and ventromedial nuclei, contribute to the coordination of autonomic responses and energy use.

The caudal brainstem is equally important. The nucleus of the solitary tract, commonly called the NTS, lies in the medulla and receives visceral sensory input. Many vagal afferents have their cell bodies in the nodose ganglia. Their peripheral endings respond to conditions in internal organs and their central processes terminate in brainstem circuits. Neurons in the NTS communicate with neighbouring brainstem regions and ascending pathways. The area postrema lies nearby and has comparatively permeable capillaries, permitting access to certain circulating chemical signals. Its special vascular properties should not be generalised to every medullary neuron.

The traditional description of a lateral hypothalamic hunger centre and a ventromedial satiety centre arose from lesion and stimulation experiments. These studies demonstrated that damaging selected regions could alter feeding, but they could not establish that the damaged region contained a single exclusive controller. A lesion destroys several cell types and may disrupt axons passing through an area. Changes in arousal, motor activity, endocrine function and expenditure may accompany changes in feeding. Modern cell-type-specific experiments refine rather than erase the historical observation that the hypothalamus is important. Their major advance is to identify particular populations and connections that contribute to a measurable response.

A distributed control network allows signals of different duration and origin to influence behaviour. A sensory cue may change neural activity within seconds, gastric distension develops during a meal, and adiposity-related endocrine information acts across longer intervals. These are overlapping timescales, not mutually exclusive categories. A major challenge in studying the system is to determine which signal reaches which cells under which physiological conditions.

To understand the position of the arcuate nucleus, begin with a sagittal view of the brain. The hypothalamus lies below the thalamus, near the ventral part of the third ventricle. The arcuate region is near the floor of this ventricle and lies close to the median eminence, a specialised region involved in neuroendocrine communication. The proximity is physiologically important because circulating signals can influence nearby neural populations through regulated interfaces. It does not mean that every circulating molecule has unrestricted access to every hypothalamic neuron. The blood-brain barrier is a cellular and molecular interface, and permeability and transport vary among regions and physiological states. Anatomical proximity is therefore evidence of a plausible route of communication, not proof that a particular hormone acts directly on every cell in that region.

In a simplified circuit, a first-order neuron receives information about a peripheral or internal stimulus and transmits signals to another neuron. A second-order neuron integrates this information with other inputs. The terms first-order and second-order describe the position in a selected pathway rather than a rigid classification of neuronal types. A neuron in the paraventricular hypothalamus may receive inputs from arcuate neurons, other hypothalamic nuclei and the brainstem. Its output depends on the balance of those inputs, its intrinsic excitability and the receptors that it expresses. Consequently, a circuit diagram showing one input and one output represents a selected causal route through a much larger network.

The lateral hypothalamic area contains several kinds of neuron, including populations associated with orexin, also called hypocretin, and melanin-concentrating hormone. The orexin system is particularly helpful in illustrating the relationship between energy-related behaviour and arousal. Searching for food requires an animal to be awake and capable of movement. An alteration in arousal can therefore change feeding without directly changing the size of a meal through a classical satiation pathway. Melanin-concentrating hormone is another peptide associated with feeding and energy regulation. Its name should not lead to confusion with the melanocortin peptides produced by POMC; these are distinct signalling systems. The lateral hypothalamus is not a single neuronal population with a single neurotransmitter or an invariably orexigenic output.

The paraventricular region similarly contains populations with different functions. Some project to neural targets that influence ingestion; other populations participate in neuroendocrine and autonomic regulation. In the broader context of animal physiology, paraventricular neurons contribute to the hypothalamic control of pituitary-related processes and responses to stress. A manipulation of the entire anatomical region may thus alter several processes together. Selective manipulation of neurons expressing a particular receptor can reveal a more specific contribution, provided the method really is restricted to the relevant population. Even then, the conclusion is about the manipulated cells and the experimental conditions, not necessarily about every cell bearing that receptor elsewhere in the brain.

Classical lesion studies were informative because they established that normal eating depends upon integrity of central nervous structures. However, a lesion has limited anatomical and cellular specificity. Damage may affect nearby neurons, axons passing through the injured region and local connections. Furthermore, changes in activity, thirst or endocrine secretion may contribute to body mass changes following a lesion. If an animal becomes heavier after hypothalamic injury, it is necessary to measure intake and expenditure separately and to determine whether changes in tissue composition account for the difference. If the investigator merely measures final mass, an explanation involving overeating remains one of several possibilities. The movement from lesion studies to molecularly targeted perturbations was therefore an improvement in the specificity of causal questions, rather than a reason to dismiss the early experiments as worthless.

The hindbrain provides an anatomically different route to similar behavioural outcomes. The nucleus of the solitary tract receives sensory information from internal organs, including the digestive tract, while adjacent structures of the dorsal vagal complex contribute to the processing of visceral information and autonomic responses. A signal in the hindbrain may alter eating through local circuits or through pathways ascending to other brain regions. The fact that an animal can reduce food intake in response to a signal acting in the hindbrain demonstrates why an account restricted to the hypothalamus is incomplete. The anatomical organisation also explains why removal of one pathway need not eliminate every response to a peripheral signal: a second pathway can transmit related information through a different route.

Regional names are necessary for navigating the subject, but learning the names without identifying the direction of communication leaves the mechanism unclear. For each region, ask what kind of signal reaches it, which neuronal population is receiving the signal, what its output targets are, and which measurable physiological variable might change. For example, a neuron receiving mechanosensory input from the digestive tract need not express a receptor for a circulating adiposity signal. Its output may nonetheless be modulated by a different neuron sensitive to that hormone. The two forms of information can thereby interact without both being detected by the same cell. Integration is a property of the connected network, not necessarily a property of a single universal energy sensor.

3. Arcuate neurons and the melanocortin pathway

The arcuate nucleus contains two much-studied populations with opposing average effects on feeding. One expresses agouti-related peptide (AgRP) and neuropeptide Y (NPY); many of these neurons also release gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). The second expresses pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), a precursor polypeptide from which several biologically active peptides are generated. Some POMC neurons also express cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART). These names identify molecular features of neurons; they do not imply that every member of a population has identical connections, receptor expression or electrophysiological responses.

In the melanocortin pathway, enzymatic processing of POMC produces alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), among other products. Peptide processing matters because synthesis of a precursor and secretion of a biologically active product are different events. The complement of processing enzymes in a cell determines which products accumulate. Thus, expression of POMC messenger RNA alone cannot establish the quantity of α-MSH released at a particular synapse.

α-MSH activates melanocortin-4 receptors (MC4R) on target neurons, including populations in the paraventricular hypothalamus. MC4R is a G-protein-coupled receptor whose canonical signalling includes stimulation of adenylyl cyclase through Gs and increased intracellular cyclic AMP. The relationship between this molecular signal and neuronal electrical activity depends on the target cell and its signalling machinery. Increased melanocortin signalling generally constrains feeding and participates in expenditure regulation. MC4R is not a receptor for leptin: leptin can alter the activity of cells upstream of melanocortin-responsive neurons.

AgRP opposes melanocortin signalling, acting as an antagonist and, in appropriate receptor assays, an inverse agonist. An antagonist limits the response to an agonist. An inverse agonist can also reduce constitutive receptor activity, meaning receptor signalling present even without added agonist. This distinction provides a molecular reason why an increase in AgRP and a reduction in α-MSH are not necessarily equivalent changes. Both can reduce melanocortin output, but the receptor state and the duration of peptide action can differ.

NPY and GABA introduce additional pathways. GABA can rapidly inhibit target neurons through inhibitory receptors; NPY acts through its own family of G-protein-coupled receptors. AgRP neurons therefore need not stimulate feeding exclusively by antagonising MC4R. An intervention that blocks one transmitter may alter the duration or magnitude of a response without eliminating it. This is a useful illustration of parallel signalling from a single neuronal population: cotransmitters can act on different targets and timescales.

A fasting animal commonly shows enhanced activity of AgRP neurons. The resulting pattern promotes food seeking and food intake, but the precise response depends on the animal's environment and the availability of food. The presence of a food cue can rapidly modify the activity of this population, even before nutrient absorption. Consequently, a recording showing reduced AgRP activity does not, by itself, establish that energy stores have been replenished. Neural activity is influenced by predicted as well as current physiological state.

The names AgRP and POMC identify neurons by particular gene products rather than by their only possible signal. Neurons are able to synthesise several biologically active substances, and the relative importance of those substances depends on the target cells and the time over which the response is measured. A fast transmitter released into a synaptic cleft can affect the membrane potential of the receiving cell over a short interval. A neuropeptide may exert an effect through a receptor coupled to intracellular signalling and can modify responses for a longer period. Thus the measured activity of an AgRP-expressing cell and the measured effect of purified AgRP applied to a receptor-bearing cell answer different questions. The former can reflect the combined action of several outputs; the latter isolates one molecular component of the circuit.

Consider the production of melanocortin peptides from POMC as an example of why gene expression cannot be equated with the amount of active transmitter. Translation of POMC produces a precursor, which must be processed in a cell-specific secretory environment. Cleavage by prohormone convertases and subsequent modifications generate biologically active peptides. Different cells express different processing machinery. The products found in the pituitary therefore need not be identical in relative abundance to those produced by hypothalamic POMC neurons. In particular, ACTH is an important pituitary product, whereas melanocortins such as alpha-MSH participate in central energy regulation. Detection of POMC messenger RNA in a tissue does not, on its own, establish which peptide reaches a target or in what quantity.

A receptor is not an enzyme that permanently consumes the agonist. Instead ligand binding favours receptor conformations associated with signalling. In the canonical MC4R pathway an agonist promotes signalling through a stimulatory G protein, Gs. Gs activates adenylyl cyclase, which catalyses formation of cyclic AMP from ATP. Cyclic AMP can then modify cellular processes through effectors such as protein kinase A. The relation between intracellular cyclic AMP and the output of a particular neuron is not fixed for every cell; other channels, signalling systems and synaptic inputs influence excitability. The important examination principle is to separate the identity of the receptor, its canonical second messenger and the final physiological output. A receptor that supports suppression of feeding is not necessarily an inhibitory receptor in the electrophysiological sense.

Constitutive activity means that some receptor signalling can occur in the absence of an added agonist. A neutral competitive antagonist reduces signalling in the presence of an agonist by competing with it, but in an idealised system it does not reduce the receptor's agonist-independent basal activity. An inverse agonist can reduce that basal activity by favouring a less active receptor conformation. If receptor-expressing cultured cells produce ten arbitrary units of cyclic AMP without added ligand, thirty units after agonist application and six units after addition of a candidate inverse agonist alone, the third observation is consistent with inverse agonism, provided nonspecific effects on cyclic AMP production have been excluded. In a separate experiment the same compound might reduce the agonist-induced signal by competition. The two observations are compatible but require different comparisons. This reasoning is particularly relevant to AgRP, whose actions at melanocortin receptors include antagonism and inverse agonism in appropriate assays.

A direct inhibitory synapse has a different causal logic from antagonism at a receptor for another peptide. When GABA opens an appropriate chloride-conducting receptor in a mature neuron, the response can restrain the generation of action potentials through changes in membrane potential or shunting of excitatory current. An inhibitory input to a neuron that itself limits feeding can consequently increase food intake. In circuit terms, inhibiting an inhibitor is disinhibition. Students sometimes assign the same sign to all steps of a pathway because the initial transmitter is inhibitory. Instead trace each interaction separately: suppressing activity in a feeding-restraining target can have a positive effect on feeding even though the synaptic influence itself is negative.

NPY acts through multiple receptor subtypes, which have distinct anatomical distributions and cellular effects. It is not valid to infer the function of every NPY-family signal from a single receptor. An inhibitory presynaptic receptor may decrease transmitter release from a feeding-promoting neuron, whereas a receptor on another target may produce a different net effect on ingestion. This distinction prepares the comparison with PYY, whose truncated form can engage the Y2 receptor. The shared family name does not imply that NPY and PYY have identical behavioural effects in all conditions. Receptor subtype, target cell, concentration and route of administration must all be specified.

A useful perturbation sequence is to compare three conditions: normal melanocortin signalling, selective reduction of MC4R function and activation of AgRP neurons in an animal with reduced MC4R function. If MC4R is already nonfunctional, a manipulation whose only action is to antagonise that receptor should have little further effect through that mechanism. However, activation of the whole AgRP neuronal population could still recruit GABAergic or NPY-mediated pathways and promote feeding. An observation of feeding in the third condition therefore does not prove that melanocortin receptors are irrelevant to energy regulation. It instead challenges the narrower claim that all outputs of AgRP neurons act exclusively through MC4R.

Opposing pathways do not need to be exact mirror images. A reduction in activity of one cell population can remove a brake on feeding while excitation of another can actively promote food seeking. Their responses to changes in hormonal state can occur on different timescales. The total behavioural outcome depends on downstream integration, not merely the arithmetic difference between numbers of spikes in the two populations. This distinction explains why a two-arrow diagram of arcuate control is helpful for recognising the principal directions of influence yet insufficient for predicting the response to every intervention. A scientifically defensible prediction states which component has changed and whether alternative pathways remain intact.

4. Leptin and the distinction between a signal and its receptor

White adipose tissue stores triglycerides and also functions as an endocrine organ. Its adipocytes secrete leptin, a protein hormone whose circulating concentration generally relates to adiposity but also responds to nutritional state and other regulatory influences. During energy deprivation, circulating leptin can fall before there has been a proportionate reduction in fat mass. This makes leptin particularly useful as a signal of inadequate energy availability, rather than merely a static indicator of fat quantity.

The long signalling isoform of the leptin receptor belongs to the cytokine receptor family. Receptor activation engages the associated tyrosine kinase JAK2 and signalling pathways including STAT3. Phosphorylated STAT3 can influence gene expression. Other intracellular pathways contribute to changes in neuronal activity and metabolism. The receptor does not itself have an intrinsic tyrosine kinase domain comparable to that of the insulin receptor. This distinction is important when mapping a physiological outcome to the correct signalling mechanism.

In the simplified arcuate model, leptin tends to restrain AgRP neuronal activity and promote melanocortin-associated anorexigenic signalling. These changes reduce the drive to acquire food when the signal indicates energy sufficiency. Leptin also influences endocrine and autonomic responses through additional neural populations. During fasting, a decline in leptin contributes to a coordinated adjustment that favours energy conservation and food acquisition. These responses should not be interpreted as a guarantee of constant body mass under all environmental conditions.

Classical mouse genetics clarified the difference between production of a circulating signal and responsiveness to it. In parabiosis, two animals share a circulation after surgical joining. Douglas Coleman's experiments with genetically obese mice indicated that the ob/ob animal lacked an effective circulating factor, whereas the db/db animal was unable to respond normally to the factor. The direction of the response in the partner animal was critical: sharing a circulation with an animal that produced the signal but could not respond could strongly suppress feeding in a responsive partner. Later identification of the ob gene by Zhang and colleagues in 1994 established the molecular identity of leptin. Mutations in the leptin receptor explain the classical db phenotype.

The reasoning generalises to endocrine systems. Supplying a missing ligand can restore signalling if the responding machinery remains functional. Supplying more ligand cannot ordinarily restore the absent function of a receptor that has lost its capacity to transmit that signal. However, a partial loss of function can differ from a complete loss, and an endocrine intervention may act through additional tissues or pathways. The expected response must be derived from the actual defect, not simply from the phenotype of the animal.

In many individuals with high adiposity, leptin is not deficient. High hormone concentration can coexist with reduced responsiveness of portions of the system, commonly described as leptin resistance. This term covers several potential mechanisms rather than a single universally demonstrated molecular defect. It is incorrect to infer that every person with obesity has a mutation in LEP or LEPR, or that a measurement of plasma leptin alone reveals the sensitivity of all target neurons.

The relationship between fat mass and circulating leptin is best understood by considering what an adipocyte does with an excess supply of energy. When fatty acids are incorporated into triglycerides, the chemical energy is retained in a form that can be mobilised later. Adipocytes also communicate aspects of their metabolic state to distant organs through secretion of signalling proteins. Leptin is important because the nervous system can receive information about a compartment located far from the brain, without directly measuring the triglyceride content of each adipocyte. The signal is not a simple weighing scale: both the number and condition of adipocytes and current endocrine and nutritional influences can change the amount of hormone released.

A fall in leptin during food deprivation can occur before a large change in measured fat mass. This observation helps explain why leptin is often considered a signal of energy availability as well as adiposity. For a short fasting experiment, an investigator should compare the change in hormone concentration with changes in tissue composition rather than assuming that the entire fall in circulating hormone is proportional to fat loss. Acute deprivation changes insulin secretion and the metabolic conditions under which adipocytes synthesise and release proteins. The resulting endocrine message can influence feeding and other energy-demanding functions before all energy reserves are depleted.

The receptor mechanism provides an opportunity to connect endocrine physiology with the principles of intracellular signalling. A cytokine-family receptor does not itself possess the intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity characteristic of the insulin receptor. The long leptin receptor isoform associates with a kinase, JAK2. Ligand binding promotes activation of associated signalling machinery and phosphorylation of receptor sites. These sites permit recruitment of signalling molecules, including STAT3. Activated STAT3 can enter the nucleus and alter transcription of target genes. Additional pathways can modify neuronal activity on shorter timescales. It is important not to describe the entire endocrine effect as a single obligatory change in transcription: leptin also acts through a distributed set of responsive cells, and changes in their electrical and synaptic behaviour contribute to its physiological action.

Receptor signalling can also induce mechanisms that limit its own duration. SOCS3 is an example of a protein that can be induced downstream of cytokine-related signalling and restrain components of the pathway. This is an intracellular negative feedback mechanism, distinct from the whole-body feedback in which increased energy stores change leptin secretion and hence feeding. Both processes involve a response that limits an initiating signal or deviation, but they operate at different levels of organisation. In an examination, increasing a molecular inhibitor of leptin signalling in a responsive neuron would be expected to weaken that particular response, not automatically to reduce leptin secretion from every adipocyte. A further causal chain must connect the altered neuronal response to changes in adiposity and hormone release.

The logic of the classic genetic comparison can be reconstructed before the molecular identities of the genes are introduced. Suppose strain A lacks a substance necessary for restraining food intake. Joining its circulation to that of a healthy animal should provide the missing factor if the affected strain still possesses the machinery required for responding to it. Strain B instead makes the substance but cannot respond appropriately. Connecting strain B to another animal with an intact response system may expose that partner to a strong signal while leaving strain B relatively unaffected. This is a prediction about the direction of a transferred endocrine effect, not about transfer of the genes themselves. The somatic cells in the affected animal retain their original genotype.

The ob and db mouse models fulfilled this conceptual distinction, which later molecular findings placed on a firmer basis. The former classical mutant has defective production of functional leptin, whereas the latter has impaired leptin receptor signalling. The molecular identification of the ob gene in 1994 established the identity of the hormone predicted by earlier physiological experiments. Neither a shared-circulation experiment nor a positional cloning experiment alone answers all questions about the normal feedback circuit. The first indicates that a circulating component is involved, and the second connects a gene to the phenotype. Receptor identification and tests of biological activity help bridge these findings to the mechanism of hormone-dependent regulation.

Exogenous leptin and a targeted receptor agonist would not be interchangeable interventions in all mutants. If the deficient component is the ligand, restoring an active ligand can permit signalling through the existing receptor. If the receptor is absent, a second molecule acting solely through that receptor has no functional target. Activating a downstream receptor or neuron directly could, in principle, bypass a defect in the upstream leptin pathway, although such a rescue would not reproduce every normal leptin-dependent function. This reasoning is useful in genetic epistasis questions: a phenotype corrected by a downstream intervention can support the ordering of pathway components, but a complex organism may contain parallel routes as well as the proposed linear chain.

Leptin resistance must be interpreted operationally. High plasma leptin is compatible with impaired leptin-dependent responses but does not independently prove which mechanism is impaired. Possible explanations include changes in hormone access to particular neurons, receptor signalling or downstream integration. An experiment measuring reduced phosphorylation of STAT3 after a defined leptin challenge tests a particular cellular response; an experiment measuring reduced suppression of food intake tests an integrated behavioural response. A defect in one does not establish the precise defect underlying the other. Comparing matched hormone exposure, tissue-specific responses and behavioural measurements gives a more informative interpretation than using circulating hormone concentration alone.

5. Insulin and ghrelin: distinct information from pancreas and stomach

Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to nutrient-related stimulation. Its peripheral actions coordinate uptake and storage of nutrients, while insulin signalling in the nervous system can influence feeding and the autonomic regulation of metabolism. The receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, and its downstream pathways include insulin receptor substrates and phosphoinositide 3-kinase signalling. Central and peripheral actions must be distinguished experimentally because changing circulating insulin also changes glucose and other metabolic variables.

In contrast to a stable long-term measure of adiposity, insulin concentration can change substantially during a meal. Basal insulin levels also reflect insulin sensitivity and nutritional state. It can therefore convey overlapping information about recent food intake and metabolic status. A high concentration does not necessarily mean that every tissue is responding strongly to insulin. The distinction between signal concentration and responsiveness, introduced for leptin, applies here as well.

Ghrelin is produced predominantly by endocrine cells of the stomach. Its acylated form activates the growth hormone secretagogue receptor, GHSR1a. Acylation by ghrelin O-acyltransferase is essential for this canonical receptor activation. Plasma ghrelin commonly increases before meals and declines after eating, although the pattern is influenced by meal composition, timing, metabolic state and individual variation. Ghrelin can stimulate AgRP-associated pathways and promote feeding. Its name reflects its discovery in relation to growth hormone release, not a restriction to pituitary regulation.

A pre-meal rise in a hormone may be a contributing cause of meal initiation, a response to an anticipated meal, or both. Measuring the hormone before a meal does not distinguish these possibilities. To test causality, investigators may change receptor signalling while controlling meal availability, or assess whether the hormonal response persists when scheduled feeding cues are altered. Ghrelin's action on feeding should not be described as the only mechanism of hunger. Food seeking can occur when ghrelin signalling is reduced because other neural, metabolic and sensory pathways remain.

The three signals can be contrasted without assigning each a rigid time category. Leptin often provides information about stored energy and recent energy deprivation; insulin responds strongly to nutrient exposure and metabolic status; ghrelin can provide information associated with anticipation of a meal and fasting. All are interpreted by cells within a distributed network. Their combined effect depends on receptor availability, access to the brain, prior state and the concurrent inputs arriving at those cells.

The insulin receptor is an instructive contrast with the leptin receptor. It belongs to the receptor tyrosine kinase family and, after activation, phosphorylates substrates that can recruit further signalling proteins. Phosphoinositide 3-kinase and Akt participate in important insulin responses. In peripheral tissues these pathways help regulate nutrient uptake, storage and metabolism, but neurons do not all use insulin for the same immediate purpose as skeletal muscle or adipocytes. Insulin-sensitive brain cells can change their electrical activity, transcriptional state or output to other cells. The final effect on behaviour depends on the circuitry in which the cells are located. It is therefore inaccurate to apply a statement about insulin-dependent GLUT4 trafficking in muscle directly to all hypothalamic neurons.

A physiological meal produces several signals at once. Glucose and other nutrients enter the circulation, pancreatic insulin secretion rises under suitable conditions, and gastrointestinal hormones and neural afferents report events in the digestive tract. If feeding subsequently decreases, an increase in insulin may be associated with that change even if other meal-derived signals make important causal contributions. To test an insulin-specific effect, one must alter insulin exposure or signalling while accounting for other consequences of the intervention. Peripheral insulin administration can lower blood glucose and thereby provoke counterregulatory responses, so a change in eating following such administration is not automatically evidence of a direct appetite-suppressing action in the brain. The direction of the integrated outcome can differ from the direction of one selected central pathway.

For conceptual purposes, a study comparing central insulin signalling in animals under matched glycaemic conditions can address a different question from a study that simply injects insulin into the peripheral circulation. Matching glucose reduces the possibility that a behavioural difference is explained only by hypoglycaemia. It does not remove every confounding influence: insulin may have effects on other tissues, and the procedure used to deliver or maintain concentrations may itself alter the organism. The central claim becomes stronger when receptor-dependent neuronal effects and appropriately controlled behavioural changes support the same interpretation. This is an illustration of why a hormone that regulates both metabolic and behavioural variables requires more than a single end-point measurement.

Ghrelin illustrates an additional layer of specificity: a post-translational modification changes the ability of a peptide to engage its canonical receptor. Ghrelin is synthesised as a precursor and processed; ghrelin O-acyltransferase attaches a fatty acyl group to the mature peptide at a characteristic serine residue. The acylated hormone can activate GHSR1a, a G-protein-coupled receptor. Measuring total ghrelin, which includes molecular forms differing in acylation, is not necessarily equivalent to measuring the concentration of receptor-active acyl-ghrelin. This issue is common in endocrine experimentation, in which an immunoassay may detect several related forms of a hormone but the functional response depends strongly on one of them.

GHSR1a is expressed in cells involved in energy regulation as well as in other physiological systems. Its canonical signalling includes coupling to Gq-related pathways, which can stimulate phospholipase C and downstream intracellular responses. The receptor's name reflects its relationship with growth hormone secretion, not an exclusive function in pituitary physiology. In the context of appetite regulation, ghrelin can enhance activity of feeding-associated pathways including AgRP neurons. Nevertheless, an experiment showing that ghrelin evokes eating does not establish that the effect is mediated exclusively by a particular arcuate cell. Receptor distribution, neural connections and the route of delivery all influence the interpretation.

The temporal pattern of ghrelin secretion is particularly interesting because a signal can be related to the expectation of food as well as the absence of ingested nutrients. Imagine an animal accustomed to receiving food at a regular time. If a ghrelin increase appears before the expected feeding period, two interpretations are possible: accumulated deprivation contributes to the increase, or the learned temporal pattern and associated physiology contribute to anticipation. Shifting the feeding schedule and comparing animals matched for deprivation can help distinguish these explanations. Observing a pre-meal increase is therefore consistent with a role in meal initiation but is not sufficient to establish that the hormone determines the timing of every spontaneous meal.

Another useful comparison concerns how nutritional state changes the meaning of a hormone concentration. A given insulin concentration may have different effects in animals with different insulin sensitivity. The same acyl-ghrelin concentration may occur in animals with different recent meal history, access to food or responses of downstream circuits. Leptin may be influenced by both energy stores and acute deprivation. A graph of hormone concentration against time is thus a description of an input, not a complete prediction of the output. To make a physiological prediction, identify the functional receptors and the relevant neuronal populations, then consider competing signals and the conditions of measurement.

Leptin, insulin and ghrelin can be arranged as a three-input model of central energy regulation, with the first two generally contributing information consistent with energy sufficiency and the third contributing information associated with the need or opportunity to obtain food. The model remains useful only if its simplifications are explicit. Insulin is a rapid nutrient-responsive signal as well as a longer-term metabolic correlate; leptin responds to acute negative energy balance; ghrelin influences functions beyond feeding. No universal algebraic rule says that doubling one hormone exactly cancels the effect of halving another. Neural responses can saturate and depend on the recent history of signalling. The benefit of comparing the three hormones lies in understanding the types of information conveyed and the mechanisms by which the signals enter the circuit.

6. Gastrointestinal signals and meal termination

During ingestion, the stomach expands and its contents begin to enter the small intestine. Neural receptors and enteroendocrine cells detect mechanical and chemical changes. Afferent signals report these changes to central circuits, permitting regulation before all nutrients are absorbed. This early feedback is essential because waiting for complete absorption would delay information about meal size.

Cholecystokinin (CCK) is released by intestinal I cells in response to nutrients, particularly products of fat and protein digestion. It participates in digestive coordination and can reduce meal size through mechanisms involving CCK1 receptors on vagal afferents. The pathway illustrates how a hormone released in one organ can influence a distant neural circuit indirectly: the hormone acts locally on a nerve ending whose axon transmits information to the brainstem. An experiment that interrupts vagal signalling may attenuate some effects of CCK, but the result must be interpreted in relation to the type and completeness of the interruption.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is released by intestinal enteroendocrine L cells in response to nutrient exposure. It supports glucose-dependent insulin secretion and also participates in regulation of gastric emptying and food intake. There are central GLP-1-producing neurons as well, so the location of hormone synthesis and the location of receptor activation must not be conflated. Endogenous intestinal GLP-1 is rapidly degraded in circulation; the effects of pharmacologically prolonged receptor activation cannot automatically be used to infer the normal reach or duration of endogenous signals.

Peptide YY (PYY) is another nutrient-responsive product of enteroendocrine cells, including L cells. Its processed form PYY3–36 can activate Y2 receptors and influence feeding-related pathways. The receptor subtype matters: members of the NPY receptor family can have different locations and consequences. A peptide belonging to the same broad signalling family as NPY does not necessarily increase intake in the same physiological setting.

Meal-related signals usually combine with mechanical feedback rather than replacing it. A stomach containing a large volume of nonnutritive material can activate distension-sensitive pathways without producing the same endocrine response as a nutrient-rich meal. A small-volume energy-dense meal may create a different combination of endocrine feedback and gastric pressure. Thus, both volume and nutrient content are potential explanatory variables in experiments comparing feeding responses. A meaningful comparison requires explicit control of whichever variable is not the primary object of study.

The terms satiation and satiety help organise these mechanisms. Reduced meal size following a nutrient-related signal suggests a change in satiation if the animal begins eating normally but terminates sooner. A longer interval before the next meal suggests enhanced post-meal satiety. Both observations can occur together, and neither alone demonstrates a persistent reduction in total energy intake because later compensatory changes may occur.

The stomach and intestine are positioned to report events that precede systemic nutrient availability. The mechanical expansion of a hollow organ gives information about its contents, but not necessarily their chemical energy. Distension-sensitive afferents can respond to the deformation of tissue, while cells in the intestine respond to the identity and concentration of nutrients and products of digestion. A meal can therefore evoke related mechanical and chemical signals even before much of its energy is available for tissue metabolism. The temporal advantage is important: if cessation of eating depended only on restoration of total body energy stores, feedback would arrive too late to regulate the size of an individual meal effectively.

Enteroendocrine cells are specialised epithelial cells that detect changes in the luminal or local tissue environment and release biologically active substances. Their distribution along the gastrointestinal tract is not uniform, and their hormone products need not circulate at high concentrations throughout the body to exert effects. Some substances act locally on neighbouring structures or sensory endings; others enter the bloodstream and can influence distant targets. This arrangement provides several routes by which nutrients can communicate with the nervous system. When a textbook calls an intestinal peptide a hormone, the term does not exclude important paracrine actions near its site of secretion.

CCK provides a useful example of how a meal-related peptide can coordinate digestion and behaviour. Products of fat and protein digestion stimulate secretion by intestinal I cells. The peptide contributes to pancreatic enzyme secretion and gallbladder contraction, helping the digestive system process the arriving meal. Through CCK1 receptor-dependent pathways, including those involving vagal sensory neurons, it can also contribute to limiting meal size. These actions are complementary: one adjusts digestive capacity while another influences the amount of material entering the system. They need not have identical dose-response relationships or use precisely the same target cells. The presence of a digestive response does not alone prove a behavioural response, and vice versa.

The timing of a CCK intervention matters. A compound that terminates an ongoing meal may have little effect on the interval before the next meal if the animal compensates for the smaller amount consumed. For a simple experiment, record the amount eaten before termination, the latency to begin the next meal and the total energy obtained over a full observation period. If only the first measure changes, the evidence supports an effect on satiation more directly than an effect on longer-term satiety. If the second also changes, evaluate whether the effect is independent of the smaller meal. This is an instance of a general principle: changing one stage of a physiological sequence changes the inputs to subsequent stages, so later effects can be indirect.

GLP-1 provides an example of why peptides with the same name must sometimes be distinguished by their source. Intestinal L cells release GLP-1 in response to nutrient-related stimulation, and the peptide supports nutrient-dependent endocrine responses, including potentiation of insulin secretion when glucose is elevated. There are also central neurons capable of producing GLP-1 from proglucagon-derived products. The existence of a peripheral peptide and a central population producing a related signal does not prove that the former travels directly to the latter. Experiments must distinguish local intestinal signalling, access to receptor-bearing targets from the circulation and activation of central sources. The next node develops the incretin aspect in detail; the central concern here is how signals associated with the presence of nutrients can contribute to regulation of ingestion.

Native active GLP-1 is susceptible to enzymatic inactivation, including cleavage by dipeptidyl peptidase-4. This limits the duration and distribution of active circulating peptide. A long-acting experimental agonist can engage a receptor for a much longer period than endogenous peptide and may reach targets differently. Thus a strong response to such an agonist demonstrates a capacity of the receptor-dependent system to produce that response but does not necessarily establish the relative importance of every route used by the physiological meal signal. This distinction prevents confusion between pharmacological sufficiency and normal endogenous regulation. A molecule can be valuable as an experimental probe without faithfully reproducing the complete spatial and temporal pattern of a native hormone.

PYY is released in several molecular forms; processing of PYY1–36 can generate PYY3–36, a form with preference for Y2 receptors among the receptor family relevant here. At appropriate sites Y2 receptors can reduce transmitter release from neurons, which offers a possible mechanism by which feeding-promoting signalling is restrained. This is not a contradiction of the fact that NPY itself can promote feeding: different members of a peptide family interact with different receptor subtypes and cellular populations. If a Y2 receptor antagonist weakens the response to PYY3–36 in a controlled study, the observation supports a contribution of Y2-dependent mechanisms under those conditions. It does not justify assigning all endogenous meal termination to PYY.

Mechanical and chemical feedback can be tested by varying meal composition independently of volume. For an illustrative design, compare a small nutrient-rich intragastric load with an equal-volume low-energy load, and then compare a larger low-energy load with the original low-energy load. The first comparison helps test the effect of nutrient composition when volume is controlled; the second helps test the effect of distension when energy content is approximately matched. Gastric emptying and osmolarity may differ and would need consideration in a real experiment. The strongest interpretation relies on several comparisons rather than assuming that equal volume produces identical mechanical signals or equal energy produces identical endocrine signals.

Meal termination and the subsequent interval before eating are also shaped by the energy deficit preceding the meal. A fixed nutrient load may inhibit eating differently in a severely deprived animal and one that has just eaten. This dependence is physiologically appropriate: a meal-related feedback signal must be interpreted in the context of longer-term requirements. If leptin-associated signalling changes the sensitivity of a network to gastrointestinal input, the integrated system can respond differently to an identical gastric stimulus without a change in the physical distension itself. Such interaction explains why separate short-term and long-term categories are convenient descriptions of typical signal timing, not independent control systems that never influence each other.

7. The vagus, NTS and ascending communication

The vagus nerve carries sensory and motor fibres. Afferent fibres transmit information towards the central nervous system; efferent fibres transmit commands towards peripheral targets. Direction is defined by the relationship to the CNS, not by whether a fibre is anatomically above or below an organ. The sensory component provides information about gastric distension, intestinal nutrient-related conditions and selected chemical signals.

Vagal afferent endings convert local stimuli into altered patterns of action potentials. Their central processes communicate with neurons in the nucleus of the solitary tract. Glutamatergic transmission is important at many of these sensory synapses. Brainstem processing can influence local autonomic reflexes, feeding movements and ascending signals to forebrain regions. The NTS is therefore neither merely a passive relay nor a separate controller operating independently of the hypothalamus.

Afferent activity is interpreted in context. For example, gastric expansion may be expected during a meal, while unusual visceral signals may produce avoidance or discomfort. Both situations can decrease food consumption. Circuit studies therefore need behavioural measurements beyond food weight alone. Latency to initiate eating, meal duration, movement and avoidance of a previously associated flavour are informative complementary variables. The goal is not to assign every reduction of intake to a normal satiety signal.

Some routes of communication use the bloodstream rather than a vagal synapse. Circulating compounds may act at specialised neurovascular regions or reach receptors by other routes. Distinguishing a direct central action from a vagal-mediated one requires appropriately designed interventions. Systemic administration demonstrates a whole-organism effect but does not identify the receptor-bearing cell or the communication route. A comparison of systemic and local interventions can narrow the possibilities, especially if peripheral changes in glucose, gastric emptying or movement are also measured.

The relationship between hindbrain and hypothalamic circuits is reciprocal. Information about recent nutrient exposure can alter forebrain responses, while descending signals can modify autonomic output and the interpretation of incoming sensory information. This organisation enables feeding behaviour to adapt to both immediate meal conditions and the longer-term state of energy reserves.

A sensory neuron of the vagus is commonly described as a pseudounipolar neuron because a process from its cell body divides into a peripheral and a central branch. The cell body is located in a sensory ganglion, not in the wall of the gut or in the nucleus of the solitary tract. A stimulus acting on the peripheral ending can alter firing transmitted along the central branch. The first central synapse of a selected pathway thus occurs in the brainstem, where transmitter release changes the activity of recipient neurons. This anatomical sequence is worth learning because it prevents the frequent reversal of afferent and efferent directions in diagrams of the gut–brain axis.

Mechanotransduction begins when physical changes at a sensory ending are translated into changes in membrane conductance. If the resulting electrical response reaches the conditions required for action potential generation, a train of impulses carries information towards the brainstem. The amplitude of each action potential does not continually increase in proportion to the size of the meal; the pattern of firing, the number of recruited afferents and the persistence of the stimulus are more relevant. A mechanosensitive pathway can therefore provide graded information while using regenerative electrical events. A different pathway can be activated by a peptide acting on a receptor, and both types of activity may converge upon central neurons.

Within the medulla, neurons receiving visceral input can also receive descending inputs from other regions. This architecture permits changes in the interpretation of gut information as the animal passes from one nutritional state to another. Suppose an identical distending stimulus produces a larger feeding-suppressing response after restoration of energy sufficiency than during prolonged deprivation. The mechanical receptor need not itself change sensitivity; the difference might instead arise in the processing of its output by central circuits. To distinguish these possibilities one could compare afferent responses to matched stimuli and central responses to comparable afferent activity. A change at the first stage and a change at a later stage predict different patterns of measurements.

The area postrema, adjacent to the NTS, is a circumventricular structure with specialised access to circulating substances. This contributes to the detection of certain blood-borne signals and to responses that may include protective avoidance of harmful ingestion. The dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus contains neurons contributing to parasympathetic output to visceral organs. Although these structures are anatomically close, they perform distinct functions. A schematic labelled only as a vagal centre conceals the distinction between detecting a peripheral event, processing it within the brain and returning a neural command to an organ. Their close arrangement is useful for coordinating digestion and ingestion with local visceral conditions.

A reduction in eating caused by malaise is not equivalent to physiological satiation. Animals may avoid a food whose taste they associate with an unpleasant internal consequence; a conditioned avoidance test can therefore help distinguish some aversive effects from ordinary meal limitation. Reduced locomotion or general disruption of behaviour may also reduce access to food. No single control completely settles this distinction, and the absence of a particular avoidance response does not prove the absence of every adverse internal sensation. A convincing investigation combines behavioural measures with appropriate physiological evidence. This issue is especially important when an experimental intervention targets a structure that participates in both ingestion and protective visceral responses.

Loss-of-function studies on vagal signalling demand special care. Interruption of a mixed nerve can alter both sensory communication and motor control of gastrointestinal function, leading to changes in gastric emptying or digestion. If the feeding response to an intestinal peptide is altered after such an intervention, this is evidence that the intact nerve contributes to the observed response, but it does not identify a particular sensory receptor without further experiments. Selective interference with a receptor or sensory population and measurement of gut physiology can provide more specific support. A residual response may indicate incomplete disruption or an alternative route, rather than failure of the entire gut–brain model.

8. Homeostatic regulation, reward and anticipation

Food has sensory properties that can influence its acquisition independently of an immediate physiological deficit. The term hedonic regulation refers to the contributions of reward-related and motivational processes, rather than an alternative system wholly disconnected from homeostatic control. Hunger changes how attractive food cues are, and the cues themselves can influence circuits that respond to nutritional state.

Dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area and their projections to the nucleus accumbens participate in learning, motivation and the evaluation of rewarding stimuli. Dopamine signalling should not be equated directly with the subjective pleasure of eating. An animal may work to acquire a food without displaying a proportionately increased hedonic reaction to its taste. These behavioural variables measure different components of reward, and experimentally manipulating dopamine can affect learning and action initiation as well as feeding.

Learned associations are particularly important when food availability follows a regular pattern. A cue that previously predicted food can evoke anticipatory behaviour. Chen and colleagues reported in 2015 that food-associated sensory information rapidly changed activity in arcuate feeding circuits in mice before food consumption. This demonstrates that neural responses associated with energy deficit can be modified by the prospect of obtaining food. It does not demonstrate immediate restoration of energy stores by seeing food. The distinction between predictive and restorative signals is therefore essential.

One can design a teaching experiment in which a fasting mouse is presented with visible food behind a barrier, then the barrier is removed in a second trial. If neuronal activity changes when food becomes visible, before ingestion, the early response cannot be attributed to absorption of the newly presented nutrients. If the activity returns towards its fasting pattern when the cue is removed and no meal occurs, the pattern supports the interpretation that access to food was predicted rather than achieved. Control conditions should account for handling, unrelated sensory stimulation and the animal's prior experience.

The homeostatic and reward-related contributions to feeding converge on behaviour, but one should not divide a measured meal into a fixed percentage of biological hunger and a fixed percentage of reward. The relative contributions change with context. Sensory properties, learning, arousal and physiological state influence the same organism at the same time.

An animal can learn that a sensory event predicts a nutrient outcome. Before learning, a neutral sound may have little effect on food-directed behaviour; after repeated association with access to food, the same sound can elicit anticipation. The association does not require the sound to carry energy itself. Its relevance is that the organism can prepare for a future event by responding to information obtained in the past. This is a feedforward process that interacts with feedback from the nutrients subsequently obtained. If the anticipated food is no longer available, the usefulness of the cue declines and the learned response may be modified by new experience.

Reward-related processes can be separated conceptually into the attractiveness of a stimulus, the motivation to work to obtain it and the learning that links actions or cues to outcomes. These distinctions help prevent the simplistic use of dopamine as a synonym for pleasure. A change in the strength of an animal's food-directed effort does not automatically demonstrate that the sensory enjoyment of food has changed. For example, an animal may consume an available food normally while making fewer responses to obtain the same food in a task requiring effort. The two observations imply that performance of a motivated action and consumption once food is available should be considered separately.

Physiological need can change the significance of a learned cue. The smell of food may be highly effective in promoting approach behaviour after a period of deprivation and much less effective immediately after a meal. The physical stimulus is identical, but its behavioural value changes with internal state. This interaction is central to understanding why homeostatic and hedonic feeding are not mutually exclusive categories of meals. The same neural system that processes the significance of an external event can be influenced by information about the current condition of the organism. It is therefore possible for endocrine and reward-related mechanisms to contribute to a single behavioural decision.

The finding that food-associated stimuli can rapidly suppress activity of AgRP neurons before consumption challenges an overly simple account in which neural hunger activity must remain high until energy has entered the circulation. Such a response is understandable if sensory information is used to predict the resolution of a deficit. The prediction should be distinguished from its confirmation. A food cue may briefly alter a neural recording, but sustained changes can depend on nutrient delivery and longer-term restoration of energy availability. The rapidity of the response constrains the mechanism: nutrients cannot have been absorbed from food that has not yet been eaten. It therefore supplies evidence for an anticipatory contribution without excluding slower feedback.

An illustrative recording experiment could compare four trials: an inaccessible nonfood object, an inaccessible familiar food, a familiar food that can be consumed and an unfamiliar edible object. The nonfood object controls for some aspects of sensory novelty. Inaccessible familiar food helps isolate prediction from nutrient consumption, while allowing ingestion reveals later changes associated with the meal. The unfamiliar edible object can help examine the role of prior experience, although differences in smell or attractiveness complicate this comparison. To interpret these trials, compare neural activity before the cue, immediately after its presentation and after sufficient time for gastrointestinal feedback. Changes confined to different intervals support different classes of mechanism.

Temporal structure also matters for experiments involving stimulation of AgRP neurons. A neural population can help establish a motivational state before eating begins, even if natural activity declines when food is found. A brief activation can initiate changes in downstream signalling that outlast the stimulation, while a prolonged manipulation may produce a different pattern of behaviour. The causal claim that stimulating a population promotes feeding is therefore compatible with the observation that its natural firing changes before or during a meal. One experiment perturbs a pathway and measures an outcome, while another records the normal activity of that pathway. Their apparent disagreement can reveal the temporal organisation of the circuit.

9. Autonomic output and adaptive energy expenditure

The brain regulates more than the decision to eat. Descending pathways influence sympathetic and parasympathetic outputs, endocrine secretion and behaviour that determines how much energy is expended. The hypothalamus can therefore coordinate the availability of incoming nutrients with the distribution and use of existing reserves.

Sympathetic nerves participate in regulating brown adipose thermogenesis. Norepinephrine released from sympathetic endings activates adrenergic signalling in brown adipocytes, promoting mobilisation and oxidation of fuels. Uncoupling protein 1 permits proton conductance across the inner mitochondrial membrane, reducing the proportion of the proton-motive force used for ATP synthesis and increasing heat production. The mechanism is especially important in the thermoregulatory physiology of small mammals. Its quantitative contribution to total human energy expenditure varies with environmental and physiological conditions.

A fall in energy availability can produce adaptive adjustments in expenditure as well as increased feeding motivation. Some responses reflect a reduction in the metabolically active tissue mass of the body after prolonged energy deficit, whereas others reflect changes in regulation that are not explained solely by tissue mass. Lower leptin contributes to changes in neuroendocrine signalling associated with conserving energy. Activity and thermogenesis can change in context-dependent ways, so a reduction in expenditure should not automatically be attributed to a single hormone.

Autonomic effects are tissue-specific. A change in sympathetic outflow to brown adipose tissue does not necessarily imply an identical change in sympathetic outflow to the heart or liver. The nervous system uses different efferent pathways, and the physiological responses of tissues depend on their receptors and metabolic state. Measurements of heart rate cannot establish the magnitude of thermogenic sympathetic output without further evidence.

Central regulation of glucose production can also interact with feeding circuits. This is a link to the next node, which examines the gut–pancreas–liver axis. The present focus is on the principle that a neural population can affect several physiological outputs and that feeding behaviour alone cannot reveal the total metabolic effect of manipulating that population.

Total energy expenditure includes the energy needed to sustain essential physiological functions, the energetic cost of processing food, physical activity and adaptive changes in heat production. These categories are useful for accounting but do not all respond independently to a given perturbation. If an animal eats less, the energetic cost of processing food may also fall. If it moves less, muscular energy use changes. A neural intervention can therefore affect expenditure directly through autonomic or endocrine output and indirectly through behaviour and feeding. Determining the relative contributions requires measurements beyond the difference between initial and final body mass.

The sympathetic pathway to thermogenic adipocytes provides a clear mechanistic example. An increase in sympathetic activity can increase release of norepinephrine at the target tissue. Adrenergic receptors can activate intracellular signalling that supports fuel mobilisation and thermogenic processes. In brown adipocytes, UCP1 in the inner mitochondrial membrane allows a portion of the proton motive force to be dissipated without the corresponding synthesis of ATP. Electron transport and substrate oxidation can continue as energy is released as heat. The important distinction is between the generation of a proton gradient by respiratory activity and the use or dissipation of that gradient. UCP1 does not directly hydrolyse stored triglycerides, and norepinephrine does not itself transport protons across the mitochondrial membrane.

Ambient temperature changes how thermogenesis contributes to measured energy expenditure. An animal maintained below its thermoneutral range must expend energy to defend body temperature; a change in sympathetic thermogenic output may therefore have a larger measurable effect under cool conditions than in a warmer environment. A comparison between genetically different groups without matching thermal conditions can confuse the contribution of a neural pathway with the response to environmental demand. Similarly, a result obtained in a small laboratory rodent with substantial thermogenic requirements cannot be converted directly into an expected quantitative effect in a human. The general mechanism is transferable, but its relative importance must be assessed in the organism and condition being studied.

The response to sustained energy deficit illustrates coordinated allocation of limited resources. Feeding motivation can increase while energy-demanding physiological functions are adjusted. Some endocrine responses associated with low energy availability contribute to reductions in energy expenditure or in investment in processes that are less immediately essential for survival. For example, thyroid-related and reproductive neuroendocrine functions may be altered during substantial deprivation. This does not imply that all endocrine output declines uniformly or that a single hormone produces every adaptation. Different feedback loops respond to different inputs, and the timing and magnitude of their adjustments depend on the duration and severity of the deficit.

A distinction is needed between a reduction in energy expenditure expected from loss of metabolically active tissue and a reduction beyond that expected for the altered body composition. If an animal loses tissue mass, maintaining the smaller body may require less energy even in the absence of any special adaptive response. A further change in expenditure after accounting for relevant body composition, activity and environmental conditions can provide evidence consistent with adaptive metabolic regulation. Normalising expenditure simply by total body mass can be misleading because the energy requirements of adipose tissue and other tissues differ. The appropriate comparison depends on the biological question and the available measurements.

The parasympathetic nervous system contributes to the regulation of visceral organs, including aspects of gastrointestinal motility and secretion. Its participation illustrates that central energy control involves preparation for nutrient processing as well as the regulation of expenditure. It is nevertheless incorrect to treat parasympathetic output as a universal signal that stores energy and sympathetic output as a universal signal that consumes it. Both divisions contain pathways to multiple organs, and their activities may be patterned differently according to current demands. A change in the direction of a single autonomic output should be stated together with the target organ and the expected physiological response.

10. Quantifying energy balance and interpreting intake

Let E denote the chemical energy stored in the body, measured in kilojoules, I the metabolizable energy acquired per unit time and X total energy expenditure per unit time. Over a sufficiently short interval in which average rates are meaningful, the change in stored energy is given by ΔE = (I − X)Δt. More generally, the difference in rates must be integrated over the interval. This accounting relationship does not specify how intake or expenditure is regulated.

Consider an illustrative animal with mean metabolizable energy intake of 80 kJ per day and mean energy expenditure of 74 kJ per day over three days. Its energy balance is positive by 6 kJ per day. Multiplying by three gives a gain of 18 kJ in stored energy under the assumptions of the model. If a neuronal intervention reduces daily intake to 70 kJ but also reduces expenditure to 64 kJ, the net balance remains positive by 6 kJ per day. These hypothetical values show why reduced consumption alone does not establish reduced energy storage.

Food mass and energy intake are distinct measurements. Suppose two diets provide 12 and 20 kJ of metabolizable energy per gram respectively. Consumption of 5 g of the first diet provides 60 kJ; consumption of 4 g of the second provides 80 kJ. The animal consuming less mass receives more energy. Experimental food-intake records should therefore specify diet composition and, when appropriate, metabolizable energy density. Spillage and evaporation can also affect measured disappearance of food.

For meal-pattern analysis, total intake over a day can be expressed as the sum of the energy acquired at each meal. A signal that decreases average meal size may provoke a compensatory increase in meal frequency. For example, four meals of 15 kJ each provide the same daily intake as six meals of 10 kJ each. If meal frequency is not measured, a decrease in meal size can be incorrectly interpreted as a sustained decrease in daily energy intake.

Energy expenditure can be estimated using indirect calorimetry, which measures respiratory gas exchange and applies relationships between substrate oxidation and energy release. Oxygen consumption is informative, but it is not an identical measure of energy expenditure regardless of substrate. Comparisons across animals of different body composition also require care: simple division by body mass may create artefacts. Detailed interpretation of gas exchange and metabolic flexibility belongs to a later node. Here the essential principle is to distinguish a measured quantity, a derived estimate and the physiological process being inferred.

Energy accounting can be applied to an intervention that changes both feeding and heat production. Consider two hypothetical groups observed for five days. Group A receives an average of 60 kJ of metabolizable energy daily and expends 57 kJ daily. Group B receives 54 kJ daily and expends 49 kJ daily. Group A retains an average of 3 kJ per day, equivalent to 15 kJ across the observation period. Group B retains 5 kJ per day, equivalent to 25 kJ. Despite its lower intake, Group B has the larger positive energy balance. This example is deliberately simplified, but it demonstrates why an appetite-suppressing effect alone does not determine the direction of changes in stored energy. The interpretation depends on changes in both rates.

A second calculation concerns the contributions of meal size and meal frequency. Suppose one animal consumes six meals each supplying 10 kJ, giving 60 kJ in total. After an intervention the mean meal energy is 8 kJ and the animal consumes seven meals. Daily intake is now 56 kJ, a decrease of 4 kJ or about 6.7 percent, even though the mean meal energy declined by 20 percent. The increased number of meals partially compensates for the smaller meals. If meal energy and frequency are both measured, one can locate a plausible behavioural contribution to the change in total intake. If only daily intake is measured, the compensatory change in meal frequency is hidden. All values in this example are hypothetical, not observations from a published experiment.

The definition of a meal must also be consistent across groups. Automated food intake measurements may record small episodes of eating separated by short pauses. An investigator often groups those episodes using a defined interval separating meals. Changing the interval used to define a meal can change the calculated mean meal size and meal frequency even if the raw measurements are unchanged. For analytical questions, a comparison is valid only if the criterion is applied consistently or the influence of the criterion is explicitly evaluated. Meal structure is a derived behavioural measure, not an inherent label supplied by the animal.

A measurement of disappearance of food from a container is not necessarily a measurement of ingestion. Spillage, fragmentation and food hoarding may produce a discrepancy. Equal masses of foods with different water content and energy density can also represent very different energy inputs. If an animal loses 2 g from a food container but 0.5 g is recovered as spillage, a corrected estimate of consumption is 1.5 g. For a diet with metabolizable energy density of 16 kJ per gram this corresponds to 24 kJ, not 32 kJ. This simple correction illustrates how a measurement error can propagate through an energy balance calculation and generate a false physiological conclusion.

Indirect calorimetry is useful for distinguishing intake effects from changes in expenditure, but it estimates energy use from respiratory exchange rather than measuring the energy stored in the body directly. During changing metabolic conditions, gas exchange may be affected by factors that complicate interpretation of substrate oxidation. The experiment should also account for physical activity, temperature and differences in body composition when comparing groups. A ratio of expenditure to total body mass is not a substitute for all these controls. In a question involving a neural perturbation, the most defensible inference is generally the one that matches the measured variables without attributing an expenditure difference to a specific organ solely on the basis of whole-body gas exchange.

11. Experimental evidence and causal inference

An important advance in appetite physiology came from identifying neurons by molecular characteristics instead of destroying entire regions of the brain. Aponte, Atasoy and Sternson reported in 2011 that optogenetic activation of AgRP neurons in mice could rapidly evoke feeding. Optogenetics uses a genetically introduced light-responsive protein to influence selected cells with light. The result demonstrates sufficiency under the conditions of the experiment: stimulating the targeted population can produce a feeding response. It does not establish that those cells are the only possible source of feeding motivation.

Necessity is a different causal question. A population is necessary for a specified response under specified conditions if suppressing its function prevents or substantially impairs that response. A population can be sufficient but not necessary if parallel circuits can produce the same output. Conversely, a cell population may be necessary for a complex behaviour without being sufficient to initiate it in isolation. Examination questions frequently confuse the two forms of evidence.

Targeted gene disruption provides a second approach. Huszar and colleagues reported in 1997 that disruption of MC4R in mice produced a syndrome that included excessive feeding and increased body mass. This supports an important role for MC4R in normal energy regulation. A whole-body germline mutation acts throughout development, however, and its resulting phenotype can reflect changes in several populations or compensatory responses. Cell-specific and adult-onset perturbations can help separate these possibilities.

Interpretation of recordings also requires attention to what is actually measured. A calcium-dependent fluorescence signal reflects changes in intracellular calcium and is often used as an indirect measure of neural activity. It does not report every action potential with perfect fidelity. Movement, changes in fluorescence unrelated to calcium and differences in reporter expression can influence the measurement. Appropriate controls and independent validation strengthen the link between observed fluorescence and neuronal activity.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which mice expressing a light-sensitive excitatory protein in AgRP neurons eat more during light delivery than mice expressing an inactive fluorescent control protein. All groups receive identical light delivery and access to the same food. This contrast supports an effect of the specific neural manipulation rather than light exposure alone. If the intervention also increases general movement, food-directed behaviour should be examined to determine whether the increased intake reflects targeted feeding motivation or merely altered access to food.

Combining genetic, endocrine and behavioural evidence produces a stronger explanation than relying on any one technique. The goal is to trace the chain from peripheral signal to receptor, neuronal response and physiological outcome, while stating which links were measured and which remain inferred. Rodent experiments establish powerful mechanistic principles, but the magnitude and context of behavioural responses in humans cannot be assumed to be identical.

The logic of sufficiency and necessity can be demonstrated without memorising the names of experimental techniques. If activation of a selected neuronal population in an otherwise fed animal evokes food-directed behaviour, the activation is sufficient to promote that behaviour under the experimental conditions. If inhibition of the population in a deprived animal prevents an otherwise reliable feeding response, its activity is necessary for that response under those conditions. Neither proposition automatically implies the other. A system may contain two partially redundant routes, each capable of promoting an outcome when activated but neither individually essential for every instance of that outcome.

Optogenetic activation is based on the expression of a light-sensitive protein in selected cells. When the appropriate stimulus is applied, the protein can alter ionic conductance and thereby influence neuronal activity. The specificity of the result depends on the restriction of expression and stimulation to the relevant cells, as well as appropriate control conditions. A control group exposed to comparable stimulation but lacking the functional light-sensitive protein helps account for effects of the procedure itself. A separate check of neuronal activation strengthens the conclusion that the intended perturbation actually occurred. These are conceptual experimental design principles rather than instructions for a specialised laboratory procedure.

Genetic deletion of a receptor differs from a short-lived reduction in neuronal activity because the receptor may be absent throughout development. Developmental compensation can modify the eventual phenotype, and effects in cells outside the circuit of interest can contribute to the measured outcome. A cell-selective deletion or a change introduced in adulthood can help narrow the interpretation, while restoration of receptor function in a selected population may help test the contribution of that population. A partial rescue is informative: it can indicate that a particular component contributes to the phenotype while other defects remain. It does not necessarily imply that the restored population accounts for every action of the receptor.

Pair-feeding provides a useful design when body mass or expenditure changes after a manipulation that alters intake. Consider an experimental group consuming less food than untreated controls. A second control group can receive an energy intake matched to that of the experimental group. If both groups show similar changes in stored energy, the reduced intake may account for a substantial part of the response. If their expenditure or body composition differs despite matched energy intake, other effects should be investigated. Pair-feeding does not perfectly match meal timing, stress, nutrient absorption or spontaneous behaviour unless these aspects are also addressed. Thus it strengthens a specific inference without eliminating every difference between groups.

A simple hypothetical receptor experiment can separate signalling from nonspecific effects. Cultured cells expressing MC4R are compared with cells lacking the receptor. An agonist increases cyclic AMP in the receptor-bearing cells but does not increase it in the receptor-negative control. A candidate antagonist reduces the agonist response in the receptor-bearing cells. Before inferring receptor-specific antagonism, the experiment should establish that the candidate does not generally suppress cyclic AMP production or compromise cell viability. A response to a different stimulus activating the same intracellular enzyme can be useful for this comparison. This is a molecular counterpart of the whole-organism experiments: the interpretation becomes stronger when alternative explanations are tested.

Finally, distinguish experimental evidence obtained in rodents from the physiological conclusions that can be drawn about humans. Controlled genetic and neuronal experiments in mice can establish causation under defined conditions with a degree of specificity that is rarely available in humans. Human genetic variants and carefully characterised physiological responses can provide converging evidence that related mechanisms operate in people. But a behavioural response in a mouse does not by itself establish the precise subjective sensation of a human, nor does it define a universal quantitative response to an intervention. The most useful examination answer states the organism, the intervention, the outcome and the limit of the inference before integrating it with the wider model.

Common misconceptions

Incorrect assumption: Hunger equals hypoglycaemia. Correction: Hunger is influenced by several neural and endocrine inputs; normal circulating glucose does not exclude feeding motivation. Examination reasoning: A test may hold glucose constant and alter leptin or cues; predict behaviour from all changed inputs. Why the trap arises: Glucose is an easily measured fuel, so it is tempting to confuse one circulating substrate with stored energy.

Incorrect assumption: Satiety and satiation are identical. Correction: Satiation limits an ongoing meal; satiety delays subsequent intake. Examination reasoning: Distinguish meal size from intermeal interval. Why the trap arises: Everyday language uses the two words interchangeably, although physiological experiments distinguish separate phases.

Incorrect assumption: All hypothalamic neurons share one function. Correction: Nuclei contain diverse cells and circuits. Examination reasoning: Do not infer cell-specific function from a lesion. Why the trap arises: Early lesion diagrams label entire regions, which obscures cell and pathway heterogeneity.

Incorrect assumption: POMC is a mature peptide. Correction: POMC is a precursor processed into several peptides. Examination reasoning: Precursor abundance cannot substitute for secretion measurements. Why the trap arises: The precursor and its mature products are often shown in a single pathway arrow.

Incorrect assumption: Leptin directly binds MC4R. Correction: Leptin acts through LEPR and modulates upstream pathways. Examination reasoning: Identify ligand and receptor separately. Why the trap arises: Leptin and melanocortin activation both tend to restrain feeding, which encourages confusion of pathway stages.

Incorrect assumption: AgRP neurons use only AgRP. Correction: NPY and GABA are important cotransmitters. Examination reasoning: Blocking MC4R interaction need not abolish every AgRP-neuron effect. Why the trap arises: A population named for a single marker can appear to use only that marker as its signal.

Incorrect assumption: All obesity implies low leptin. Correction: High adiposity commonly accompanies elevated leptin. Examination reasoning: Distinguish deficiency from response impairment. Why the trap arises: Classical ligand-deficient mice are memorable but do not represent all obesity mechanisms.

Incorrect assumption: LEPR is an intrinsic tyrosine kinase. Correction: The long receptor isoform recruits JAK2. Examination reasoning: Compare receptor classes with insulin signalling. Why the trap arises: Both insulin and leptin can induce tyrosine phosphorylation, but they recruit catalytic activity differently.

Incorrect assumption: Ghrelin is always the sole initiator of eating. Correction: Meal timing, sensory cues and other physiological signals contribute. Examination reasoning: A ghrelin perturbation need not abolish all intake. Why the trap arises: A common pre-meal hormonal pattern is easily mistaken for an obligatory initiating event.

Incorrect assumption: All GLP-1 comes from gut. Correction: Central neurons can also produce GLP-1. Examination reasoning: Localise the source before inferring the route. Why the trap arises: Gut-derived incretin action is often introduced before central peptide-producing cells.

Incorrect assumption: The vagus is exclusively motor. Correction: It includes sensory afferents and motor efferents. Examination reasoning: Identify signal direction before interpreting interruption experiments. Why the trap arises: Textbook autonomic diagrams emphasise motor output and can omit sensory limbs.

Incorrect assumption: Any reduced intake proves satiation. Correction: Malaise and locomotor impairment can reduce intake. Examination reasoning: Evaluate alternative behavioural outcomes. Why the trap arises: A total-intake endpoint hides changes in motor performance and aversion.

Incorrect assumption: Dopamine is simply pleasure. Correction: Dopaminergic systems participate in motivation and learning as well. Examination reasoning: Separate approach from taste-reactivity measures. Why the trap arises: Popular accounts often use reward as a synonym for a pleasurable sensation.

Incorrect assumption: Less food mass always means less energy. Correction: Energy density can differ. Examination reasoning: Multiply mass by metabolizable energy density. Why the trap arises: Food mass is convenient to measure but ignores differences in chemical composition and water.

Incorrect assumption: Reduced intake guarantees negative balance. Correction: Expenditure may decrease at the same time. Examination reasoning: Calculate intake minus expenditure. Why the trap arises: Intake is the visible behavioural output, while expenditure is harder to observe.

Incorrect assumption: Sufficiency proves necessity. Correction: A selectively activated pathway may be redundant in natural behaviour. Examination reasoning: Distinguish activation and inhibition experiments. Why the trap arises: A dramatic activation result may seem decisive about normal physiological requirement.

Incorrect assumption: A calcium signal equals an exact spike count. Correction: Fluorescence is an indirect activity measure. Examination reasoning: Check reporter calibration and movement artefacts. Why the trap arises: Fluorescence traces resemble electrical recordings even though the reporter has its own dynamics.

Original examination practice

Part B — core concepts

1. Which variable is an accounting measure rather than a regulatory process?

  1. Hunger
  2. Energy balance
  3. Satiety
  4. Satiation
Answer and explanation

B. Energy balance — Energy balance is the difference between metabolizable intake and expenditure; the other terms describe behavioural regulation. The other three terms describe motivation or control of eating rather than a signed energy difference.

2. Which nucleus contains well-characterised AgRP/NPY neurons?

  1. Arcuate nucleus
  2. Cochlear nucleus
  3. Suprachiasmatic nucleus
  4. Red nucleus
Answer and explanation

A. Arcuate nucleus — The arcuate nucleus includes AgRP/NPY and POMC populations. Other nuclei have distinct primary roles. The SCN is associated with circadian rhythms; cochlear and red nuclei concern auditory and motor systems.

3. What is POMC?

  1. A monosaccharide
  2. A leptin receptor
  3. A peptide precursor
  4. An adrenergic receptor
Answer and explanation

C. A peptide precursor — POMC is proteolytically processed into bioactive peptides, including α-MSH in relevant neurons. A precursor can yield multiple products; neither a receptor nor a sugar can account for POMC peptide processing.

4. Which peptide directly activates MC4R in the classical model?

  1. CCK
  2. Leptin
  3. α-MSH
  4. Insulin
Answer and explanation

C. α-MSH — α-MSH is a melanocortin agonist; the other hormones signal through their own receptors. CCK, leptin and insulin each have separate receptors and act upstream or in parallel.

5. Which peptide counteracts MC4R signalling?

  1. Orexin
  2. CCK
  3. GLP-1
  4. AgRP
Answer and explanation

D. AgRP — AgRP is an endogenous melanocortin antagonist/inverse agonist, unlike the other listed peptides. GLP-1, orexin and CCK act through their own receptors; none is the endogenous MC4R antagonist in this model.

6. Which transmitter is also released by many AgRP neurons?

  1. Acetylcholine exclusively
  2. Adrenaline exclusively
  3. Thyroxine
  4. GABA
Answer and explanation

D. GABA — Many AgRP neurons are GABAergic. Thyroxine is an endocrine hormone, not their conventional transmitter. The alternatives incorrectly claim exclusive adrenergic or cholinergic transmission or confuse a thyroid hormone with a synaptic transmitter.

7. The canonical MC4R pathway involves which second messenger?

  1. cGMP exclusively
  2. cAMP
  3. IP3 exclusively
  4. ATP secretion as sole messenger
Answer and explanation

B. cAMP — MC4R canonically couples to Gs and adenylyl cyclase; additional context-dependent mechanisms do not negate this. IP3 and cGMP are second messengers in other pathways; ATP is the substrate of adenylyl cyclase, not the canonical downstream messenger here.

8. What is the principal source of circulating leptin?

  1. Erythrocytes
  2. Gastric parietal cells
  3. Pancreatic alpha cells
  4. Adipocytes
Answer and explanation

D. Adipocytes — White adipose tissue is a major endocrine source of leptin, not just a fuel store. Parietal cells secrete acid, alpha cells produce glucagon and erythrocytes do not serve as leptin-producing endocrine cells.

9. Which kinase is associated with the long leptin receptor isoform?

  1. DNA polymerase
  2. Intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase of LEPR
  3. JAK2
  4. Pyruvate kinase
Answer and explanation

C. JAK2 — LEPR is a cytokine-family receptor that engages JAK2; it does not possess intrinsic receptor kinase activity. Pyruvate kinase is a metabolic enzyme and DNA polymerase copies DNA; neither accounts for the receptor-associated signalling kinase.

10. The insulin receptor differs from LEPR because it has what feature?

  1. A catalytic RNA domain
  2. Intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity
  3. A GABA channel pore
  4. A ligand-binding domain only in nuclei
Answer and explanation

B. Intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity — Insulin binds a receptor tyrosine kinase; LEPR instead recruits associated kinases. The insulin receptor is a membrane-spanning protein kinase, not a nuclear-only receptor, ribozyme or GABA channel.

11. Which modification enables canonical ghrelin activation of GHSR1a?

  1. Glycogen branching
  2. Acylation
  3. Deacetylation of histones
  4. DNA methylation
Answer and explanation

B. Acylation — Ghrelin O-acyltransferase modifies ghrelin; the acylated peptide activates the classical receptor. The modification is a fatty acyl attachment to the peptide, not DNA or histone regulation or glycogen processing.

12. Which organ is the major source of circulating ghrelin?

  1. Spleen
  2. Thyroid
  3. Stomach
  4. Adrenal medulla
Answer and explanation

C. Stomach — Ghrelin is produced predominantly by gastric endocrine cells, with additional sources. The other listed organs have important functions but are not the dominant source of circulating ghrelin.

13. Which intestinal endocrine cell class produces CCK?

  1. Pancreatic beta cells
  2. I cells
  3. Gastric chief cells
  4. Erythrocytes
Answer and explanation

B. I cells — I cells release CCK in response to nutrients including lipid and protein digestion products. Beta cells release insulin and chief cells release pepsinogen; erythrocytes are not intestinal endocrine cells.

14. Which receptor mediates an important vagal action of CCK?

  1. CCK1 receptor
  2. Insulin receptor
  3. MC4R
  4. LEPR
Answer and explanation

A. CCK1 receptor — CCK1 receptors on vagal sensory pathways participate in meal-related feedback. MC4R, LEPR and insulin receptor recognise other signals, rather than mediating the classical CCK action.

15. Which hormone is produced by intestinal L cells and has an incretin action?

  1. GLP-1
  2. Cortisol
  3. AgRP
  4. NPY
Answer and explanation

A. GLP-1 — GLP-1 potentiates glucose-dependent insulin secretion and participates in feeding regulation. Cortisol is a steroid, while NPY and AgRP are associated with feeding-promoting pathways rather than the intestinal incretin.

16. PYY3–36 preferentially acts through which receptor in a commonly taught feeding pathway?

  1. GHSR1a
  2. Y2
  3. CCK1
  4. MC4
Answer and explanation

B. Y2 — The processed PYY peptide acts through Y2 receptors; receptor subtype determines physiological effect. The alternative receptors belong to different peptide signalling pathways; Y2 is the relevant NPY-family receptor here.

17. Where do many vagal visceral afferents terminate centrally?

  1. Primary visual cortex
  2. Spinal ventral horn exclusively
  3. Cerebellar vermis exclusively
  4. NTS
Answer and explanation

D. NTS — The NTS in the medulla receives major visceral afferent input. The visual cortex and cerebellar vermis do not constitute the major initial central destination of vagal visceral sensory fibres.

18. A reduction in the size of an ongoing meal most directly reflects which process, other causes excluded?

  1. Post-meal satiety only
  2. Glycogenolysis
  3. Thermogenesis
  4. Satiation
Answer and explanation

D. Satiation — Satiation terminates a meal; satiety concerns inhibition of subsequent eating. Satiety is the interval-related process; glycogen breakdown and heat production are metabolic processes rather than meal termination.

19. Which protein directly facilitates proton leak for brown-fat thermogenesis?

  1. UCP1
  2. MC4R
  3. STAT3
  4. GLUT4
Answer and explanation

A. UCP1 — UCP1 is an inner mitochondrial membrane protein that enables regulated uncoupling. GLUT4 transports glucose, STAT3 transduces cytokine-related signals and MC4R is a cell-surface receptor.

20. Which term describes the direction of a vagal signal travelling from stomach to medulla?

  1. Afferent
  2. Paracrine only
  3. Efferent
  4. Autocrine
Answer and explanation

A. Afferent — Afferent signals travel towards the CNS; efferent signals travel towards peripheral targets. Paracrine and autocrine refer to local chemical signalling; neither defines the direction of this neural transmission.

Part C — analytical reasoning

1. A leptin-deficient mouse has an intact LEPR. A second mouse lacks functional LEPR. Which is the most direct predicted response to supplying leptin?

  1. Neither responds because both are obese
  2. Only the receptor-null mouse responds
  3. The deficient mouse can respond; the receptor-null mouse cannot respond through LEPR
  4. Both respond identically
Answer and explanation

C. The deficient mouse can respond; the receptor-null mouse cannot respond through LEPR — The first has a missing ligand with intact downstream machinery; the second cannot transduce the ligand signal. The phenotype does not identify the molecular defect. Providing the same ligand cannot overcome an absent receptor, so equal responses or rescue restricted to the receptor-null animal are not predicted.

2. Food appearance reduces AgRP calcium activity before a fasting animal eats. Which conclusion follows?

  1. AgRP neurons cannot regulate feeding
  2. Adipose stores have been restored
  3. Sensory information can alter the activity before nutrient absorption
  4. Leptin concentration must have doubled
Answer and explanation

C. Sensory information can alter the activity before nutrient absorption — No nutrient from the presented food has yet entered the body, so anticipation can explain the early response. The result does not quantify hormone levels. Neither restoration of fat stores nor a doubling of leptin follows from a cue alone; an anticipatory response does not negate a causal role in feeding.

3. Selective AgRP activation elicits eating, but suppression of one projection does not abolish natural feeding. Which inference is justified?

  1. The activation proves exclusive necessity
  2. The outcomes are logically incompatible
  3. The suppression proves AgRP is never involved
  4. Activation can be sufficient while parallel pathways support feeding
Answer and explanation

D. Activation can be sufficient while parallel pathways support feeding — Sufficiency and necessity are distinct causal properties. Redundant output pathways can preserve behaviour after one is suppressed. The inhibition result pertains to one projection, so it cannot establish either universal irrelevance or exclusive necessity of the population.

4. MC4R-null neurons receive more α-MSH. What is the expected receptor-specific consequence?

  1. No restoration of MC4R-dependent signalling in null cells
  2. Leptin receptor becomes MC4R
  3. Normal MC4R activity resumes
  4. Ghrelin is necessarily eliminated
Answer and explanation

A. No restoration of MC4R-dependent signalling in null cells — The agonist requires functional receptor; other melanocortin receptors may still signal but do not restore the missing MC4R. The receptor is absent; an upstream agonist cannot regenerate it, and neither ghrelin elimination nor conversion of LEPR follows.

5. An intervention changes daily intake from 80 to 70 kJ and expenditure from 74 to 64 kJ. What happens to net daily storage in the simplified accounting model?

  1. It remains +6 kJ
  2. It becomes zero
  3. It falls to −10 kJ
  4. It increases to +14 kJ
Answer and explanation

A. It remains +6 kJ — Subtract expenditure from intake in both conditions: 80−74=6 and 70−64=6 kJ. Both rates change equally. The reduction in intake is exactly matched by the reduction in expenditure; options describing a change in net balance confuse absolute rates with their difference.

6. Diet A provides 12 kJ/g and diet B 20 kJ/g. Animals consume 5 g and 4 g respectively. Which consumes more energy?

  1. B, by 8 kJ
  2. B, by 20 kJ
  3. Both equally
  4. A, by 12 kJ
Answer and explanation

B. B, by 20 kJ — A receives 60 kJ and B receives 80 kJ. Grams of food cannot substitute for metabolizable energy. The two quantities must first be converted to energy; neither the difference in masses nor a comparison of density alone gives the answer.

7. A hormone shortens meals from 15 to 10 kJ, but meal number rises from four to six daily. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Expenditure necessarily decreases
  2. Satiety must increase
  3. Satiation may increase without changing daily energy intake
  4. Daily intake falls by one-third
Answer and explanation

C. Satiation may increase without changing daily energy intake — Both meal patterns deliver 60 kJ per day. Meal frequency compensates for reduced meal size. A claim about subsequent satiety or expenditure requires additional observations. The reduction in meal size does not imply a one-third reduction in daily intake.

8. A systemic insulin infusion reduces feeding while producing hypoglycaemia. Why is the experiment difficult to interpret?

  1. Blood glucose is irrelevant to the brain
  2. Insulin cannot affect peripheral tissues
  3. Hypoglycaemia proves MC4R activation
  4. Central receptor effects and peripheral glucose changes are confounded
Answer and explanation

D. Central receptor effects and peripheral glucose changes are confounded — The intervention alters multiple signals. A glucose-matched control can help distinguish insulin-associated from glycaemic effects. A glucose change is itself a physiological stimulus; it cannot identify MC4R activation, and insulin clearly also affects peripheral tissues.

9. A peptide decreases food intake but also produces conditioned flavour avoidance. What is the key interpretation problem?

  1. Food intake necessarily measures energy expenditure
  2. Visceral malaise may contribute to the reduced intake
  3. The peptide must activate POMC
  4. Flavour avoidance proves normal satiety
Answer and explanation

B. Visceral malaise may contribute to the reduced intake — Avoidance is evidence that an aversive state may be involved, so reduced intake is not enough to establish physiological satiation. Avoidance cannot prove normal meal termination or identify a specific neuronal population; intake and expenditure are different outcomes.

10. A nonnutritive gastric load reduces meal size while plasma gut peptide levels barely change. Which pathway could explain the response?

  1. Distension-sensitive afferents
  2. Ob gene deletion
  3. Increased nutrient absorption
  4. Loss of UCP1
Answer and explanation

A. Distension-sensitive afferents — Mechanical feedback can inform the brainstem without requiring absorbed nutrients. The experiment does not establish that other pathways are absent. The gastric stimulus is nonnutritive, so nutrient absorption is not required; genetic changes in ob or UCP1 are not implied.

11. A vagal intervention attenuates CCK-induced reduction in meal size but does not eliminate it. What is the best conclusion?

  1. All remaining effects prove direct MC4R binding
  2. CCK acts only through the vagus in all circumstances
  3. The vagus contributes; incomplete intervention or other pathways remain possible
  4. CCK has no feeding effect
Answer and explanation

C. The vagus contributes; incomplete intervention or other pathways remain possible — Partial attenuation does not establish exclusive mediation. Intervention completeness and alternative mechanisms must be assessed. An incomplete loss of response cannot establish either exclusive vagal mediation or a specific alternative receptor without further controls.

12. Two groups have the same fat mass but different leptin after acute fasting. What does this show?

  1. LEPR necessarily differs between groups
  2. Leptin is produced only in stomach
  3. Leptin can reflect recent energy status as well as fat mass
  4. Fat mass measurements must be impossible
Answer and explanation

C. Leptin can reflect recent energy status as well as fat mass — Nutritional state can modify leptin secretion without equivalent changes in adiposity. The result does not identify receptor function. The observation does not establish a genetic difference in receptors; the hypothesis concerns the hormone concentration at matched adiposity.

13. An animal shows high plasma leptin and weak response to injected leptin. Which is the cautious interpretation?

  1. LEPR gene is certainly deleted
  2. All neurons are resistant
  3. Leptin is absent
  4. Reduced responsiveness is plausible but mechanism remains undetermined
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced responsiveness is plausible but mechanism remains undetermined — High ligand and a weak response are compatible with resistance; receptor loss is only one of many possible explanations. Neither ligand deficiency nor universal resistance of all neurons is established by the two measurements.

14. A POMC neuron has increased precursor mRNA but unchanged α-MSH secretion. Which mechanism can reconcile these findings?

  1. MC4R synthesises POMC
  2. POMC is identical to α-MSH
  3. mRNA directly activates MC4R
  4. Peptide processing, translation or release may be independently regulated
Answer and explanation

D. Peptide processing, translation or release may be independently regulated — Precursor expression is not a direct measure of mature peptide production and release. Transcription, translation, processing and release are separate steps. Precursor RNA does not itself activate the target receptor.

15. A drug prevents AgRP binding to MC4R but leaves NPY and GABA release intact. What is a defensible prediction?

  1. All feeding responses must vanish
  2. Some feeding effects of AgRP neurons may remain
  3. GABA cannot inhibit any target
  4. NPY becomes an MC4R agonist
Answer and explanation

B. Some feeding effects of AgRP neurons may remain — AgRP neurons use parallel transmitters, so blocking one mechanism need not eliminate all responses. The blocked interaction is only one component; it does not prevent other transmitters from reaching their own targets.

16. Light is delivered to mice expressing a neuronal opsin and to mice expressing a fluorophore only. Why use the second group?

  1. To prove that every neuron is necessary
  2. To eliminate all developmental effects
  3. To measure leptin directly
  4. To control for light delivery and associated procedures
Answer and explanation

D. To control for light delivery and associated procedures — A matched control helps attribute differences to opsin-mediated perturbation rather than light or handling. Matched light exposure does not address every developmental confound and cannot establish necessity from an activation test.

17. A mouse with lifelong MC4R disruption is obese. Which follow-up best isolates adult function?

  1. An unrelated gastric lesion
  2. Another germline mutation without controls
  3. Adult-onset receptor perturbation with suitable controls
  4. A single measurement of food mass
Answer and explanation

C. Adult-onset receptor perturbation with suitable controls — An adult-onset manipulation reduces ambiguity due to developmental compensation, although it still needs careful control. A further uncontrolled germline perturbation cannot remove the developmental ambiguity; food mass or a gastric lesion would not isolate adult MC4R function.

18. Brown adipose sympathetic activity and measured local heat production rise with no detectable change in daily food intake. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. All sympathetic organs must respond equally
  2. Central output can alter a component of expenditure independently of intake
  3. Food intake measures heat directly
  4. Energy balance cannot change
Answer and explanation

B. Central output can alter a component of expenditure independently of intake — Autonomic outputs can regulate tissue-specific metabolism. Net storage depends on the actual change in expenditure. The new measurement supports a tissue-specific response, but no conclusion about every sympathetic target follows; net balance needs whole-body expenditure.

19. A food cue increases approach behaviour but does not increase measured taste reactions. Which distinction is illustrated?

  1. Homeostasis never influences reward
  2. Incentive motivation and hedonic response can diverge
  3. The cue must increase leptin
  4. Dopamine is identical to subjective pleasure
Answer and explanation

B. Incentive motivation and hedonic response can diverge — Approach and taste reactivity are different behavioural measures; neither alone characterises all reward processes. Behavioural motivation and sensory responses can vary independently. Neither leptin concentration nor a simple equation of dopamine with pleasure follows from these data.

20. A recording shows larger calcium fluorescence during a behavioural task. Which additional evidence most directly strengthens interpretation as neuronal activity?

  1. Measurement of body mass only
  2. Replacement of the recording with a meal-size estimate
  3. Validation of reporter response with electrophysiology and motion controls
  4. Assuming fluorescence counts action potentials exactly
Answer and explanation

C. Validation of reporter response with electrophysiology and motion controls — Calcium signals are indirect and can be affected by artefacts; independent validation links them more securely to electrical activity. Body mass and intake do not validate the optical reporter, and assuming a perfect spike count is precisely the unsupported inference.

21. In an illustrative MC4R assay, basal cAMP is 12 units, α-MSH produces 30 units, and compound X alone lowers basal cAMP to 7 units. Receptor-negative cells show no change. Which interpretation is best?

  1. X is necessarily a neutral antagonist only
  2. X is consistent with an inverse agonist at MC4R
  3. X proves that GABA is absent
  4. X must be a receptor agonist
Answer and explanation

B. X is consistent with an inverse agonist at MC4R — The decrease below basal receptor-dependent activity supports inverse agonism. A neutral antagonist should not lower basal activity in the idealised assay. Agonism in this canonical assay would increase cAMP, and no measurement of GABA was made.

22. Experimental mice consume 48 kJ/day and expend 43 kJ/day. Control mice consume 60 and expend 58 kJ/day. Assuming stable rates, what is the difference in retained energy after four days?

  1. Both groups retain 8 kJ
  2. Experimental mice retain 20 kJ less
  3. Experimental mice retain 48 kJ less
  4. Experimental mice retain 12 kJ more
Answer and explanation

D. Experimental mice retain 12 kJ more — The experimental group retains 5 kJ/day, or 20 kJ over four days; controls retain 2 kJ/day, or 8 kJ. The difference is 12 kJ. The lower intake does not establish a negative balance; subtraction of expenditure is necessary.

23. In a matched-volume experiment, a nutrient load reduces food intake more than a nonnutritive load; vagal sensory disruption attenuates both effects. Which interpretation is most justified?

  1. The vagus is exclusively efferent
  2. Only absorption into adipose tissue can explain both effects
  3. Volume and nutrient composition both plausibly contribute, with a vagal component
  4. The experiment proves exclusive GLP-1 mediation
Answer and explanation

C. Volume and nutrient composition both plausibly contribute, with a vagal component — The equal-volume comparison supports a composition-related contribution, and attenuation supports a vagal contribution. The experiment does not isolate GLP-1, exclude all additional routes, or show exclusive motor function.

24. A pair-fed control and a centrally manipulated animal consume the same metabolizable energy. The manipulated animal expends less energy and retains more. Which follow-up most directly distinguishes a thermogenic contribution from a change in activity?

  1. Measure movement and tissue-specific heat production under matched ambient temperature
  2. Increase food in the control without measuring expenditure
  3. Measure leptin once and assume the mechanism
  4. Measure only body mass at the end
Answer and explanation

A. Measure movement and tissue-specific heat production under matched ambient temperature — Movement and thermogenesis are separable components of expenditure and depend on environmental temperature. Body mass alone cannot identify the relevant output; altering intake breaks the matched comparison, and leptin concentration alone cannot determine the pathway.

25. Statements: I. POMC mRNA may rise without increased α-MSH release. II. AgRP cell activation can promote intake through pathways other than MC4R. III. A receptor-null cell necessarily responds normally when agonist concentration is doubled. Which are correct?

  1. I and III only
  2. I and II only
  3. II and III only
  4. All three
Answer and explanation

B. I and II only — I is correct because processing and release are independently regulated. II is supported by multiple AgRP-neuron transmitters and targets. III is false because a ligand cannot restore receptor-dependent signalling when its functional receptor is absent.

SET and mixed applications

1. Define the difference between energy homeostasis and energy balance.

Answer and explanation

Energy balance is quantitative energy accounting. Homeostasis is the interacting control processes that regulate energy acquisition and use; transient imbalance can be normal.

2. Explain why the traditional hunger-centre and satiety-centre model is insufficient.

Answer and explanation

Lesions affect mixed cell populations, axons and multiple functions. Distributed hypothalamic, hindbrain and reward circuits jointly influence feeding.

3. Predict the change in energy stores over five days if mean intake is 55 kJ/day and expenditure is 61 kJ/day.

Answer and explanation

ΔE = (55−61)×5 = −30 kJ, assuming mean rates represent the interval. This is energy, not a direct estimate of body mass.

4. Assertion: A hormone that lowers meal size necessarily lowers daily energy intake. Reason: Later meals can compensate for smaller initial meals.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true. Daily intake depends on both meal size and frequency.

5. Assertion: AgRP and α-MSH can oppose one another at MC4R. Reason: α-MSH activates MC4R while AgRP can oppose its signalling.

Answer and explanation

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. AgRP neurons also use other transmitters.

6. Why is systemic GLP-1 receptor agonism not identical to the physiology of endogenous gut GLP-1?

Answer and explanation

Exposure time, concentration and access to different receptors may differ; endogenous peptide degradation and local routes matter.

7. Describe a control for distinguishing food-associated cues from general sensory arousal.

Answer and explanation

Present a matched non-food stimulus and control prior experience, timing and handling; compare neuronal activity before consumption.

8. Why does a high leptin measurement not diagnose a particular molecular defect?

Answer and explanation

Ligand concentration is distinct from receptor access, signalling and network response; each requires separate evidence.

9. Differentiate vagal afferent and efferent functions in a meal response.

Answer and explanation

Afferents convey visceral state to central circuits, while efferents convey motor/autonomic commands to peripheral organs.

10. What is the central limitation of extrapolating a mouse optogenetic experiment to human appetite?

Answer and explanation

Species, behavioural context and the artificial nature of the stimulation constrain extrapolation; the experiment supports a mechanism under its tested conditions.

Revision and mastery

Feeding emerges from distributed hypothalamic, brainstem and reward networks. Energy-status hormones and meal-related signals alter these networks, which coordinate behaviour and autonomic output. Use the molecular identity of each ligand and receptor to predict the consequences of a specific perturbation. Distinguish current state from predicted food availability, and physiological satiation from nonspecific avoidance.

Key relationship: ΔE = (I − X)Δt for constant mean rates, with intake and expenditure in matching energy-per-time units.
