Why separate a cell into fractions?

A cell is not a homogeneous solution of interchangeable molecules. Its proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and carbohydrates occupy compartments with different physical properties and biological functions. Some enzymes act inside mitochondria, others operate in the cytosol, and others face the lumen of a membrane-bound compartment. A measurement on an entire cell homogenate reveals the combined behaviour of these populations but cannot, by itself, identify their original locations. The central problem of biological fractionation is therefore to simplify a complex sample without losing the properties that make its components biologically informative.

Suppose a researcher wants to distinguish a soluble enzyme from an enzyme bound to a membrane. A measurement of catalytic activity in a whole homogenate may establish that the enzyme exists, but it does not establish where the activity resides. If the homogenate is separated into material that sediments and material that remains suspended, the distribution of activity provides a first physical clue. This clue becomes stronger when the fractions are compared with markers of established compartments and when the enzyme remains active throughout the separation. The reasoning is essentially a relationship between an experimental manipulation and a predicted distribution, not a simple act of collecting a visible deposit.

Centrifugation accelerates the movement of suspended material by rotating a sample. It exploits differences in how particles respond to the resulting radial acceleration. A particle here may be an entire cell, an organelle, a membrane vesicle, a ribosome, a protein assembly or an individual macromolecule. A dissolved salt ion also responds to an acceleration field, but its very small buoyant mass and strong diffusive motion make it quite different from a large organelle. A centrifugal field does not act like a molecular sieve with a fixed pore size. Separation reflects the combined influence of the particle, its surrounding medium and the instrument.

A homogenate is the suspension obtained after controlled disruption of a biological sample. Homogenisation may release organelles while preserving their membranes, or may rupture the organelles and release their soluble contents. Which outcome is desirable depends on the biological question. A scientist studying an intact mitochondrial membrane needs a different level of preservation from a scientist measuring total mitochondrial protein. It would be a mistake to assume that the most complete disruption always produces the best starting material. Greater disruption can destroy the structural evidence needed to interpret localisation.

The material deposited toward the outer end of a centrifuge tube is called the pellet, and the liquid remaining above it is the supernatant. These labels describe physical positions after a particular separation; they do not define biochemical identities. A pellet can contain several kinds of particles, and its supernatant may contain small particles as well as dissolved molecules. The same supernatant can yield another pellet under a different centrifugal field or after a longer interval. Thus a sample described as soluble after a particular separation is operationally soluble under those conditions rather than necessarily molecularly dispersed.

An enriched fraction contains a higher proportion of a desired component than the starting sample. A purified fraction approaches freedom from unwanted constituents, while recovery measures how much of the original desired component has been retained. These three ideas are not equivalent. A small pellet containing almost exclusively a desired organelle might represent high purity and poor recovery. A large mixed pellet may retain nearly all of that organelle but offer little purification. The decision about what to retain must be guided by the eventual measurement. This distinction connects centrifugation with the broader principles of sample quality introduced earlier in the subunit.

The modern understanding of cellular compartmentation owes much to complementary evidence from microscopy, biochemical analysis and fractionation. Electron microscopy visualises internal membrane architecture, whereas biochemical assays report activities and chemical composition. When an activity follows a particular set of structures across a series of fractions, a hypothesis about its subcellular localisation becomes testable. Fractionation is particularly valuable because it makes separated material available for subsequent functional measurements. Nevertheless, an isolated organelle is not in the same physiological environment as an organelle inside a living cell, so observations on isolated components must be interpreted within that limitation.

Classical work on ultracentrifugation in the twentieth century also helped establish that apparently soluble proteins could behave as discrete macromolecular particles. The Svedberg and his colleagues developed instruments that allowed sedimentation of molecules to be observed optically. These studies transformed sedimentation from a purely preparative technique into a physical measurement of the properties of macromolecules in solution. The historical and methodological background is discussed in the references on cell fractionation and analytical ultracentrifugation. The distinction remains central: a preparative centrifuge is used primarily to recover separated material, whereas an analytical ultracentrifuge is used to observe and interpret concentration distributions during rotation.

To understand both applications, it is helpful to begin with the mechanics of circular motion. The apparently simple instruction to spin a sample faster conceals important questions: how far is the sample from the axis, how rapidly does it rotate, what forces oppose the movement of a particle, and what determines the time needed for a particle to reach a collection position? The following sections develop these relationships before introducing different biological separation strategies.

Circular motion and relative centrifugal force

A rotating object moves along a curved path. Even if the magnitude of its velocity remains constant, its direction changes continuously. This requires an acceleration directed toward the centre of rotation, called centripetal acceleration. A centrifuge rotor provides the inward force required to keep its tubes on circular paths. In a reference frame rotating with the sample, it is convenient to describe sedimentation using an apparent outward centrifugal acceleration. The outward direction in this frame is the direction of increasing distance from the axis of rotation.

Let r represent the radial distance from the axis, measured in metres, and let ω represent angular speed in radians per second. A point on the rotor has tangential speed ωr. The magnitude of centripetal acceleration is the square of tangential speed divided by radius. Substitution gives a = ω²r. The centrifugal acceleration used to describe the rotating sample has the same magnitude and the opposite direction. The dependence on angular speed is quadratic, while the dependence on radius is linear.

Centrifuge instrument displays frequently specify rotational speed in revolutions per minute, abbreviated rpm. One revolution corresponds to 2π radians and one minute contains sixty seconds. If N is the numerical speed in rpm, then ω = 2πN/60. Substituting this expression into the acceleration equation yields a = (2πN/60)²r. The quantity has units of metres per second squared when r is in metres. For comparing centrifugal acceleration with ordinary gravitational acceleration, divide by the standard acceleration g₀ = 9.80665 metres per second squared.

Relative centrifugal force, abbreviated RCF, is this ratio a/g₀. Despite its conventional name, it is a dimensionless acceleration ratio rather than a force measured in newtons. When the radius is entered in centimetres, conversion of centimetres into metres leads to the convenient relation RCF ≈ 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r(cm) × N². An RCF of 1,000 means that the local centrifugal acceleration is approximately one thousand times standard gravitational acceleration. It does not state the net force on a particular particle because the particle's buoyant mass is not yet included.

For a rotor radius of 10 centimetres at 3,000 rpm, N² is 9,000,000. Multiplying by 10 and then by 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ gives 1,006.2, conventionally reported as approximately 1,006 × g. The same rotational speed at a radius of 5 centimetres gives 503.1 × g. The difference is due entirely to the geometry of the rotor. A centrifugation condition specified solely in rpm therefore cannot be transferred reliably between instruments without knowing the relevant radial position.

If the rotational speed in the first example is doubled to 6,000 rpm, the new acceleration ratio is four times its initial value, or 4,024.8. Doubling rpm does not double RCF. Conversely, if the desired RCF is doubled at fixed radius, the speed needs to increase by a factor of the square root of two, approximately 1.414. These proportional arguments are often faster and less error-prone than recomputing absolute values in an examination.

The inverse problem is equally useful. To reproduce 10,000 × g at a radius of 12 centimetres, rearrange the relation to N = √[RCF/(1.118 × 10⁻⁵r)]. The denominator is 0.00013416, so the argument of the square root is about 74.54 million. The required speed is approximately 8,634 rpm. Such a calculated value describes the mathematical condition only; the allowable speed of an actual instrument depends on its rotor and tube specifications and must never be exceeded.

A tube is extended in space rather than concentrated at a single radius. The point closest to the axis, often near the liquid meniscus, experiences a lower acceleration than the outermost point. A protocol may quote RCF at the maximum radius, the minimum radius or a defined average radius. For example, at 5,000 rpm, radii of 6 and 10 centimetres correspond to about 1,677 and 2,795 × g respectively. Both are correct descriptions of different locations in the same rotating sample. Reporting only one number without identifying the convention can obscure significant differences in sedimentation distance.

Rotors with the same maximum RCF may have different sample paths. One might place a particle only a short distance from the collecting surface, whereas another might require movement across most of a long tube. A particle's movement is governed by its acceleration and by the time spent travelling through the medium. For this reason the total outcome is not determined by maximum RCF alone. Acceleration and deceleration periods may also contribute to the displacement or affect the distribution of delicate separated zones. In a textbook calculation one normally assumes a constant final speed unless a different history is explicitly given.

The distinction between acceleration and force clarifies why particles of different sizes do not move at identical speeds in a centrifuge. At a given radius every particle experiences the same local centrifugal acceleration in the rotating description, but its mass determines its nominal centrifugal force. More importantly, the surrounding liquid has mass and is also under acceleration. The resulting pressure gradient produces buoyancy, which may reduce or even reverse the net force on a particle. Resistance to movement through the liquid also matters. The next section therefore treats the particle and solvent together rather than applying the acceleration equation in isolation.

Sedimentation, buoyancy and friction

Sedimentation is the directed motion of a suspended or dissolved species relative to its surrounding medium under an external acceleration field. In a liquid under ordinary gravity, denser suspended particles tend to move downward. In a centrifugal field, the corresponding direction is toward larger radius. A particle less dense than its medium tends to move toward smaller radius instead. This behaviour is sometimes described as flotation, and it is an important counterexample to the idea that everything in a centrifuge necessarily moves outward.

The origin of buoyancy can be understood by considering the pressure within a rotating liquid. The pressure at the outer face of a liquid element must exceed the pressure at its inner face to supply the net inward force that maintains circular motion, so pressure increases outward. In the simplest homogeneous liquid of density ρ, the radial pressure gradient is dp/dr = ρω²r. Imagine removing a small volume V of liquid and replacing it with a particle occupying the same volume. The pressure forces that acted on the original liquid now act on the particle. Their resultant opposes the outward centrifugal force by an amount equal to the centrifugal force associated with the displaced fluid.

If the particle has density ρp, its mass is ρpV. Its effective outward force is therefore V(ρp − ρ)ω²r, before considering resistance to motion. The difference between particle and medium densities determines the sign. At equal densities the two terms cancel. A very dense but small particle can still experience a relatively modest force because the difference is multiplied by its small volume. A large particle with almost exactly matching density may likewise move slowly despite having a large actual mass.

Motion through a viscous liquid encounters friction. Viscosity describes resistance to deformation of a fluid during flow. At the small scales and slow relative speeds relevant to many biological particles, the drag can often be approximated as proportional to velocity u, with a proportionality constant f called the translational friction coefficient. Drag acts opposite to the direction of the movement. A particle rapidly approaches a terminal relative velocity at which the driving force and friction balance. The word terminal does not imply that the particle has stopped; it implies that these opposing forces have balanced and the speed can be described by their ratio.

Equating f u with V(ρp − ρ)ω²r gives u = [V(ρp − ρ)/f]ω²r. For a rigid sphere with radius a in a Newtonian liquid of dynamic viscosity η, Stokes' law gives f = 6πηa, and the spherical volume is 4πa³/3. Substituting the expressions and simplifying yields u = 2a²(ρp − ρ)ω²r/(9η). The model shows why increasing a particle radius can strongly increase its sedimentation speed. The force increases with the cube of particle radius, whereas the friction coefficient for this ideal sphere increases linearly.

This simple result has conditions of validity. The particle must be approximately spherical and rigid, the relative flow must have a low Reynolds number, the medium must behave as a continuum with approximately Newtonian viscosity, and the particle must be far enough from walls and other particles that their effects are negligible. Biological macromolecules are hydrated, flexible and often non-spherical. A mitochondrial fragment or a flattened membrane sheet is not a perfect rigid sphere. The formula is therefore valuable as a model for predicting directions of change but should not be used to assign exact physical sizes to arbitrary biological particles without independent information.

At fixed acceleration, two ideal spheres of identical density in the same liquid but with radii a and 2a have sedimentation velocities in the ratio one to four. If their density contrasts are instead in the ratio one to two, the velocities are in the ratio one to two. If the viscosity is doubled with all other quantities held constant, both velocities are halved. An increase in solvent density, when particle density is fixed and greater than solvent density, reduces the driving force. If the solvent density is raised beyond particle density, the direction of sedimentation reverses. These are independent predictions of the model and demonstrate why a size-only explanation of centrifugation is inadequate.

For a numerical illustration, consider an ideal sphere with radius 1 micrometre, a density 100 kilograms per cubic metre above that of its medium, a viscosity of 0.001 pascal seconds and a local acceleration of 1,000g₀. Expressing the radius as 10⁻⁶ metres and acceleration as 9,806.65 metres per second squared, the numerator of Stokes' expression becomes 2 × 10⁻¹² × 100 × 9,806.65. The denominator is 0.009 pascal seconds. The predicted speed is approximately 2.18 × 10⁻⁴ metres per second, or 218 micrometres per second. This is an ideal local velocity, not a measured organelle sedimentation speed. In an actual rotor the acceleration increases along the outward radial path and may not remain constant.

The hydrodynamic properties of a particle are shaped by more than the geometrical contour visible in a diagram. Water associated with a macromolecule and changes in its molecular conformation affect the resistance it experiences. Two proteins of equal molar mass may therefore have different sedimentation rates if one is elongated and the other compact. Aggregation can increase the buoyant mass of a protein assembly while also increasing friction; the resulting change in sedimentation coefficient must be interpreted as a combined effect. Sedimentation alone is not a direct measurement of molecular mass without further assumptions or measurements.

Brownian motion adds another dimension to the picture. Thermal collisions produce random displacements that spread a concentration distribution. A small solute can diffuse substantially while its average directed displacement is modest. In a macroscopic suspension of large particles, directed sedimentation may dominate. In analytical ultracentrifugation of macromolecules, diffusion contributes meaningfully to the width of the observed sedimentation boundary. The presence of spreading does not mean that centrifugation has failed; it is part of the physical process being measured.

When the medium is not homogeneous, density and viscosity may vary with radial position. The driving force then changes both because the radius changes and because the density contrast changes. A particle travelling outward through a density gradient may slow as the fluid becomes denser. If its effective density equals that of the surrounding medium, it has reached a position at which buoyant driving force vanishes. A particle displaced from this position into denser medium tends to return inward, while one displaced toward a less dense region tends to move outward. This restoring tendency is the physical basis of equilibrium density banding.

A density gradient does not eliminate diffusion. At finite temperature, particles can be distributed over a zone rather than occupying an infinitely narrow geometrical surface. The observed width depends on factors such as thermal motion, the gradient slope and the heterogeneity of the particles. A broad zone might reflect genuinely different particle populations, a shallow gradient, incomplete equilibration or disruption during handling. A correct interpretation should distinguish these possibilities rather than infer purity from the visual appearance of a band.

The sedimentation coefficient and the Svedberg unit

A particle moving through a uniform solvent at terminal velocity has a radial speed proportional to ω²r under the ideal assumptions just discussed. Dividing the observed radial velocity by the local centrifugal acceleration defines the sedimentation coefficient s = u/(ω²r). This definition removes the direct effect of acceleration and gives a measure of the particle's behaviour in a specified solvent at a specified temperature. The units of s are time because velocity has dimensions of length per time and acceleration has dimensions of length per time squared.

The conventional unit is the Svedberg, denoted S. One S is defined as 10⁻¹³ seconds. A sedimentation coefficient of 5 S is thus 5 × 10⁻¹³ seconds, not five seconds and not a mass. The use of a very small unit follows from the small ratio of macromolecular velocity to the very large accelerations used to measure it. Ribosomal subunits are commonly named by their approximate sedimentation coefficients. The familiar bacterial 30S and 50S subunits associate to form the 70S ribosome. The names do not violate arithmetic because the coefficient is not an additive quantity.

For a macromolecule, define M as molar mass, Nₐ as Avogadro's constant and v̄ as partial specific volume, which describes the change in solution volume per unit mass of solute under defined conditions. The mass per molecule is M/Nₐ. The buoyancy factor in a simple dilute solution is 1 − v̄ρ. Its dimensionless nature can be checked by combining units of volume per mass with density in mass per volume. The effective buoyant mass is (M/Nₐ)(1 − v̄ρ), and the sedimentation coefficient becomes s = M(1 − v̄ρ)/(Nₐf).

A convenient illustration is to consider two proteins of equal molar mass and equal partial specific volume in the same solvent. If the elongated protein has a friction coefficient 1.5 times that of the compact protein, its sedimentation coefficient is two-thirds of the compact protein's value. It is not less massive simply because it sediments more slowly. In a similar way, proteins of different molar masses can have comparable coefficients when differences in friction or buoyancy compensate for differences in actual mass.

The same friction coefficient determines translational diffusion through the Einstein relation D = kBT/f, where kB is Boltzmann's constant and T is absolute temperature in kelvin. Elimination of f from this expression and the sedimentation equation leads to M = sRT/[D(1 − v̄ρ)], where R = NₐkB is the molar gas constant. This relationship is often called the Svedberg equation for molar mass. It connects the directed and random movement of a molecule. Measuring sedimentation alone leaves the friction coefficient unknown, whereas measuring diffusion supplies the complementary information required to eliminate it.

For an illustrative calculation, take s = 4.0 S, D = 7.0 × 10⁻¹¹ square metres per second, T = 293 K, R = 8.314 joules per mole per kelvin and a buoyancy factor of 0.27. Convert the coefficient to 4.0 × 10⁻¹³ seconds. The numerator sRT is about 9.744 × 10⁻¹⁰ in SI units. The denominator is 1.89 × 10⁻¹¹ square metres per second. Division gives approximately 51.6 kilograms per mole, equivalent to 51,600 grams per mole or 51.6 kilodaltons when describing the molecular mass of individual molecules. These values are chosen for teaching rather than drawn from a measured protein.

The equation requires compatible measurements of sedimentation and diffusion for the same species in the same state, with a known buoyancy correction and appropriate dilute-solution behaviour. If a protein reversibly associates during the experiment, a single apparent sedimentation coefficient and a separately measured average diffusion coefficient may not describe one molecular species. The resulting molar mass could be misleading. Errors in the buoyancy factor can be especially important when that factor is small, because it appears in the denominator.

An alternative approach to predicting motion begins with dr/dt = sω²r. Under constant angular speed and constant sedimentation coefficient, divide by r and integrate from initial radius r₀ to final radius r₁. The result is ln(r₁/r₀) = sω²t. The time needed to traverse that path is therefore ln(r₁/r₀)/(sω²). The logarithm contains a ratio of radii measured in the same units, so its argument is dimensionless. The result is more accurate than dividing distance by a velocity evaluated at just one radius when the change in radius is appreciable.

Suppose two ideal species have coefficients of 10 S and 20 S and travel between the same two radial positions under the same constant speed. The second takes half the time of the first. If the speed is doubled, the predicted travel times fall to one-quarter. But these predictions assume that the sedimentation coefficient does not change with concentration, position or the solvent. In a density gradient, or during a reaction that changes molecular association, the simple integration is no longer generally valid. The mathematical result therefore supports experimental reasoning only within the stated model.

Rotors, sample geometry and separation efficiency

The centrifuge converts mechanical rotation into a controlled acceleration field. The rotor is the rotating structure that supports the sample vessels, and its design determines the geometry of the liquid column during spinning. A tube positioned at a fixed angle to the axis does not retain the same orientation relative to the acceleration field as a tube that swings outward into a nearly radial position. This distinction has consequences for sedimentation path length, location of deposited material and interpretation of separated bands.

In a fixed-angle rotor, the tube is held at a predetermined angle. A particle moving outward relative to the solvent may encounter the outer side of the tube rather than moving all the way along its longitudinal axis. Deposited material often accumulates toward the outer side and outer end of the tube. This geometry can provide a relatively short route to the collecting surface and is particularly useful when the aim is to recover a compact pellet. The location of that pellet should be understood before manipulating the separated supernatant. It is not necessarily centred at the lowest point of a stationary tube.

In a swinging-bucket rotor, a pivot allows the sample holder to swing outward as the rotor accelerates. At operating speed the tube is approximately aligned with the radial direction. Different positions along the liquid column therefore correspond to different radial distances in a straightforward manner. This geometry is convenient for observing a series of zones separated along the length of a tube. The apparent bottom of the tube when the instrument stops corresponds to the outer end during rotation, assuming the sample column remains undisturbed during the change in orientation.

A vertical rotor holds tubes roughly parallel to the axis of rotation. The relevant radial sedimentation distance across a tube may be short. Such geometry can be favourable for some equilibrium density separations, where rapid establishment of a radial density distribution matters more than forming a pellet at a conventional tube bottom. However, the radial arrangement of zones during rotation is not the same as the orientation of the tube at rest. Redistribution during acceleration and deceleration is part of the physical context for interpreting the result. Rotor choice is thus linked to the kind of separation being attempted, not merely to the maximum speed printed on an instrument.

The path-length difference can be expressed quantitatively using the integrated sedimentation relation. For an ideal particle with constant s and constant ω, time to travel from rmin to rmax is proportional to ln(rmax/rmin). Two rotors with the same outer radius and speed but different inner radii can therefore require different times to achieve complete sedimentation. If the outer radius is 12 centimetres, a path beginning at 6 centimetres has logarithmic term ln 2, approximately 0.693, whereas a path beginning at 9 centimetres has term ln(4/3), approximately 0.288. In this ideal comparison, the shorter radial path takes only about 42 percent as long.

Rotor performance is sometimes described by a clearing factor, commonly called the k factor. Its conventional numerical definition packages radial geometry and angular speed so that, for a sedimentation coefficient expressed numerically in Svedberg units, an ideal clearing time in hours can be estimated as t = k/s. Starting with the integrated relation in seconds and converting the numerical coefficient from Svedbergs and time into hours gives k = 10¹³ ln(rmax/rmin)/(3600ω²). A smaller k value corresponds to a shorter ideal clearing time for a given sedimenting species. The calculation is a model of complete radial migration, not a guarantee of recovery from an actual biological suspension.

Consider two rotors rated at their stated operating conditions with k factors of 200 and 100. The estimated clearing times for an ideal 50 S particle are four hours and two hours, respectively. If an examination asks which rotor is more efficient for collecting that particle, the latter provides the shorter ideal time. This does not imply that it is superior for every fractionation task. A gradient separation may favour a geometry that preserves broad spatial separation between neighbouring zones rather than one that minimises time to reach a surface.

The clearing factor depends on actual speed. If a given rotor operates at half the angular speed used to calculate its published k factor, its ideal k factor is four times larger. A conversion based on rotor geometry alone is therefore incomplete. The manufacturer's stated reference conditions and the experimental medium must also be taken into account. Since viscosity and buoyancy influence the actual sedimentation coefficient in a particular liquid, a coefficient standardised to water at twenty degrees Celsius cannot simply be substituted for a coefficient in a very viscous gradient without correction.

Temperature affects biological stability and physical transport at the same time. Cooling may preserve susceptible enzymes and membranes by reducing chemical degradation, but it often increases solvent viscosity. Increased viscosity increases friction and reduces the sedimentation speed for a particle of otherwise unchanged properties. Temperature can also alter protein association equilibria or the volume occupied by an organelle. Consequently a change in temperature is not always an independent intervention that affects only one physical parameter. When comparing results, recording and controlling temperature helps distinguish experimental variation from biological variation.

The need to balance a rotor follows from mechanics. An off-axis distribution of mass produces an unbalanced rotating load that can cause dangerous vibration and mechanical stress. Two equal volumes need not have equal masses if their densities differ, which matters particularly when comparing different gradient media. Rotor integrity, compatible vessels, manufacturer's specified limits, and institutional laboratory safety practices are essential constraints on an actual experiment. The theoretical RCF equation cannot establish safe operating conditions. A calculated speed outside the approved rotor or tube range is simply an invalid experimental choice.

In the context of examination reasoning, instrument selection begins with the desired outcome. Collection of large cellular debris, collection of smaller membrane fragments, preservation of narrow zones, and observation of macromolecular concentration profiles are different goals. A preparative centrifuge is designed to separate and recover material, whereas an analytical instrument is designed to record the evolution or final distribution of concentrations in a specialised observation cell. Some preparative instruments are ultracentrifuges, and some analytical instruments are ultracentrifuges, but the word ultracentrifuge by itself does not specify the type of measurement being made.

Differential centrifugation: sequential enrichment

Differential centrifugation is a sequence of separations in which a mixed suspension is subjected to conditions that allow different populations to sediment to different extents. Its underlying principle is the difference in sedimentation rate among the populations, combined with the finite time and distance available before a pellet is collected. In its usual application to subcellular material, the initial homogenate contains particles spanning a wide range of effective sizes, densities and frictional properties.

The largest and fastest-sedimenting structures are preferentially collected at an early stage. The supernatant is then used as the input to a subsequent separation designed to collect slower-sedimenting material. Repeating the logic progressively removes classes of material from the suspension. The most important bookkeeping rule is that the starting material for a later separation is the supernatant from the preceding stage, not a new aliquot of the original homogenate, unless the experimental design explicitly says otherwise. Treating each pellet as if it originated independently from the full sample would invalidate recovery calculations.

A typical educational scheme for animal cell fractionation begins with a homogenate containing intact nuclei, unbroken cells, mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes, vesicles derived from intracellular membranes, ribosomes and soluble cytoplasmic molecules. The first relatively mild separation enriches nuclei and other large structures in a pellet. Some mitochondria and membrane fragments may be trapped in this material. The supernatant is depleted of large objects but retains many smaller organelles and macromolecules.

A subsequent separation of the first supernatant can produce a fraction enriched in mitochondria. Lysosomes and peroxisomes often overlap substantially with this fraction because their sedimentation properties are not sufficiently distinct for perfect separation by differential pelleting. Describing the pellet as a mitochondrial fraction is useful shorthand only when the enrichment has been established. It does not mean that every protein found in the pellet is a mitochondrial protein. Further fractionation or independent localisation measurements are necessary when distinguishing organelles with overlapping properties.

After removal of many large organelles, smaller membrane vesicles remain. Such vesicles may derive from the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, plasma membrane or other membranes disrupted during homogenisation. A microsomal fraction is commonly enriched in these small vesicles. Microsomes are not a separate class of organelle that normally exists intact in a living cell. They are experimental structures formed from disrupted membranes that can reseal into vesicles. Rough microsomes may retain ribosomes on their exposed cytosolic surface, whereas smooth vesicles lack attached ribosomes. These differences alter composition and sometimes sedimentation behaviour.

At still more demanding sedimentation conditions, ribosomes and other large macromolecular assemblies may be collected from suitable preparations. The final supernatant is enriched in smaller soluble constituents. This textbook sequence is useful because it links the relative physical scale of cellular structures to an experimental strategy. It is not a universal chronological order for all tissues, organisms or homogenisation methods. Plant cells, for example, have plastids and large vacuoles with distinctive physical properties; their disruption can yield a different mixture from an animal cell homogenate.

Why is the first pellet not entirely composed of the fastest class? Imagine two identical fast particles placed at different positions in a tube. The one initially close to the collecting surface reaches it earlier than the one near the inner end. Now add a slower particle that begins close to that surface. It may arrive before a faster particle that begins far away. In a homogeneous starting suspension, the distance to be travelled varies across particles of the same type. As a result, finite-time pelleting produces overlapping recovery distributions even if the sedimentation coefficients of the particle classes are well separated.

This starting-position problem explains why differential centrifugation can be good for bulk enrichment without offering the highest possible resolution. It also explains why increasing a separation time can improve recovery of a desired population while simultaneously decreasing its purity. Slower contaminants have more time to reach the collecting surface. An apparently stronger centrifugal separation is not necessarily a more selective separation. The desired condition is a compromise between retention of the target and exclusion of contaminants.

Resuspending an enriched pellet and separating it again can reduce soluble contaminants trapped in residual liquid. It can also redistribute loosely associated particulate contaminants. However, repeated washing can result in loss of desired material, damage to fragile structures or detachment of proteins of biological interest. A genuine membrane-associated protein may appear in the supernatant if association is disrupted. Conversely, a soluble protein may remain associated with a pellet because it becomes trapped in vesicles or adsorbs to surfaces. A washing experiment has to be interpreted alongside markers of membrane integrity and specificity.

The phrase postnuclear supernatant illustrates the operational character of fraction names. It denotes material remaining after a separation intended to remove most nuclei, not a chemically defined solution from which every nuclear protein has disappeared. Nuclear fragments or soluble nuclear contents can remain in the postnuclear supernatant if nuclei were damaged during sample preparation. A nuclear marker in that fraction can therefore indicate incomplete removal of intact nuclei, release of soluble nuclear components, or both. These explanations imply different defects and require different follow-up observations.

Differential centrifugation can also be applied to mixtures outside the context of whole cells. A suspension of insoluble precipitate and dissolved molecules may be clarified by collecting the precipitate. A mixture of large protein aggregates and smaller soluble assemblies may be separated into aggregate-rich and soluble-rich fractions. Such outcomes depend on the same principles of buoyant mass and friction. There is no general rule that a soluble protein cannot sediment; sufficiently large soluble macromolecules and assemblies can exhibit measurable sedimentation even when they remain chemically soluble.

A crucial distinction exists between precipitation and sedimentation. Precipitation is a change in the physical or chemical state of a component, often involving reduced solubility and formation of particles or aggregates. Sedimentation is the movement of material relative to the liquid. A precipitated protein may be collected by sedimentation, but the centrifuge has not necessarily caused the precipitation. Conversely, native intact particles can be sedimented without changing their chemical solubility. In an examination, the observation of a pellet does not by itself reveal whether the material was insoluble before the field was applied.

It is useful to follow the conservation of a stable molecular marker through a complete series of differential fractions. If a homogenate contains 100 arbitrary units of a marker and sequential pellets contain 12, 60 and 8 units while the final supernatant contains 15 units, total measured recovery is 95 units, or 95 percent. The middle pellet contains most of the recovered marker, but the remaining activity is distributed elsewhere. The unaccounted five units may reflect loss, altered activity or measurement error. A report of 60 units in the middle pellet is not sufficient to establish its biochemical purity because the amount and identity of other components in that pellet are unknown.

The choice of an endpoint depends on the aim. If the purpose is to compare the activity of a mitochondria-associated enzyme between samples, reproducible enrichment and preservation of activity may be more important than obtaining the purest imaginable fraction. If the purpose is to assign a newly discovered protein to a particular organelle, contamination and alternative explanations become more consequential. The appropriate next step is often a separation based on a different aspect of particle behaviour, such as buoyant density, together with independent evidence of cellular localisation. Differential pelleting thus provides a useful starting point rather than an automatic final answer.

Density gradients as separation environments

A density gradient is a liquid column in which the density changes systematically with position. For the usual outward-increasing gradient, the inner part of the column is less dense and the outer part is denser. In a stationary upright tube this arrangement corresponds to a lighter upper region and a denser lower region. The radial description is more fundamental during centrifugation because the direction of the effective acceleration is outward from the rotational axis, not necessarily vertically downward.

A gradient modifies the environment through which particles travel. Its density determines the local buoyancy correction, and its viscosity may determine the local friction. The gradient can also help stabilise the separation against mixing by placing denser liquid at larger radius. If a denser liquid were placed inside a much lighter one in the centrifugal field, the arrangement would be unstable and the liquids could rearrange. A stable gradient helps preserve spatial information as distinct particle populations move through the column.

The medium used to produce a gradient must be appropriate for the physical and biological properties under investigation. Sucrose gradients are widely discussed in textbook descriptions of organelles, membrane vesicles and ribosomal particles. A dense salt such as cesium chloride is a classical example for equilibrium separation of nucleic acids. Other media such as iodixanol or coated colloidal silica are used in different biological contexts because their combinations of attainable density, viscosity, osmotic effects and interactions with particles differ. The medium is part of the experiment, not an interchangeable background.

Osmotic effects are especially important when the separated material includes membrane-bound structures. A gradient solute that alters water movement across a membrane can change the volume and density of an organelle. The organelle may lose water and become denser or gain water and become less dense, depending on the osmotic conditions and membrane permeability. Two fractions obtained in different media can therefore have different observed buoyant densities even if they originated from the same organelle population. A useful density separation requires preservation of the properties that the investigator intends to compare.

A continuous gradient changes density smoothly, whereas a discontinuous or step gradient contains regions with distinct densities separated by interfaces. Continuous gradients are convenient for interpreting spatial distributions or assigning approximate densities to bands when the density profile has been measured. Step gradients can concentrate material at an interface because a particle may enter one layer but move slowly or in the opposite direction in the next. However, an interface is not automatically an exact measurement of particle density. A particle can accumulate there because of changes in transport rate or incomplete migration as well as buoyancy.

Another distinction concerns how a gradient is established. Some gradients are prepared before centrifugation and provide a defined initial spatial arrangement. Other media redistribute under the centrifugal field so that a gradient develops during the run. Classical equilibrium nucleic acid separations in cesium chloride exploit the latter behaviour. In either case, the final relation between radius and density must be known or measured if the position of a band is to be translated into a numerical buoyant density. Assuming a linear profile when the actual profile is nonlinear can bias the interpretation.

Gradient steepness influences both separation and interpretation. In an isopycnic separation, the radial distance between equilibrium positions depends on how much the medium density changes per unit distance. If two species differ in density by 0.02 grams per millilitre, and a hypothetical linear gradient changes by 0.01 grams per millilitre per centimetre, their equilibrium positions are separated by two centimetres. If the gradient slope is doubled, the positions are only one centimetre apart. A steeper gradient compresses the spatial separation of species of similar density, though it can also produce stronger restoring forces that affect zone widths. The net resolving power depends on both distance and band broadening.

The initial loading geometry is another independent variable. In a rate-zonal experiment, a narrow starting zone is used to reduce variation in the distance travelled by particles of the same population. In many equilibrium density separations, it is not essential that all particles start in the same narrow layer because the final band position is determined by buoyant density rather than the initial location. Starting positions can affect the time required to reach equilibrium, however. These distinctions are part of the mechanism of the two separation strategies rather than superficial differences in how a tube is filled.

A visible band is a region of relatively high local particle concentration. Its brightness, turbidity or absorbance depends on the detection method and the optical properties of the particles and gradient medium. A strongly visible band need not contain more moles of material than a weak band if their detection responses differ. A band can contain several populations with overlapping physical properties. Conversely, two distinct bands may represent two physical states of one molecular species rather than two unrelated molecules. The collection and identification of fractions are separate analytical tasks.

Once a gradient has separated a sample, successive portions of the column can be analysed by measuring marker activity, protein concentration, absorbance or another appropriate signal. A plot of signal versus fraction number is an elution-like profile, but the fraction number corresponds to spatial collection order rather than chromatographic retention. The direction must be stated: numbering from the inner end to the outer end gives the reverse order of numbering from the outer end inward. Without that convention, a peak at a high fraction number cannot be identified as a high-density peak.

Fraction volumes should be taken into account when converting concentration measurements into total quantities. A narrow high-concentration peak may contain less material than a broader lower-concentration peak. If each fraction has the same volume and the assay is linear, summing the appropriately background-corrected signals can be proportional to recovered material. When fraction volumes differ, each measured concentration must be multiplied by its volume before the values are summed. This principle is shared with chromatography and other analytical separation methods.

The gradient medium itself can influence downstream measurements. A dense or strongly absorbing solute may contribute background to an optical assay; viscosity can complicate accurate volume measurement; and a solute may alter enzyme activity or interfere with detection chemistry. Appropriate blank fractions and matrix-compatible standards help distinguish the signal of the biological component from the signal of the surrounding medium. Where quantitative comparisons are important, the analytical response should be calibrated rather than presumed to be identical across fractions of different composition.

Rate-zonal centrifugation

Rate-zonal centrifugation separates components according to differences in how far they travel during a finite interval of centrifugation. It addresses the starting-position problem of differential pelleting by placing the mixture initially in a relatively narrow zone near the inner end of a gradient. As the field acts on the sample, particles with different sedimentation properties move outward at different rates. Spatial separation develops because the particles have different displacements from a common starting region.

The gradient is usually selected such that particles of interest remain denser than the surrounding medium throughout the intended separation path. This condition prevents the desired separation from turning primarily into equilibrium density banding. Faster-sedimenting particles travel farther than slower particles within a suitable observation window. Their actual motion can be influenced by the local density and viscosity, so the displacement is not necessarily a simple constant-velocity function of time. The name rate-zonal identifies the principal mechanism, not an assumption that every region of the gradient has identical transport properties.

Consider an illustrative mixture of two native macromolecular assemblies that have similar composition and buoyancy but differ in effective size and friction. They start in one zone. After a suitable interval the faster population has moved farther outward and the slower population remains closer to the initial region. If their zones overlap at first, continued centrifugation can initially increase the distance between their centres. But it also allows diffusion to broaden the zones, and eventually the faster material may reach the outer surface of the tube. The experiment must therefore be interpreted relative to a finite separation time.

The separation does not become automatically better if the run continues indefinitely. If both populations are denser than every part of the medium, sufficiently long centrifugation may bring them both to the collecting surface. Their previously distinct zones are then lost as both become part of a pellet. This is a defining examination distinction from isopycnic separation. A question that describes a separation deliberately stopped before any species pellets is often pointing toward rate-zonal rather than equilibrium separation.

Sample-zone width is important because it sets part of the initial overlap. If two populations have identical initial radial locations but different sedimentation coefficients, their centres begin to separate immediately. If they are distributed across a broad region at the start, particles of the slower population can begin ahead of many faster particles. The difference in their mean displacements must overcome the broad initial distribution before the populations can be resolved. Loading a wide region can therefore reduce resolution without changing the intrinsic sedimentation coefficients.

The zone can also be broadened by heterogeneity of particle shape, association state or size. A mixture of different oligomeric forms may produce several partially overlapping zones. If interconversion is slow compared with the separation interval, the species may behave approximately independently. If interconversion is rapid, particles can exchange between states while moving, and the observed distribution may not correspond to a simple mixture of fixed species. Analytical ultracentrifugation provides more formal tools for interpreting such effects, but the conceptual warning applies equally to preparative profiles.

Ribosomes and polysomes offer a familiar biological context for rate-based separations. Multiple ribosomes translating one messenger RNA form a polysome or polyribosome. Relative to individual ribosomal particles, polysomes frequently show greater sedimentation coefficients because the assembly has increased buoyant mass and altered hydrodynamic properties. A gradient profile can reveal changes in the relative abundance of subunits, monosomes and more rapidly sedimenting assemblies. However, a shift of material into a rapidly sedimenting region is not, in isolation, a direct measurement of protein synthesis rate. An assembly could accumulate because ribosomes are stalled rather than because translation is more productive.

The physical prediction in a simple rate-zonal question is often expressed in terms of displacement rather than absolute density. Suppose two ideal species have s values in the ratio 1:3 and initially occupy the same radial position in a uniform medium. For a sufficiently short interval during which their radial positions do not differ much from the initial radius, the directed displacement ratio is approximately 1:3. At longer intervals the exponential dependence of radius on time means that this approximate ratio need not hold exactly. In a real gradient, local solvent properties introduce further deviations. The appropriate calculation depends on whether the question is asking for a local comparison or a complete migration time.

Rate-zonal separation is useful when differences in hydrodynamic motion are greater than differences in buoyant density. Two particles of identical equilibrium density but very different sedimentation coefficients can often be resolved by a suitable finite-time separation. They would not, however, form distinct equilibrium density bands solely on the basis of that size difference. The distinction reveals why one should choose a separation method by identifying the physical variable that actually differs between the target and its contaminants.

Collection of rate-separated zones provides enriched material for later analyses, such as protein composition or functional assays. The collected fraction may still contain other particles with comparable sedimentation rates. An independent measurement is therefore necessary to assign identity. For instance, observing a band in the expected region for a ribosomal assembly supports but does not by itself prove the assembly's composition. Measurements of RNA content, protein constituents and other relevant characteristics strengthen the conclusion.

A useful comparison is with a race in which participants begin together and move different distances before a fixed stopping time. It captures the importance of initial position and duration. The analogy fails if taken literally, because particles do not voluntarily move, their velocities vary with the medium and field, and thermal diffusion broadens their distributions. It also cannot describe equilibrium density banding, where particles are driven toward preferred positions rather than continuously competing to travel the greatest distance.

Isopycnic separation and buoyant-density equilibrium

The term isopycnic means equal density. In isopycnic centrifugation, the important endpoint is the position at which the effective density of a particle matches the local density of the medium. Unlike the transient zones of a rate-zonal separation, the bands of an ideal isopycnic separation represent preferred positions in a stable density distribution. Movement toward those positions may take different amounts of time for different particles, but the final band positions need not follow the order of sedimentation coefficients measured in a uniform solvent.

Consider a gradient whose density increases from 1.05 grams per millilitre at its inner end to 1.30 grams per millilitre at its outer end. Imagine a particle with an effective density of 1.15 grams per millilitre. If placed near the inner end, it is denser than its surroundings and therefore moves outward. As it enters denser regions, the density contrast decreases and the directed outward movement slows. If the particle were instead placed near the outer end, it would be less dense than the surrounding liquid and its directed motion would be inward. Both trajectories approach the position where the local density equals 1.15 grams per millilitre.

The equilibrium position is stable because the sign of the buoyant driving force changes on either side. An accidental inward displacement brings the particle into a less dense environment that promotes outward movement. An accidental outward displacement places it in a denser environment that promotes inward movement. This mechanism is analogous to a floating object finding a level at which buoyancy balances its weight, although the centrifuge contains a continuous liquid density profile rather than a simple air-water interface. Thermal motion means that a molecular population forms a distribution around the preferred position rather than a mathematically infinitesimal surface.

A gradient must span the relevant particle densities. If the effective density of a particle exceeds the maximum density of the medium, the particle remains denser than its surroundings along the entire available path. It cannot find an internal isopycnic position, and may collect at the outer end. If its effective density is below the minimum medium density, it may accumulate near the inner boundary instead. Finding material at an endpoint can therefore indicate that the chosen gradient failed to bracket the particle density. It does not prove that the particle is inherently impossible to separate.

For a simple quantitative example, consider a gradient rising linearly from 1.10 to 1.30 grams per millilitre over a radial distance of ten centimetres. The density slope is 0.020 grams per millilitre per centimetre. A particle with density 1.16 grams per millilitre reaches its ideal equilibrium position three centimetres outward from the inner end, because its density is 0.06 grams per millilitre above the initial density. A second particle with density 1.24 grams per millilitre reaches seven centimetres. The two positions are separated by four centimetres. No information about the masses of the particles is needed for this ideal positional calculation.

Now imagine that the two particles differ in radius by a factor of ten but have exactly the same effective density of 1.16 grams per millilitre. They approach the same equilibrium position under the simple model. Their rates of approach may differ greatly, and thermal spreading may give different band widths. Nevertheless, their band centres cannot be resolved on the basis of density alone. This is a particularly important test of conceptual understanding: in a suitable rate-zonal separation they could be distinguished by sedimentation rate, but in ideal isopycnic separation the density variable is identical.

The effect of changing centrifugal speed requires careful phrasing. If a pre-existing density profile remained exactly unchanged with speed, the density-matching position of a particle would not change just because the field increased. Greater acceleration would affect the approach to equilibrium and the balance between restoring motion and thermal spreading. However, when the gradient medium itself redistributes in response to centrifugal speed, the density profile can change. The same particle might then band at a different radius while still banding at its characteristic density. Density matching does not imply that the numerical tube position is invariant under all instrument conditions.

Equilibrium does not imply perfect purity. Two different organelles may have similar buoyant densities because the contributions of proteins, lipids, aqueous contents and membrane structures produce similar effective density. Each may coexist in a single band. Conversely, one class of organelle can have a range of densities due to differences in size, composition, internal contents or physiological condition. A broad peak may therefore represent a heterogeneous biological population rather than merely poor technique. Measuring the density of a collected fraction and measuring its composition provide complementary information.

A classical application of buoyant-density equilibrium is the study of nucleic acids in dense salt gradients. Isotopic substitution or compositional differences can alter the effective buoyant density of a nucleic acid molecule. The measured position of the band reflects interaction with the surrounding medium as well as intrinsic composition. It would therefore be misleading to calculate nucleic acid band position using only the dry mass of its bases and ignoring solvent interactions. For textbook reasoning, however, distinct heavy, light and intermediate buoyant densities can be treated as given experimental properties.

Meselson and Stahl used equilibrium density separation as a central tool in their investigation of DNA replication in 1958. The essential question was whether the parental DNA strands were conserved together, separated between daughter duplexes, or distributed in some other way. DNA containing the heavier nitrogen isotope had distinguishable buoyant density from DNA containing the lighter isotope. After cells initially containing heavy DNA were transferred to conditions allowing synthesis with light nitrogen, the density distribution of extracted DNA provided evidence about the distribution of old and new material. The historical details belong principally to the replication unit; here the methodological focus is how a density measurement distinguishes predictions.

Under the classical idealised conservative model, after one replication round a population would contain parental duplexes retaining heavy material in both strands and newly synthesised light duplexes. Two distinct density classes would be expected. Under semiconservative replication, each first-generation duplex would contain one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand, giving an intermediate-density population. A single intermediate band therefore conflicts with the simple conservative prediction, but the first-generation observation alone does not distinguish semiconservative replication from every dispersive model. A later generation is needed to test the different predictions.

In the simple semiconservative model after two replication rounds in light conditions, half the duplexes are hybrid and half contain light material in both strands. The corresponding equilibrium separation should reveal intermediate and light density classes. The important feature is the appearance of a distinct light population together with the intermediate population. The classical dispersive model instead predicts molecules containing successively decreasing fractions of old material, with a distribution tending toward the light-density position. The experiment is powerful because its physical readout tests competing inheritance models rather than merely showing that DNA has changed.

The ability to discriminate these predictions depends on suitable controls. Heavy-only and light-only reference DNA provide positions against which an intermediate band can be interpreted. The density measurement must distinguish bands sufficiently well for the predicted patterns to be observed. If a mixed heavy and light control were unresolved into two bands, a single band from experimental DNA would not provide the same strength of evidence. Band width, detector response and the possibility of incomplete separation all affect whether the physical evidence can support a biological conclusion.

Density-gradient fractionation of organelles follows a similar logic but typically involves more heterogeneous objects than DNA molecules. A membrane vesicle may contain an aqueous lumen, membrane lipids, integral and peripheral proteins and associated structures. Changing any of these contributions can alter effective density. A perturbation that changes a membrane protein's distribution could reflect redistribution of the protein itself, a change in the density of its original organelle, or disruption of the organelle into a different set of particles. Therefore a density shift of a target molecule should be interpreted alongside compartment markers, structural evidence and appropriate comparison samples.

An equilibrium band may seem stationary when observed over time even though the individual particles within it still undergo random motion. This distinction between macroscopic stationarity and microscopic mobility is fundamental in physical chemistry. A population at equilibrium has no net directional redistribution under the specified conditions. It does not mean that every molecule has become immobilised or that the centrifugal field has ceased to exist. The same idea will reappear when analytical sedimentation equilibrium is considered, but the two techniques should not be conflated: the mechanism establishing the stationary distribution need not be identical.

Choosing between differential, rate-zonal and isopycnic methods

The three principal preparative strategies can be understood by asking what event defines a successful endpoint. In differential centrifugation, the event is arrival of desired material at a collecting surface while enough unwanted material remains suspended. In rate-zonal centrifugation, the event is the development of spatially distinct moving zones before the relevant particles are lost into a common pellet. In isopycnic centrifugation, the event is the establishment of a distribution around the density-matching position. All three methods use the same physical acceleration but organise the experiment around different outcomes.

Consider first a mixture containing intact nuclei and much smaller soluble proteins. The nuclei differ strongly from the proteins in their sedimentation behaviour and can often be preferentially enriched by a suitable differential separation. A complex gradient would add time and analytical complexity without necessarily providing a useful advantage if the only goal is removal of large structures. If the intended goal were instead purification of two kinds of organelles of overlapping size, a density gradient could provide a complementary basis for discrimination.

Suppose two particle populations have equal effective density but different radii and similar shapes. In the ideal Stokes model their sedimentation rates differ according to the square of their radii. Differential centrifugation may enrich the faster population if the target reaches the collecting surface much more readily than the slower population. Rate-zonal centrifugation can increase resolution by giving both populations a common starting region. Isopycnic separation would not resolve their band centres under the assumption that their effective densities remain identical. The most appropriate method depends on whether bulk enrichment or spatial separation is needed.

Now consider two populations with similar sedimentation coefficients in a uniform solvent but different buoyant densities. Their equal coefficients could arise because greater buoyant mass in one population is offset by greater friction. A rate-zonal separation in that solvent might not distinguish them effectively. An equilibrium gradient that spans both particle densities could establish different band positions. This example demonstrates why equal rates in one solvent do not establish equal particle densities. It also explains why combining techniques that exploit different physical properties can improve selectivity.

The distinction between the methods can be tested experimentally by observing how a profile changes with duration. If distinct bands progressively move outward and eventually disappear into a pellet, the behaviour supports rate-dependent separation in a gradient that does not bracket their densities. If bands approach reproducible interior positions from different starting locations and their centres remain approximately stationary, the behaviour supports an equilibrium density mechanism. Time-dependent observations are often more informative than a single endpoint image because several different mechanisms can produce similar-looking bands at one instant.

The presence of a density gradient alone is not sufficient to identify the method. Both rate-zonal and isopycnic techniques use gradients, and a real experiment may involve both rate differences and density-dependent slowing. For example, early during a separation particles can form distinct moving zones before approaching their density-matching positions. If the final analysis is made before equilibrium has been established, observed positions reflect a combination of rate and local density effects. The investigator must know the gradient range, initial loading arrangement and intended endpoint before assigning a mechanism.

A step gradient may be used to collect desired material at an interface rather than to measure an exact equilibrium density. This can be a practical form of enrichment, but a sharply defined interface band does not prove that all particles have the exact density of the adjoining solution. A particle may slow because the next layer is more viscous or because the density contrast becomes small. Whether the zone is genuinely at equilibrium depends on the conditions and the evidence of its behaviour over time.

Method choice is also constrained by the downstream biological assay. If intact organelle function is to be measured, preserving membrane architecture, associated proteins and internal solutes may be more important than maximising physical resolution. If total abundance of a particular protein is the endpoint, a more disruptive procedure might be acceptable provided that recovery is quantified and the protein remains detectable. A separation that is excellent for one endpoint can be inappropriate for another. This is not a contradiction: purity, integrity, yield and suitability for downstream measurement are separate objectives.

In many studies, differential centrifugation is followed by a gradient because removing large unwanted structures simplifies the mixture before a more selective separation. Subsequent chromatographic or affinity-based separation can add another independent criterion. Centrifugation separates particles based on their motion or density under an acceleration field; chromatography separates analytes based on their interactions with a stationary phase and mobile phase or access to a porous matrix. Each method has limitations that the other may help overcome. Effective fractionation is often designed as a sequence of complementary decisions rather than a search for one universally superior technique.

Analytical ultracentrifugation: observing molecules in solution

Preparative fractionation is usually assessed by what material can be collected after centrifugation. Analytical ultracentrifugation, abbreviated AUC, uses an optical observation system to monitor the distribution of material within a rotating cell. Instead of examining only the pellet and supernatant after the run, the observer obtains measurements of concentration as a function of radial position, often at successive times. The physical information contained in these distributions can be used to infer properties of proteins, nucleic acids and other macromolecular assemblies in solution.

An analytical cell commonly has sector-shaped compartments arranged so that the liquid occupies a radial geometry. This geometry is useful because the cell volume available per small radial increment changes in a defined way with radial distance. Optical windows allow measurement of the sample along the radial column. Depending on the instrument and sample, signals may be recorded using absorbance, interference or fluorescence detection. The optical signal is not automatically equal to molecular number; it must be related to concentration through the relevant detection response.

Absorbance detection uses the attenuation of light by absorbing species. For a simple single component under suitable conditions, absorbance can be related to concentration by the Beer-Lambert law. Interference detection senses differences in optical refractive properties between sample and reference, providing a complementary way to observe solute distribution. Fluorescence detection may be useful for selectively observing a fluorescent species in a mixture. Each signal responds to specific physical properties of the sample. When interpreting a profile of a mixture, the possibility that different species have different signal responses must be considered.

Sedimentation velocity and sedimentation equilibrium are two major conceptual forms of AUC. Sedimentation velocity examines how concentration distributions change with time under a centrifugal field. It can reveal differences in sedimentation coefficients and heterogeneity and can support analysis of molecular size or association states. Sedimentation equilibrium instead examines a stationary concentration distribution established by the balance of directed sedimentation and diffusion. Its key advantage in an ideal dilute solution is that friction does not appear in the final equilibrium relationship.

In a sedimentation velocity experiment, an initially approximately uniform concentration of a dense macromolecular solute begins to develop a depleted region near the inner boundary. Material moves outward relative to solvent, forming a moving concentration boundary. At successive observations, the position of this boundary shifts toward larger radii. Diffusion spreads the transition between depleted and solute-containing regions. The movement of a boundary is the collective expression of a concentration distribution; it is not direct observation of the path of any one molecule.

If a simple homogeneous species has a constant sedimentation coefficient, the logarithm of the ratio of two radial boundary positions divided by the elapsed time and the square of angular speed gives an estimate of s. This follows directly from the integrated migration equation derived earlier. In actual analysis, the shape of the concentration distribution, diffusion and the radial geometry must also be considered. A sharply defined mathematical boundary position is an idealisation. Modern sedimentation velocity analysis commonly fits physically based models to the complete measured profiles rather than relying solely on visually estimated positions.

For a worked illustrative problem, suppose a representative boundary is at 6.20 centimetres at one observation and at 6.32 centimetres after another 1,800 seconds at a constant 40,000 rpm. The angular speed is 2π × 40,000/60, approximately 4,188.79 radians per second. Its square is approximately 1.755 × 10⁷ per second squared. The radial logarithm is ln(6.32/6.20), approximately 0.01917. Dividing by 1,800 × 1.755 × 10⁷ gives about 6.07 × 10⁻¹³ seconds, or 6.07 S. The result is an apparent coefficient in the stated measurement conditions, not a standardised coefficient in water.

Temperature and solvent corrections are necessary when comparing coefficients measured under different conditions. For a simple noninteracting macromolecule, the observed coefficient is proportional to the buoyancy factor and inversely proportional to solvent viscosity when the molecular conformation and hydrodynamic shape are unchanged. A coefficient denoted s₂₀,w is conventionally corrected to water at twenty degrees Celsius. Such a correction does not imply that the molecule was actually measured in water or that it would remain structurally identical if transferred into pure water. It is a reference convention designed to facilitate physical comparison.

Sedimentation coefficient distributions are often used to describe solution heterogeneity. A sample with one predominant sedimentation peak can be relatively homogeneous in its sedimentation behaviour, whereas several peaks can indicate populations with distinct transport properties. A peak at higher s may correspond to oligomers or aggregates, but this assignment requires more evidence. Sedimentation coefficients depend on friction and buoyancy as well as mass, and the detection response may not be proportional to molar concentration for all populations. A single peak also does not exclude species that have similar sedimentation coefficients or rapidly exchange between states.

A protein that reversibly associates can display concentration-dependent sedimentation behaviour. Suppose a protein exists in a monomer-dimer equilibrium. At lower total protein concentration the monomer might predominate; at higher concentration the dimer might become more abundant. If association is reversible and exchange is rapid during the experiment, the sedimentation boundary can reflect coupled transport and association rather than two completely independent species. The resulting apparent coefficient may increase with concentration. This trend supports a hypothesis of self-association, but alternative causes of concentration-dependent transport, including nonideal solution interactions, need to be considered.

Comparing results across multiple concentrations is useful precisely because different models can make different predictions. A stable mixture of unrelated species with no concentration-dependent interaction might retain similar relative peak proportions when diluted. A reversible association equilibrium may redistribute species upon dilution. Such comparisons are stronger when the sample remains in the same chemical environment and the detector response is appropriately calibrated. Changing solvent composition while changing concentration would introduce another explanation for the observed difference.

The concentration profile also records diffusion. Diffusion tends to reduce concentration differences, so a boundary spreads as it moves. Smaller molecules frequently have larger diffusion coefficients than larger molecules of similar shape and density, although exceptions arise from hydrodynamic structure. A broader boundary is therefore not automatically evidence of sample impurity. The observed broadening can have contributions from thermal diffusion, a distribution of sedimentation coefficients, chemical association and instrumental effects. The analytical task is to distinguish these effects with appropriate models and controls.

The standard transport equation used to describe sedimentation and diffusion together is the Lamm equation. At examination level, its conceptual content is more important than numerical solution of the full differential equation. It expresses conservation of solute in radial geometry while accounting for a sedimentation flux driven by the centrifugal field and a diffusion flux driven by concentration gradients. A successful physical model must explain both the motion and the spreading of the measured profiles. A model that matches only the boundary centre while ignoring its width may miss evidence of heterogeneity.

Analytical ultracentrifugation has an important advantage in studying solution behaviour: it does not require the macromolecule to be immobilised on a stationary surface or passed through a porous separation matrix. Its results nevertheless depend on the chosen solvent, temperature, concentration and model of molecular behaviour. The lack of a chromatography matrix does not eliminate artifacts such as aggregation, sample instability, nonideal interactions or detection limitations. A careful conclusion specifies the conditions under which the inferred molecular properties apply.

Sedimentation equilibrium and molar mass

In analytical sedimentation equilibrium, an applied centrifugal field tends to establish a concentration increase toward larger radii for a solute with positive buoyant mass. Diffusion tends to oppose this concentration gradient by transporting solute from more concentrated regions toward less concentrated regions. When these fluxes balance, the macroscopic concentration distribution becomes stationary. This is a dynamic balance of two fluxes, rather than the density matching of a particle to a surrounding gradient.

To derive the equilibrium relationship for a dilute, ideal, noninteracting macromolecular species in a uniform solvent, write the outward sedimentation flux per unit area as Jsed = csω²r, where c is local concentration. The diffusive flux is Jdiff = −D dc/dr, where the negative sign indicates transport down the concentration gradient. At equilibrium the total flux is zero, so csω²r = D dc/dr. Rearranging gives d ln c/dr = (s/D)ω²r. The logarithmic derivative describes the fractional change in concentration per unit radial distance.

Integrating between a reference radius r₀ with concentration c₀ and a radius r with concentration c yields ln(c/c₀) = [sω²/(2D)](r² − r₀²). Substituting the ratio s/D from the Svedberg relationship gives ln(c/c₀) = [M(1 − v̄ρ)ω²/(2RT)](r² − r₀²). The derivation shows why the friction coefficient disappears at equilibrium: the same friction influences both sedimentation and diffusion and cancels in their ratio. This does not mean that friction has no effect on how rapidly equilibrium is established.

A graph of the natural logarithm of concentration against squared radius should be linear under the simple one-species ideal model with constant buoyancy factor and temperature. Its slope b is M(1 − v̄ρ)ω²/(2RT). Rearranging gives M = 2RTb/[(1 − v̄ρ)ω²]. If radii are entered in metres, the slope has units of inverse square metres and the resulting molar mass is in kilograms per mole when other variables are expressed in SI units. A slope obtained from squared centimetres cannot be inserted into this formula without converting its numerical units.

For a worked example, imagine an ideal protein with a measured equilibrium slope of 8,000 m⁻² at 293 K, a buoyancy factor of 0.27 and a speed of 20,000 rpm. The angular speed is about 2,094.4 radians per second, and ω² is approximately 4.386 × 10⁶ s⁻². The numerator 2 × 8.314 × 293 × 8,000 is about 3.898 × 10⁷. Dividing by 0.27 × 4.386 × 10⁶ gives a molar mass of approximately 32.9 kg mol⁻¹, equivalent to 32.9 kDa for the corresponding molecular mass. The numerical values are illustrative and do not describe a specific published protein.

For another interpretation, suppose a molecule has positive buoyant mass and the solvent remains uniform. Its equilibrium concentration increases outward. If the buoyant mass is zero, there is no directed sedimentation term under this ideal model and the equilibrium concentration is spatially uniform. If the buoyant mass is negative, the equilibrium distribution decreases outward. The last case is consistent with the flotation behaviour of particles less dense than their medium. These different profiles arise from the sign of the buoyancy correction rather than a change in the direction of centrifugal acceleration.

Sedimentation equilibrium and isopycnic centrifugation are sometimes confused because both use the word equilibrium. In ordinary ideal analytical sedimentation equilibrium, the solvent can be treated as having uniform density and the solute can have nonzero buoyant mass throughout the observation cell. The concentration gradient is maintained because sedimentation and diffusion oppose one another. In an ideal isopycnic experiment, the density of the medium varies with position and the particle has a preferred density-matching position. The local directed force vanishes at that position. The two descriptions may both involve diffusion, but their defining physical variables and analytical goals differ.

An equilibrium profile must genuinely have become stationary before the equilibrium equation is applied. Repeated scans that show negligible changes provide supporting evidence. A single profile collected after an arbitrarily chosen duration cannot establish equilibrium. A slowly moving species may still be approaching its final distribution. Comparing profiles at multiple speeds or times can help evaluate whether the ideal equilibrium model describes the data. The appropriate time for a real experiment is sample- and instrument-dependent; mathematical equilibrium reasoning should not be mistaken for a universal operating procedure.

For mixtures of noninteracting components with different buoyant molar masses, the total observed signal can be a sum of distributions with different exponential dependence on squared radius. Taking the logarithm of the sum does not generally yield a straight line. Curvature can therefore reveal that a single ideal species is an inadequate explanation. However, curvature may also result from reversible association, nonideal solution effects, background offsets or variations in detection response. It is evidence against the simple model, not proof of one uniquely specified alternative.

If two ideal species have different shapes but identical molar masses and partial specific volumes in the same solvent, their equilibrium concentration profiles are predicted to have the same logarithmic slope at a given angular speed and temperature. Their sedimentation velocities may differ because their friction coefficients differ. This contrast provides a powerful reasoning tool: velocity measurements are sensitive to hydrodynamic motion, whereas equilibrium measurements can provide buoyant molar mass independent of the friction term within the ideal assumptions. Combined evidence can therefore help distinguish changes in molecular mass from changes in molecular shape.

Experimental interpretation and quantitative quality controls

A fractionation experiment becomes a biological argument only when the investigator can explain why a measured molecule or activity appears in one fraction rather than another. A centrifuge produces a physical distribution; it does not directly report the original cellular address of every molecule in the sample. In practice, an interpretation depends on the relationship between the target signal and independently characterised markers, on the preservation of the starting biological material and on accounting for the material that is not recovered.

A compartment marker is a molecule, activity or structural characteristic whose association with a compartment has been established independently. For example, a mitochondrial inner membrane enzyme can serve as a mitochondrial marker, whereas a soluble glycolytic enzyme can provide evidence of cytosolic material. A marker of endoplasmic reticulum may help identify a membrane vesicle fraction. The marker must be selected according to the biological sample and question. An enzyme with multiple isoforms in several compartments would be a poor marker if the assay cannot distinguish those isoforms.

Consider the illustrative fractionation dataset below. The target activity and an independently established mitochondrial marker are measured in the homogenate and four mutually exclusive recovered fractions. Total protein is measured separately. The values are hypothetical and the units of activity are arbitrary but internally consistent. Unlike a protein concentration, the total protein mass represents the whole fraction. Unlike a specific activity, the total target activity is already multiplied by the relevant fraction volume if the original assay was expressed per unit volume.

The target activity is 1,000 units in the starting homogenate, which contains 100 milligrams of protein. Its initial specific activity is therefore 10 units per milligram. In the mitochondrial-enriched pellet the target activity is 700 units in 20 milligrams of total protein, giving a specific activity of 35 units per milligram. Dividing by the initial value gives an enrichment factor of 3.5. The recovery of target activity in that pellet is 700 divided by 1,000, or 70 percent. Neither value alone establishes the fraction's composition. A 3.5-fold enrichment shows that the measured target activity constitutes a greater proportion of the measured total protein, subject to the validity of the activity assay.

The sum of recovered target activities is 100 + 700 + 100 + 50 = 950 units. Thus the total recovery across all four recovered fractions is 95 percent. Total protein recovery is 95 milligrams out of 100 milligrams, also 95 percent in this hypothetical example. It is possible for target recovery and total protein recovery to be very different in a real experiment. For instance, a target enzyme could lose activity while its protein mass remains detectable. This is why an activity-based yield and an abundance-based yield should not be treated as interchangeable.

The independent mitochondrial marker has its largest recovered value in the same pellet as the target, and both have smaller values in the other fractions. This pattern supports an association of the target with the mitochondrial-enriched fraction. The target's ratio of mitochondrial pellet activity to starting activity is 70 percent, while the marker's corresponding ratio is 65 percent. Similar but nonidentical distributions are expected when sample heterogeneity, measurement error and partial compartment disruption affect the two signals differently. The similarity motivates a mitochondrial localisation hypothesis, not an unqualified statement that the target can only be found in mitochondria.

A cytosolic marker, a nuclear marker and an endoplasmic reticulum marker would help test possible contamination. If the mitochondrial-enriched pellet contains a large fraction of a cytosolic marker, trapped liquid or adsorption of soluble material may contribute to its apparent target activity. If a nuclear marker remains abundant there, nuclear fragmentation or incomplete early separation may be responsible. These alternative explanations are distinguishable because they predict different distributions of independent markers. A single marker, however well established, cannot describe every potential contaminant.

An additional density-gradient separation of the mitochondrial-enriched pellet could test whether the target and mitochondrial marker continue to associate across several distinct fractions. If both peak in the same density region while a lysosomal marker peaks elsewhere, the localisation hypothesis gains support. If the target instead follows the lysosomal marker, the first apparent association was probably caused by the overlap of different organelles in the differential pellet. If both markers peak together throughout the gradient, the gradient has not provided independent discrimination between them and another kind of evidence is needed.

Normalisation requires care when comparing fraction profiles. A graph that expresses every fraction's signal as a percentage of the total recovered signal can make profiles from different samples visually comparable. However, it can conceal a large difference in overall recovery. Imagine two preparations with identical normalised profiles but recoveries of 95 percent and 20 percent. The second preparation has lost most of the measured target despite producing a similar profile. Both the distribution and the total measured recovery should therefore be available when judging an experimental result.

The denominator must correspond to the experimental design. In the sequential scheme above, all four recovered fractions are mutually exclusive components of one starting homogenate, so their measured quantities can be summed. In a different design, investigators might separate several identical aliquots of the same homogenate independently and measure the pellet obtained at each condition. These pellets are not mutually exclusive portions of a single starting sample. Adding their target activities would count overlapping material multiple times. A question about yield should first be read as a question about the sample's lineage through the experimental diagram.

The relationship between purity and recovery can also be seen by comparing successive enrichment stages. Suppose a further separation of the 20 milligram mitochondrial-rich pellet yields a fraction containing 400 target units in five milligrams of protein. Its specific activity is 80 units per milligram, representing eight-fold enrichment relative to the homogenate, but its target recovery relative to the homogenate has fallen to 40 percent. Relative to the preceding mitochondrial-rich pellet, the target recovery is 400/700, or approximately 57.1 percent. Both recovery values are meaningful; they answer different questions and must be labelled with the relevant starting stage.

When activity in a sample is lower than expected, the loss may result from physical loss of the target material, reduced catalytic activity, or interference in the detection assay. To distinguish these possibilities, an abundance measurement can complement the activity assay. A protein detected in similar amounts before and after fractionation but with lower activity suggests that its function or the assay has been altered. A proportional decline in both amount and activity is more consistent with loss of material, though it does not identify the specific stage at which loss occurred.

Membrane integrity provides another useful dimension of evidence. If a lumenal enzyme is inaccessible to an externally added substrate because an intact membrane prevents substrate entry, measured activity may rise after the membrane barrier is removed. This phenomenon, called latency in suitable assays, can provide evidence that the enzyme was enclosed within a membrane-bounded compartment. Interpretation requires a substrate and assay whose behaviour is understood. A change in activity could also result from direct effects of the treatment on the enzyme, so an appropriate comparison of enzyme behaviour outside an intact compartment is important. The result is about accessibility, not automatically about abundance.

The biological state of the starting material can affect fractionation behaviour even when every mechanical condition is held constant. A perturbation might alter organelle morphology, promote fusion or fragmentation, change lipid composition or shift the association state of a protein complex. Such changes can alter sedimentation coefficient or buoyant density without changing the total amount of target protein in the cell. A target that shifts between fractions is therefore not automatically a protein that has been transported between compartments. The investigator should compare organelle marker distributions, structural evidence and total abundance to distinguish movement of the target from movement of the compartment itself.

For an illustrative analytical challenge, imagine that a membrane protein appears at higher density after a cellular perturbation. One hypothesis is that the protein has moved from one compartment to another. A second hypothesis is that the original compartment has become denser while retaining the protein. A third is that the protein has formed a new association or aggregate during sample preparation. The first hypothesis predicts separation from the original compartment marker and association with a different marker. The second predicts a parallel shift of the original marker. The third may predict a discrepancy between independent localisation measurements and the fractionation profile. Each prediction is testable, which makes the alternatives scientifically useful.

Finally, a result obtained with an appropriate physical method still needs biological replication and measurement controls. Technical repetition can reveal instability of the centrifugation or assay, whereas biological repetition addresses variation between independent biological samples. A blank establishes background; a reference material can help assess a known detection response; and an independently established compartment marker helps interpret identity. Centrifugation is most informative when these different controls are designed around a specific inference. Its power lies in turning a complex mixture into a series of testable physical and biological comparisons, not in the visual neatness of the separated tubes.

Original examination practice

These are original practice questions, not previous-year examination questions. All numerical scenarios are illustrative.

Part B: Conceptual foundations

1. What does RCF measure?

  1. Rotational speed
  2. Force on a particle in newtons
  3. Acceleration relative to standard gravity
  4. Solvent viscosity
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Acceleration relative to standard gravity

RCF = ω²r/g₀, a dimensionless ratio. Speed is measured in rpm; force additionally requires particle buoyant mass; viscosity controls drag.

2. At fixed radius, RCF is proportional to:

  1. rpm²
  2. rpm
  3. 1/rpm
  4. √rpm
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. rpm²

Angular speed is proportional to rpm and acceleration is ω²r. The linear and inverse choices ignore the squared speed term.

3. Which quantity has units of seconds?

  1. RCF
  2. Molar mass
  3. Dynamic viscosity
  4. Sedimentation coefficient
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Sedimentation coefficient

s = velocity/acceleration has time dimensions; 1 S = 10⁻¹³ s. RCF is dimensionless, molar mass is mass per mole and viscosity has units Pa s.

4. What causes outward sedimentation of a dense sphere in a rotating liquid?

  1. Its total mass alone
  2. Positive buoyant mass
  3. Its charge alone
  4. Its density equalling the solvent
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Positive buoyant mass

The net driving term is V(ρp − ρ)ω²r. Density matching removes it; charge does not enter this simplified mechanical relationship.

5. For a rigid sphere in a Newtonian fluid under Stokes conditions, friction is:

  1. 4πa³/3
  2. 6πηa
  3. η/a²
  4. Independent of viscosity
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 6πηa

Stokes drag coefficient is 6πηa. The second expression is sphere volume; neither of the other expressions captures the linear dependence on radius and viscosity.

6. Which rotor orientation is especially convenient for zones arranged along a nearly radial tube?

  1. Vertical rotor
  2. Any rotor regardless of geometry
  3. Stationary holder
  4. Swinging bucket
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Swinging bucket

A swinging bucket pivots outward. Fixed-angle and vertical geometries provide different paths and do not place the long tube axis in the same radial alignment.

7. What does the first pellet of a typical animal-cell differential fractionation preferentially contain?

  1. Nuclei and other large structures
  2. Only soluble enzymes
  3. Pure ribosomes
  4. Only lysosomes
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Nuclei and other large structures

Large structures are enriched first, but contaminants can co-pellet. Ribosomes generally require more demanding sedimentation conditions; soluble enzymes largely remain suspended.

8. What are microsomes?

  1. A new organelle in all intact cells
  2. Purified nuclei
  3. Small resealed membrane vesicles formed during disruption
  4. Free DNA molecules
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Small resealed membrane vesicles formed during disruption

Microsomes arise during homogenisation of membranes. They are experimental structures, not a native named organelle; they may contain vesicles from several cellular membranes.

9. Which method begins with a narrow sample zone and relies on a finite stopping time?

  1. Isopycnic separation only
  2. Equilibrium AUC
  3. Differential pelleting
  4. Rate-zonal centrifugation
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Rate-zonal centrifugation

Rate-zonal separation resolves different moving zones before pelleting. Differential pelleting starts with a mixed suspension; equilibrium methods aim for stationary distributions.

10. An ideal isopycnic band centre is set by:

  1. Maximum rpm alone
  2. Molecular mass alone
  3. Matching local medium and particle densities
  4. Initial loading depth alone
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Matching local medium and particle densities

At equal densities, net buoyant driving force vanishes. Mass and loading depth may affect the approach, not the density-matching condition.

11. What happens when a dense particle becomes less dense than its local medium?

  1. It moves inward relative to the liquid
  2. Its centrifugal acceleration reverses
  3. Its viscosity becomes zero
  4. It must pellet
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. It moves inward relative to the liquid

The acceleration field is still outward in the rotating description, but the net buoyant driving term changes sign. Inward movement is flotation.

12. Why is 30 S + 50 S not 80 S for the bacterial ribosome?

  1. One subunit loses all its mass
  2. Friction and buoyancy of the assembly differ from the individual subunits
  3. S is a unit of density
  4. S values must be multiplied
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Friction and buoyancy of the assembly differ from the individual subunits

Sedimentation coefficient is a ratio of buoyant mass to friction, not a sum of masses. The associated ribosome has its own transport properties.

13. What is the key difference between precipitation and sedimentation?

  1. Precipitation changes physical solubility; sedimentation is directed movement
  2. They are synonyms
  3. Sedimentation always requires denaturation
  4. Precipitation is random diffusion
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Precipitation changes physical solubility; sedimentation is directed movement

A precipitate can be collected by centrifugation, but native particles can sediment without precipitation. Neither necessarily involves denaturation.

14. Which is a characteristic measurement in analytical sedimentation velocity?

  1. Only pellet colour after a run
  2. Only solvent pH
  3. Only total protein mass
  4. Concentration profile versus radius and time
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Concentration profile versus radius and time

AUC observes evolving radial concentration distributions. Pellet colour, pH and total mass do not directly describe sedimentation transport.

15. Under ideal analytical sedimentation equilibrium, which term cancels from the molar mass relation?

  1. Temperature
  2. Translational friction coefficient
  3. Buoyant mass
  4. Angular speed
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Translational friction coefficient

Sedimentation and diffusion both depend inversely on friction, so their ratio removes f. Temperature, buoyant mass and angular speed remain in the equilibrium slope.

16. Which property is measured by a target activity divided by total protein mass?

  1. RCF
  2. Buoyant density
  3. Specific activity
  4. Rotor k factor
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Specific activity

Specific activity has units such as units per milligram. RCF is dimensionless; density and k factor describe unrelated physical quantities.

17. Which gradient medium is classically associated with DNA buoyant density separation in the replication experiment?

  1. Air
  2. Pure ethanol
  3. Cesium chloride
  4. A stationary agarose gel
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Cesium chloride

Cesium chloride can form a density gradient under rotation and allows DNA density classes to be resolved. Agarose electrophoresis relies on migration through a gel under an electric field.

18. What does a lower rotor k factor indicate at the specified speed?

  1. Greater molecular mass
  2. Shorter ideal clearing time for the same s
  3. Higher equilibrium particle density
  4. A guaranteed purer fraction
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Shorter ideal clearing time for the same s

t = k/s in the conventional units. A smaller k means a shorter predicted clearing time but says nothing by itself about purity or particle mass.

19. Which control addresses interference by the gradient medium in an optical assay?

  1. A higher rpm setting
  2. A larger pellet
  3. A different tissue with no replication
  4. A medium-matched blank
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. A medium-matched blank

A medium-matched blank estimates background from the matrix. Speed and pellet size do not distinguish assay background from the target signal.

20. What is meant by an enriched fraction?

  1. The target proportion exceeds its starting proportion
  2. All unwanted molecules are absent
  3. All original target must be retained
  4. Its particles cannot sediment
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The target proportion exceeds its starting proportion

Enrichment concerns relative composition. Purity and recovery require separate evidence and need not increase together.

21. Which statement describes a stationary equilibrium band at finite temperature?

  1. All particles are motionless
  2. The population distribution is stationary despite microscopic motion
  3. Brownian motion has ceased
  4. The centrifugal field is absent
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. The population distribution is stationary despite microscopic motion

Dynamic equilibrium permits thermal motion with no net redistribution. Neither cessation of molecular movement nor absence of the field is required.

22. Which relationship eliminates friction by combining sedimentation and diffusion?

  1. M = sD
  2. M = D/s
  3. M = sRT/[D(1 − v̄ρ)]
  4. M = ω²r
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. M = sRT/[D(1 − v̄ρ)]

The Svedberg molar-mass relationship follows from s = M(1−v̄ρ)/(Nₐf) and D = kBT/f. The alternatives omit required quantities or are dimensionally inconsistent.

  2. 3,333 rpm
  3. 6,124 rpm
  4. 7,500 rpm

  1. 0.75
  2. 1.5
  3. 9
  4. 2.25

Answer: D. 2.25

u scales as a²Δρ/η, so 9 × 0.5/2 = 2.25. A factor of nine ignores both solvent effects.

  1. 12 S
  2. 6 S
  3. 1.5 S
  4. 3 S
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 3 S

s is inversely proportional to f at fixed buoyant mass, so 6/2 = 3 S. It does not scale directly with friction.

26. A 20 S particle takes 40 min to cross a given radial interval at fixed speed. What time would a 10 S particle need at twice that speed?

  1. 40 min
  2. 20 min
  3. 80 min
  4. 10 min
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 20 min

t ∝ 1/(sω²). Relative time = (20/10)/4 = 0.5, giving 20 min. Both speed and s must be changed in the calculation.

27. Two ideal spheres have equal radii and density contrasts 0.10 and 0.25 g/mL in the same fluid. What is the speed ratio of the second to the first?

  1. 0.4
  2. 6.25
  3. 2.5
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 2.5

At fixed radius, field and viscosity, speed is proportional to density contrast: 0.25/0.10 = 2.5. Squaring the contrast incorrectly applies the radius dependence to density.

  1. 3 cm
  2. 1 cm
  3. 2 cm
  4. 6 cm
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. 3 cm

Slope = 0.20/8 = 0.025 g mL⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Offset = (1.175−1.10)/0.025 = 3 cm. The total distance is not used without accounting for the starting density.

29. A particle of density 1.32 g/mL is placed in an ideal 1.05–1.25 g/mL gradient. Predict its endpoint tendency.

  1. Move outward without an interior density match
  2. Band at 1.20
  3. Float inward
  4. Band at midpoint
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Move outward without an interior density match

The particle remains denser than every available layer and cannot reach an interior isopycnic position. It can reach the outer boundary or pellet.

30. Two particles with equal buoyant densities but different sedimentation coefficients start in a narrow zone. Which outcome is expected?

  1. Isopycnic band centres must differ
  2. Both methods must fail
  3. Rate-zonal resolution may be possible, while ideal isopycnic band centres coincide
  4. Mass alone fixes equilibrium position
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Rate-zonal resolution may be possible, while ideal isopycnic band centres coincide

Rate-zonal uses differences in transport rate; isopycnic uses matching density. Equal density does not imply equal velocity.

31. In a differential fractionation, a target is 600 U in the homogenate and 240 U in a pellet. Protein masses are 60 mg and 8 mg. Calculate pellet recovery and enrichment.

  1. 30%, 4-fold
  2. 40%, 3-fold
  3. 40%, 0.4-fold
  4. 60%, 8-fold
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 40%, 3-fold

Recovery = 240/600 = 40%. Initial specific activity = 10 U/mg; pellet = 30 U/mg; enrichment = 3. Recovery and enrichment have different denominators.

32. Two successive purification stages contain 500 and 300 target units. The original sample had 1,000 units. What is recovery of the final stage relative to the original and to the preceding stage?

  1. 60%, 30%
  2. 50%, 60%
  3. 30%, 50%
  4. 30%, 60%
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 30%, 60%

300/1,000 = 30%; 300/500 = 60%. Do not confuse the preceding-stage yield with the cumulative yield.

33. A fractionation yields target and mitochondrial markers in the same differential pellet, but gradient peaks separate and the target follows a lysosomal marker. What is the best conclusion?

  1. Mitochondrial localisation is proven
  2. The gradient must be invalid
  3. The differential pellet co-enriched different organelles; lysosomal association is better supported
  4. The protein has no cellular location
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. The differential pellet co-enriched different organelles; lysosomal association is better supported

Differential pellets are mixed. The additional separation distinguishes competing localisation hypotheses, although independent verification is still desirable.

  1. Their distribution shapes agree, but recovery differs substantially
  2. They have identical absolute target amounts
  3. The low-recovery sample is necessarily purer
  4. The low-recovery sample contains no target
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Their distribution shapes agree, but recovery differs substantially

Normalisation removes the total signal denominator. Similar fractional distribution does not establish equivalent absolute recovery or purity.

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.415
  3. 0.75
  4. 1.0

Answer: B. 0.415

The ratio is ln(4/3)/ln 2 ≈ 0.415. Equal outer RCF does not mean equal time because the radial path differs.

36. An ideal 5 S species in one solvent has a buoyancy factor of 0.25. In another solvent its factor is 0.20 and viscosity is doubled, with unchanged conformation. What is the new s?

  1. 4 S
  2. 2.5 S
  3. 10 S
  4. 2 S

Answer: D. 2 S

s scales as buoyancy factor divided by viscosity. 5 × (0.20/0.25)/2 = 2 S. Considering only one changed variable gives the first or second distractor.

37. An analytical equilibrium ln c versus r² slope quadruples when rpm doubles, with other conditions unchanged. Which inference is consistent?

  2. The same ideal buoyant mass explains the change
  3. Friction quadrupled
  4. Temperature doubled
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. The same ideal buoyant mass explains the change

The slope is proportional to ω² for constant M, buoyancy and T. A fourfold change is expected from a twofold speed increase without a change in mass.

38. Two ideal proteins of equal buoyant molar mass have different s values. Which pattern is predicted?

  2. Different equilibrium slopes necessarily
  3. Identical velocity because masses match
  4. Neither protein diffuses

Different friction changes s and D, while the ratio s/D and equilibrium slope remain the same. Equal mass does not imply equal shape or friction.

39. A target shifts to denser fractions after treatment; its established compartment marker shifts by the same amount. What is a plausible explanation?

  4. The original compartment changed its buoyant density

A parallel marker shift supports altered compartment properties rather than necessarily molecular relocalisation. Independent localisation evidence could distinguish alternatives.

41. A rate-zonal profile has two distinct bands at 15 min, but one pellet at a much later time. What is supported?

  2. The particle densities must be identical
  3. No separation occurred at 15 min
  4. Both populations are chemically identical
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The finite-time rate separation was lost through continued pelleting

If both particles remain denser than the medium they can reach the collecting surface. A common pellet is not evidence of identity.

42. AUC velocity profiles show a larger apparent coefficient with increasing protein concentration. Which design most directly tests reversible association?

  2. Measure only one endpoint pellet
  3. Compare matched-solvent concentration series and evaluate redistribution on dilution
  4. Change concentration and solvent together
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Compare matched-solvent concentration series and evaluate redistribution on dilution

A concentration series in the same solvent probes association while controlling solvent effects. Nonideality remains a possible alternative; changing multiple variables obscures the test.

43. An ideal density gradient's slope is halved with the same starting density and sufficient range. For two fixed particle densities, what happens to their band-centre separation?

  1. Halves
  2. Remains unchanged
  3. Becomes zero
  4. Doubles
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Doubles

Δr = Δρ/gradient slope; halving the slope doubles the ideal spatial separation. Actual resolution also depends on band width.

44. Fraction A is 1 mL at 8 U/mL, fraction B is 4 mL at 3 U/mL. Which has greater total target activity?

  1. A, 8 U versus 3 U
  2. B, 12 U versus 8 U
  3. Equal
  4. Cannot be computed

Total activity = concentration × volume. A contains 8 U and B 12 U. Comparing concentrations alone reverses the conclusion.

  2. The equilibrium signal is necessarily false
  3. All components must have identical masses
  4. Overlapping transport properties or association may conceal heterogeneity

A single apparent sedimentation peak is not proof of one species. Equilibrium inconsistency motivates additional models and controls, not an automatic unique diagnosis.

50. In the classic density-label DNA replication logic, a single intermediate band after one round excludes which simple model, and why is another round informative?

Write a short reasoned response.

Answer and explanation

Model answer

The simple conservative model predicts separate parental-heavy and newly synthesised light duplexes. A first-round intermediate band fits both semiconservative and simple dispersive predictions. Subsequent intermediate and light classes distinguish the classical semiconservative prediction from a simple dispersive shift.

Misconceptions and examination traps

RPM equals RCF

RPM is angular speed expressed per minute; RCF also depends on radius and the square of speed. Instrument displays encourage confusion. When comparing rotors, equate rN² rather than rpm.

RCF is a force in newtons

RCF is acceleration divided by standard gravity. The name is misleading; calculate particle driving force only after accounting for buoyant mass.

All particles move outward

A less-dense particle moves inward relative to the surrounding fluid. Ordinary pelleting examples bias intuition; examine the sign of ρp − ρ.

Larger always sediments faster

Density contrast, shape and drag may outweigh size. Equal-shape ideal spheres are a special case, not a universal rule. Compare buoyant mass/f.

Svedberg units add

s is a transport coefficient rather than mass. Ribosome labels look arithmetic; reason from friction and buoyant mass of the combined assembly.

A pellet is a pure organelle

Labels indicate enrichment. Mixed particle paths and trapped liquid explain contamination; use multiple markers.

Supernatant contains only dissolved molecules

Smaller suspended particles may not reach the collecting surface. Consider finite duration and later separations.

Microsomes exist as intact native organelles

Microsomes are largely resealed fragments of disrupted membranes. The experimental name may look like an organelle term; connect it to homogenisation.

Density gradients always imply isopycnic separation

Rate-zonal experiments also use gradients. Determine the endpoint and density range rather than naming the method from the medium.

Rate-zonal improves indefinitely with time

Moving bands can co-pellet at excessive duration. Distinguish finite-time separation from density matching.

Isopycnic position is determined by mass

Equal effective density gives equal ideal band centres, even when masses differ. Read the density profile rather than choosing the largest particle.

A step interface proves exact particle density

Transport slowing or incomplete migration can also produce interface accumulation. Check time-dependent evidence before assigning density.

A single AUC peak proves a pure species

Overlapping transport coefficients or rapid exchange can conceal heterogeneity. Use other concentration or equilibrium evidence.

Sedimentation equilibrium means motion stops

Diffusion and directed transport balance macroscopically. Thermal motion persists; distinguish zero net flux from zero molecular motion.

Analytical equilibrium equals isopycnic equilibrium

Uniform-solvent analytical equilibrium can have nonzero buoyant mass and a stationary concentration gradient. Isopycnic banding is defined by density matching.

Higher enrichment implies higher recovery

A purification step may lose target while removing more contaminants. Calculate specific activity and cumulative recovery separately.

A peak shift proves a protein relocalised

Changes in organelle density or association can shift its physical distribution. Compare independent markers and localisation evidence.

Revision and active recall

Choose the method by the physical variable that separates the desired components. Differential pelleting enriches populations using different rates and finite path lengths. Rate-zonal separation resolves moving zones from a narrow initial region; isopycnic separation resolves buoyant-density differences. Analytical ultracentrifugation records concentration profiles, allowing inference about transport and equilibrium behaviour. No physical fraction alone proves molecular identity or cellular localisation.

Essential terminology

Homogenate
Suspension obtained after disruption of cells or tissue.
Pellet and supernatant
Collected deposited material and remaining liquid, respectively, after a specified separation.
Buoyant mass
Particle mass corrected for displacement of solvent; determines the sign and magnitude of driving force.
Sedimentation coefficient
Radial velocity divided by local centrifugal acceleration, normally expressed in Svedbergs.
Isopycnic band
A particle population concentrated near the radius at which local medium density matches its effective density.
Specific activity
Measured activity divided by total protein mass.
Marker
An independently established characteristic used to interpret the identity or enrichment of a fraction.
