1. Why reaction speed is a biological problem

A cell may contain glucose and oxygen at the same time, yet glucose does not burn in the way a piece of paper burns in air. The conversion of glucose and oxygen into carbon dioxide and water is thermodynamically favourable under many biological conditions, but the uncatalysed transformations are generally too slow to support cellular metabolism. Living systems solve this problem by using enzymes and organised reaction pathways to control how rapidly chemical transformations occur. The rate of a reaction is therefore as important for understanding life as the identity of its products.

In Node 1.A.02, we learned that water, charge, hydrogen bonds and molecular packing influence whether particular molecular arrangements are favoured. A reaction adds another question: how does a system move from one arrangement to another? Reactants must approach, reorganise and often break or form bonds. Some rearrangements occur rapidly; others encounter barriers that make them rare. A product may be strongly favoured at equilibrium yet form at an imperceptible rate. Conversely, a product can appear rapidly and subsequently convert into a different, more stable product.

Chemical kinetics is the quantitative study of reaction rates and the factors that control them. Reaction dynamics, at the introductory level used here, concerns the molecular events underlying those rates: collisions, orientation, activation barriers and elementary steps. Thermodynamics tells us about the relative favourability of states and equilibrium; kinetics tells us about the route and timescale of approach. Neither subject can replace the other. We shall develop the full thermodynamic framework in 1.A.05 and detailed enzyme kinetics in 1.D.02. This chapter supplies the mathematical and conceptual language needed for both.

From observation to a predictive model

Suppose an enzyme-free reaction is followed in the laboratory by measuring a coloured reactant every minute. Initially its concentration may fall rapidly, and later the fall may become slower. Why does that happen? One plausible explanation is that fewer reactant molecules remain to undergo reaction. But that explanation is not sufficient: a process on a saturated surface may proceed at nearly constant rate despite falling substrate concentration. A reaction involving two partners may slow dramatically as both are depleted. A product might also inhibit the pathway or take part in the reverse reaction. Similar observations can thus have different causes.

Classical chemical kinetics arose from the attempt to distinguish such possibilities by quantitative measurements. Monitoring sucrose hydrolysis through its changing optical rotation is a classical example of following chemical composition over time. Arrhenius helped establish the quantitative relationship between rate constants and temperature. We do not need the historical details to solve modern problems, but the methodological insight remains essential: measure a change, identify a mathematical relation, and test whether a molecular mechanism is consistent with that relation. An observed rate law is evidence about mechanism, not automatically a unique proof of it.

To make this idea precise we will first define reaction rate, then learn how to obtain differential and integrated rate laws. After that we will investigate experimental designs for identifying reaction order, examine multistep mechanisms and learn why temperature and catalysts alter rates. Each mathematical relation will be connected to the actual measurements from which it can be obtained. By the end, you should be able to take an unfamiliar concentration–time dataset and reason from it rather than merely recognise the name of a formula.

2. Reaction rate: concentration, stoichiometry and time

Consider a closed, well-mixed solution at constant volume containing reactant A that is converted into product P. Square brackets denote molar concentration, so [A] means moles of A per litre of solution. If [A] decreases from 0.100 to 0.080 mol L⁻¹ over 10 s, the average rate of disappearance is 0.020/10 = 0.0020 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. We use a minus sign in front of the concentration change of a consumed reactant so that its disappearance rate is positive. The corresponding appearance rate of a product uses a positive sign.

The average rate describes a finite interval, not necessarily what is occurring at one particular instant. On a graph of [A] against t it is related to the slope of the secant joining two measured points. As the time interval shrinks, this becomes the tangent slope, the instantaneous rate. The derivative d[A]/dt is negative when A decreases. The instantaneous disappearance rate is −d[A]/dt. An initial rate is the instantaneous rate just after mixing, before significant depletion or product accumulation. In practice, researchers estimate it from a sufficiently short early interval or by extrapolating a fitted curve to the starting point.

Why stoichiometric normalisation is needed

Now consider a general balanced equation aA + bB → cC + dD, where a, b, c and d are positive stoichiometric coefficients. One reaction event as represented by the equation consumes a units of A for every c units of C produced. The concentration changes are consequently not equal unless the coefficients are equal. To define a common rate for the balanced reaction we divide each species rate by the appropriate coefficient. This normalisation avoids reporting different numerical reaction rates simply because different molecules were monitored.

v = −(1/a)d[A]/dt = −(1/b)d[B]/dt = (1/c)d[C]/dt = (1/d)d[D]/dt.

The units of v are concentration per time, for example M s⁻¹, when volume is constant. If volume changes substantially, concentration changes also reflect dilution or concentration of the solution. A more general formulation uses the extent of reaction ξ in moles, defined by dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ, where nᵢ is the amount of species i and νᵢ is its signed stoichiometric number (negative for reactants). The reaction rate per volume is (1/V)dξ/dt. For a single reaction at fixed volume this reduces to the preceding concentration expression. For several simultaneous reactions, a species concentration may receive contributions from more than one pathway, so a single reaction extent cannot describe the whole network.

Worked example: Normalising reactant and product rates

For 2A + B → 3P, a product appearance rate of 0.090 M min⁻¹ corresponds to v = (1/3)(0.090) = 0.030 M min⁻¹. The disappearance rate of A is 2v = 0.060 M min⁻¹, and that of B is v = 0.030 M min⁻¹. If a question asks for d[A]/dt rather than its positive disappearance rate, the answer is −0.060 M min⁻¹. If it asks for the reaction rate in M s⁻¹, divide 0.030 by 60 to obtain 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹.

This distinction is especially important for second-order reactions written as 2A → P. If the normalised rate is v = k[A]², then −d[A]/dt = 2k[A]². Many introductory integrated-law examples instead define k directly through −d[A]/dt = k[A]². Both conventions can be used consistently; the numerical values of the constants then differ by a factor of two. In this chapter, integrated single-reactant examples define the disappearance constant directly, and any different stoichiometric convention is stated explicitly.

A further subtlety arises for reversible reactions. The net rate can be zero even if individual molecules continue to react in both directions. At equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, not absent. A measured concentration change describes net accumulation or depletion. We will return to this distinction when discussing reversible mechanisms, because interpreting a plateau as the complete cessation of molecular activity can lead to a wrong model.

3. How reaction rates are measured

A rate law is obtained by observing a measurable quantity that changes with chemical composition. Concentration itself is rarely read directly from an instrument. Instead, the investigator chooses a signal such as absorbance, fluorescence, gas pressure, conductivity, mass, optical rotation or a chemically determined amount of reactant. A calibration relates the signal to concentration. The quality of the kinetic model depends on both the measurement and that conversion. If the signal responds to several chemical species, one must account for their contributions rather than automatically interpreting it as the concentration of a single reactant.

Spectrophotometric monitoring

For a single absorbing species in a dilute homogeneous solution, the Beer–Lambert relationship is Abs = εℓc, where Abs is dimensionless absorbance, ε is the molar absorptivity in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, ℓ is optical path length in cm, and c is molar concentration. A constant blank or background signal must be subtracted. If both reactant and product absorb, the measured absorbance is approximately the sum of their individual contributions when the law is valid. If reactant and product have the same molar absorptivity at the chosen wavelength, the reaction may produce almost no change in absorbance despite proceeding normally.

Worked example: Converting an optical signal to a rate

At a wavelength where only P absorbs, ε = 5000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and ℓ = 1.0 cm. Absorbance increases by 0.15 in 30 s after background subtraction. The increase in [P] is 0.15/(5000 × 1.0) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. The average product appearance rate is 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ M s⁻¹. If two P molecules are produced per balanced reaction event, the normalised reaction rate is half of this value. If ε or ℓ changes during measurement, this simple conversion is not valid.

In an enzyme assay, NADH oxidation is frequently monitored near 340 nm because NADH absorbs strongly there while NAD⁺ absorbs much less. This is an example of selective signal choice rather than a general rule that all biochemical reactions can be monitored at that wavelength. The slope of absorbance against time is converted to a concentration rate with an appropriate extinction coefficient and optical path. An assay may also be coupled to a second reaction, which must be sufficiently rapid that the signal tracks the first process rather than the auxiliary enzyme. Detailed assay design is reserved for the enzyme and biophysical-methods units.

Continuous and discontinuous measurements

Continuous monitoring provides many points without removing material from the reaction. Discontinuous methods withdraw samples at known times and stop or strongly slow the reaction before analysing each sample. The stopping procedure is called quenching. A good quench does not itself create or destroy the species being measured in a way that invalidates the result. For very rapid reactions, ordinary manual mixing can be slower than the process of interest. Rapid-mixing instruments reduce the time between mixing and measurement; even then an initial interval may be unobservable, known as instrumental dead time.

Other signals can be useful in specific systems. The pressure of a gas-producing reaction in a fixed volume may track product formation at controlled temperature, provided gas dissolution and leaks are accounted for. Conductivity may track conversion between differently charged species, but changes in temperature and ionic mobility can affect it. Optical rotation may follow a change in stereochemical composition, as in classical sugar hydrolysis measurements. No measurement method is universally suitable. A good kinetic experiment selects a signal that reports the relevant composition reliably and with suitable time resolution.

Controls and experimental uncertainty

Keep temperature, pH, ionic strength and mixing conditions constant when testing the effect of a reactant concentration. If the buffer or salt concentration changes along with the reactant, the observed rate change may have multiple explanations. Include a blank to correct background signal and use replicates to estimate variation. Check that the instrument responds linearly across the signal range. If a sample absorbs too strongly, diluting a quenched sample may be necessary for reliable measurement, but the dilution factor must be included in the concentration calculation.

An apparent initial rate can be underestimated if the reaction begins before the first reliable measurement. Likewise, a long early interval may include substantial substrate depletion, meaning its average rate is lower than the true initial rate for an ordinary positive-order reaction. Fitting a line to the earliest useful points is not enough unless those points actually lie within the nearly linear portion of the curve. These considerations matter in data-interpretation questions: distinguish a kinetic conclusion from an artefact of the assay.

4. Differential rate laws, order and rate constants

Having learned how to measure a rate, we now ask how it changes when we deliberately vary the composition of the mixture. For many reactions over a specified concentration range, the measured rate can be represented by a power law. For example, v = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. The exponents m and n are partial reaction orders with respect to A and B, and their sum is the overall order. The constant k is the proportionality factor at fixed temperature and other specified conditions. The equation is called a differential rate law because v represents an instantaneous derivative of concentration with respect to time.

v = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; overall order = m + n.

If m = 1, doubling [A] while holding all other variables fixed doubles the rate. If m = 2, the same change multiplies the rate by four. If m = 0, the rate is independent of [A] within the range where that law applies. A zero-order reactant is not chemically unnecessary: its participation may be concealed in a saturated catalytic site or in a step that does not determine the observed rate. Negative and fractional orders can arise in complex mechanisms, and an empirical order may vary with concentration if the underlying expression is not a single power law.

It is tempting to copy the coefficients of a balanced reaction equation into the exponents of a rate law. This is not generally justified. A balanced equation records the net consumption and formation of species, but does not tell us the sequence of molecular events. Only for an elementary mass-action step, subject to its assumptions, can one directly use the number of reactant molecules in that step as the powers in its rate expression. For an overall multistep process the powers must be determined experimentally or derived from a tested mechanism.

Units of the rate constant

For a rate expressed in M s⁻¹ and a power law of total order N, the concentration factor has units Mᴺ. Therefore k must have units M¹⁻ᴺ s⁻¹ so that the entire right-hand side has units M s⁻¹. Dimensional analysis is a useful check, particularly in exam questions in which two candidate rate laws differ only by one concentration power. If time is measured in minutes, the time unit becomes min⁻¹ instead. A rate constant expressed in M-based units changes numerical value if concentrations are instead expressed in mM, unless the order is one.

Worked example: A fractional and a negative order

Suppose a measured rate is v = k[A]½[B]⁻¹. Doubling A increases the rate by √2, whereas doubling B decreases it by a factor of two. Doubling both gives √2/2 ≈ 0.707 of the original rate. The negative exponent is not a negative rate, nor can it be extrapolated to [B] = 0; the empirical expression describes a finite range, often reflecting inhibition or another mechanistic dependence.

One can also define a local apparent order by napp = d ln v/d ln[A], holding other conditions fixed. This equals the constant exponent for an exact power law, but not necessarily for a saturating expression. For v = V[A]/(K + [A]), with V and K positive constants, napp = K/(K + [A]). It approaches one at low A and zero at high A. This is a useful preview of why enzyme-catalysed reactions need not follow a single order across all substrate concentrations. 5. Determining reaction order by the initial-rate method

The simplest experimental strategy is to start several otherwise identical reactions with different initial concentrations, then measure their initial rates. Measuring early minimises complications from product buildup and reactant depletion, although it does not eliminate every possible reverse or inhibitory process if products are already present. The key is controlled comparison: change one reactant concentration while keeping the others fixed, and compare the resulting rates. Repeating this for each reactant yields its partial order.

For the rate law v = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, take two measurements with the same [B] and different [A]. Dividing the two rates cancels k and the B factor: v₂/v₁ = ([A]₂/[A]₁)ᵐ. Taking natural logarithms gives m = ln(v₂/v₁)/ln([A]₂/[A]₁). The ratios are dimensionless and the logarithm base can be either natural or common if the same base is used throughout. If the concentration ratio is one, this comparison contains no information about the order in A. If both concentrations change, the cancellation no longer isolates one exponent unless another independent comparison is available.

Worked example: Deriving the complete rate law

Compare runs 1 and 2: [A] doubles, [B] stays fixed, and the rate quadruples. Thus 2ᵐ = 4, so m = 2. Compare runs 1 and 3: [B] triples, A remains fixed and the rate triples, so 3ⁿ = 3 and n = 1. The rate law is v = k[A]²[B], overall third order.

Using run 1, k = 0.0020/(0.10² × 0.10) = 2.0 M⁻² s⁻¹. Run 4 then predicts v = 2.0 × 0.20² × 0.30 = 0.024 M s⁻¹, matching the independent observation. If [A] = 0.15 M and [B] = 0.20 M, the prediction is 2.0 × 0.15² × 0.20 = 0.0090 M s⁻¹. Notice that the unused fourth measurement tests the fitted law rather than merely determining another parameter.

Sometimes no pair of experiments varies just one concentration. Then each comparison produces a linear equation in the unknown exponents after logarithms are taken. For example, if both A and B double and the rate increases eightfold, then m + n = 3. If in a second comparison A doubles and B is halved while the rate doubles, then m − n = 1. Solving gives m = 2 and n = 1. These two equations are independent; two comparisons that both change concentrations in the same proportion would not uniquely identify both orders.

Real measurements are noisy, and an observed rate ratio may not be exactly two or four. Repeated measurements and a regression of log rate against log concentrations can estimate an order with uncertainty. A measured slope near 1.9 may support a second-order model within experimental error, but should not be rounded to two without examining the uncertainty and concentration range. In a mechanism with changing apparent order, a log–log plot may curve. It is better to acknowledge that a simple power law fails than force all the data into a convenient integer exponent.

The initial-rate method determines concentration dependence but does not by itself predict the complete time course when concentrations change, especially when reverse reactions or product inhibition become important. To connect an instantaneous law with later measurements, we must integrate it over time. This brings us to the three classical single-reactant rate laws and their diagnostic plots.

6. Integrated rate laws: turning a rate into a time course

A differential equation describes the slope of a concentration–time curve at any point. An integrated equation gives the concentration itself at an elapsed time, subject to an initial condition. We will write C = [A], C₀ = [A] at t = 0 and use the disappearance rate −dC/dt = kCᴺ. For the following models, the reaction is irreversible to a good approximation, volume is constant, k is constant, the chosen concentration law applies throughout the interval, and other reactants are either absent from the law or effectively constant. The values of k in different orders have different units and cannot be compared numerically without context.

Zero order: a constant rate of consumption

In a zero-order process the slope is independent of the amount of A still present, within its valid range. Write dC/dt = −k. Separating and integrating from C₀ to C and from 0 to t gives C − C₀ = −kt. Therefore the remaining concentration falls linearly with time. A plot of C versus t is a straight line of slope −k and intercept C₀. A possible biological example is a catalytic reaction when all available catalytic sites remain saturated; the rate is then limited by the processing capacity, not the substrate concentration. As substrate becomes sufficiently depleted, the zero-order approximation fails.

Zero order: C = C₀ − kt; t½ = C₀/(2k).

The half-life t½ is the time required for concentration to fall from C₀ to C₀/2. Substituting that value into the integrated equation gives t½ = C₀/(2k). A larger starting concentration has a longer first half-life because the consumption rate stays constant. The ideal depletion time is C₀/k. The formula must not be used to predict a negative concentration after depletion. If the rate changes as the catalyst becomes unsaturated before that time, the actual late-time curve departs from the linear prediction.

Worked example: A capacity-limited process

A substrate begins at 0.080 M and is consumed at 0.0040 M min⁻¹ over a zero-order interval. After 10 min, C = 0.080 − 0.0040 × 10 = 0.040 M. The first half-life is 10 min. From 0.040 M to 0.020 M requires another 5 min, not another 10 min. The ideal linear model reaches zero at 20 min; no negative value is physically meaningful.

First order: a constant fractional loss per unit time

For a first-order process, each molecule of A has a constant probability per unit time of being converted under the specified conditions. Twice as many molecules then give twice as many conversions per unit time. The differential equation is dC/dt = −kC. Separating variables gives dC/C = −k dt. Integrating gives ln(C/C₀) = −kt. Exponentiation produces C = C₀ exp(−kt). The logarithm involves a dimensionless ratio; when plotting ln C, one implicitly divides concentration by a fixed reference unit such as 1 M.

First order: C = C₀e⁻ᵏᵗ; ln(C/C₀) = −kt; t½ = ln 2/k.

At half concentration C/C₀ = 1/2, giving kt½ = ln 2 ≈ 0.693. Unlike zero order, the half-life does not depend on C₀. Successive half-lives have the same duration. A plot of concentration versus time is curved, but ln(C/C₀) versus time is a straight line of slope −k and intercept zero. A plot of log₁₀(C/C₀) versus time has slope −k/2.303, not −k. The distinction between natural and common logarithms is a frequent source of wrong numerical answers.

Worked example: From half-life to percentage remaining

A first-order degradation has t½ = 20 min. Then k = 0.693/20 = 0.03466 min⁻¹. After 60 min, three half-lives have elapsed, so C/C₀ = (1/2)³ = 0.125: 12.5% remains and 87.5% has reacted. For 90% conversion, C/C₀ = 0.10 and t = ln(10)/k ≈ 66.4 min. A first-order process approaches zero asymptotically in the mathematical model; it does not reach an exact zero at any finite time.

Radioactive decay also follows first-order-like mathematics for a single independently decaying isotope, although it is a nuclear process rather than a chemical reaction. In biology, first-order loss is commonly used as an approximation for degradation of a substance under conditions where the relevant processing capacity is not saturated. A measured first-order time course does not prove that the molecular reaction is unimolecular: another reactant may be held in large excess, or a multistep mechanism may produce the same observed law.

Second order: the square of concentration

When the disappearance rate is proportional to C², dC/dt = −kC². Separating gives C⁻² dC = −k dt. The integral of C⁻² is −1/C, so −1/C + 1/C₀ = −kt. Rearranging gives 1/C = 1/C₀ + kt. The reciprocal concentration plot is linear with slope k and intercept 1/C₀. Its slope is positive, even though the actual reactant concentration is decreasing, because the reciprocal increases as concentration falls.

Second order: 1/C = 1/C₀ + kt; C = C₀/(1 + kC₀t); t½ = 1/(kC₀).

For a half-life substitute C = C₀/2 to obtain 2/C₀ = 1/C₀ + kt½. Hence t½ = 1/(kC₀). A higher initial concentration produces a shorter first half-life, because the reaction rate increases more strongly than the amount available. Successive halvings of concentration take increasingly long: if the first half-life is τ, the next is 2τ and the next 4τ. This differs sharply from a first-order process. The same mathematical form applies to A + B → P with rate k[A][B] when the starting concentrations are equal and both are consumed one-for-one.

Worked example: Predicting a second-order time course

A reaction has C₀ = 0.20 M and k = 0.50 M⁻¹ s⁻¹. The first half-life is 1/(0.50 × 0.20) = 10 s. After 30 s, 1/C = 5 + 0.50 × 30 = 20 M⁻¹, so C = 0.050 M. This represents 75% conversion, not the 87.5% conversion of a first-order process with a 10 s half-life. The second halving required 20 additional seconds.

For an arbitrary constant order N other than one, integration of dC/dt = −kCᴺ gives C¹⁻ᴺ = C₀¹⁻ᴺ + (N − 1)kt. This follows from integrating the power C⁻ᴺ, provided the concentration is positive and the assumed power law is valid. Substituting N = 0 and N = 2 recovers the zero- and second-order relations. N = 1 must be treated separately because the integration gives a logarithm. The general half-life for N ≠ 1 is (2ᴺ⁻¹ − 1)/[(N − 1)kC₀ᴺ⁻¹]. This is useful for comparing half-life dependence rather than memorising isolated formulas.

7. Identifying the order from kinetic plots and half-lives

When a full concentration–time dataset is available, the integrated rate laws provide a second method of identifying order. Plot concentration versus time for zero order, its natural logarithm versus time for first order, and reciprocal concentration versus time for second order. Under ideal conditions, the correct transformation gives a straight line. The slope and intercept then provide kinetic parameters and an independent check on the measured initial concentration. A graphical test should cover a substantial range of conversion; over a very short interval almost any smooth curve can look approximately linear.

Worked example: Finding order from two half-lives

At C₀ = 0.10 M, t½ = 40 s. At C₀ = 0.20 M, t½ = 20 s. The concentration doubles while half-life halves, so the data support second order. k = 1/(0.10 × 40) = 0.25 M⁻¹ s⁻¹. The second experiment independently gives k = 1/(0.20 × 20) = 0.25 M⁻¹ s⁻¹. If the rate were first order, the two half-lives would instead be equal.

Linearisation is a valuable teaching tool, but it can change how measurement error is weighted. Reciprocals greatly magnify errors when concentrations are small. Logarithms also change the uncertainty structure. In practical data analysis one may fit the original concentration–time equations directly and compare residuals, the differences between observations and predictions. A high correlation coefficient for one transformed plot alone is not conclusive if the range is narrow or if the error model is inappropriate. Look for consistent parameters, sensible residuals and predictions that agree with independent measurements. This is the level of experimental reasoning expected in analytical questions.

8. Two-reactant second-order kinetics and isolation methods

The single-variable second-order expression is not automatically valid whenever the overall reaction order is two. Consider A + B → P with rate k[A][B]. If A and B start at the same concentration and react one-for-one, their concentrations remain equal and the square law applies. But if their initial concentrations differ, the rate depends on two changing quantities. We can still integrate the law by using the reaction stoichiometry to express both concentrations in terms of a single extent of conversion.

Let a and b denote the initial concentrations of A and B in M. Let x be the concentration consumed from each at time t, so [A] = a − x and [B] = b − x. At constant volume dx/dt = k(a − x)(b − x). Rearrange to dx/[(a − x)(b − x)] = k dt. For a ≠ b, the integrand equals [1/(a − x) − 1/(b − x)]/(b − a). Integrating from x = 0 to x at time t gives the following relation.

kt = [1/(b − a)] ln{a(b − x)/[b(a − x)]}, for a ≠ b.

The logarithm argument is dimensionless. The product kt has units M⁻¹, which matches 1/(b − a). The formula is valid before the limiting reactant is fully consumed and assumes irreversible mass-action kinetics with constant k. When a = b, the expression has an apparent zero denominator and one should use the equal-concentration square-law solution rather than substitute directly. The limit of the unequal-concentration expression is the familiar 1/[A] − 1/a = kt.

Worked example: Unequal starting concentrations

Take a = 0.10 M, b = 0.20 M, and k = 0.50 M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Find the time to consume x = 0.050 M. Then a − x = 0.050 M and b − x = 0.150 M. The ratio inside the logarithm is (0.10 × 0.150)/(0.20 × 0.050) = 1.5. Thus kt = ln(1.5)/0.10 = 4.0547 M⁻¹, and t ≈ 8.11 s. If one had incorrectly used 1/(ka) as the half-life, the answer would be 20 s, because that formula assumes equal concentrations or a square law in A.

Pseudo-first-order behaviour

Suppose B starts at a concentration much larger than A. During the reaction, every molecule of A consumes one B, but the fractional change in B may be very small. We can then approximate [B] ≈ b, so −d[A]/dt = k[A][B] ≈ kb[A]. The observed law is first order in A with kobs = kb. It is called pseudo-first-order because the underlying bimolecular rate law has not changed; one concentration has been made effectively constant. If B is held in excess by a reservoir rather than merely a large initial amount, the approximation may be even better.

kobs = k[B]excess; [A] ≈ [A]₀e⁻ᵏᵒᵇˢᵗ.

This is the principle behind the isolation method. Keep all but one reactant in sufficiently large excess, follow the remaining reactant's kinetics, and vary the excess concentration in separate runs. If kobs is proportional to [B], the data support a first-order dependence on B in the original law. The slope of kobs versus [B] gives the underlying bimolecular rate constant. If kobs includes an additional first-order pathway, the plot may instead have a nonzero intercept, which can help identify competing processes.

Worked example: Recovering the bimolecular constant

With B fixed at 0.10 M in large excess, the observed first-order constant is 0.040 s⁻¹. Thus k = 0.040/0.10 = 0.40 M⁻¹ s⁻¹. At B = 0.25 M, the same law predicts kobs = 0.100 s⁻¹ and t½ ≈ 6.93 s. If A initially equals 1 mM, complete conversion consumes at most 1 mM of B, only 1% of the 0.10 M excess. The approximation is much less reliable if A and B both start at 0.10 M.

Hydrolysis provides a familiar example. Water is a reactant but in dilute aqueous solution its activity or effective concentration may be approximately constant. A rate law containing a water-dependent factor can then be expressed using an observed constant. The fact that water does not explicitly appear in a measured concentration law does not mean no water participates in the molecular mechanism. Similarly, a buffered hydrogen-ion activity can be treated as fixed for a particular experiment, while the observed k may change substantially when the buffer pH is changed between experiments. These links connect the present chapter to solution chemistry and to enzyme pH–rate profiles.

9. Elementary reactions, molecularity and reaction mechanisms

A balanced overall equation describes what is consumed and formed, not necessarily the route between them. A reaction mechanism proposes a series of elementary steps, each representing a molecular event or a simple transformation in a kinetic model. Adding the elementary equations and cancelling species formed and subsequently consumed should reproduce the overall reaction. This is a necessary check on a proposed mechanism. A second check is whether the mechanism predicts the experimentally observed rate law in the appropriate regime. A third is whether the proposed intermediates, conditions and molecular events are chemically plausible.

Molecularity is a property of an elementary step

A unimolecular elementary step involves one reacting molecular entity, such as an activated molecule undergoing rearrangement. Its mass-action rate is first order in that entity. A bimolecular step involves two reacting entities, either different species or two of the same species, and has the corresponding second-order mass-action dependence. An elementary termolecular event would involve three entities in a single event; simultaneous appropriate encounters are relatively uncommon, and many overall third-order laws are explained by a sequence of simpler steps instead. Molecularity is a positive integer by definition and is not assigned to a general multistep overall equation.

Reaction order, in contrast, is an experimentally determined or model-derived concentration exponent. It may be zero, fractional, negative or concentration-dependent. It can happen to equal molecularity for a single elementary mass-action step, but the two concepts are not synonyms. In the reaction A + B → P with a measured rate law proportional to [A] alone, we cannot conclude that B is absent from the overall process. B may participate after a slow unimolecular transformation of A, or its concentration may be held effectively constant. The experimental conditions and mechanism distinguish these alternatives.

Intermediates and transition states

An intermediate is a chemical species formed during one step and consumed in another. It may have a measurable lifetime and, in favourable cases, can be detected or trapped. A transition state is an activated configuration near the barrier separating reactant and product regions on an energy or free-energy profile. It is not a normal stable species that accumulates in a bottle. A two-step mechanism may contain one intermediate and two distinct transition states. The intermediate corresponds to a local minimum in a schematic reaction profile; each transition state corresponds to a barrier maximum along the depicted reaction coordinate.

Rate-limiting steps and their limits

Consider a simple sequence A → I → P. If conversion of A to I is slow and I rapidly converts to P, the product formation rate after an initial short adjustment can be governed largely by the first step. The intermediate does not accumulate much because it is removed rapidly. In the opposite limit, I can accumulate while product formation is slow. The measured disappearance of A and appearance of P may then occur on different timescales. Calling one step rate-determining is a useful approximation in the appropriate limit, not a universal rule for every time point and mechanism.

A common exam mistake is to choose the highest peak in a multistep free-energy diagram solely from its height on the page. A local barrier is the difference between a transition state and the state from which that step begins. A large absolute peak may follow a high-energy intermediate and therefore have a smaller local barrier than another step. In addition, populations, reverse rates and prefactors can affect which step controls the overall flux. A qualitative barrier diagram supports a rate-limiting-step assignment only when the relevant assumptions are clear.

As an example of a classical mechanistic puzzle, some reactions have a measured rate proportional to the square of one reactant and first power of another, although a simultaneous three-body encounter is unlikely. A fast reversible association of two molecules followed by slower reaction of the resulting intermediate with the third reactant can generate this law. The exponent of two arises from the concentration of the intermediate, not necessarily from a three-body collision. To derive that result we need the pre-equilibrium and steady-state ideas developed next.

10. Deriving mechanism-based rate laws: pre-equilibrium and steady state

Fast pre-equilibrium followed by a slow step

Suppose A and B rapidly establish a reversible association to form I: A + B ⇌ I, with forward constant k₁ and reverse constant k₋₁. The intermediate then reacts slowly with C to form P, with rate constant k₂. If the first step maintains near-equilibrium as I is consumed, its forward and reverse rates are approximately equal: k₁[A][B] ≈ k₋₁[I]. Define K = k₁/k₋₁, with units M⁻¹ for this concentration association model. Then [I] ≈ K[A][B]. The rate of product appearance is k₂[I][C]. Substituting for I gives v ≈ k₂K[A][B][C].

Fast pre-equilibrium: [I] ≈ (k₁/k₋₁)[A][B]; v ≈ (k₂k₁/k₋₁)[A][B][C].

The overall reaction A + B + C → P is third order under this approximation even though neither elementary step needs a three-body collision. The intermediate is removed from the final rate law because we express its concentration through species that can be prepared and measured. The approximation requires the equilibration of the first step to be much faster than the disturbance caused by product formation. If I is consumed rapidly, the first step may not remain near equilibrium and the resulting law can be different.

Steady-state approximation

For the same mechanism, instead of assuming equilibrium in the first step, write the intermediate balance explicitly. Formation of I occurs at k₁[A][B]. Removal occurs by reversal at k₋₁[I] and reaction with C at k₂[I][C]. Therefore d[I]/dt = k₁[A][B] − k₋₁[I] − k₂[I][C]. If I adjusts rapidly compared with the slow change of the other concentrations, its net rate of accumulation may be small over the interval of interest. Set d[I]/dt ≈ 0, giving a steady-state expression for its concentration.

[I] ≈ k₁[A][B]/(k₋₁ + k₂[C]); v ≈ k₁k₂[A][B][C]/(k₋₁ + k₂[C]).

Steady state is not equilibrium. The intermediate is continuously formed and removed; the two fluxes approximately balance while product accumulates. In pre-equilibrium the forward and reverse rates of a particular reversible step approximately balance. In steady state the sum of all formation fluxes approximately balances the sum of all consumption fluxes. The intermediate concentration need not be exactly constant over the whole reaction, and the approximation can fail during the early transient or when the separation of timescales is lost.

The derived steady-state law explains changing apparent order. If k₂[C] is much smaller than k₋₁, the denominator is approximately k₋₁ and we recover the pre-equilibrium result with first-order dependence on C. If k₂[C] greatly exceeds k₋₁, the denominator is approximately k₂[C]; cancellation gives v ≈ k₁[A][B], independent of C. The order in C shifts between one and zero without changing the elementary steps. At high C almost every intermediate proceeds to product rather than reverting, so the rate is limited by formation of I. At low C most intermediate molecules revert, and increasing C increases the probability of successful onward reaction.

Worked example: Testing a mechanistic limit

Let k₁ = 2.0 M⁻¹ s⁻¹, k₋₁ = 4.0 s⁻¹, k₂ = 20 M⁻¹ s⁻¹, [A] = [B] = 0.10 M and [C] = 0.20 M. The two removal terms are k₋₁ = 4 s⁻¹ and k₂[C] = 4 s⁻¹. The steady-state intermediate estimate is 2.0 × 0.10 × 0.10/8 = 0.0025 M. The predicted product rate is 20 × 0.0025 × 0.20 = 0.010 M s⁻¹. The limiting rate at very high C is 0.020 M s⁻¹. At C = 0.20 M both removal routes are equally important, so the pre-equilibrium approximation would not be justified.

This method is the conceptual foundation of many biochemical kinetic models. In an enzyme mechanism E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, the enzyme–substrate complex is an intermediate whose formation and consumption can be balanced approximately under suitable conditions. The detailed derivation of Michaelis–Menten kinetics requires conservation of enzyme, initial-rate assumptions and a distinction between the kinetic constant Km and equilibrium binding constants. These belong to 1.D.02; here the aim is to understand how eliminating an intermediate yields a measurable rate law and why saturation can arise from a molecular mechanism.

Matching a rate law does not uniquely establish a mechanism. Distinct mechanisms may produce the same limiting expression. Evidence such as detection of an intermediate, isotope substitution, changes in temperature or independently varied reactants can test further predictions. The scientifically sound conclusion from a successful rate-law derivation is that a mechanism is consistent with the data under the stated assumptions, not that all alternative mechanisms have been excluded.

11. Reversible, parallel and consecutive first-order reactions

Many biological processes involve a network rather than one isolated irreversible conversion. A molecule may return to its original state, choose between competing products, or pass through a transient intermediate before reaching the final product. Simple first-order models allow us to learn how these situations alter observed rates without entering specialist mathematical kinetics. They also provide a framework for interpreting relaxation, branching and pathway delays.

Reversible first-order reaction

Let A ⇌ B have forward constant kf and reverse constant kr, both in s⁻¹. For a closed system with a conserved total CT = [A] + [B], d[B]/dt = kf[A] − kr[B]. At equilibrium these opposing fluxes are equal, so [B]eq/[A]eq = kf/kr in this ideal concentration model. Substituting [A] = CT − [B] into the derivative gives d[B]/dt = kfCT − (kf + kr)[B]. Because [B]eq = CTkf/(kf + kr), subtract the equilibrium value to obtain an ordinary first-order decay of the deviation.

[B](t) = [B]ₑq + ([B]₀ − [B]ₑq)e⁻⁽ᵏᶠ⁺ᵏʳ⁾ᵗ.

The system relaxes toward equilibrium with observed constant kf + kr. The half-time of the deviation from equilibrium is ln 2/(kf + kr), not necessarily the time for A to reach half of its initial concentration. A plateau above zero can be a genuine equilibrium rather than evidence that the reactant has become chemically inactive. A conformational change between two molecular states is often modelled by this kind of reversible scheme as a first approximation.

Worked example: Recovering both rate constants from relaxation

At equilibrium 75% of molecules are B, and the relaxation constant is 0.80 s⁻¹. Then kf/(kf + kr) = 0.75 and kf + kr = 0.80 s⁻¹. Therefore kf = 0.60 s⁻¹ and kr = 0.20 s⁻¹. Their ratio is 3. The half-time of relaxation is 0.693/0.80 ≈ 0.866 s. If B begins at zero, after one relaxation half-time it has reached half of its equilibrium concentration, or 37.5% of the total.

Parallel reactions and branching

Consider irreversible competing paths A → B with constant k₁ and A → C with constant k₂. Both consume the same A. The total disappearance rate is −d[A]/dt = (k₁ + k₂)[A], so [A] = A₀exp[−(k₁ + k₂)t]. Product formation rates are k₁[A] and k₂[A]. Their ratio is k₁/k₂ at all times when the constants are fixed. If B and C initially equal zero and have no further reactions, integration gives [B] = A₀k₁/(k₁ + k₂) × (1 − e⁻⁽ᵏ¹⁺ᵏ²⁾ᵗ), and the analogous expression for C with k₂. The limiting product fractions are the individual rate constants divided by their sum.

Worked example: Predicting yield from competing paths

If k₁ = 0.030 s⁻¹ and k₂ = 0.010 s⁻¹, total A disappearance is first order with k = 0.040 s⁻¹. The half-life is 17.33 s, and the limiting yields are 75% B and 25% C. At one half-life, half the A has reacted; B therefore equals 0.375A₀ and C equals 0.125A₀. Raising k₁ while keeping k₂ fixed changes both the overall disappearance timescale and the product distribution. These conclusions require irreversible paths without subsequent reactions or changing conditions.

Consecutive reactions and transient intermediates

Now let A → I → P, with first-order constants k₁ and k₂, respectively. Starting with A₀ and no intermediate or product, [A] = A₀e⁻ᵏ¹ᵗ. The balance on I is d[I]/dt + k₂[I] = k₁A₀e⁻ᵏ¹ᵗ. Multiply by the integrating factor eᵏ²ᵗ, so the left-hand side becomes d([I]eᵏ²ᵗ)/dt. Integration from zero to t gives [I]eᵏ²ᵗ = k₁A₀(e⁽ᵏ²⁻ᵏ¹⁾ᵗ − 1)/(k₂ − k₁). Rearranging gives the intermediate time course.

[I](t) = A₀ k₁(e⁻ᵏ¹ᵗ − e⁻ᵏ²ᵗ)/(k₂ − k₁); [P] = A₀ − [A] − [I].

The expression assumes k₁ ≠ k₂ and one-to-one conversion, no reverse or side reaction. If k₁ = k₂ = k, the limiting expression is [I] = A₀kte⁻ᵏᵗ. The intermediate initially increases because its production exceeds its removal. At its maximum d[I]/dt = 0, so k₁[A] = k₂[I]. Solving with the expressions above gives tmax = ln(k₂/k₁)/(k₂ − k₁) for unequal constants. If the constants are equal, tmax = 1/k. After the maximum, removal exceeds production and the intermediate declines.

Worked example: When does an intermediate peak?

For k₁ = 0.40 s⁻¹ and k₂ = 0.90 s⁻¹, tmax = ln(0.90/0.40)/(0.90 − 0.40) ≈ 1.62 s. At that instant, [A]/A₀ ≈ 0.523 and [I]/A₀ = (k₁/k₂)[A]/A₀ ≈ 0.232. By conservation, [P]/A₀ ≈ 0.245. The intermediate is largest when formation equals removal, not when A is completely consumed. At t = 0, product formation rate is zero because I is initially absent.

If k₂ is much larger than k₁, the intermediate remains relatively small after an early transient and product formation closely tracks slow consumption of A. If k₁ is much larger than k₂, I accumulates and later disappears as P forms. Observing a transient peak can support a sequential mechanism, but a similar peak may arise from other network structures. The crucial reasoning is to use mass balance, the shape of each time course and controlled changes in rate constants to test which mechanism explains all the data. Complex metabolic networks will be studied later; these minimal models are sufficient to explain many exam problems involving intermediates and flux.

12. Collision theory, transition states and activation barriers

Why does increasing concentration often increase rate? Molecules move in a random thermal environment, and a reaction between two different molecules usually requires them to encounter one another. More molecules per unit volume can increase the frequency of encounters. But not every encounter produces a chemical transformation. The molecules must have an appropriate relative arrangement and must reach configurations in which old interactions can be reorganised into new ones. Collision theory is a simplified molecular explanation that connects encounter frequency, energetic requirements and orientation to the rate of elementary reactions.

For gas-phase elementary bimolecular reactions, the encounter model is especially intuitive. In solution the solvent repeatedly collides with the reactants, affects their relative motion and can stabilise or destabilise particular configurations. Molecular diffusion may limit how rapidly two partners meet. Once they meet, reaction may still be limited by a chemical rearrangement or conformational change. A very rapid association in solution can approach a diffusion-controlled limit, whereas many reactions are much slower because the probability of conversion per encounter is small. A larger collision frequency does not guarantee a larger reaction probability if orientation or activation requirements change.

What is an activation barrier?

The reactant state has an arrangement of bonds, charges and solvent contacts that is locally favourable. To reach product, the system must usually pass through less favourable configurations. The activation barrier describes the energetic or free-energy cost of reaching the transition-state region from the reactant state. In a simplified potential-energy profile this is the difference between the peak and the starting level. In an aqueous biological system, the relevant kinetic description also includes entropy and solvent rearrangement. Thus an activation energy estimated from the temperature dependence of k is not automatically identical to a directly depicted free-energy barrier ΔG‡.

A reaction coordinate is a conceptual measure of progress along a chosen molecular pathway. It is not the time axis of a concentration graph. A high barrier in a drawing does not specify exactly how many seconds a reaction takes without a quantitative model and prefactor. The transition state is also not simply the molecule with the highest temperature; individual configurations fluctuate and can exchange energy with their environment. The purpose of the profile is to compare the relative barriers and state energies for a proposed pathway.

In a simple potential-energy picture with reactant energy ER, product energy EP, and the same transition-state energy ETS for the forward and reverse routes, the barrier heights are ETS − ER and ETS − EP. Their difference is EP − ER. For an exothermic-like potential-energy profile with products lower than reactants, the reverse barrier is larger. For actual temperature-dependent Arrhenius parameters, this simple diagram should not be extended without assumptions about mechanism and thermodynamic quantities.

Worked example: Reading a barrier diagram

A schematic profile assigns 20 kJ mol⁻¹ to reactants, 85 kJ mol⁻¹ to a transition state and 5 kJ mol⁻¹ to products. The forward barrier is 85 − 20 = 65 kJ mol⁻¹ and the reverse barrier is 85 − 5 = 80 kJ mol⁻¹. The product–reactant energy difference is 5 − 20 = −15 kJ mol⁻¹, equal to 65 − 80. A favourable energy difference does not eliminate the 65 kJ mol⁻¹ forward barrier. The numbers describe the model profile, not measured Arrhenius parameters for a real biological reaction.

Temperature increases the importance of thermal fluctuations and often the fraction of molecular encounters able to overcome a positive activation barrier. In the classical collision model, this energy dependence motivates an exponential factor. The Arrhenius equation expresses this temperature effect empirically and quantitatively, connecting measured rate constants at different temperatures with an activation energy.

13. Arrhenius equation, temperature dependence and activation energy

Arrhenius described the temperature dependence of many reaction rate constants using an exponential law. Its central idea is that access to sufficiently activated configurations becomes more probable as absolute temperature rises. The rate constant depends on both an energetic factor and a pre-exponential factor. For many reactions over a moderate temperature interval, these parameters can be approximated as constant. This yields a convenient expression for comparing measured k values and estimating an apparent activation energy.

k = A e⁻ᴱᵃ⁄ᴿᵀ; ln k = ln A − Eₐ/(RT).

k is the rate constant for the specified law; A is the pre-exponential factor, with the same units as k; Eₐ is the Arrhenius activation energy in J mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in kelvin. The exponent must be dimensionless. If Eₐ is given in kJ mol⁻¹, convert it to joules or use R = 0.008314 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Never insert degrees Celsius directly into the reciprocal temperature. The logarithmic form implicitly uses a fixed reference unit for k and A. The prefactor can reflect encounter frequency, orientation and other factors, rather than a universal frequency that is identical for all reactions.

The Arrhenius plot

Compare ln k = ln A − (Eₐ/R)(1/T) with the straight-line equation y = b + mx. A plot of ln k versus 1/T has slope −Eₐ/R and intercept ln A if the parameters are constant. Since higher T corresponds to smaller 1/T, a normal positive activation energy gives a negative slope. When the horizontal coordinate is 1000/T rather than 1/T, the slope becomes −Eₐ/(1000R); forgetting that scale factor produces an activation energy off by a factor of 1000. If the vertical axis uses log₁₀ k, the slope is −Eₐ/(2.303R) for the unscaled 1/T axis.

The two-temperature equation

To remove the unknown pre-exponential factor, write the logarithmic equation at T₁ and T₂, then subtract. ln k₂ − ln k₁ = −Eₐ/R × (1/T₂ − 1/T₁). Hence ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). When T₂ exceeds T₁ and Eₐ is positive, the right-hand side is positive and k₂ exceeds k₁. This sign check is useful before performing numerical calculations. The two constants must describe the same kinetic law and pathway, and the pre-exponential factor must be approximately constant across the temperatures.

ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂).

Worked example: Estimating an activation energy

Suppose k₁ = 0.020 s⁻¹ at 300 K and k₂ = 0.080 s⁻¹ at 320 K. The ratio is 4. The reciprocal-temperature difference is 1/300 − 1/320 = 0.00020833 K⁻¹. Therefore Eₐ = R ln 4/(0.00020833) ≈ 55,320 J mol⁻¹ = 55.3 kJ mol⁻¹. Since the reaction accelerates on warming, a positive activation energy is consistent with the observation. The numerical value is an apparent Arrhenius energy for this interval.

Worked example: Predicting a temperature-induced rate change

For Eₐ = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, compare 298 K with 308 K. ln(k₂/k₁) = (50000/8.314)(1/298 − 1/308) ≈ 0.655. Exponentiating gives k₂/k₁ ≈ 1.93. If the initial rate is proportional to k and the concentrations are held fixed, the initial rate also increases by this factor. For a first-order process the half-life decreases to approximately 1/1.93 of its previous value.

Temperature coefficient Q₁₀

The temperature coefficient Q₁₀ is the ratio of rates or rate constants separated by 10 °C (equivalently 10 K). Under a single Arrhenius model at constant concentrations it is exp[(Eₐ/R)(1/T − 1/(T + 10))]. Many biological rates increase roughly twofold over certain temperature ranges, but Q₁₀ is not a universal constant of life. Even for a single reaction with constant Eₐ it changes with T. A change in substrate availability, enzyme activity, membrane state or pathway control can modify an observed physiological Q₁₀. It is a descriptive ratio unless the underlying model and conditions justify a mechanistic interpretation.

Why Arrhenius plots sometimes curve

A straight Arrhenius plot is evidence that a constant apparent activation energy describes the measured interval. Curvature may arise if the mechanism changes, if a pre-equilibrium changes the population of reactive molecules, if diffusion or viscosity becomes important, or if a catalyst changes conformation or loses activity. Some composite processes even have negative apparent activation energy over a range: their measured rate decreases when temperature increases because an equilibrium or another controlling process changes. This does not imply that all elementary energy barriers are negative. An observed slope describes the composite kinetic behaviour under those conditions.

Enzymes illustrate an important distinction. Warming an enzyme solution can increase the rate constant of the chemical transformation while also increasing the rate at which enzyme becomes inactive. The measured activity may therefore rise initially and then fall at higher temperatures. The temperature of maximum measured activity depends on incubation time, substrate, buffer and assay design; it cannot simply be interpreted as the Arrhenius optimum of a single elementary step. To estimate activation energy from enzyme data, one must select a range where the enzyme state and mechanism are sufficiently constant. This is another example of why the mathematical model must match the experiment.

14. Catalysis and transition-state stabilisation

A catalyst increases the rate at which a reaction approaches equilibrium by providing an alternative reaction pathway. It may temporarily form intermediates with reactants and products, but is regenerated in the net catalytic cycle. Catalysts do not change the overall standard free-energy difference between the same reactants and products or the equilibrium constant for that reaction at a given temperature and medium. They change kinetic accessibility, not the thermodynamic definition of the initial and final states. If the reaction is reversible, a catalyst consistent with microscopic reversibility accelerates approach from both sides without shifting the true equilibrium.

Activation free energy and biological catalysis

In transition-state theory, the rate of an elementary unimolecular process is related to the free-energy barrier by k ≈ κ(kBT/h)exp(−ΔG‡/RT). Here kB is Boltzmann's constant, h is Planck's constant, κ is a transmission factor and ΔG‡ is the molar activation free energy relative to the reactant state. The prefactor has units s⁻¹. This relation is a useful conceptual bridge, not a required specialist derivation in this chapter. Bimolecular reactions require an appropriate concentration or standard-state convention. ΔG‡ includes enthalpic and entropic effects, unlike a purely potential-energy picture.

If a catalyst provides an alternative route with a smaller activation free-energy barrier while other relevant prefactors remain comparable, the exponential dependence can produce a very large rate enhancement. A catalytic site may favour an activated geometry by orienting substrates, stabilising developing charges, transferring protons, coordinating a metal ion or temporarily forming a covalent intermediate. The noncovalent interactions described in Node 1.A.02 are therefore directly relevant: they help position and stabilise molecular arrangements along the pathway. Simply binding a substrate very strongly is not necessarily catalytic. If the ground state is stabilised more strongly than the transition-state region, the barrier relative to that bound state can become larger.

Worked example: Estimating a rate enhancement from a barrier difference

Assume two otherwise comparable unimolecular paths at 298 K have activation free energies differing by 12.0 kJ mol⁻¹, with the catalysed barrier lower. If the prefactors are equal, kcat path/kuncat path ≈ exp[12000/(8.314 × 298)] ≈ exp(4.843) ≈ 127. This illustrates why a modest barrier change on the molar scale can strongly influence rate. It does not establish an exact enhancement when the pathways have different kinetic orders, prefactors or rate-limiting steps.

Homogeneous, heterogeneous and biological catalysts

A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants, for example an acid dissolved in the reaction solution. A heterogeneous catalyst acts at an interface between phases, such as adsorption on a solid surface followed by conversion and desorption. A biological enzyme typically provides a structured active site that binds molecular partners and creates a chemically favourable environment. The categories describe different physical arrangements; the common requirement is a catalytic route and regeneration of the catalyst. An enzyme is not necessarily an ideal catalyst under all conditions because it can be inhibited, denatured or chemically modified.

Acid–base catalysis is especially important in biochemistry. Proton transfer can make a leaving group more favourable, activate a nucleophile or stabilise developing charge. Changing pH can therefore alter the rate by changing the concentrations of reactive protonation states. A buffer itself may participate in general acid–base catalysis, so two buffers at the same pH but different concentrations or identities can sometimes produce different rates. An experiment must distinguish a pH effect from a buffer-concentration effect. Detailed pH and buffer chemistry belongs to 1.A.03, and specific enzyme catalytic mechanisms to 1.D.01.

Enzymes change the speed of biochemical reactions and can channel pathways toward particular products, but an isolated catalyst cannot make an unfavourable equilibrium favourable merely by accelerating it. In metabolism, an unfavourable reaction may be coupled to another favourable reaction, changing the net thermodynamic process. Coupling and catalysis are distinct: coupling changes the net reaction being considered, whereas catalysis changes how rapidly a specified reaction occurs. That distinction prepares the way for our next chapter on thermodynamics and equilibrium.

15. Environmental effects, biological applications and experimental inference

Reaction rates in cells depend on more than the amounts of reactants and the temperature. The chemical identity of the reactants determines how readily bonds can be rearranged; the solvent and ionic environment influence charge interactions; pH affects protonation; viscosity and compartmentalisation affect molecular encounters; and catalysts create alternative routes. These factors are not interchangeable. If a rate changes when an experimental condition is varied, the challenge is to determine which step or population has changed, rather than simply assigning a new label to the reaction.

pH and reactive fractions

Suppose only the deprotonated form A⁻ reacts and the acid–base equilibrium HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ is fast relative to conversion. Under an ideal dilute approximation, the fraction of total A that is deprotonated is 1/(1 + 10pKa−pH). If the intrinsic conversion of A⁻ is first order with constant k, then the observed first-order constant for total A is kobs = k/(1 + 10pKa−pH). This is an example of a population effect: changing pH changes how much of the molecule is in the reactive state, even if its intrinsic reaction constant is unchanged. Other mechanisms, such as direct proton catalysis, can give different pH–rate profiles.

Worked example: Predicting an observed pH effect

For pKa = 7.0 and k = 0.20 s⁻¹, at pH 6 the reactive fraction is 1/11 and kobs ≈ 0.0182 s⁻¹. At pH 7 it is 1/2 and kobs = 0.100 s⁻¹. At pH 8 it is 10/11 and kobs ≈ 0.182 s⁻¹. The high-pH rate approaches the intrinsic k rather than increasing without limit. The formula assumes a fixed pH, rapid acid–base equilibration, and negligible reaction of HA.

Diffusion, solvent and surfaces

In a dilute, well-mixed liquid, reactant concentrations may adequately describe encounter frequency. In a crowded cell, molecules diffuse within compartments, bind to surfaces, and may encounter restricted geometries. If a rate is limited by diffusion, increasing viscosity can reduce the observed association rate. If an internal chemical transformation is much slower than molecular encounters, viscosity may have little direct effect on that step. Solvent changes can also affect activation barriers and reactive-state populations, so a viscosity experiment requires controls that avoid changing many solvent properties simultaneously.

For a heterogeneous reaction, the exposed surface area may determine how many reactants can contact catalytic sites. Finely divided material can react faster than the same mass in a single compact piece because its accessible area is larger. If the sites become saturated, adding more reactant may produce little rate increase. Stirring can improve delivery to a surface, but it does not necessarily change the intrinsic chemical rate constant. Distinguish transport limitation from molecular conversion limitation. This distinction is useful later in membrane transport, enzyme immobilisation and bioprocess kinetics.

Isotope effects as mechanistic evidence

Replacing hydrogen with deuterium changes nuclear mass and vibrational energies without substantially changing the identity of the electronic bonding partners. If breaking or forming a bond involving that hydrogen plays a significant role in a rate-controlling transition, the measured rate may change. A kinetic isotope effect is often expressed as a ratio kH/kD. A normal primary effect gives a ratio above one when cleavage of a hydrogen-containing bond contributes to the relevant kinetic barrier. A ratio close to one does not rule out proton transfer: other steps may control the observed rate or mask the effect. Isotope substitution may also alter equilibria or solvent properties, so the interpretation must be tied to a particular experiment.

For instance, suppose otherwise matched substrates containing H and D give kH = 0.48 s⁻¹ and kD = 0.12 s⁻¹. The ratio is four. This supports the involvement of hydrogen-sensitive processes in the observed kinetics, but by itself does not uniquely identify the atom transferred, the reaction sequence or the precise transition-state structure. The strongest evidence comes from combining the isotope result with the rate law, intermediate measurements and other controls. This is an example of classical evidence-based reaction dynamics at the level needed for NET reasoning, without entering specialist mechanistic theory.

Biological time scales and steady flux

A first-order model can describe the disappearance of a labelled pool of protein or metabolite under suitable conditions. But in an intact cell a substance may be synthesised while it is degraded. If production occurs at constant rate s (M s⁻¹) and first-order removal occurs at kC, then dC/dt = s − kC. At steady state Css = s/k; the concentration can be constant despite ongoing turnover. Rearranging the equation gives d(C − Css)/dt = −k(C − Css), so deviations from the steady concentration decay exponentially. A steady concentration alone cannot reveal how rapidly molecules are being replaced.

Worked example: A pool with ongoing production and degradation

A metabolite is produced at 6 μM min⁻¹ and removed with k = 0.20 min⁻¹. The steady concentration is 30 μM. If the system begins at 10 μM, then C(t) = 30 + (10 − 30)e⁻⁰·²ᵗ μM. After 5 min, C ≈ 22.64 μM. If production stops and the removal constant is unchanged, the existing pool then decays with half-life 3.47 min. Two cells can have equal steady concentrations but different production and removal rates; measuring the pool size alone does not determine its turnover.

This last example illustrates a central connection between chemical kinetics and biology. Dynamic steady state is a balance of fluxes, not a lack of molecular motion and not necessarily thermodynamic equilibrium. A cell may continuously consume energy to sustain fluxes and concentrations away from equilibrium. 17. Consolidation: a problem-solving map

Begin each kinetic problem by writing the actual quantity being measured. Is it a species disappearance rate, a product appearance rate, or the stoichiometrically normalised reaction rate? Next write the differential law and check its dimensions. If initial rates are supplied, compare runs with fixed conditions to extract concentration exponents. If time-course data are supplied, choose an integrated model and test its predictions beyond one interval. Remember that a second-order law in two different reactants does not necessarily reduce to a square law in one of them. If another reactant remains effectively constant, identify the observed pseudo-order and the underlying rate constant separately.

For multistep mechanisms, first sum the elementary equations to verify the overall stoichiometry. Write formation and removal terms for intermediates. Decide whether pre-equilibrium or steady state is justified, and eliminate the intermediate from the observed rate expression. If the problem concerns competing or reversible reactions, use mass balance and include all applicable fluxes. For temperature problems, use kelvin, consistent energy units and the correct logarithm and axis scaling. For catalysis, distinguish a change in activation barrier from a change in the free-energy difference between initial and final states.

Common misconceptions

Reaction order is not generally stoichiometry or molecularity. A rate constant does not have the same units for every order. A first-order half-life refers to a constant fractional decrease, while a second-order half-life depends on initial concentration. A zero-order law cannot continue past depletion. A pseudo-first-order reaction need not be unimolecular. An intermediate is not a transition state, and steady state is not necessarily equilibrium. A negative Arrhenius slope on a reciprocal-temperature plot usually corresponds to positive activation energy. A catalyst does not change the equilibrium constant of the same reaction. A favourable reaction can still be very slow.
