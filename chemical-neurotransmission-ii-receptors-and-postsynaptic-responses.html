1. From a chemical message to a cellular decision

At a chemical synapse, the arrival of an electrical impulse in one neuron does not require the same electrical impulse to appear in the next cell. Between the two membranes lies an extracellular space across which molecules can move, but across which the cytoplasm of the two cells is not continuous. The previous chapter described how the presynaptic neuron releases neurotransmitter from vesicles. The task of the receiving cell is different: it must recognize those molecules and translate the recognition into an appropriate response. A receptor provides the molecular connection between an event outside the cell and a change within the cell. The postsynaptic response is consequently a property of the receiving membrane and its associated machinery as much as it is a property of the transmitting neuron.

Consider a motor neuron that contacts a skeletal muscle fibre and a neuron that contacts another neuron within the spinal cord. In the first case the motor command is normally transmitted with high reliability; the muscle membrane generates its own electrical impulse. In the second case the arrival of a single signal may produce only a small voltage displacement that cannot initiate an impulse by itself. Both contacts can employ chemical communication, although their purposes differ. The motor contact transfers a command to an effector, whereas many neuronal contacts provide information that must be combined with other information. Understanding these different arrangements requires separating the chemistry of transmission from the electrical and physiological consequences of receiving a message.

The postsynaptic membrane contains proteins with regions that recognize particular extracellular ligands. A ligand is a molecule capable of binding to a particular molecular site. Binding can stabilize a receptor conformation that differs from the conformation favoured in the absence of ligand. At some receptors this structural rearrangement immediately changes the accessibility of a water-filled passage through the membrane. At other receptors the rearrangement activates a separate intracellular signaling protein, which in turn changes an enzyme, an ion channel or another cellular target. Both designs allow an extracellular transmitter to influence the cytoplasm without requiring entry of the transmitter itself. The first design is the foundation of rapid ligand-gated transmission; the second provides more varied and frequently longer-lasting modulation.

The specificity of recognition is not the same as specificity of response. A neurotransmitter molecule may be capable of interacting with several structurally distinct receptor proteins. Each receptor may couple recognition to a different type of response. Even two cells expressing the same receptor can respond differently if their resting membrane voltages, intracellular ion concentrations or downstream effector proteins differ. It follows that knowing which neurotransmitter was released is useful but insufficient to predict the behaviour of the receiving cell. To predict the response, identify the receptor, identify what it controls, and examine the initial state of the target.

The receiving cell does not need to absorb a large fraction of all the transmitter molecules released in order to respond. Receptor occupancy changes the probability that molecular machinery will occupy an active conformation. When this machinery includes an open ion channel, many individual ions can traverse the membrane during the lifetime of the opening. The large number of charges moved is not matched by a requirement for the transmitter to cross the membrane with them. Chemical recognition controls permeability, while gradients established before the message arrived supply the immediate driving force for ion movement. This distinction connects receptor signalling to the physical chemistry of membrane transport studied in Unit 2.

It is also necessary to distinguish a response recorded from the postsynaptic membrane from the complete sequence of communication. A weak response could arise because few vesicles were released, because little transmitter was available in each vesicle, because fewer functional receptors were accessible, or because the membrane conditions reduced the ionic driving force. These alternatives can produce superficially similar voltage traces. Experimental biology addresses the ambiguity by manipulating the releasing and receiving sides independently. A direct application of transmitter to the receiving membrane, for example, can test reception without requiring an action potential in the presynaptic neuron. Normal responsiveness in such a test is informative, although it does not prove that all aspects of receptor organization at the intact synapse are normal.

A graded response has a magnitude that can vary according to the number and timing of active synapses, rather than following the all-or-none description of a propagated action potential. The local displacement produced by a synapse may decay before reaching the region of the cell that generates spikes. A second input may arrive while the first displacement is still present, or a neighbouring synapse may draw current away from it. In this way, chemically activated membrane conductances provide a means of combining signals. The final decision to generate an action potential depends on the state of the membrane at the relevant trigger region, not on a count of transmitter molecules released into a particular cleft.

Throughout the chapter, a distinction will be maintained between electrical excitation, electrical inhibition and biochemical modulation. An increase in a second messenger can alter subsequent responsiveness even if it produces little immediate voltage change. An inhibitory conductance can oppose another signal without visibly hyperpolarizing the cell at rest. Conversely, a depolarization can fail to increase firing if it simultaneously reduces the membrane's sensitivity to other excitatory inputs. These possibilities are not obscure exceptions; they follow from the basic properties of channels, gradients and membrane resistance. The purpose of studying receptors is to understand the chain of causes linking ligand binding to the actual output of a cell.

2. How evidence separated release from reception

The chemical explanation of synaptic transmission became convincing through experiments that asked what changes when a nerve is stimulated and what can reproduce that change in the absence of a nerve impulse. Otto Loewi's early experiments with frog hearts showed that stimulating a nerve could release a transferable substance capable of altering the activity of another heart. The significance was not that all synapses use the same transmitter or that every nerve contact is chemical. Rather, an effect that could be transferred in a fluid supported the existence of a chemical intermediary. In a preparation where the receiving tissue remained functional but was not directly stimulated by the original nerve, the transferred material still changed its behaviour. Later work identified acetylcholine as an important mediator of such cholinergic effects.

The neuromuscular junction offered a particularly useful opportunity to examine reception directly. The motor end plate is a restricted membrane region of a skeletal muscle fibre that lies opposite the nerve terminal. Electrical recording close to this site can distinguish a local junctional depolarization from electrical activity propagating along the muscle. In their 1951 intracellular recording study, Paul Fatt and Bernard Katz examined the end plate potential as an electrical event with spatial and temporal properties. Their 1952 report described spontaneous small electrical responses associated with the end plate. These events could be detected even when the motor nerve was not deliberately stimulated, supporting the idea that transmitter could be released in discrete packages.

A spontaneous miniature end plate potential should not be confused with the opening of one receptor channel. A single packet of transmitter can activate numerous receptors. The voltage response reflects their combined electrical effect together with the electrical properties of the fibre. A different level of measurement is necessary to observe individual channel openings. The conceptual advantage of comparing miniature responses with evoked responses is that a miniature provides information about the postsynaptic effect of a small release event. If a manipulation changes the number of events without changing their usual size, a change in release is a reasonable hypothesis; if their size changes, receptor responsiveness, vesicle filling and driving force become important possibilities.

Suppose a hypothetical muscle preparation produces spontaneous miniature potentials of similar average amplitude before and after treatment, but nerve stimulation evokes responses only half as large as before. One possible explanation is a reduction in the average number of vesicles released by stimulation. This interpretation becomes stronger if transmitter applied directly to the muscle produces a response comparable to that of the untreated preparation. It becomes weaker if the treatment also changes receptor sensitivity or the geometry of transmitter diffusion. The comparison illustrates why the location of a disturbance cannot be inferred from one measurement of the evoked response alone. Miniature amplitude and event frequency are different kinds of information, and neither measures receptor protein abundance directly.

Another historical step was the ability to measure membrane current while controlling the membrane voltage. In a voltage-clamp experiment, an amplifier supplies a compensating current to maintain the membrane near a chosen voltage. The measured current reveals the electrical effect of activated channels at that voltage. If transmitter is applied repeatedly while the holding voltage is changed, the resulting currents may change magnitude and eventually reverse sign. The reversal voltage constrains the identity of the ions that carry the current. This observation offers more information than the direction of a postsynaptic voltage displacement at one resting potential, because the latter is specific to that starting condition.

A further level of evidence became available through patch-clamp recordings developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann. A small membrane area can be electrically isolated sufficiently to resolve the discrete currents produced when individual channels open and close. When a transmitter is presented to an appropriate membrane patch, the appearance of discrete channel currents supports the idea that ligand recognition is linked to the switching of conductive pores. The same unitary current amplitude may occur repeatedly, whereas the duration or frequency of openings varies. A change in the average whole-cell current can therefore reflect changed gating rather than a changed current through each open channel. This is a mechanistic distinction that cannot be made by measuring voltage alone.

The usefulness of an isolated membrane patch depends on the receptor mechanism. A receptor that directly contains the ion-conducting pore can often retain responsiveness when the membrane is separated from much of the cytoplasm. A receptor whose normal response needs soluble intracellular factors may lose part of its signaling ability after isolation. Failure to obtain a response from an excised patch is thus not sufficient proof that the membrane contained no receptors. Orientation of the patch, exposure of the ligand-binding face, preservation of the necessary signaling proteins, and channel stability all influence the observation. The experimental design must match the particular question about the receptor rather than treating all recordings as equivalent.

Evidence for receptor subtypes also came from selective pharmacological responses. A substance can stimulate one receptor class but not another, or interfere with one pathway while leaving a second pathway functional. In a preparation containing several receptor classes for the same transmitter, different components of the response can be separated using appropriate controls. Selectivity is rarely absolute at all concentrations, so the dose range and independent evidence for receptor identity matter. The strongest conclusions combine functional recordings with molecular identification or genetic evidence. Receptor classification is consequently based on structure, ligand recognition and coupling mechanisms rather than only the name of the physiological transmitter.

The historical progression from a transferable chemical effect to a local voltage response, quantal events, voltage-clamp measurements and individual channel currents illustrates the importance of scale. An organ response does not establish the identity of the receptor pore, and a channel opening does not establish how the entire circuit behaves. Each experimental approach connects observations to a different part of the causal chain. A well-supported explanation must preserve those distinctions while showing how the molecular events can account for electrical responses and, ultimately, cellular output.

3. Membrane potential, charge and the driving force for ions

The membrane potential is the electrical potential of the inside of the cell relative to the outside. With this definition a resting value of minus seventy millivolts means that the inside is seventy millivolts more negative than the surrounding fluid. The voltage arises from a small separation of charge near the two sides of the membrane, rather than from a large electrical imbalance throughout the entire cytoplasm. The lipid bilayer is a poor conductor of ions; consequently it can maintain a voltage even when each bulk solution is almost electrically neutral. Charged ions cross readily only through suitable transport pathways such as channels and carriers.

Two influences act on an ion near the opening of a membrane channel. The first is the concentration gradient, which favours net movement from higher to lower chemical activity. The second is the electrical force due to the membrane potential. For a positive ion, movement towards a more negative compartment is electrically favourable. For a negative ion, the electrical preference is opposite. These forces can reinforce each other or oppose each other. The direction of spontaneous net movement through an open channel depends on their combined influence, described as the electrochemical gradient. Merely examining which compartment contains more ions does not in general determine the direction of movement.

The equilibrium potential of an ion is the membrane voltage at which its chemical and electrical tendencies balance. At that voltage there is no net passive movement of that ion through a selective channel, even though individual ions can continue to move in either direction. In an ideal dilute-solution approximation, equality of electrochemical potentials across the membrane gives RT ln(Cin/Cout) + zF(Vin − Vout) = 0. Rearrangement gives Eion = (RT/zF) ln(Cout/Cin). R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, F is Faraday's constant, z is the signed ionic charge number and C represents the concentration on the indicated side. Using activities rather than concentrations improves accuracy for nonideal solutions.

At approximately 37 degrees Celsius, this relationship can be written Eion ≈ (61.5 mV/z) log10(Cout/Cin). The sign of z matters: chloride is a monovalent anion and its charge number is minus one. For a potassium concentration of 140 mM inside and 5 mM outside, the calculated equilibrium potential is close to −89 mV. At a membrane voltage of −65 mV, opening a potassium-selective channel favours net outward movement of positive charge and draws the voltage towards the more negative equilibrium value. The opposite prediction would result if the cell were held at a voltage more negative than the potassium equilibrium potential. A channel is not a pump and does not impose a fixed direction of ion movement.

For sodium, an illustrative concentration of 145 mM outside and 15 mM inside gives an equilibrium voltage near +61 mV at the same temperature. At ordinary negative resting voltages, both the concentration gradient and the electrical gradient favour inward movement of sodium ions. The movement transfers positive charge into the cell and tends to make the membrane voltage less negative. However, a sodium-selective channel cannot drive the voltage above its own equilibrium value by passive movement alone. As the voltage approaches that value, the net driving force decreases. Sodium influx through a receptor should therefore not be described as a source of an unlimited fixed depolarization.

For chloride, consider an outside concentration of 120 mM and an inside concentration of 10 mM. Substituting the signed charge number gives an equilibrium voltage close to −66 mV. At a voltage of −50 mV, opening a chloride-selective channel will favour net movement of chloride into the cell, which is the movement of negative charge inward and is conventionally described as outward electrical current. The membrane is driven towards a more negative voltage. At −80 mV, the same chloride channel favours chloride leaving the cell and tends to depolarize it towards −66 mV. The channel has not changed molecular identity or selectivity; the starting voltage has changed relative to its equilibrium voltage.

The Nernst relationship estimates the equilibrium potential for one ion. Many neurotransmitter receptors are not exclusively selective for a single ion. A nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, for example, can conduct several cations. Sodium and potassium may move in opposite directions at the same moment through such a receptor. The net current is the sum of the charge flows. The reversal potential of the entire receptor current is the voltage where those net flows balance, not necessarily the equilibrium potential of either sodium or potassium individually. For many nonselective cation receptors, the reversal potential is near zero millivolts under ordinary physiological conditions, although the exact value depends on the ion gradients and relative permeabilities.

A change in permeability modifies the membrane's electrical connection between the inside and outside solutions. Conductance, denoted g, is a measure of the ease with which current can flow and has units of siemens. In a simple linear approximation to a channel current, I = g(V − Erev). Here V and Erev are the membrane voltage and reversal voltage, and the convention is that positive current represents net outward movement of positive charge. The difference V − Erev is the signed driving force. If g is expressed in nanosiemens and voltage in millivolts, the product is in picoamperes. This unit relationship follows because one nanosiemens multiplied by one millivolt equals one trillionth of an ampere.

The formula helps separate activation from driving force. A receptor may be fully activated but produce no net current when V equals Erev. If the cell voltage then changes while the receptor remains open, current will appear without any new ligand binding. Conversely, a large driving force cannot generate a receptor current if the relevant conductance remains zero. A small measured current can therefore indicate weak gating, weak driving force or both. At a fixed voltage, changing the number of open channels changes the conductance; at a fixed conductance, changing the voltage changes the driving force. Experimental manipulations that separate these factors are especially valuable for identifying receptor defects.

A channel does not create the ion gradient that supplies its current. Gradients are maintained over longer periods by active transport and ion-coupled transport systems. Sodium and potassium gradients depend strongly on the sodium-potassium ATPase, while intracellular chloride may be regulated by cation-chloride cotransporters. Receptor opening dissipates part of the stored electrochemical energy. The energetic cost of communication includes the subsequent maintenance or restoration of gradients, not a direct requirement for an ATP molecule to be hydrolysed each time an ion passes through an ordinary ligand-gated pore. The energetic distinction becomes important when considering why healthy cellular metabolism is needed for sustained neuronal activity even though a channel conducts passively.

The voltage change produced by a current also depends on the electrical capacitance of the membrane. Capacitance represents the ability to store separated charge and is measured in farads. For a capacitor, Q = CV. If capacitance C is approximately constant, current changes its charge at a rate I = dQ/dt. Thus the voltage cannot change infinitely rapidly when a finite current flows. The membrane takes time to charge or discharge, even when the underlying receptor channel opens quickly. Postsynaptic currents and postsynaptic potentials therefore need not have identical time courses. This physical fact will explain the duration of graded potentials and their ability to overlap during signal integration.

4. Ionotropic receptor architecture and gating

An ionotropic receptor contains both a ligand recognition mechanism and an ion-conducting pore within the same molecular complex. This arrangement is particularly useful when a receiving cell needs a rapid response to a brief release of transmitter. The ligand can bind on the extracellular side and change the conformation of the protein complex. Structural movements alter the narrow region of the pore that normally prevents conduction. Once the conducting conformation is occupied, suitable ions can move through a hydrated passage. The ligand does not need to traverse this passage, and the movement of ions is governed by the electrochemical driving force rather than by the direction in which the ligand approached the receptor.

Most familiar synaptic ionotropic receptors are assembled from multiple protein subunits. A central pore is surrounded by several subunits that contribute to ligand recognition, gating or ion selectivity. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, GABA type A receptor, glycine receptor and serotonin 5-HT3 receptor belong to a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel family. Pentameric means that the functional receptor complex contains five subunits. Ionotropic glutamate receptors belong to a different structural family, normally assembled as tetramers. The shared role of converting extracellular chemical recognition into ion flow is not evidence that all of these receptors share the same number of subunits or the same detailed pore architecture.

A channel's selectivity is determined by the chemical environment and geometry of the conducting pathway. Different ions differ in charge, hydrated radius and interactions with amino acid side chains. A pore suitable for cations need not be favourable for anions. Nevertheless a cation-selective channel need not distinguish perfectly between sodium, potassium and calcium. The ratio of permeabilities and the cellular gradients together influence the net current. A receptor's extracellular binding specificity and its pore selectivity are distinct characteristics. A channel can bind one chemically specific neurotransmitter yet conduct several chemically different ions, because the ligand-binding site and the ion permeation pathway serve different molecular functions.

Binding of a ligand does not mean that the receptor remains continuously open while the ligand is present. Individual receptors fluctuate among conformations. A minimal model distinguishes an unbound closed state, a ligand-bound closed state, an open state and a ligand-bound nonconducting desensitized state. The exact number of physical states depends on the receptor. Ligand binding favours particular conformations and usually increases the probability of opening, but stochastic opening and closing remain possible. Desensitization means that responsiveness can diminish even while agonist is present; it is not identical to removal of ligand, enzymatic degradation of transmitter or a reduction in vesicle release.

A useful model of a whole-cell receptor conductance is g = NγPo, where N is the number of available identical channels, γ is the conductance of one open channel and Po is the fraction of time the channels spend open on average. This approximation assumes that the channels can be treated as equivalent and that their currents add. It explains three distinct molecular ways to change a response. Loss of receptor protein decreases N; a pore mutation can change γ; and an altered ligand-binding or gating mechanism can change Po. A reduction in the whole-cell current does not distinguish these mechanisms on its own. In a recording from a single channel, however, a change in current amplitude can be separated from a change in the fraction of time spent open.

For illustration, if one open receptor has a conductance of 20 pS and the driving force has a magnitude of 50 mV, the magnitude of its current is 1 pA. If 100 such channels are available and their mean open probability is 0.25, their mean combined current has a magnitude of 25 pA, assuming the driving force is held constant. These numbers are a teaching model rather than a measurement of a particular receptor. The computation illustrates why the number of physical receptors is not equal to the number of channels open at any instant. The whole-cell conductance expresses a population average over channels that fluctuate between conducting and nonconducting conformations.

Ligand-gated and voltage-gated channels are distinguished by the variable principally controlling their opening. In the former, occupation of a ligand-binding site changes the conformational equilibrium. In the latter, a change in membrane voltage influences the conformation of the channel. The distinction is functional rather than absolute: some ligand-gated receptors have voltage-sensitive permeation properties, and the response of a voltage-gated channel can be modified by intracellular signals. The NMDA receptor is an important example of a transmitter-gated channel whose current is also strongly influenced by membrane voltage because extracellular magnesium can obstruct its pore.

Rapid neurotransmission requires both opening and a return to a nonconducting state. The receptor response cannot track rapid changes in transmitter availability perfectly because binding, unbinding and conformational changes each occur at finite rates. A very brief transmitter pulse can produce a current that continues after most free transmitter has disappeared. Different receptor families have different activation and deactivation kinetics. These properties contribute to the time course of the synaptic current. A long-lasting response does not, by itself, prove that a G protein is involved, and a fast response does not mean that all receptors in the synapse have identical kinetics.

The organization of receptor molecules within the membrane also matters. A cluster of channels positioned opposite a release site encounters a high transient transmitter concentration efficiently. The same number of receptors dispersed over a distant region of membrane might be less strongly activated by a vesicle released at that site. Anchoring and scaffolding proteins can therefore contribute to the functional strength of a synapse without changing the basic chemistry of receptor recognition. A molecular measurement of total cellular receptor abundance does not necessarily reveal how many functional channels are correctly localized at the postsynaptic surface. This distinction is central to interpreting mutations that affect receptor trafficking rather than receptor conduction.

5. Acetylcholine: one transmitter and two receptor strategies

Acetylcholine is an ester formed from choline and an acetyl group. The charged quaternary ammonium region helps explain why it does not simply pass through the lipid bilayer of a target cell to initiate its usual rapid synaptic effect. Instead, extracellular recognition sites of acetylcholine receptors determine how the signal is received. Acetylcholine is an instructive transmitter because it demonstrates two different designs of receptor in the same organism. Nicotinic receptors directly conduct ions, whereas muscarinic receptors activate G proteins. Their names refer to substances that helped distinguish the receptor types pharmacologically; they do not imply that nicotine or muscarine is the normal chemical message released at these synapses.

At an adult vertebrate skeletal muscle motor end plate, the major nicotinic receptor contains two alpha1 subunits and one each of beta1, delta and epsilon. The developmental form has a gamma subunit in place of epsilon. This example shows how a stable overall molecular design can be modified by the expression of different subunits. The mature receptor contains extracellular ligand-binding regions and a central transmembrane ion pathway, with each subunit contributing membrane-spanning segments. Acetylcholine normally binds at two appropriate extracellular interfaces, helping to stabilize an activated conformation. The movement of the subunits is coupled to an opening of the ion pathway, not to the physical transport of acetylcholine through the pore.

The open muscle nicotinic receptor permits sodium and potassium movements. At the normal negative resting voltage of a muscle fibre, sodium entry dominates the net electrical effect, while potassium can leave at the same time. The resulting inward current depolarizes the end plate. As the depolarization increases, the driving force for sodium entry decreases and the driving force for potassium exit increases. The net receptor current consequently declines as voltage approaches its reversal value, often close to zero millivolts. An explanation that identifies the receptor as a purely sodium-selective channel may correctly predict a depolarization at rest but fails to explain its measured reversal potential.

Neuronal nicotinic receptors also belong to the pentameric family, but different subunit compositions produce different functional properties. Some neuronal nicotinic receptors have greater relative calcium permeability than the muscle form. This can influence intracellular signaling as well as membrane voltage. A comparison between neuronal and muscle receptors should therefore preserve the common principle of ligand-gated cation conduction while avoiding the assumption that all receptors labelled nicotinic have identical activation kinetics, selectivity or distribution. At the examination level, it is sufficient to recognize that variation within a receptor family has meaningful consequences for the receiving cell.

Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors illustrate the other major route of transduction. These receptors contain seven membrane-spanning helices characteristic of G-protein-coupled receptors. The binding of acetylcholine does not create an ion-conducting pathway through the muscarinic receptor itself. Instead, the activated receptor interacts with an intracellular heterotrimeric G protein. The identity of the associated G protein and its available effectors determines which downstream process is altered. A muscarinic receptor can consequently change membrane conductance indirectly, alter second-messenger concentrations or influence the responsiveness of the target to subsequent signals.

A particularly clear comparison is provided by skeletal muscle and cardiac pacemaker tissue. At skeletal muscle the nicotinic response depolarizes the motor end plate. At cardiac pacemaker cells, M2 muscarinic receptor activation can promote opening of G-protein-regulated inwardly rectifying potassium channels through beta-gamma subunits and reduce cyclic AMP signaling through Gi. These changes tend to slow pacemaker activity. The detailed generation of the cardiac rhythm belongs to animal physiology, but the receptor comparison establishes a broader principle: one transmitter does not have one universally prescribed electrical effect. The difference is explained by the receiving protein systems rather than by a change in the chemical identity of acetylcholine.

The name inward rectifier can itself be misleading. A potassium channel with inward rectification conducts more readily in one voltage range than another. Under relevant physiological conditions it can nevertheless carry outward potassium current, helping to draw the membrane towards the potassium equilibrium potential. Rectification describes a relationship between current and voltage rather than an assertion that all potassium always enters the cell through that channel. For simple questions involving a potassium conductance, begin by comparing membrane voltage with the equilibrium potential. If a detailed current-voltage curve is supplied, use that curve instead of assuming the conductance is constant at every voltage.

Selective stimulation or inhibition of the nicotinic and muscarinic receptor pathways allows these actions to be distinguished experimentally. A recording from a muscle end plate that shows a rapid inward current in response to acetylcholine, with sensitivity to an appropriate nicotinic antagonist, supports the involvement of the channel receptor. A delayed change in potassium conductance in another target, sensitive to a muscarinic antagonist and dependent on intracellular G-protein function, supports a distinct pathway. Neither observation alone establishes that every receptor in the tissue has been identified. A tissue may contain multiple cellular populations, so the most informative comparison uses defined cells and measures their receptor responses directly.

A ligand that binds a receptor but does not activate it may act as an antagonist by competing with an activating ligand. At a neuromuscular junction, reduced activation of nicotinic receptors can decrease the local end plate potential and prevent it from triggering a muscle action potential. The motor neuron can still conduct impulses and release transmitter. The failure is at the step linking extracellular recognition to the receiving membrane response. This provides an elementary example of how a pharmacological perturbation can interrupt communication at one stage while leaving a preceding stage functional, without requiring an extensive catalogue of therapeutic agents.

6. Glutamate receptors: rapid excitation and coincidence dependence

Glutamate is a major excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate central nervous system. It is also an amino acid involved in metabolism and protein synthesis. These roles do not mean that all cellular glutamate is participating in synaptic communication. The signal depends on controlled release into the extracellular space near receptors and on the ability of receiving cells to recognize it. Intracellular glutamate that participates in metabolism and transient extracellular glutamate acting at a receptor occupy different functional contexts. Understanding the difference prevents the common assumption that a metabolically abundant substance cannot also serve as a specific signal.

The principal ionotropic glutamate receptor groups are AMPA, kainate and NMDA receptors, named after compounds used to distinguish them. Each is a multisubunit channel assembled in a tetrameric organization. Their extracellular ligand-binding regions recognize transmitter, and conformational changes are coupled to membrane-spanning regions that form the ion pathway. The three classes have different kinetics and permeation properties. The major conceptual comparison is between channels that mediate much of the rapid excitatory current at many central synapses and NMDA receptors, whose ionic current depends additionally on membrane voltage and the occupancy of a co-agonist site.

At many glutamatergic synapses, AMPA receptors produce a rapid postsynaptic depolarization. They conduct cations, principally sodium and potassium, and their reversal potential is commonly near zero millivolts. The inward current at a negative resting voltage is therefore expected from the driving force. AMPA receptor calcium permeability varies with subunit composition and RNA editing. The presence of the usual edited GluA2 subunit greatly reduces calcium permeability. It is inaccurate to characterize all AMPA receptors as impermeable to calcium, just as it is inaccurate to classify all ionotropic glutamate receptors by the properties of a single subtype.

The distinction between permeability and current is particularly important for calcium. Calcium can have a very large inward electrochemical driving force at a negative membrane voltage because its free cytosolic concentration is ordinarily far below its extracellular concentration. Even if calcium contributes only a fraction of the total charge carried through a channel, the change in local cytoplasmic calcium can be biologically significant. Calcium acts as an intracellular signal, so an ionotropic receptor can couple extracellular transmitter recognition to biochemical processes without a G protein being the immediate transducer. This does not change the classification of the receptor, which rests on the physical coupling of ligand recognition to the channel.

Kainate receptors are also glutamate-activated cation channels. In selected synapses they contribute to postsynaptic responses and can have other regulatory roles. Their inclusion in a receptor comparison is important because the AMPA versus NMDA distinction is not a complete taxonomy of ionotropic glutamate receptors. However, a student need not memorize every subunit or synaptic distribution to reason about the general mechanisms. If the question specifies a kainate receptor with a reversal voltage close to zero, its current at a negative membrane voltage can be predicted using the same electrical principles as for another cation conductance. Differences in timing or location require additional information.

The NMDA receptor is distinguished by several coupled requirements. Glutamate binds to appropriate extracellular receptor regions, and a co-agonist such as glycine or D-serine must also occupy its site for normal channel activation. At many physiological resting voltages, extracellular magnesium can enter and obstruct the pore. The obstruction is favoured at negative membrane voltages and relieved as the membrane depolarizes. Thus receptor activation by ligands does not guarantee a large ionic current at rest. A membrane that already has a depolarizing conductance, such as one generated by AMPA receptors, may allow a much larger NMDA receptor current in response to the same glutamate signal.

This behaviour makes the NMDA receptor useful for understanding a form of coincidence dependence. A presynaptic event provides the transmitter while postsynaptic depolarization relieves a major limitation on ion permeation. The combination permits stronger conduction than either condition alone. A postsynaptic depolarization without glutamate does not ordinarily activate a glutamate-dependent NMDA receptor by itself; nor does glutamate binding at a very negative voltage necessarily generate a large current when extracellular magnesium is present. The receptor is still classified as ionotropic because ligand recognition and the conductive pore reside within the same complex. Its voltage-dependent block adds another condition to the expression of the response rather than converting it into a conventional voltage-gated channel.

NMDA receptors conduct sodium and potassium and allow calcium entry under many physiological conditions. Calcium entering through these receptors can participate in intracellular signaling, linking the electrical state of the receiving cell to subsequent changes in its responsiveness. The detailed molecular pathways of long-term synaptic plasticity are not necessary to establish this principle. The examination-relevant consequence is that the history of depolarization can alter the effect of a current transmitter pulse. A synapse is not a fixed conversion device if its receptor current depends on the voltage generated by other inputs.

A useful experiment compares glutamate-evoked current at several holding voltages in the presence and absence of extracellular magnesium. In a hypothetical preparation with an isolated NMDA receptor component, the current at −70 mV might be small in the presence of magnesium and substantially larger when magnesium is removed. At a more depolarized holding voltage the difference could be reduced. To attribute this pattern to the NMDA receptor, one must ensure that other receptor currents are appropriately separated and that the ligand and co-agonist are available. The nonlinearity of the current-voltage relationship is informative: it demonstrates why a simple constant-conductance model is inadequate for every glutamate receptor.

Glutamate can also activate metabotropic glutamate receptors, which are G-protein-coupled receptors rather than ion-conducting pores. Some members couple to Gq-associated signaling and others to Gi/o-associated pathways. A metabotropic glutamate response may change excitability without producing the same rapid cation current as an AMPA receptor. The target cell may thus combine a rapid electrical consequence of glutamate release with a slower change in its state. A selective antagonist that suppresses one component of a compound response but leaves another intact is consistent with several receptor mechanisms operating in parallel. It is not necessary to assume that two different transmitters were released merely because two response time courses are observed.

7. GABA and glycine: inhibitory transmission and the chloride problem

Gamma-aminobutyric acid, abbreviated GABA, is an important transmitter of inhibitory communication in many parts of the vertebrate nervous system. Glycine also mediates rapid inhibitory transmission, especially in regions such as the spinal cord and brainstem. Both can activate ionotropic receptors that conduct anions. Their actions illustrate a type of cellular communication that does not simply trigger activity but regulates the conditions under which other signals can trigger it. Inhibition is not an absence of transmission. It is an active, receptor-dependent alteration of the receiving membrane that can reduce the probability of firing.

The GABA type A receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated ion channel. In many mature neurons it is permeable predominantly to chloride, with bicarbonate also contributing to its current. The receptor's effective reversal voltage may therefore differ from the Nernst equilibrium potential calculated for chloride alone. For an elementary model in which chloride dominates, the chloride equilibrium potential provides a useful first approximation. If the problem specifies bicarbonate permeability or supplies a measured receptor reversal potential, the measured or appropriate combined value should take priority over the simplified chloride-only calculation.

The glycine receptor is also a pentameric anion-conducting ligand-gated receptor. The shared anion selectivity explains why both GABA type A receptors and glycine receptors frequently generate inhibitory postsynaptic responses in mature vertebrate neurons. Their ligands and receptor proteins are nevertheless distinct. Glycine has another role as a co-agonist of NMDA receptors, but that role does not turn the ionotropic glycine receptor into a cation channel. The name of a ligand can therefore appear in two different physiological contexts with different electrical consequences. It is the complete receptor name and its coupling mechanism that should guide an examination prediction.

To examine chloride conduction, imagine a neuron resting at −65 mV with an inhibitory receptor reversal potential of −75 mV. Opening the receptor favours a net current that moves the membrane towards −75 mV. The resulting hyperpolarization increases the voltage distance to a typical spike threshold of −45 mV. If an excitatory input arrives during the inhibitory conductance, the inhibitory channels also provide a pathway through which some of the excitatory current is balanced. Thus a hyperpolarizing receptor can oppose excitation through both a change in resting voltage and an increase in membrane conductance.

Now consider a second neuron with a chloride-dominated reversal potential of −65 mV, equal to its initial resting voltage. Opening the receptor produces little or no initial net current in the simplified model. This does not mean the receptor is ineffective. Once an excitatory input tends to depolarize the membrane, the open inhibitory conductance generates a current opposing that deviation from −65 mV. The increase in total membrane conductance reduces input resistance, meaning that the same additional excitatory current produces a smaller voltage displacement. This form of inhibition is commonly called shunting inhibition. It can be substantial even when the inhibitory synapse alone generates almost no visible voltage deflection.

A third neuron has an inhibitory receptor reversal voltage of −55 mV. At a resting voltage of −70 mV the opening of the receptor tends to depolarize the membrane. If the relevant spike threshold is −40 mV, the reversal voltage lies below threshold. The receptor can restrain a strong excitatory input that would otherwise raise the voltage above −55 mV; its conductance also reduces the voltage response to incoming current. The fact that the isolated receptor response is depolarizing does not by itself establish that it is excitatory in the sense of promoting firing. Whether firing becomes more or less likely depends on the timing, location and magnitude of all conductances and on other voltage-dependent processes. A reversal potential between rest and threshold is a situation in which shunting and depolarization must both be considered.

The intracellular concentration of chloride is not identical in all cells or at all developmental stages. Cation-chloride cotransporters can modify the gradient by coupling chloride movement to sodium or potassium gradients. In many developing neurons, chloride equilibrium potentials are relatively depolarized compared with those found in many mature neurons. NKCC1 commonly promotes chloride accumulation by coupling its movement to sodium and potassium, whereas neuronal KCC2 generally extrudes chloride together with potassium using the potassium gradient. Developmental changes in transporter expression can shift the electrical response to GABA. It is too strong to conclude that GABA is necessarily excitatory in every immature neuron or that depolarizing GABA always triggers spikes. The exact effect depends on the reversal potential in relation to threshold and on the full physiological context. This example connects membrane transport, developmental changes in gene expression and synaptic function.

GABA also activates a metabotropic receptor known as GABA type B. Its functional receptor is formed by two different receptor subunits, and it couples to Gi/o proteins. A familiar postsynaptic consequence is activation of G-protein-regulated potassium conductance. This can generate a slower inhibitory response than the rapid anion current mediated by GABA type A receptors. GABA type B receptors also have presynaptic roles, but detailed feedback onto release is reserved for the following chapter. For the present purpose, the contrast demonstrates that the same transmitter can activate both a pentameric ionotropic receptor and a structurally distinct G-protein-coupled receptor.

An experiment comparing responses to GABA under different intracellular chloride conditions can distinguish a receptor effect on ion permeability from a fixed chemical voltage instruction. Under otherwise matched conditions, shifting intracellular chloride changes the equilibrium potential predicted by the Nernst equation. If the GABA-evoked reversal voltage shifts accordingly, the result supports an important chloride contribution to the current. An altered reversal voltage is not equivalent to a reduction in the number of activated receptors. If the conductance is unchanged, current can decrease at one holding voltage and increase or reverse at another. Measuring current at several voltages is therefore more informative than recording a single response amplitude.

The physiological importance of inhibition becomes clear when several excitatory synapses converge upon a cell. Without mechanisms that oppose incoming currents or reduce responsiveness, each excitatory event would tend to push the cell in a similar direction. Inhibitory synapses introduce conditional control. A neuron can respond to excitation at one time while ignoring comparable excitation at another time because the receiving membrane has changed its conductance. Such regulation helps distinguish coincident, relevant inputs from unrelated activity. The examination lesson is that inhibition must be analysed as a positive mechanism with identifiable receptors, ions and timing, not merely as the opposite of transmitter release.

8. Monoamines and the broader logic of metabotropic reception

Monoamine transmitters provide representative examples of chemically related signaling molecules whose receptor responses are diverse. Dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin are familiar monoamines of the vertebrate nervous system. Their classification as monoamines describes features of their chemical structures, not a uniform type of postsynaptic response. Most receptors for these transmitters are G-protein-coupled receptors. The principal exception emphasized at this level is the serotonin 5-HT3 receptor, which is a ligand-gated cation channel. An examination statement that every monoamine receptor is metabotropic is therefore incorrect.

A dopamine receptor of the D1-like family commonly stimulates cyclic AMP formation through Gs or related G proteins, whereas a D2-like receptor commonly couples to Gi/o and can inhibit adenylyl cyclase. These contrasting pathways give dopamine the capacity to regulate cells differently according to receptor expression. Neither a rise nor a fall in cyclic AMP should be equated automatically with a particular voltage direction. Cyclic AMP can influence different protein targets in different cells, and the relationship between a biochemical signal and firing depends on the available effector machinery. The receptor family provides the first causal step, not a complete prediction of all downstream behaviour.

Noradrenaline acts through adrenergic receptors, including alpha and beta families with different G-protein coupling preferences. An alpha2 receptor commonly couples to Gi/o, whereas many beta receptors couple to Gs. An alpha1 receptor commonly couples to Gq/11. The physiological importance of these distinctions can be understood without reproducing the full signaling pathways of Subunit 4.A. A cell receiving noradrenaline through one receptor may reduce an intracellular second messenger, while another cell expressing a different receptor may increase it. The transmitter identity is shared, but the molecular route by which its presence is interpreted is not.

Serotonin has multiple receptor families, most metabotropic. The 5-HT3 receptor belongs to the same broad pentameric ion channel architecture as nicotinic, GABA type A and glycine receptors, but it conducts cations rather than being an inhibitory anion channel. A rapid serotonin-induced current that survives disruption of intracellular G-protein signaling may consequently reflect direct channel gating. This example is useful when time-course data or receptor topology are provided in a question and the expected answer cannot be obtained from transmitter chemistry alone.

The difference between fast ionotropic reception and slower modulation should not be interpreted as a strict division between transmitters that carry information and transmitters that only regulate it. A rapid synaptic channel can change the probability that later signals are detected, while a metabotropic response can directly influence ongoing electrical activity. Both can participate in synaptic communication. The design of the receptor determines how a message is translated into a cellular variable. Channel opening immediately connects the membrane to ionic gradients, whereas activation of a G protein creates a molecular signal that may reach more than one effector.

In a representative metabotropic sequence, transmitter binds the extracellular receptor, receptor conformation changes, the receptor promotes nucleotide exchange on the associated heterotrimeric G protein, and the activated G-protein components interact with their targets. These targets may include a membrane channel or an enzyme that generates or removes a second messenger. A second messenger can then change protein activity and indirectly alter conductance. Each intermediate must be physically available and functionally coupled. If a receptor remains capable of binding transmitter but loses its ability to activate the G protein, the downstream response may be absent despite normal receptor abundance and ligand recognition.

Not every metabotropic effect requires a freely diffusible second messenger. A G-protein beta-gamma complex can directly regulate certain membrane channels, including the potassium conductances associated with some inhibitory responses. The term metabotropic reflects the indirect nature of the coupling between transmitter-binding receptor and ion pathway rather than a universal requirement for a long sequence of enzyme reactions. In an experiment where an isolated response persists despite blocking a particular second-messenger pathway, one should not immediately conclude that the receptor is ionotropic. An alternative G-protein-dependent route may still operate.

The timing of metabotropic responses depends on both activation and inactivation processes. A receptor can lose its bound transmitter before all activated G proteins or downstream molecules have returned to their initial states. The biochemical consequence may therefore outlast the extracellular signal. The receiving cell can retain a temporary change in sensitivity to other signals, such as altered potassium conductance or altered responsiveness of voltage-sensitive channels. Such persistence is not necessarily pathological and does not prove that transmitter remains within the synaptic cleft. In the next node, the separate processes of transmitter clearance and regulation of receptor responsiveness will be considered together.

An important experimental prediction follows from this distinction. Suppose a neuron responds to acetylcholine with an early current and a later change in conductance. An antagonist that selectively blocks the nicotinic receptor removes the early current, while interruption of the relevant G-protein pathway removes the late component. These observations are consistent with separate receptor mechanisms. A definitive interpretation also requires control experiments demonstrating that each manipulation does not nonspecifically damage the membrane or suppress the other pathway. The same experimental logic can be used for glutamate or GABA, both of which have important ionotropic and metabotropic receptor groups.

A useful comparison of neurotransmitters should consequently be organized around a small number of representative receptor families rather than a long list of chemical names. The question to ask is whether the receptor contains a pore, which ions the pore conducts if present, or which intracellular transducer the receptor activates if it does not contain a pore. The resulting conductance or biochemical pathway must then be interpreted in the context of the receiving cell. This approach makes apparently diverse examples such as a nicotinic muscle receptor, a GABA type A neuronal receptor, a muscarinic pacemaker receptor and a dopamine receptor parts of the same conceptual framework.

9. Excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials

The distinction between a postsynaptic current and a postsynaptic potential is fundamental. A current measures the rate at which charge crosses the membrane, whereas a potential measures the voltage difference across that membrane. The current is the electrical effect of the activated conductance at a given voltage. The potential is the consequence of this current interacting with membrane capacitance and all other currents present. Two neurons can exhibit similar transmitter-activated currents but different potential displacements if their input resistances or capacitances differ. An experimental trace labelled EPSC must therefore not be interpreted as though it were automatically an EPSP.

An excitatory postsynaptic potential, or EPSP, ordinarily denotes a graded voltage displacement associated with increased likelihood of spike initiation. Common excitatory receptor channels conduct cations and have reversal potentials positive to the spike threshold. Their opening at rest tends to depolarize the membrane towards those reversal voltages. An excitatory receptor need not drive the entire cell all the way to its own reversal potential. The voltage is determined by competition among the activated receptor conductance, the resting conductances and any other active inputs. Many excitatory synapses contribute only a small fraction of the voltage displacement needed to initiate a spike.

An inhibitory postsynaptic potential, or IPSP, is often illustrated as a hyperpolarization caused by opening chloride or potassium channels. This is a useful representative example, but a complete definition must consider the functional effect on spike initiation. A conductance with a reversal potential near rest may have a pronounced inhibitory influence without producing a readily visible hyperpolarization. In some conditions an inhibitory receptor produces a depolarizing voltage displacement yet constrains the voltage below firing threshold during excitation. Because inhibition can operate through conductance changes, identifying excitation or inhibition from the direction of a solitary potential trace can be unreliable.

Suppose a neuron rests at −70 mV and fires when its trigger region reaches approximately −50 mV. An excitatory receptor with a reversal potential of 0 mV tends to move the voltage in the direction needed for firing. An inhibitory receptor with reversal at −80 mV tends to move it away from threshold. A third conductance with reversal at −70 mV initially produces no current at rest, but limits changes away from −70 mV when other receptors are activated. These are not three unrelated rules. They follow directly from the signed driving force and the change in total membrane conductance. The functional consequence depends on the receptor current at the voltages actually reached during communication.

The word threshold is itself a simplification. In an intact excitable cell, spike initiation depends on voltage-gated channels whose availability can vary with recent electrical history. A threshold value supplied in an examination question is generally a useful model of the voltage at which regenerative inward current becomes sufficient to trigger a spike. The value is not an immutable molecular constant applicable to every cell and time. A sustained depolarization, for example, can modify availability of voltage-gated sodium channels. The chapter will use fixed threshold values when a problem explicitly assumes them, without extending that approximation into a universal physiological law.

Another useful distinction is between the spatial extent of a postsynaptic potential and the extent of the receptor current. Ionotropic receptors may be concentrated within a small region of dendritic membrane. Their activation changes local voltage, but that voltage change influences adjoining regions through passive flow of current within the cell and across its membrane. Some current is lost through resting conductances along the way. The potential recorded at the soma may thus be smaller than the local potential near the activated receptors. A large local response can be functionally modest at the spike initiation region if the electrical connection between these regions is weak.

A synaptic potential usually returns towards the baseline after the activating conductance declines, assuming no action potential or other regenerative response is triggered. Resting conductances permit charge to redistribute and the membrane capacitance to return towards its original voltage. The decay of the potential does not require an additional transmitter to instruct the membrane to repolarize. The electrical properties already present in the membrane contribute to the recovery. Conversely, a sustained current may maintain a displaced voltage as long as the current persists. The time course reflects both the receptor's molecular kinetics and the electrical properties of the cell.

A useful examination distinction is therefore between the sign of a current and the name of a potential. Under the conventional outward-current definition, a negative current can arise from positive charge entering or negative charge leaving. Both processes tend to depolarize a passive membrane. A positive current can arise from positive charge leaving or negative charge entering and tends to hyperpolarize the membrane when other conditions are unchanged. However, the effect of increasing conductance during a competing input depends on the current it produces over the full voltage range. The ion carrying the current, its reversal voltage and the cell's initial and target states must all be considered.

10. A quantitative conductance model for synaptic integration

A simple electrical model can unite the preceding explanations. Represent the resting membrane by a leak conductance gL with reversal voltage EL. Represent an excitatory synapse by gE with reversal voltage EE, and an inhibitory synapse by gI with reversal voltage EI. For the moment suppose that the relevant part of the cell behaves like one electrically uniform compartment and that the conductances are approximately linear. The outward current through each pathway is the product of its conductance and its own driving force. The sum of these currents determines whether the membrane capacitor charges or discharges. This model is deliberately simpler than a real neuron but is sufficient to explain many predictions involving synaptic input.

With outward current defined as positive, charge conservation gives C dV/dt = −gL(V − EL) − gE(V − EE) − gI(V − EI), in the absence of externally injected current. The left side is the rate at which charge associated with the membrane voltage changes. The negative signs on the right express the convention that positive outward current removes positive charge from the inside and lowers the membrane voltage. The conductances may change with time during synaptic activity. If they are held constant for a sufficient duration, the voltage approaches a steady value at which the net current is zero.

Setting dV/dt to zero gives Vsteady = (gL EL + gE EE + gI EI)/(gL + gE + gI). The steady voltage is a conductance-weighted average of the reversal voltages, not an arithmetic sum of postsynaptic potentials. Each conductance determines how strongly its reversal voltage influences the result. If all conductances are nonnegative, this passive steady voltage must lie within the range spanned by the contributing reversal voltages. This is a useful check on a calculation. If an answer lies beyond all of the supplied reversal voltages, either the arithmetic is wrong or the assumed model has omitted a current source or active process.

Consider a hypothetical neuron with resting conductance 10 nS and leak reversal voltage −70 mV. In the absence of other currents, its steady voltage is −70 mV. An excitatory conductance of 5 nS with reversal voltage 0 mV produces a predicted steady voltage of (10 × −70 + 5 × 0)/15 = −46.67 mV. If the neuron is represented by a fixed spike threshold of −50 mV, this steady value is on the firing side of threshold. It does not guarantee that a short-lived excitatory input will trigger a spike, because the membrane must have enough time to approach the threshold before the conductance disappears. Duration and capacitance matter in addition to the steady calculation.

Now add an inhibitory conductance of 10 nS with reversal voltage −70 mV while keeping the excitation unchanged. The predicted steady voltage becomes (−700 + 0 − 700)/25 = −56 mV. Without excitation the inhibitory conductance would not shift the resting voltage in this model because its reversal coincides with the leak reversal. Yet during excitation it shifts the predicted steady voltage from −46.67 to −56 mV. It has converted a potentially suprathreshold input into a subthreshold one. The same inhibitory conductance can therefore have a strong physiological effect without generating an isolated hyperpolarizing potential.

The model also explains the phenomenon of reduced input resistance. At steady state, an additional small current applied to a passive cell produces a voltage displacement equal to the current divided by total conductance. Input resistance is the inverse of total conductance for this uniform linear model. With 10 nS resting conductance, resistance is 100 MΩ. Opening another 10 nS pathway reduces the resistance to 50 MΩ. An imposed inward current of 100 pA would produce a 10 mV voltage change before the additional conductance opened and only 5 mV afterwards, if other conductances and their reversal potentials remained constant. The reduction in voltage response illustrates electrical shunting directly.

The same equation describes a voltage that changes with time. For constant conductances, write G = gL + gE + gI and Eweighted for their conductance-weighted reversal. Then C dV/dt = −G(V − Eweighted). Dividing by C gives a differential equation whose solution is V(t) = Eweighted + [V(0) − Eweighted] exp(−t/τ), where τ = C/G. The time constant is the interval during which the remaining distance from the new steady value falls to about 36.8 percent of its initial value. Equivalently, after one time constant the voltage has completed approximately 63.2 percent of the change towards the new steady state.

For a membrane capacitance of 100 pF and total conductance of 10 nS, the time constant is 10 ms. If a shunting conductance increases the total to 20 nS, the time constant becomes 5 ms. This illustrates another way in which inhibition can influence integration: it can shorten the period during which a voltage displacement persists. A smaller voltage deflection is not the only consequence of higher conductance. The receiving membrane also loses its temporary electrical memory more quickly in the passive approximation. The synaptic current's own decay and the electrical time constant together determine the duration of the actual postsynaptic potential.

Return to the excitatory example with a total conductance of 15 nS and a capacitance of 100 pF. The time constant during the excitatory conductance is approximately 6.67 ms. Starting from −70 mV, a conductance step lasting 2 ms gives a voltage near −46.67 + (−23.33) exp(−0.30), or about −63.95 mV. The steady voltage would have been above threshold, but the voltage reached after two milliseconds is not. This distinction between predicted asymptote and finite-time response is crucial whenever a problem provides synaptic duration. It also explains why simply comparing steady voltage with threshold can overestimate the effectiveness of a very brief input.

The simplified conductance model makes several assumptions that must be kept visible. It treats the cell as electrically uniform, does not allow voltage-gated channels to change during the subthreshold calculation, and assumes that the stated conductances behave linearly over the relevant voltage range. A true dendrite can possess spatially distributed conductances, and a receptor such as NMDA can show a strongly nonlinear current-voltage relationship. The model also holds the ionic reversal voltages fixed, which is reasonable for many brief signals but not necessarily for sustained large ion fluxes. These limitations do not undermine the model's educational value; they identify which additional information is needed when its predictions fail.

11. Temporal and spatial summation

Many neurons receive individual synaptic inputs that are too small to produce a spike independently. Such an arrangement allows the cell to respond selectively when several signals occur in a suitable pattern. The two conventional categories of summation are temporal and spatial. Temporal summation concerns overlap among responses generated at different times, often by repeated activation of the same synaptic input. Spatial summation concerns overlap among signals from different sites on the receiving cell. The distinction is about the arrangement of events, not about a different class of neurotransmitter or receptor. Both processes arise because receptor-mediated currents modify a continuous electrical system with finite recovery times.

To understand temporal summation, begin with a single brief excitatory input that produces a local depolarization. Once the input ends, the membrane voltage gradually returns towards its starting value. If a second input occurs only after complete recovery, the second response begins from nearly the original baseline. If it occurs earlier, a portion of the first voltage displacement remains. The second response begins from that altered state, and the total displacement can be larger than the displacement caused by either input alone. The opportunity for summation is therefore set by the interval between events relative to the duration of the postsynaptic potential.

A simple small-signal model assumes that each brief event instantaneously adds a voltage increment A and that its contribution subsequently decays exponentially with time constant τ. If a second identical event occurs after interval Δt, the remaining contribution from the first is A exp(−Δt/τ). Immediately after the second event, the sum in the linear approximation is A[1 + exp(−Δt/τ)]. This model ignores the time required for receptor current to reach its peak and assumes that the second input has the same strength as the first. It illustrates a principle of overlap rather than reproducing the complete waveform of a real synapse.

For example, let the voltage increment produced by an isolated small input be 4 mV and let τ be 20 ms. With an interval of 10 ms, the residual contribution is 4 exp(−0.5), approximately 2.43 mV. Immediately after the second input, the predicted displacement is 6.43 mV. If the interval were 40 ms, the remaining contribution would be approximately 0.54 mV and the total only 4.54 mV. The shorter interval gives greater overlap without requiring increased transmitter release at either individual event. This is distinct from presynaptic facilitation, in which the amount of transmitter released in response to a later stimulus may change.

Repeated events can be analysed by adding the residual contributions from earlier events, provided the assumptions of the linear model remain reasonable. After many equally spaced inputs the idealized displacement immediately after an event approaches A/(1 − exp(−Δt/τ)). As the interval decreases, the expression grows. This mathematical growth should not be interpreted as an unlimited voltage rise in a real neuron. Receptor driving forces decline with depolarization, conductances can saturate, membrane nonlinearities appear and spikes may be generated. The linear expression is useful only while the voltage displacements remain small enough that these additional processes can be neglected.

Spatial summation requires considering where currents enter and leave the cell. A dendrite is not a perfect wire, and current generated by a synapse can escape through membrane conductances before reaching the soma or axon initial segment. The effective contribution of a distal synapse to spike initiation may therefore be smaller than that of an equally strong proximal synapse. Distance alone is not the complete explanation because dendritic diameter, branching, membrane conductances and active channel distribution can all influence transmission of a voltage displacement. A simple diagram showing two equal local EPSPs does not imply that their effects at the trigger region must be equal.

In a passive linear approximation, suppose an excitatory synapse generates a 6 mV local voltage response and a fraction of 0.5 reaches the trigger region, whereas another synapse generates a 4 mV local response with a fraction of 0.75 reaching the same region. Each contributes 3 mV at the trigger region. If they arrive simultaneously and their interactions are weak, their combined contribution is approximately 6 mV. The fractions here are hypothetical parameters representing attenuation, not universal constants of dendrites. If one input arrives after the other has substantially decayed, their total contribution at any moment will be smaller.

It is tempting to treat excitatory and inhibitory inputs as numbers that can simply be added with opposite signs. For sufficiently small voltage deflections this approximation can sometimes be useful, but the conductance mechanism explains why it fails more generally. Activating one synapse can change the voltage that provides the driving force for another. Inhibition can also reduce the effective electrical resistance through which excitatory current acts. As a result, the voltage response to two simultaneous conductances can differ from the sum of their separately recorded voltage responses. In particular, the fact that an inhibitory input produces a zero-millivolt response when activated alone does not imply that adding it to excitation leaves excitation unchanged.

The location of inhibition can matter as much as its strength. An inhibitory conductance positioned along the pathway through which an excitatory current spreads can draw current away before the resulting voltage displacement reaches a trigger region. The exact magnitude depends on the electrical geometry of the cell. This is a conceptual reason why neurons can use inhibitory contacts to selectively control particular inputs. A single uniform-compartment model can illustrate shunting, but it cannot predict the different effects of placing the same conductance at distinct sites on a dendritic tree. A spatial problem must therefore supply information about location or attenuation, or be answered qualitatively with the limitation stated.

A further distinction separates synaptic integration from the generation of a propagated action potential. Graded synaptic potentials reflect the net electrical response of the receiving membrane to input. When the relevant trigger region reaches conditions sufficient to activate regenerative voltage-gated currents, the cell can initiate an action potential. That event is not simply the linear extension of an EPSP. It is a new dynamical process with different channel mechanisms. A neuron may receive many subthreshold inputs without firing, and the absence of a spike does not mean that receptor activation or synaptic transmission failed.

Temporal and spatial summation can operate together. Imagine two excitatory pathways converging on one neuron, each producing a small graded response. If the first pathway fires twice in close succession, its responses overlap temporally. If the second pathway becomes active at the same time, its contribution can overlap spatially with the first pathway's contribution. An inhibitory input arriving during that combined depolarization can reduce the peak through an additional conductance. The receiving cell's output thus reflects a pattern of events in time and space. It cannot generally be inferred from the total number of presynaptic impulses without knowing the properties of the synapses and the state of the receiving membrane.

12. The motor end plate as a reliable receiving membrane

The vertebrate skeletal neuromuscular junction provides a contrast to the many central synapses that contribute small increments of information to a neuronal decision. A motor neuron normally communicates with a muscle fibre in a way that produces a muscle action potential with a high probability. The nerve terminal releases acetylcholine, and the receiving membrane has a specialized region called the motor end plate. The membrane folds in this region help organize the components required for transmission and excitation. Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors are concentrated near the crests of the junctional folds, whereas voltage-gated sodium channels are especially concentrated in deeper portions of the folds. This spatial organization supports the conversion of a local receptor potential into a regenerative electrical event.

The sequence on the receiving side begins when acetylcholine reaches the extracellular receptor sites. Occupancy of the relevant binding interfaces increases the probability of pore opening. Sodium and potassium conduct through the receptors, and under ordinary resting conditions the net current is inward. The inward current charges the muscle membrane and produces a local depolarization, the end plate potential. Current spreading from this region depolarizes nearby membrane containing voltage-sensitive sodium channels. When the voltage is sufficient to recruit regenerative inward sodium current, a muscle action potential begins and propagates along the excitable membrane. The later processes linking that impulse to contraction are important in muscle physiology but are not required to explain the receptor event itself.

The end plate potential is not a muscle action potential. The former is graded and generated principally by acetylcholine receptor conductance; the latter is regenerative and generated by voltage-gated channels. This distinction can be tested by suppressing the muscle's regenerative sodium current while leaving ligand-gated receptor currents functional. Under those conditions an end plate response can still be detected even though a propagated muscle action potential does not occur. Conversely, direct electrical stimulation of the muscle away from a defective synapse can sometimes elicit an action potential when nerve stimulation fails. The two tests help distinguish impairment of synaptic reception from impairment of the receiving membrane's capacity for excitation.

The reliability of the normal neuromuscular junction depends in part on a safety margin. Under usual conditions, the evoked end plate depolarization is sufficient to reach the threshold required to initiate a muscle action potential. The receptor-dependent signal may thus be reduced somewhat without immediately producing transmission failure. If the reduction becomes sufficiently severe, the end plate potential may no longer cross threshold. A change in the magnitude of a graded junctional response can therefore have a nonlinear consequence for the final output: muscle action potentials may remain reliable over a range of junctional response amplitudes and then begin to fail as the safety margin is lost.

Consider an illustrative muscle fibre with a resting voltage of −90 mV and an assumed threshold of −55 mV. A local end plate response that would depolarize the trigger region by 50 mV in the absence of a regenerative spike exceeds the required 35 mV displacement. If receptor blockade reduces the predicted displacement to 25 mV, the resulting value of −65 mV falls below the assumed threshold. These are simplified numbers used to illustrate the safety margin rather than a measurement from a particular species. In an actual preparation, once threshold is reached, voltage-gated currents contribute to the observed trace, so the isolated graded potential should not be estimated by subtracting the resting voltage from the peak of a propagated spike.

Acetylcholinesterase in the junctional extracellular environment hydrolyses acetylcholine and contributes to the termination of its action. The distinction between receptor activation and transmitter removal matters when examining the duration of the end plate response. A receptor antagonist can reduce receptor occupancy by acetylcholine without directly accelerating hydrolysis. An intervention that inhibits acetylcholinesterase can prolong transmitter availability without directly increasing the number of receptors. If the intervention also affects receptor state or membrane excitability, the resulting response may be more complex. The mechanisms governing transmitter clearance and repeated transmission are considered in the following chapter, so the present example is limited to identifying which stage of the receiving process is affected.

A comparison between miniature end plate potentials and evoked end plate potentials is useful for reasoning about receptor mutations. If functional receptor density falls while the release of individual vesicles remains similar, the average electrical consequence of one released packet can decrease. A sufficiently large reduction can also reduce the evoked potential below the safety margin. But the size of a miniature potential can change for other reasons, including changes in vesicle transmitter content, driving force or muscle input resistance. A receptor-specific interpretation should be supported by additional evidence such as a direct transmitter response under controlled voltage or a measurement of functional receptors at the membrane.

The evolutionary comparison between different animal neuromuscular junctions provides another warning against overgeneralization. Acetylcholine is the characteristic transmitter at vertebrate skeletal motor end plates, but the excitatory neuromuscular junction in the fruit fly uses glutamate. Both arrangements connect a motor signal to an effector membrane through chemical release and ionotropic receptors, yet the ligand and receptor families differ. This illustrates how the functional principle of fast transmitter-gated excitation can be conserved without the precise receptor type being identical in every animal. It is incorrect to infer the identity of a transmitter solely from the fact that the target is muscle.

The vertebrate motor end plate therefore brings together concepts introduced throughout the chapter: receptor localization, ligand recognition, mixed cation conduction, graded voltage displacement, threshold and reliable cellular output. It is a particularly useful comparison for examinations because the failure of the signal can be localized to a specific stage of a short causal chain. A student who understands the distinction between the receptor current, the end plate potential and the muscle action potential can analyse the consequences of a receptor blocker or receptor mutation without memorizing a list of disconnected clinical effects.

13. Experimental discrimination of receptors, conductances and altered responses

An experiment designed to identify a postsynaptic mechanism begins with an explicit question. For instance, does an unknown receptor produce current by opening its own pore, or does it first activate an intracellular signaling pathway? A useful starting observation is the time course of the response to a brief transmitter pulse. A rapid current suggests direct gating, especially when it begins soon after ligand exposure. A delayed current suggests the possibility of additional molecular steps. But timing alone does not establish receptor structure: ligand delivery, receptor kinetics, voltage-dependent block and the recording method can all affect the measured response. A second line of evidence is needed to test the proposed coupling mechanism.

One approach compares the response before and after interference with the relevant intracellular G-protein pathway. If the rapidly activated current remains functional but a delayed conductance change is lost, separate coupling routes are plausible. If both components disappear, several interpretations remain possible, including a common dependency on the targeted pathway or a nonspecific disturbance of cell physiology. A suitable control verifies that the basic excitability of the cell and an unrelated direct channel response are preserved. A selective receptor antagonist can provide further evidence, but its interpretation depends on selectivity under the conditions used. The objective is to link the response to a mechanism, not merely to record that it changes after treatment.

Current-voltage relationships offer a different kind of evidence. Imagine an unknown transmitter-sensitive current measured at holding potentials of −80, −40, 0 and +40 mV. Suppose the isolated currents are −160, −80, 0 and +80 pA respectively. This hypothetical dataset is consistent with a linear conductance of 2 nS and reversal potential of 0 mV. Its sign changes across zero. A mixed cation conductance is a reasonable interpretation under ordinary gradients, but the reversal value alone does not identify the receptor protein or even uniquely identify the ions that carry the current. A change in extracellular sodium concentration or another controlled ion substitution could test whether the reversal voltage shifts in the expected direction.

Now consider a second current with measured values of −30 pA at −80 mV, +30 pA at −60 mV and +90 pA at −40 mV. The slope is 60 pA divided by 20 mV, or 3 nS. The zero-current voltage inferred by linear interpolation is −70 mV. If this current is mainly carried by chloride, raising intracellular chloride while maintaining extracellular chloride would be expected to move the reversal voltage in the positive direction. If the manipulation produces a reversal shift without an important slope change, altered ionic driving force is a stronger explanation than loss or gain of functional channels. A measured slope is only interpretable as a constant conductance when the relevant current-voltage relationship is approximately linear.

The isolation of a receptor current is a critical part of both examples. Whole-cell current includes contributions from leak pathways, voltage-gated conductances and potentially several receptor types. Comparing the current after ligand application with a matched baseline can help estimate the ligand-dependent component. If the ligand also changes existing conductances indirectly, this difference still represents its net effect but may not correspond to a single channel type. The experimenter must know which currents are included in the difference. Poor control of voltage across a spatially extended cell can further complicate interpretation because different membrane regions may not experience the same holding voltage.

Genetic perturbations provide another way to distinguish receptor properties. Consider a mutation that disrupts an extracellular ligand-binding interface. If the receptor still reaches the membrane and its pore remains structurally intact, a higher transmitter concentration may be needed to produce a given response, depending on the nature of the mutation. A mutation in the permeation pathway can instead alter unitary conductance or selectivity. A change in an intracellular anchoring site may reduce the concentration of receptors at the synapse while preserving their ability to open elsewhere. These three changes can all decrease an evoked postsynaptic response, but they make different predictions for ligand concentration-response curves, reversal voltages and surface localization.

The distinction between a receptor's affinity and its functional response is especially important in concentration-response experiments. An equilibrium binding measurement reports occupancy or binding of ligand under the chosen conditions. A functional experiment reports an electrical or biochemical consequence of that binding. The concentration producing half of the maximal functional response need not equal the binding dissociation constant, because the response depends on the coupling between occupation, channel gating, downstream amplification and cellular properties. A shift in the concentration producing half-maximal current is therefore not definitive evidence of altered affinity. It may reflect changed efficacy or coupling. If a question supplies a binding measurement and a functional curve separately, each must be interpreted on its own terms.

Rescue experiments can strengthen a genetic inference. If a receptor-deficient cell loses a transmitter-sensitive response and reintroduction of a normal receptor restores that response, the result supports a role for the receptor in the tested pathway. Failure of a mutant receptor to restore the response does not alone prove that the mutation affects its pore. The mutant may be poorly expressed, misfolded, retained within the cell or unable to associate with necessary partners. A surface-expression measurement and a test of binding or channel activity can help distinguish these possibilities. Rescue provides evidence for causal involvement, but additional measurements are required to specify the molecular defect.

Measurements of miniature synaptic currents can also help localize an altered evoked response. A reduction in miniature event amplitude with unchanged frequency may suggest reduced postsynaptic sensitivity, but it can also be explained by less transmitter per vesicle or altered ionic gradients. A reduction in frequency with stable amplitude may suggest altered spontaneous release, but small events may fall below detection threshold after a receptor change. The interpretation of miniature frequency must therefore consider detection sensitivity and the distribution of event amplitudes. Comparing miniature currents at the same holding voltage is useful because changes in driving force due only to differences in resting voltage are then reduced, although changes in reversal potential can still matter.

For a functional assay of inhibition, it is often informative to measure how a fixed excitatory stimulus behaves before and during activation of a candidate inhibitory receptor. The central question is not only whether the receptor itself produces hyperpolarization. If the input resistance decreases and the same excitatory current produces a smaller voltage deflection, there is evidence for shunting. If the receptor also moves the resting voltage, the two effects must be separated. An experimental design that records both baseline voltage and the response to a small test current can identify a change in conductance that would otherwise be hidden in the voltage trace.

The examples illustrate a general examination strategy. Identify the independent variable, the measured variable and the candidate causal step. Determine which aspects of the preparation are held constant, including voltage, ion gradients and ligand exposure. Look for a control that excludes an alternative explanation. Finally, draw a conclusion whose strength matches the observations. A reduction in a current is a measurement; a reduction in receptor abundance is a hypothesis unless supported by evidence that distinguishes it from a change in driving force, gating or conductance. This discipline applies broadly to cellular signaling experiments and prevents treating an observed physiological effect as proof of a particular molecular mechanism.

14. Biological integration, perturbation and the limits of the elementary models

The postsynaptic receptor is an intersection of several biological systems that have already been studied separately. Protein structure explains why a receptor recognizes selected ligands and how conformational changes can influence other parts of the protein. Membrane organization explains why a channel must provide a pathway across an otherwise poorly ion-permeable bilayer. Transport biology explains how ion gradients are established. Chemical kinetics explains why ligand binding and receptor activation have finite rates. Cellular signaling explains how a surface receptor can activate an intracellular transducer. The distinctive contribution of this chapter is to connect these mechanisms to the response of a receiving cell during chemical communication.

A cell can alter its sensitivity to a transmitter without changing the transmitter-producing cell. Changes in expression of receptor subunits may modify how many receptors reach the surface, the kinetics of channel openings or the permeability to particular ions. Changes in transporter expression may modify the reversal potential of a receptor current while leaving the receptor protein unchanged. Changes in leak conductance may modify the size of the voltage response to an unchanged receptor current. A perturbation of the receiving membrane can thus have important consequences even when the presynaptic action potential and transmitter release remain normal.

The regulation of receptor function creates several levels of biological control. A ligand can change channel gating through its primary binding site. An allosteric modulator binds elsewhere and changes the receptor's response to the ligand. For instance, certain GABA type A receptor modulators can enhance the effect of GABA without simply acting as additional molecules of the physiological transmitter. The precise action depends on the receptor composition and the modulator. This is an example of structure-dependent control rather than a requirement to study therapeutic pharmacology. A positive allosteric modulation of an inhibitory conductance can reduce neuronal responsiveness even though the amount of GABA released does not increase.

Receptor-mediated responses are also affected by the state of the cell at the time of stimulation. A receptor may be activated normally but conduct little current because the voltage is close to its reversal potential. A neuron may receive a substantial excitatory current but produce a small voltage response because its input resistance is low. A metabotropic signal may change its electrical effect if a necessary downstream effector is absent. These situations have different causal explanations. When an examination question describes the same transmitter being applied to cells with different resting states, it is necessary to ask which of these variables differs before assuming that receptor subtype expression is the only explanation.

An important boundary is the distinction between cellular excitation and the behaviour of a network. Exciting an inhibitory interneuron can increase inhibitory communication to another target. Inhibiting an inhibitory neuron may reduce its inhibition of a downstream cell, a phenomenon termed disinhibition. The direct receptor effect in the first receiving cell is not necessarily the same as the net effect on the circuit. A question that supplies a chain of connected cells can be solved one connection at a time: determine the direct effect at the first target, then consider how that changes the transmitter output to the next target. Detailed circuit anatomy is unnecessary for understanding this logical relationship.

The availability of ion gradients connects synaptic response to physiological homeostasis. If intracellular chloride rises, an anion receptor's reversal voltage can shift towards more positive values. If sodium and potassium gradients deteriorate, the driving forces for cation conduction change. A receptor agonist may remain effective at binding its target even when these altered conditions prevent a normal electrical response. Long-term maintenance of the receiving cell requires energy-dependent processes and intact membrane organization. Brief synaptic signaling relies on these established conditions; it does not independently create them. This provides a molecular explanation for why changes in cellular physiology can alter communication without requiring changes in neurotransmitter chemistry.

The simplified models used in examination reasoning are best understood as controlled approximations. Nernst calculations refer to equilibrium of an individual ion. Linear current equations assume a conductance that is approximately independent of voltage over the specified range. A conductance-weighted voltage is a passive steady-state prediction. Exponential voltage summation assumes a small signal in a simple electrical compartment. None of these expressions can be applied automatically to a regenerative action potential or an arbitrarily complex receptor current. The value of a model is that it isolates a relationship whose consequences can be examined. Its assumptions indicate when a more detailed explanation is needed.

The next chapter will examine how the availability of transmitter and the responsiveness of synaptic machinery are controlled over time. The distinction established here between a transmitter's presence and a receptor's action will be essential. A prolonged signal can arise from slower removal of extracellular ligand, altered receptor kinetics, or persistent downstream signaling. A smaller response can reflect reduced release or a reduced ability of the receiving cell to respond. Only by separating these possibilities can regulation of chemical neurotransmission be understood as the coordinated control of several processes rather than a single change in transmitter concentration.

1. What distinguishes an ionotropic neurotransmitter receptor from a metabotropic receptor?

  1. It requires a separate G protein to open its own pore.
  2. It always conducts only sodium.
  3. Its ligand recognition and conducting pore belong to the same molecular complex.
  4. Its ligand must enter the cytoplasm.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Its ligand recognition and conducting pore belong to the same molecular complex.

Ionotropic classification depends on direct structural coupling of ligand recognition to a pore. Neither sodium selectivity nor ligand entry is required.

2. Which combination correctly describes the adult vertebrate muscle nicotinic receptor?

  1. Two α1, one β1, one δ and one γ subunit
  2. Four identical glutamate receptor subunits
  3. Two α1, one β1, one δ and one ε subunit
  4. A pair of seven-helix GPCRs
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Two α1, one β1, one δ and one ε subunit

The ε-containing pentamer is the typical mature muscle receptor. The γ-containing form is characteristic of development; glutamate channels and GPCRs have different architecture.

3. Which receptor is a representative anion-conducting ionotropic receptor?

  1. GABA-A
  2. GABA-B
  3. Muscarinic M2
  4. Dopamine D1
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. GABA-A

GABA-A forms an anion-selective pore. GABA-B, M2 and D1 are G-protein-coupled receptors rather than anion channels.

4. Which serotonin receptor is the classic ionotropic exception to predominantly metabotropic serotonin receptors?

  1. 5-HT3
  2. 5-HT1A
  3. 5-HT2A
  4. 5-HT7
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 5-HT3

5-HT3 is a pentameric cation channel. The other listed receptors signal through G proteins.

5. Which condition is especially important for a substantial NMDA receptor current under physiological magnesium?

  1. Agonist and co-agonist binding together with relief of voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block
  2. Only depolarization, with no ligand
  3. Only a G-protein-mediated rise in cAMP
  4. Chloride entry through a pentamer
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Agonist and co-agonist binding together with relief of voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block

The NMDA channel requires ligand/co-agonist occupancy and is strongly constrained by Mg²⁺ block at negative voltages. Voltage alone does not provide glutamate.

6. What does the reversal potential of a receptor current represent?

  1. Voltage at which all its ligands detach
  2. Voltage at which every individual ionic flux ceases
  3. Voltage at which its net electrical current is zero
  4. The universal action-potential threshold
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Voltage at which its net electrical current is zero

Net charge flow balances at reversal. Several ionic fluxes can remain nonzero even though total current is zero.

7. Which receptor family usually provides much of the rapid glutamatergic current at central excitatory synapses?

  1. GABA-B receptors
  2. AMPA receptors
  3. Muscarinic receptors
  4. D2-like receptors
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. AMPA receptors

AMPA receptors are glutamate-gated cation channels. The other examples have different physiological ligands and indirect coupling.

8. What is meant by receptor desensitization?

  1. Enzymatic destruction of all transmitter
  2. A mandatory decrease in presynaptic vesicle number
  3. Conversion of a channel into a pump
  4. Reduced response while agonist may still be bound or present
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Reduced response while agonist may still be bound or present

Desensitization concerns receptor state and reduced gating/coupling, not necessarily clearance or transmitter release.

9. A muscle end plate potential is generated primarily by which event?

  1. Activation of ligand-gated nicotinic conductance
  2. Opening of voltage-gated sodium channels along the entire fibre
  3. Direct transfer of cytoplasm from nerve to muscle
  4. Hydrolysis of ATP by each passing sodium ion
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Activation of ligand-gated nicotinic conductance

The graded junctional potential precedes the regenerative muscle spike; the synaptic cleft separates cytoplasm and the receptor pore is passive.

10. Which statement describes temporal summation?

  1. Two different ions move through one receptor simultaneously.
  2. Two receptors form a heterodimer.
  3. An action potential travels along an axon without decrement.
  4. Responses from successive events overlap before prior voltage changes decay.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Responses from successive events overlap before prior voltage changes decay.

Temporal overlap depends on event interval and recovery. Spatial convergence, receptor assembly and propagation are separate concepts.

11. Which statement describes spatial summation?

  1. Inputs arising at distinct synaptic sites contribute to the target's membrane voltage.
  2. A single receptor remains occupied for a long period.
  3. All transmitter is transported into one vesicle.
  4. The voltage at each dendritic site is necessarily identical.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Inputs arising at distinct synaptic sites contribute to the target's membrane voltage.

Spatial summation concerns distinct sites, and attenuation can make their contributions unequal.

12. Which measurement directly expresses input resistance in the small-signal steady-state approximation?

  2. Capacitance multiplied by conductance
  3. Reversal potential divided by receptor number
  4. Voltage change divided by imposed current change
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Voltage change divided by imposed current change

The small-signal relation ΔV = RΔI defines resistance. Its reciprocal is conductance.

13. Why can a GABA-A receptor produce inhibition without a large hyperpolarization?

  2. GABA necessarily blocks all sodium pumps.
  3. No ions can move through GABA-A receptors.
  4. It directly destroys glutamate receptors.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Opening it can lower membrane resistance and oppose excitatory voltage changes.

A shunting pathway can be near its reversal voltage at rest but oppose a voltage displacement caused by excitation.

14. Which description of GABA-B is correct?

  1. A tetrameric calcium-selective pore
  2. A pentameric glycine-gated channel
  3. A heterodimeric GPCR commonly coupled to Gi/o
  4. A transporter that hydrolyses extracellular GABA
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. A heterodimeric GPCR commonly coupled to Gi/o

GABA-B uses G proteins; activation of postsynaptic potassium channels is one representative consequence.

15. What does the chloride charge number z equal in the Nernst equation?

  1. +1
  2. 0
  3. −2
  4. −1
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. −1

Chloride is a monovalent anion. The signed charge number reverses the sign relative to a monovalent cation.

16. Which variable is most directly a measure of the number of charges crossing per unit time?

  1. Current
  2. Voltage
  3. Capacitance
  4. Resistance
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Current

Current is charge transfer rate; voltage is potential difference, capacitance is charge storage per voltage, and resistance relates voltage to current.

17. Which statement about monoamine receptors is correct?

  1. All monoamine receptors are cation channels.
  2. Dopamine must enter a target neuron to bind its GPCR.

  4. D1-like and D2-like dopamine receptors commonly differ in G-protein coupling.

Correct answer: D. D1-like and D2-like dopamine receptors commonly differ in G-protein coupling.

D1-like receptors usually couple to Gs-like pathways and D2-like receptors to Gi/o. The other statements confuse transmitter recognition with permeability.

18. What distinguishes a miniature end plate potential from a single-channel current?

  1. A miniature is always one ion passing through one pore.
  2. A miniature is the aggregate voltage response to a small quantal release event.
  3. A miniature is a propagated muscle action potential.
  4. A miniature can occur only when the nerve is stimulated.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. A miniature is the aggregate voltage response to a small quantal release event.

Spontaneous quantal release can recruit many receptors. A single-channel current is measured at a different scale.

19. Which membrane component maintains the major sodium and potassium gradients over time?

  1. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase
  2. Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor
  3. NMDA receptor
  4. Glycine receptor

Correct answer: A. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase

The pump uses metabolic energy to maintain gradients. Ligand-gated channels exploit rather than establish those gradients.

20. Which comparison of vertebrate skeletal muscle and fruit-fly excitatory neuromuscular transmission is correct?

  1. Both necessarily use identical nicotinic receptor subunits.
  2. Vertebrate junctions are typically cholinergic; fruit-fly excitatory junctions use glutamate.

  4. Vertebrate skeletal muscle uses GABA-A for the normal excitatory end plate response.

The conserved functional principle is rapid chemical excitation, not identical transmitter or receptor identity.

Correct answer: D. I = −60 pA; chloride tends to leave.

The driving force is −15 mV, giving −60 pA. Negative current through an anion channel corresponds to outward negative charge, tending to depolarize.

  1. Approximately −51.0 mV
  2. −40 mV exactly
  3. −59.0 mV
  4. −70 mV
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Approximately −51.0 mV

V = −40 −30/e ≈ −51.04 mV. The steady voltage is approached exponentially, not reached after one time constant.

42. A receptor knockout abolishes an evoked current, and wild-type rescue restores it. A mutant rescue fails but surface expression is not measured. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. The mutant specifically lacks a conducting pore.
  2. The normal receptor contributes causally; the mutant defect cannot yet be assigned to gating versus trafficking.
  3. The receptor knockout proves no presynaptic release.
  4. The mutation proves the ligand is degraded more slowly.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The normal receptor contributes causally; the mutant defect cannot yet be assigned to gating versus trafficking.

Wild-type rescue supports receptor involvement, but failed mutant rescue alone cannot identify the affected molecular step.

43. A transmitter evokes the same current in two neurons held at the same voltage, but the current-clamp EPSP is smaller in neuron B. Which additional observation would support passive electrical differences?

  1. B has lower input resistance.
  2. B necessarily has more released vesicles.
  3. B necessarily has fewer receptors.
  4. B necessarily has no ion gradients.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. B has lower input resistance.

The same current produces less voltage displacement when effective input resistance is lower. Receptor abundance is not determined from EPSP size alone.

44. An excitatory receptor is activated on an inhibitory interneuron that normally suppresses a second neuron. Assuming the interneuron fires more, what is the downstream prediction?

  1. The second neuron must be excited directly by the first receptor.
  2. The second neuron may receive more inhibition.
  3. No communication can occur because excitation and inhibition cancel at the receptor.
  4. The original receptor changes from cation-selective to anion-selective.
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The second neuron may receive more inhibition.

Circuit output depends on the sign of each connection; excitation of an inhibitory cell can increase inhibition of its target.

Mixed-format advanced practice

45. Assertion: All depolarizing postsynaptic conductances increase spike probability. Reason: A conductance whose reversal lies below threshold can oppose stronger excitation by shunting. Choose the correct relationship.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Assertion false; reason true

Depolarization is not a sufficient criterion for excitation. The reason is a valid example of depolarizing inhibition.

46. Assertion: A metabotropic receptor can alter ion-channel conductance. Reason: Activated G-protein components may regulate a separate ion channel. Choose the correct relationship.

  1. Both true but reason unrelated
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Both true and reason explains assertion

Indirect channel regulation is one mechanism of metabotropic reception; a diffusible second messenger is not always obligatory.

47. Which set of statements is correct? I: Ionotropic receptors contain a pore. II: Every ionotropic receptor is pentameric. III: GABA-B is metabotropic. IV: 5-HT3 is ionotropic.

  1. I and II only
  2. II, III and IV only
  3. I, III and IV only
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. I, III and IV only

Glutamate receptor channels are typically tetramers, so II is false. I, III and IV correctly describe receptor architecture.

48. Match the receptor to its representative mechanism: 1 nicotinic ACh; 2 NMDA; 3 GABA-A; 4 GABA-B. P anion pore; Q Gi/o-linked; R cation pore; S cation pore with voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block.

  1. 1–P, 2–Q, 3–R, 4–S
  2. 1–R, 2–S, 3–P, 4–Q
  3. 1–S, 2–R, 3–Q, 4–P
  4. 1–Q, 2–P, 3–S, 4–R
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 1–R, 2–S, 3–P, 4–Q

Nicotinic receptors conduct cations; NMDA exhibits Mg²⁺ block; GABA-A conducts anions and GABA-B uses Gi/o.

49. In the shunting diagram in section 9, the trace with inhibition remains below the dashed threshold despite identical initial baseline. Which experimental measurement would distinguish shunting most directly?

  1. Higher baseline voltage in every trial
  2. Smaller steady voltage deflection to a fixed test current
  3. No measurable transmitter in the presynaptic cell
  4. Identical total membrane conductance in both conditions
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Smaller steady voltage deflection to a fixed test current

Reduced test-current voltage response demonstrates increased total conductance. The unchanged baseline is compatible with an inhibitory reversal equal to rest.

50. The temporal summation diagram shows a second pulse arriving before decay of the first. Which change, all else equal in the linear model, reduces the second peak?

  1. Increase the time constant
  2. Increase inter-event interval
  3. Increase each individual response amplitude
  4. Decrease the inter-event interval
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Increase inter-event interval

Increasing the interval reduces residual voltage exponentially. Increasing τ or pulse amplitude, or decreasing interval, tends to increase overlap.

51. An isolated current changes sign between −80 mV (−40 pA) and −60 mV (+40 pA), with a linear relationship. Which pair of values is correct?

  1. Erev = −70 mV; g = 4 nS
  2. Erev = +70 mV; g = 4 nS
  3. Erev = −70 mV; g = 0.25 nS
  4. Erev = −60 mV; g = 8 nS
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Erev = −70 mV; g = 4 nS

The zero crossing is halfway between the two voltages. The slope is 80 pA/20 mV = 4 nS.

52. Choose the correct causal order for vertebrate motor end plate reception after transmitter arrives: P muscle action potential; Q ACh receptor binding; R ligand-gated cation current; S end plate depolarization.

  1. R → Q → P → S
  2. Q → R → S → P
  3. P → S → Q → R
  4. S → P → R → Q
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Q → R → S → P

Ligand binding permits cation current; charge transfer produces the graded potential, which may reach the threshold for regenerative voltage-gated excitation.

Examination traps and corrections

  1. The neurotransmitter has an intrinsic fixed excitatory or inhibitory sign. Identify receptor coupling and electrochemical state. Acetylcholine acts differently at muscle and pacemaker cells. Why the error arises: Confusing the transmitter name with the target mechanism. Examination test: Test an ACh response in muscle versus cardiac pacemaker tissue.
  2. An ionotropic receptor always conducts sodium alone. Mixed cation channels can pass sodium and potassium together; a near-zero reversal is not the sodium equilibrium potential. Why the error arises: Sodium entry is often the largest inward component, so students forget simultaneous potassium exit. Examination test: Ask why the receptor reverses near zero rather than at ENa.
  3. Every ligand-gated receptor is a pentamer. Nicotinic and GABA-A receptors are pentameric; ionotropic glutamate receptors are tetrameric. Why the error arises: Many familiar channel examples are pentamers. Examination test: Compare subunit counts of GABA-A and AMPA receptors.
  4. GABA always hyperpolarizes. Its anion response depends on reversal and starting voltage. Depolarizing GABA may still inhibit. Why the error arises: Textbook drawings often use one mature-neuron voltage gradient. Examination test: Supply a different ECl and ask for the initial voltage direction.
  5. Inhibition requires an isolated negative voltage deflection. At reversal equal to rest, an inhibitory conductance can still reduce input resistance and oppose excitation. Why the error arises: An isolated voltage trace hides conductance changes. Examination test: Compare the amplitude of a fixed test-current voltage response before and after GABA.
  6. A zero current proves a receptor is inactive. At reversal potential, opposite fluxes can balance even while channels are open. Why the error arises: Students equate channel opening with a nonzero measured current. Examination test: Hold a ligand-gated channel at Erev and then change the holding voltage.
  7. Positive current always means positive ions leave. Inward movement of negative charge also gives positive outward-convention current. Why the error arises: The current sign convention is often confused with the charge of the permeating ion. Examination test: Ask whether positive chloride current means chloride influx or efflux.
  8. A negative Nernst potential means the ion always enters. Compare V with E and respect ion charge; equilibrium potential alone does not specify direction. Why the error arises: A negative equilibrium potential is mistaken for a directional instruction. Examination test: Give V = −90 mV and ECl = −70 mV and ask for ion movement.
  9. EPSP and EPSC are interchangeable. One is voltage, the other current. Capacitance and resistance connect them. Why the error arises: Both traces can have a similar rising and falling shape. Examination test: Compare the units of voltage-clamp and current-clamp records.
  10. A small EPSP proves release is defective. Receptor gating, gradients, resistance and location also influence amplitude. Why the error arises: The evoked potential is commonly used as a single measure of transmission. Examination test: Contrast direct transmitter application with nerve stimulation.
  11. An end plate potential is a propagated action potential. The graded ACh response can initiate but is distinct from the regenerative spike. Why the error arises: Both events depolarize the muscle. Examination test: Block voltage-gated sodium channels but retain receptor current and predict the recordings.
  12. A depolarized steady value proves a brief stimulus reaches threshold. Finite-time charging may leave the membrane far below the steady asymptote. Why the error arises: A steady-state calculation omits charging time. Examination test: Give a short conductance pulse and a long time constant.
  13. NMDA is a conventional voltage-gated channel. Its ligand-gated pore has a voltage-dependent magnesium block and a co-agonist requirement. Why the error arises: Voltage-dependent magnesium block resembles voltage-sensitive gating. Examination test: Remove glutamate while depolarizing the membrane and predict NMDA activation.
  14. All serotonin receptors are GPCRs. 5-HT3 is an ionotropic cation channel. Why the error arises: Most monoamine receptors are indeed metabotropic. Examination test: Identify the 5-HT3 exception in a receptor classification question.
  15. Metabotropic signaling always needs a soluble second messenger. Some G-protein subunits directly modulate a separate membrane channel. Why the error arises: GPCR pathways are usually illustrated with cAMP or IP3. Examination test: Ask whether Gβγ can directly regulate a separate potassium channel.
  16. Two EPSPs always sum exactly arithmetically. Linear addition is an approximation; conductance interactions and spatial attenuation can change the result. Why the error arises: Linear summation is taught first because it is mathematically simple. Examination test: Supply overlapping excitatory and inhibitory conductances and ask for a weighted steady voltage.
  17. A reduced miniature amplitude uniquely proves fewer postsynaptic receptors. Vesicle content, driving force and detection bias are alternative explanations. Why the error arises: Miniature amplitude is often used as a proxy for postsynaptic sensitivity. Examination test: Ask which controls distinguish receptor loss from a change in vesicle filling.
  18. Temporal summation proves increased release at the second stimulus. Residual postsynaptic voltage can sum even when individual release events are unchanged. Why the error arises: Both summation and facilitation can make successive responses larger. Examination test: Compare the decay of the first postsynaptic voltage with the release-dependent current of the second event.

Revision and active recall

Reception transforms extracellular chemical recognition into direct conductance changes or indirect signaling. The effect of a conductance depends on its reversal potential and interaction with other currents. Graded potentials overlap in time and space, and the final output depends on the receiving cell's electrical state and threshold.

Active recall

  1. Trace the path from transmitter binding to a postsynaptic voltage displacement without using the word action potential until threshold is reached.
  2. Explain why a nicotinic channel can pass Na⁺ and K⁺ in opposite directions at the same time.
  3. Derive the signed Nernst relationship and explain the sign for chloride.
  4. Distinguish ligand binding, open probability, channel conductance and receptor number.
  5. Explain how Mg²⁺ makes NMDA current voltage dependent without turning NMDA into a conventional voltage-gated receptor.
  6. Compare GABA-A and GABA-B structure, timing and mechanisms.
  7. Explain an inhibitory conductance that produces no isolated voltage change at rest.
  8. Derive the conductance-weighted passive steady voltage and state two assumptions.
  9. Explain how capacitance and conductance determine a passive time constant.
  10. Distinguish spatial summation, temporal summation and presynaptic facilitation.
  11. Design a recording comparison that separates a receptor defect from altered transmitter release.
  12. Compare the end plate potential with the propagated muscle action potential.
  13. Predict how raising intracellular chloride affects a chloride-dominated reversal potential.
  14. Explain why current reversal alone does not prove the molecular identity of a receptor.
  15. Describe one way a metabotropic response can outlast a transmitter pulse.

Mastery checklist

  • Distinguish the representative transmitter and receptor families.
  • Predict signed current and ion movement from voltage and reversal potential.
  • Explain shunting, depolarizing inhibition and summation.
  • Interpret current–voltage, miniature-event and receptor-rescue data without overclaiming.
  • Connect receptor activation to the graded end plate potential and muscle spike.

Formula and assumption sheet

  • Eion = (RT/zF) ln(Cout/Cin), using concentrations as an ideal approximation.
  • I = g(V − Erev), outward-positive convention; assumes approximately linear conductance.
  • g = NγPo, for equivalent independent channels.
  • Vsteady = Σ(giEi)/Σgi, passive uniform compartment at steady state.
  • τ = C/Σgi, for constant conductances.
  • V(t) = Vsteady + [V(0) − Vsteady] exp(−t/τ), for a constant-conductance step.
  • Two-pulse increment ≈ A[1 + exp(−Δt/τ)], small-signal brief-event model.
