1. Why a circulation requires chemical regulation

A mammal that has been quietly resting suddenly begins to climb a hill. Its skeletal muscles consume ATP more rapidly and require increased oxygen delivery. Its heart must provide additional flow, while the brain must continue receiving blood even though it is not performing proportionately more metabolic work. A moment later the animal stops, and much of the extra flow is no longer necessary. These changes cannot be accomplished simply by making every blood vessel wider. If all resistance vessels dilated equally without an appropriate rise in pumping capacity, the arterial pressure that drives flow through the entire circulation would fall. Regulation therefore has two simultaneous tasks: to distribute a limited cardiac output according to organ needs, and to preserve enough pressure to make distribution possible.

The term perfusion denotes the delivery of blood through a tissue's vascular bed. Perfusion is a flow, not a pressure. Pressure supplies the driving force for flow; local vascular resistance determines how much flow a given driving force produces. Blood flow may rise when arterial pressure falls, provided that the resistance of the relevant organ falls by a sufficiently large proportion. Conversely, raising arterial pressure does not guarantee improved oxygen delivery. If a vascular bed constricts strongly enough, its perfusion may actually decrease. These distinctions are essential whenever a question asks how a hormone affects both blood pressure and tissue blood supply.

At the simplest level, the steady flow through an organ is Q = (Pa − Pv)/R, where Q is volume per unit time, Pa and Pv are the arterial and venous pressures at its boundaries, and R is the effective vascular resistance. With pressures expressed in mmHg and flow in mL/min, resistance has units mmHg·min/mL. This is a hydraulic analogue of a circuit relation, not an assertion that living blood vessels behave as rigid electrical resistors. Resistance is regulated, blood is not an ideal Newtonian liquid in all circumstances, and arterial flow pulsates. Nevertheless, mean quantities allow this relationship to organise physiological reasoning.

For systemic circulation as a whole, the corresponding approximation is MAP − RAP = CO × SVR. MAP denotes mean arterial pressure, RAP right atrial pressure, CO cardiac output and SVR systemic vascular resistance. A commonly used simplification neglects RAP because it is normally small relative to MAP. This simplification becomes less accurate when right atrial pressure rises substantially. It is especially important not to substitute systolic arterial pressure for MAP when interpreting a mean-flow relationship.

Most systemic organs receive arterial blood through beds arranged predominantly in parallel. If their downstream pressures are comparable, their conductances, defined as the reciprocals of their resistances, add to give total conductance. A large change in the resistance of one organ can therefore change total systemic resistance. When exercising muscles become strongly vasodilated, their collective conductance may increase enough to alter the relationship between cardiac output and systemic pressure. The heart and other vascular beds must respond to the new load.

The previous chapters developed the neural reflexes and mechanical features responsible for rapid adjustments of this system. The present chapter examines the additional information carried by chemicals. Endocrine hormones travel through circulating blood and can influence multiple organs. Paracrine substances act in a relatively local neighbourhood after release from nearby cells. Autocrine signals act on the producing cell or nearby cells of the same kind. Neurotransmitters released from nerve terminals provide yet another mechanism of chemical communication. These categories describe the route and range of a signal, not an absolute classification of every molecule: norepinephrine, for example, can act locally as a neurotransmitter or circulate as a hormone.

The location of a signal source matters because an endocrine signal alone does not specify which organ should receive additional flow. A hormone circulates widely, but its effects depend on the receptors, intracellular machinery and physiological state of the cells it encounters. In contrast, a chemical released by a working muscle may accumulate most strongly where oxygen consumption and metabolite production are elevated. Local signals can thus help match blood supply to regional demand without requiring the central nervous system to calculate the needs of every capillary. The resulting circulation is neither exclusively centrally controlled nor exclusively locally controlled.

A further problem is time. A fall in arterial pressure caused by standing must be opposed within seconds; water and sodium conservation cannot restore lost central blood volume that quickly. A sustained loss of fluid, however, cannot be fully corrected by increasing sympathetic tone indefinitely. The circulation needs fast changes in heart rate and vascular tone as well as slower changes in fluid balance. A homeostatic response may begin with one controller and later depend more heavily on another. The controllers overlap in time, so their roles should be distinguished by their mechanisms and characteristic time courses rather than by assigning each to a rigid chronological stage.

In every integrated problem in this chapter, begin by specifying the disturbance. Is there a fall in total blood volume, a redistribution of unchanged volume, an increase in tissue oxygen demand, or a rise in arterial pressure? Next identify the variable sensed by the controller. A receptor may detect stretch rather than pressure directly, salt delivery rather than sodium concentration alone, or local shear stress rather than total blood flow. Then trace the effector and determine its influence on cardiac output, resistance or fluid volume. Finally ask whether the response reduces the original disturbance, and whether other simultaneously operating mechanisms might oppose or reinforce it. This approach makes cardiovascular regulation a problem in causal reasoning instead of a catalogue of hormones.

2. The vascular smooth muscle cell as a regulated effector

Before studying individual cardiovascular hormones, it is useful to understand the common target of many of them: vascular smooth muscle. The cells surround the lumen of muscular arteries and arterioles. When they contract, the vessel tends to narrow; when their contractile activity decreases, a vessel under positive transmural pressure can widen. The transmural pressure is the pressure inside the vessel minus the surrounding tissue pressure. A change in diameter therefore reflects the interaction of active muscle force, passive wall properties and distending pressure. It does not uniquely report the amount of intracellular calcium.

Vascular smooth muscle contains actin and myosin but is organised differently from striated skeletal muscle. Calcium binds calmodulin, and the calcium-calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase, usually abbreviated MLCK. This enzyme phosphorylates regulatory light chains of myosin and promotes actin-myosin interaction. Myosin light-chain phosphatase reverses that phosphorylation. The balance between kinase and phosphatase activity helps determine contraction at a given instant. Some vasoactive signals increase contraction by raising cytosolic calcium, whereas others increase the sensitivity of the contractile machinery to a given calcium concentration.

Calcium can enter the cell across the plasma membrane through several routes. Depolarisation opens voltage-dependent calcium channels; receptor activation can stimulate other channels and the release of calcium from intracellular stores. Receptors coupled to the Gq family of G proteins frequently activate phospholipase C. The resulting inositol trisphosphate, often called IP3, opens calcium release channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Another product, diacylglycerol, participates in signaling through protein kinase C. These are useful general mechanisms for understanding alpha-1 adrenergic, angiotensin AT1 and vasopressin V1a receptor effects, though their signaling networks are not identical.

Relaxation is not simply the absence of a hormone. It can be an actively regulated state. Cyclic AMP and cyclic GMP are important second messengers that often favour relaxation in vascular smooth muscle. Cyclic AMP activates protein kinase A, which can decrease contractile activation through multiple targets, including MLCK regulation and ion channel effects. Cyclic GMP activates protein kinase G, which influences intracellular calcium handling and myosin light-chain phosphorylation. The final outcome depends on the cells' receptor types, ion channels and baseline state. In cardiac muscle, by contrast, increasing cyclic AMP through beta-1 adrenergic receptors enhances calcium-dependent contraction. Knowing a second messenger is insufficient to infer the direction of a whole-organ effect without knowing the effector cell.

Changes in vessel radius have disproportionate effects on resistance. For steady laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid through a straight, rigid cylindrical tube, the Poiseuille relation gives R = 8ηL/(πr⁴), where η is dynamic viscosity, L tube length and r internal radius. If length and viscosity are unchanged, a reduction in radius to 80 percent of its original value increases calculated resistance to 1/(0.8⁴), approximately 2.44 times the starting resistance. If pressure difference remains fixed, calculated flow becomes 0.4096 of its original value. This is an idealisation, but it explains why changing arteriolar calibre is such an effective way to redistribute perfusion.

In real vessels, neighbouring segments contribute series resistance and other organ beds affect the systemic pressure source. A single narrowed arteriole does not necessarily reduce total regional flow by the exact ideal-tube factor. Capillary haematocrit, effective viscosity, vessel length and the number of perfused routes can also change. The correct use of the fourth-power relation is therefore to predict the strong directional and approximate quantitative consequences of radius changes under stated assumptions, not to treat every biological network as one isolated glass tube.

Veins also contain smooth muscle and can respond to chemical regulation. Because systemic veins are highly compliant, changes in their tone can redistribute blood between relatively unstressed and pressure-generating vascular volume. Venoconstriction can increase the driving pressure for venous return when other conditions allow. This is distinct from the effect of arteriolar constriction on resistance to tissue flow. During a haemodynamic challenge, simultaneous venous and arterial constriction may support central filling and arterial pressure respectively, but the response must be evaluated in the context of the heart's pumping capacity.

3. Catecholamines: one circulating signal, different tissue responses

Catecholamines are compounds that include epinephrine, also called adrenaline, and norepinephrine, also called noradrenaline. Sympathetic postganglionic nerve endings commonly release norepinephrine near target tissues, while the adrenal medulla releases both catecholamines into the circulation in response to preganglionic sympathetic activation. The endocrine component extends the influence of sympathetic activation beyond the immediate neighbourhood of nerve terminals. Its spatial distribution and duration need not coincide exactly with the release of transmitter from local nerves.

The effects of catecholamines are organised by adrenergic receptor subtype. Alpha-1 receptors in many vascular smooth muscle cells activate Gq-associated pathways and increase contractile tone. The resulting narrowing of resistance vessels tends to increase resistance and reduce local conductance. Alpha-1 receptors in veins can contribute to venoconstriction. This is useful when the organism needs to mobilise blood toward the heart or sustain arterial pressure during a reduction in central volume. The same constriction may compromise regional flow if it is excessive or if driving pressure remains low.

Beta-1 receptors are important in the heart. Their activation stimulates Gs, adenylyl cyclase and cyclic AMP production. In pacemaker tissue, signaling increases the rate of spontaneous depolarisation and can accelerate heart rate. In contractile cardiomyocytes, protein kinase A modifies proteins involved in calcium entry, storage and reuptake, increasing the force of contraction and facilitating relaxation. These effects can increase cardiac output, but the result is conditional: output is the product of rate and stroke volume, and very rapid beating reduces the time available for ventricular filling. If the heart is underfilled, increasing rate and contractility alone cannot restore the missing volume.

Beta-2 receptors also commonly couple to Gs. In many vascular smooth muscle cells, especially in skeletal muscle vascular beds, activation by circulating epinephrine favours relaxation. Thus epinephrine is not simply a universal vasoconstrictor. At different concentrations and in different organs, alpha-mediated constriction and beta-mediated relaxation can contribute unequally to the observed response. Norepinephrine generally has less beta-2 activity than epinephrine, but neither molecule has a single exclusive receptor subtype. An examination stem must be read for the agonist, receptor population and measurement conditions.

Consider an isolated vascular preparation in which alpha-1-mediated contraction and beta-2-mediated relaxation are both possible. If a low epinephrine concentration produces relaxation, and a selective beta-2 antagonist removes that response or reveals contraction, the observations support a beta-mediated component. They do not establish that every in vivo effect of epinephrine is beta-2-mediated. An intact animal also has baroreflexes, cardiac responses, differences in regional drug exposure and metabolic feedback. Removing a receptor pathway may change the baseline tone as well as the response to a new agonist.

A particularly useful distinction is direct versus reflex action. Imagine a compound that increases arterial pressure primarily by constricting systemic arterioles. The direct effect of the compound on vascular smooth muscle may coexist with a reflex decrease in heart rate because arterial stretch receptors detect the rise in pressure. Measuring a slower heart rate in the intact animal does not establish that the compound directly inhibited the sinoatrial node. To separate the possibilities, one could compare responses in isolated cardiac tissue or compare intact responses with those obtained when reflex transmission is experimentally interrupted. Appropriate time-matched controls would be necessary because autonomic activity and baseline pressure influence the results.

During exercise, sympathetic activation and local muscle vasodilation occur at the same time. This does not represent a contradiction. Sympathetic activity helps support cardiac pumping, venous return and resistance in vascular beds not engaged in strenuous work. In active skeletal muscle, metabolites and other local factors reduce resistance and can attenuate the effectiveness of sympathetic vasoconstrictor signaling. This attenuation is often called functional sympatholysis. It is relative and context-dependent; it does not mean that every sympathetic influence is abolished. The final regional flow represents the combined effects of perfusion pressure and local vascular conductance.

An endocrine catecholamine response is also not equivalent to chronic pressure regulation. It can alter cardiovascular function rapidly, but the circulating concentrations depend on continuing secretion and clearance. If blood volume remains deficient, sympathetic-adrenal responses cannot create new extracellular fluid. Their principal immediate contribution is to improve the use and distribution of existing volume and to modify cardiac performance. Sustained volume control requires coordination with renal and endocrine mechanisms, introduced in the following sections.

4. Renin and the construction of a hormonal cascade

A low arterial pressure can arise from a loss of extracellular fluid, but a low effective arterial filling state is not always equivalent to a low total body fluid content. The kidney is positioned to participate in the distinction because it continuously receives blood and controls the excretion of sodium and water. Its response links short-term cardiovascular changes to longer-term maintenance of extracellular volume. This link is mediated partly by the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, abbreviated RAAS.

Renin is an enzyme released into blood by specialised granular cells associated with the renal afferent arteriole. These cells respond to several converging signals. A fall in renal perfusion pressure can stimulate release; reduced sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa can promote renin secretion through local communication; and sympathetic activation of beta-1 receptors can increase release. These mechanisms are related but not identical. Renal perfusion pressure, tubular salt delivery and sympathetic firing are separate inputs, so an experimental manipulation of one need not reproduce changes in the others.

The first substrate of the circulating cascade is angiotensinogen, a protein supplied predominantly by the liver. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen to form the ten-residue peptide angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme, or ACE, then removes two amino acids to generate the eight-residue angiotensin II. ACE occurs on vascular endothelial surfaces, prominently in the pulmonary circulation, but it is not restricted to the lungs. The distinction between an enzyme and its peptide products is important: renin controls the rate of generation of a signal, whereas angiotensin II has direct receptor-mediated actions on target tissues.

Angiotensin II acts prominently through the AT1 receptor to increase vascular smooth muscle tone. This raises resistance and can help restore arterial pressure when the circulation is underfilled. It also promotes secretion of aldosterone from the adrenal cortex, facilitates thirst and vasopressin release, and influences renal sodium handling. These actions couple the immediate pressure effect of vasoconstriction to a slower recovery of effective circulating volume. The AT2 receptor can mediate contrasting effects in some tissues and contexts, so treating angiotensin II as a molecule with only one possible effect would be an oversimplification. The central NET-level mechanism, however, is the AT1-mediated support of pressure and sodium conservation.

A hormonal cascade can amplify and prolong a stimulus. A small amount of an enzyme can generate many product molecules, and downstream hormone receptors can recruit multiple intracellular signaling pathways. This organisation also creates distinguishable steps for experimental perturbation. If renin activity falls while other inputs remain constant, angiotensin I generation should decrease. If ACE activity is inhibited, conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II is reduced; other pathways and feedback can modify the final measured concentrations. If AT1 receptors are blocked, angiotensin II may still circulate and even increase because its normal negative feedback on renin release is interrupted. The concentration of a hormone and the strength of its receptor-mediated physiological action can therefore move in opposite directions.

The negative feedback of the system becomes clear if an initial fall in renal perfusion stimulates renin. Angiotensin II promotes vascular tone and the conservation of salt and water. These responses tend to improve arterial and renal filling, diminishing the initiating stimulus. Angiotensin II also directly contributes to feedback inhibition of renin. A successful response does not require the hormonal signal to remain elevated indefinitely. If the disturbance persists, however, renin secretion may remain stimulated despite partial restoration of pressure, because the inputs to the system have not all returned to their original states.

Aldosterone is a steroid hormone produced in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex. Its secretion is stimulated by angiotensin II and by increased extracellular potassium. In the distal nephron it promotes sodium reabsorption through changes in the abundance and activity of transport proteins, including epithelial sodium channels and sodium-potassium ATPase. Potassium secretion is often enhanced. Sodium conservation favours extracellular fluid retention when water is available, but aldosterone is not itself a water channel and cannot directly increase water permeability in the same manner as vasopressin. The detailed epithelial transport mechanisms belong to the excretory physiology chapter.

A useful experimental comparison is between rapid angiotensin-mediated vascular responses and the later effects of aldosterone-dependent transport. If an isolated artery contracts shortly after exposure to angiotensin II, this response cannot require a prior increase in total body sodium. If renal sodium excretion falls after a sustained hormonal stimulus, the result may reflect direct renal actions, altered haemodynamics, aldosterone or combinations of these. Demonstrating one component does not automatically establish the mechanism of the whole-body response. These distinctions also explain why a pressure change can precede a measurable change in extracellular fluid volume.

Finally, RAAS activity must be interpreted in the context of physiological need. In a volume-depleted animal, pressure support and conservation of sodium can be protective. If vasoconstrictor and sodium-retaining pathways remain excessively activated when their initiating disturbance has resolved, they can contribute to sustained hypertension or inappropriate volume expansion. The same mechanism may be adaptive in one context and maladaptive in another. This principle will recur when endocrine regulation is integrated with neural reflexes and local autoregulation.

5. Vasopressin: separate the water-conserving and vascular effects

The name antidiuretic hormone describes a substance that reduces the excretion of water. The alternative name vasopressin refers to its capacity to increase vascular tone. Both names describe the same peptide in mammals, but they draw attention to different effector organs. To understand its cardiovascular role, it is necessary to ask why a water-conserving hormone would also have a direct action on blood vessels.

Vasopressin is synthesised by magnocellular neurosecretory neurons primarily in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus. It is transported along their axons to the posterior pituitary, where it can be released into the blood. The posterior pituitary is therefore a release site rather than the principal site of synthesis. This anatomical distinction is a useful reminder that a secretory cell's body and its site of hormone discharge can be separated by a considerable distance.

A major physiological stimulus for vasopressin release is an increase in plasma osmolality, the concentration of osmotically active solute particles per kilogram of water. Specialised sensing mechanisms in the hypothalamic region respond to changes in extracellular tonicity and regulate vasopressin secretion and thirst. Water loss without equivalent solute loss raises effective extracellular osmotic concentration and tends to shrink cells. By conserving water and encouraging intake, the resulting response tends to lower the concentration toward its earlier value. The signal is not a direct measurement of total blood volume.

A second important stimulus is a sufficiently large reduction in effective circulating volume or arterial pressure. Reduced stretch-sensitive afferent activity and the accompanying central signals can increase vasopressin release. Angiotensin II participates in the coordination of this response. During severe volume depletion, nonosmotic stimulation may be strong even when osmolality would ordinarily favour relatively little hormone release. This illustrates how a regulatory system can integrate competing inputs rather than rely on one independent set point.

In the renal collecting duct, vasopressin binds V2 receptors on principal cells. The receptor activates Gs-dependent cyclic AMP signaling and promotes the insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane. This increases the ability of water to move down an osmotic gradient into the cell and ultimately return to the circulation. The hormone does not actively pump water against its chemical potential. Water reabsorption depends on the osmotic gradient generated by renal mechanisms explained in Subunit 7.F. An increase in permeability without a suitable gradient would not guarantee the same antidiuretic effect.

In many vascular smooth muscle cells, V1a receptors couple to calcium-mobilising signaling and favour contraction. This action can raise systemic resistance and support arterial pressure. At ordinary physiological concentrations the kidney is especially sensitive to the antidiuretic action; the systemic vasoconstrictor contribution becomes particularly relevant in major haemodynamic stress. It would therefore be misleading to infer that every modest increase in vasopressin necessarily produces a large increase in arterial pressure. The response depends on concentration, receptor sensitivity, regional vascular characteristics and opposing regulatory processes.

Consider two selective experimental interventions. If a V2 receptor antagonist prevents aquaporin trafficking, water conservation can be impaired even if vascular responsiveness to vasopressin remains intact. If V1a signaling in smooth muscle is prevented, the direct vascular component can be attenuated without necessarily abolishing the collecting duct response. These outcomes are predictions from receptor-specific mechanisms, not descriptions of a clinical treatment plan. They also show why measuring urine volume cannot by itself establish whether a vascular receptor was activated.

Dehydration and haemorrhage provide contrasting examples of the regulatory inputs. Loss of predominantly water tends to raise osmolality while reducing fluid volume. Acute haemorrhage initially removes whole blood, so a strong osmotic increase is not required for the release of vasopressin. In both cases a reduction in water excretion may contribute to volume conservation, but the initiating disturbances differ. An examination question that states unchanged plasma osmolality after haemorrhage is not inconsistent with increased vasopressin; volume-sensitive inputs can supply the signal.

The ultimate cardiovascular effect of conserving water is mediated by the distribution of the retained fluid between body compartments, the state of sodium balance and the capacity of the cardiovascular system to accommodate it. Water retention does not automatically translate millilitre for millilitre into increased plasma volume, because water equilibrates across cellular membranes in response to osmotic gradients. This is one reason why sodium conservation and water conservation are distinct but interdependent processes. The next section considers a hormonal response that opposes inappropriate expansion.

6. Atrial natriuretic peptide and the physiological cost of excess volume

Many students first encounter the heart as an electrically active muscular pump. Its role as an endocrine organ is less obvious, yet it provides an important link between central filling and renal function. Atrial cardiomyocytes contain secretory granules and release atrial natriuretic peptide, abbreviated ANP, in response to increased atrial wall stretch. The stimulus has a mechanical origin, and the resulting peptide carries information about cardiac filling to distant target organs.

Atrial stretch depends on chamber volume, wall properties and pressure. A rise in stretch often indicates that central blood volume or filling pressure has increased, but a given stretch is not a perfectly calibrated measure of total body sodium. The distinction matters when cardiac function changes: a circulation with poor forward pumping may have elevated filling pressures despite insufficient effective arterial perfusion. Thus the endocrine heart and the renal sensing mechanisms may receive apparently contradictory information. This is not evidence that either receptor system is defective; each detects a different part of the physiological state.

The term natriuresis means an increase in sodium excretion in urine, whereas diuresis means an increase in urine volume. The processes often occur together but need not be identical. ANP favours sodium excretion through renal actions and opposes renin and aldosterone signaling. It can also reduce vascular smooth muscle tone. When atrial stretch is elevated by volume expansion, these effects tend to limit further expansion and reduce the load on the circulation. The integrated response is not simply the reverse of one particular vasoconstrictor pathway; it alters both vascular and renal determinants.

A major ANP receptor, natriuretic peptide receptor A, has intrinsic guanylyl cyclase activity. When the peptide activates this receptor, cyclic GMP production increases within the target cell. This differs from the soluble guanylyl cyclase activated by nitric oxide, even though both pathways can converge on the same second messenger. The receptor distinction provides a useful experimental prediction: an intervention selective for soluble guanylyl cyclase need not abolish cyclic GMP formation through ANP's membrane receptor. Conversely, disruption of the natriuretic peptide receptor need not remove every cyclic GMP-dependent vascular response.

The renal response to ANP includes a reduction in sodium-retaining influences and changes in renal haemodynamics and tubular transport. The relative contributions depend on physiological conditions. Its tendency to inhibit renin and aldosterone helps oppose the RAAS during volume expansion. The detailed renal microcirculation and transport mechanisms will be treated in Subunit 7.F. At this point the central concept is the antagonism between conservation and excretion of sodium as an integrated means of regulating extracellular volume.

To reason through a simple experiment, suppose an otherwise stable animal receives an isotonic fluid load. If enough of the added fluid enters the vascular compartment, central venous filling increases and atrial stretch tends to rise. ANP secretion should increase, while reduced sympathetic drive and changes in renal signals can suppress renin. Because the fluid is isotonic, a large increase in vasopressin driven by hyperosmolality is not expected; nonosmotic inputs may instead favour a reduction. The predicted hormonal pattern is therefore different from the pattern after water deprivation, even though both interventions change body fluid content.

Now consider why a single elevated hormone concentration is difficult to interpret. A high ANP concentration can arise because atrial pressure is high, even if the hormone is not successfully restoring normal filling. A simultaneous increase in RAAS activity can occur when the kidney receives inadequate effective arterial perfusion despite venous congestion. Thus opposing hormones can be elevated at the same time. Their concentrations report the activity of regulatory pathways, not necessarily the success of those pathways. A quantitative interpretation requires accompanying information about pressure, volume, organ perfusion and receptor responses.

The principle of antagonistic control is common throughout physiology. A system that can increase a variable only by activating a single pathway may be difficult to regulate precisely across a broad range. By combining volume-conserving hormones with volume-limiting hormones, the cardiovascular and renal systems can respond in both directions. Their actions unfold at different rates: changes in tone can occur rapidly, whereas the consequences of altered excretion accumulate over time. The correct comparison is therefore among controlled variables, stimulus conditions, effector organs and time courses, rather than among memorised lists of hormones that raise or lower blood pressure.

7. Endothelium: the vessel wall senses its own environment

A blood vessel is not merely a passive pipe lined by a waterproof surface. Its luminal lining consists of endothelial cells that participate actively in regulating permeability, coagulation, leukocyte interactions and vascular smooth muscle tone. An endothelial cell lies at an informative interface. Its luminal surface experiences moving blood and circulating chemicals, while its abluminal surface is close to the cells that adjust vessel diameter. It can translate changes at one surface into signals that reach the other.

Wall shear stress is a tangential force per unit area exerted by flowing blood on the vascular wall. It differs from blood pressure, which exerts a normal force per unit area. A vessel can experience substantial pressure when flow is stopped, but the shear associated with bulk flow is then greatly reduced. For steady laminar flow through a cylindrical tube with a Newtonian fluid, wall shear stress is 4ηQ/(πr³). Here η is dynamic viscosity, Q volume flow and r internal radius. In SI units the result is in pascals. The relation is an ideal approximation; pulsatile flow, branching and varying blood viscosity complicate actual wall shear.

The cubic dependence on radius makes the interpretation of shear particularly interesting. If flow is imposed and radius falls, shear increases strongly. If pressure difference is imposed instead and the assumptions of Poiseuille flow hold, flow itself varies with the fourth power of radius. Substituting that relation into the shear equation gives wall shear proportional to radius for a fixed axial pressure gradient. Thus the same geometric change can have different predicted effects on shear depending on the experimental boundary condition. NET-level problems often hinge on precisely this distinction between a pressure-controlled experiment and a flow-controlled experiment.

An increase in endothelial shear can stimulate vasodilator signaling, including nitric oxide production. This provides a mechanism by which a change in flow through a downstream bed can be transmitted to the upstream conduit supplying it. When a working muscle dilates its small resistance vessels, upstream flow increases; endothelial responses can contribute to dilation of larger vessels, reducing their resistance and helping accommodate the altered flow. The exact response depends on location and stimulus history, and not all endothelium-dependent dilation is mediated by a single substance.

Nitric oxide, usually written NO, is a small diffusible signaling molecule synthesised from L-arginine by nitric oxide synthases. In healthy vascular endothelium, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) provides an important source. Enzyme activity is influenced by calcium-calmodulin and phosphorylation-dependent pathways. NO diffuses from the endothelium toward smooth muscle, where it activates soluble guanylyl cyclase. The enzyme increases cyclic GMP production, which promotes relaxation through protein kinase G and its downstream effects on calcium handling and contractile regulation. The diffusibility and short effective range of NO suit it to local rather than prolonged circulating communication.

A key feature of this pathway is that the signaling sequence crosses a cell boundary. The endothelial cell detects the stimulus and synthesises NO; the smooth muscle cell detects the NO through an intracellular enzyme and modifies its contractile state. Removing the endothelium can therefore prevent a response even though the smooth muscle cell is still capable of relaxing. This provides a particularly elegant example of how experimental design established a new physiological role for a tissue once regarded mainly as a lining.

In 1980, Robert Furchgott and John Zawadzki reported that acetylcholine-induced relaxation of arterial smooth muscle depended on the presence of functional endothelial cells. In a simplified account of their experimental logic, artery preparations were contracted to provide measurable tone, and responses to acetylcholine were compared between tissues with preserved and damaged endothelium. Relaxation depended on endothelial integrity. The essential inference was that acetylcholine could stimulate endothelial release of a relaxing influence rather than merely act directly on smooth muscle to produce relaxation. Their study established the endothelial requirement; subsequent research identified nitric oxide as an important identity of the relaxing signal. These are distinct historical conclusions, and the original observation should not be credited with proving every step of the later molecular pathway.

An experiment designed to distinguish endothelial damage from loss of smooth muscle responsiveness would also test a vasodilator that bypasses the endothelium. If acetylcholine-induced relaxation is lost but relaxation to an exogenous NO donor remains, the downstream smooth muscle pathway is still functional. If both are lost, endothelial damage alone may be an inadequate explanation. A contractile response to a standard agonist and comparable baseline precontraction would help confirm that the tissue preparations are viable and that the relaxation comparison is meaningful. The relevant outcome is not simply whether a drug was added, but where in the causal pathway the experimental interruption lies.

NO signaling is also limited by its chemical environment. Haem proteins can bind or react with NO; superoxide can decrease its bioavailability through rapid reactions. Thus the amount of biologically effective NO can decline without a proportional decrease in eNOS protein abundance. The result may be altered vascular responses to shear and endothelial agonists. This helps explain why the expression of a signaling enzyme, the concentration of its product, and the measured functional response are related but nonidentical variables.

Finally, the endothelium can provide both relaxing and contracting signals. A healthy vessel need not remain maximally dilated at all times. A basal tone permits flow to be adjusted upward or downward. Endothelial pathways must therefore be understood as components of a regulated system rather than as an automatic source of relaxation. The next section compares two other endothelial products with NO and explains why apparently opposing signals are useful.

8. Prostacyclin, endothelin and opposing endothelial signals

Prostacyclin is a lipid-derived signaling molecule produced through the cyclooxygenase pathway from arachidonic acid-derived intermediates. It is often denoted PGI2. In vascular smooth muscle, activation of its IP receptor can raise cyclic AMP and favour relaxation. In platelets, prostacyclin signaling inhibits activation and aggregation. The dual action is biologically coherent: a surface that limits inappropriate platelet accumulation and preserves a patent lumen supports continuous blood flow. The detailed mechanisms of haemostasis belong to Subunit 7.A, but the shared signaling at the endothelial interface is worth recognising.

A comparison with thromboxane A2 illustrates the importance of identifying the product and source within the same broad family of lipid mediators. Activated platelets generate thromboxane A2, which promotes aggregation and can favour vascular constriction. Prostacyclin, by contrast, generally favours an antiplatelet and vasodilator state. The two substances are not interchangeable just because both derive from arachidonic acid metabolism. Their distinct synthetic enzymes, receptors and target cells determine their opposing physiological effects.

Endothelin-1 is a peptide produced by vascular endothelial cells and other cell types. It can cause powerful and relatively sustained smooth muscle contraction. Its ETA receptors on vascular smooth muscle are particularly associated with vasoconstrictor signaling. ETB receptors require more careful interpretation. Endothelial ETB activation can stimulate release of relaxing factors including NO and prostacyclin and contributes to endothelin clearance, whereas ETB receptors on smooth muscle may support contraction in some vascular beds. The receptor location is therefore essential to predicting the direction of response.

Suppose a selective intervention reduces endothelial NO formation but leaves endothelin production and smooth muscle receptor function unchanged. The balance of vascular influences can shift toward increased tone even without an increase in measured endothelin concentration. This example demonstrates a recurring principle: the net response of a vascular bed reflects several interacting pathways, and a change in one can unmask the action of another. Observing vasoconstriction after inhibition of a relaxing pathway does not prove that a new contracting substance was released.

An additional local relaxing mechanism in many small arteries involves endothelium-dependent hyperpolarisation. Endothelial signaling can increase potassium conductance and transmit hyperpolarising influences to smooth muscle through intercellular communication or local mediators. Hyperpolarisation tends to reduce activation of voltage-dependent calcium channels and thereby reduce contractile tone. The mechanism is important because it prevents an overly rigid account in which NO and prostacyclin are assumed to explain every endothelium-dependent response. Different vascular beds and experimental conditions show different relative contributions of these pathways.

The experimentally useful comparison is between inhibiting a single pathway and removing the endothelial layer. A nitric oxide synthase inhibitor suppresses NO production, but it need not remove prostacyclin production or endothelial hyperpolarising responses. Endothelial removal can affect several signaling mechanisms at once, as well as the physical state of the vessel surface. If a residual dilation remains after inhibition of one pathway, the result may indicate parallel signaling mechanisms rather than failure of the inhibitor. Appropriate concentration, selectivity and independent measures of the inhibited step are needed before making a strong inference.

The endocrine and endothelial cyclic nucleotide pathways studied in this chapter can now be compared at a shared mechanistic level. Beta-2 receptors and IP receptors commonly signal through cyclic AMP in vascular smooth muscle. ANP and nitric oxide can both increase cyclic GMP, but they activate structurally different guanylyl cyclases. Alpha-1, AT1 and V1a receptors commonly recruit Gq-dependent calcium signaling, although additional pathways may contribute. These similarities help organise the mechanisms, but they do not erase the importance of receptor distribution, cell type and physiological context. A student who learns only that cyclic AMP relaxes muscle will fail to explain the positive inotropic action of beta-1 receptors on cardiac muscle. A student who learns only that endothelin contracts vessels will fail to interpret an experiment involving endothelial ETB receptors.

Endothelial mediators also connect changes in tissue blood flow to changes in vessel wall structure over longer periods. Persistent alterations in the haemodynamic environment can modify vascular tone and influence remodeling. Acute signaling should nevertheless be distinguished from long-term structural adaptation. The former can change diameter within seconds or minutes without requiring a change in wall mass. The latter can alter wall thickness, lumen dimensions and compliance over a much longer interval. Both contribute to vascular function, but a short experimental tracing cannot establish that structural remodeling has occurred.

9. Metabolic control: the demand for oxygen becomes a local signal

If the only determinants of regional blood flow were systemic pressure and sympathetic activity, two nearby muscles supplied by the same arterial source might be expected to receive similar proportions of flow regardless of their activity. In reality, the distribution can differ greatly when one muscle contracts and the other rests. The chemical environment around the resistance vessels of the active muscle changes, providing local information about the relationship between supply and demand. This relationship is the basis of metabolic regulation.

Imagine a small region of tissue whose oxygen consumption suddenly increases. Oxygen delivery is the product of blood flow and arterial oxygen content. If extraction cannot immediately increase enough to meet the new demand, local oxygen availability may decline. Metabolic products can also accumulate because they are produced more rapidly or because their removal by blood flow is relatively slow. Together, these changes can influence vascular smooth muscle and endothelial signaling. Dilation of resistance vessels increases perfusion if an adequate pressure gradient is available, increasing oxygen delivery and facilitating removal of soluble products. The response tends to reduce the supply-demand mismatch.

No single metabolite is an obligatory universal mediator of this response in all tissues. Adenosine, potassium, carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, oxygen-related signals and nitric oxide can contribute under different conditions. Adenosine can be generated during nucleotide metabolism and may activate vasodilator receptors in susceptible vascular beds. Moderate elevations in extracellular potassium can activate inwardly rectifying potassium channels and sodium-potassium pump activity in some vascular smooth muscle cells, favouring hyperpolarisation and relaxation. A sufficiently large potassium elevation can instead depolarise cells and favour contraction. The direction of the response therefore depends on the magnitude of the concentration change and the tissue's ion transport machinery.

Carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions are particularly important in interpreting cerebral circulation, where increased arterial carbon dioxide can increase cerebral flow through changes in extracellular pH and vascular signaling. However, one should not infer that carbon dioxide is the sole controller of flow in every organ. The coronary circulation, skeletal muscle and brain have overlapping but nonidentical regulatory mechanisms. An examination question that reports failure of one mediator's blockade to abolish active hyperaemia need not be interpreted as evidence against metabolic control altogether. Parallel pathways can compensate for the loss of one contribution.

Oxygen availability itself can modify vascular tone. In many systemic tissues, reduced oxygen availability favours vasodilation, thereby supporting oxygen delivery. In the pulmonary circulation, low local alveolar oxygen instead promotes constriction of nearby pulmonary resistance vessels. This specialisation helps divert pulmonary blood away from poorly ventilated regions toward regions where gas exchange can occur more effectively. If hypoxia is widespread rather than local, the same mechanism can raise pulmonary vascular resistance without providing the same redistribution benefit. The contrast is best explained by the different functions of systemic and pulmonary vascular beds rather than by assigning hypoxia one universal vascular effect.

The relation between oxygen consumption and local blood flow can be expressed using the Fick principle. At steady state, oxygen consumption by an organ is Q multiplied by the difference between arterial and venous oxygen content. If content is measured in mL of oxygen per mL of blood and flow in mL of blood per minute, their product has units mL of oxygen per minute. The equation is a mass balance: oxygen entering the organ per minute minus oxygen leaving in the venous blood equals oxygen taken up, provided there is no substantial accumulation in the tissue over the averaging interval. It is not by itself a model of the mechanisms controlling resistance.

For example, suppose a resting region receives 100 mL blood per minute, arterial oxygen content is 0.20 mL oxygen per mL blood, and venous oxygen content is 0.15. Oxygen delivery is 20 mL per minute, and oxygen uptake is 100 multiplied by 0.05, or 5 mL per minute. If uptake must increase to 15 mL per minute and venous content can fall to 0.10, the required flow is 15 divided by 0.10, or 150 mL per minute. The region meets three times the original uptake by combining a 50 percent increase in flow with a doubling of extraction per unit volume of blood. If extraction could not increase, flow would instead have to triple. These values are hypothetical and illustrate conservation of mass.

The calculation explains why changes in flow and extraction can compensate for one another over a limited range. Venous oxygen content cannot decline below zero, and long before zero is reached oxygen gradients and diffusion limitations may restrict uptake. Organs with high baseline extraction therefore have less remaining extraction reserve than organs with lower baseline extraction. This fact will help explain coronary regulation. It also illustrates why preservation of arterial pressure does not establish preservation of oxygen consumption: oxygen content, local resistance, extraction and diffusion all matter.

In laboratory experiments, separating cause from correlation requires care. If adenosine concentration increases when tissue flow increases, both changes may be consequences of increased tissue activity. To test a causal contribution of adenosine, a selective receptor intervention can be combined with a controlled workload and measurement of local flow or vascular tone. Baseline perfusion should be documented, and the intervention's ability to affect its intended receptor pathway should be established. A partial reduction in hyperaemia supports a contribution without establishing that the mediator is sufficient or indispensable. A lack of change may indicate redundancy, inadequate intervention or a genuinely minor role under the tested conditions. This style of inference is central to experimental physiology.

Metabolic regulation is therefore best understood as a distributed negative-feedback process. A mismatch between supply and demand modifies the local chemical environment, which changes vascular conductance and tends to improve delivery. The feedback is not perfectly precise because the relevant signals also depend on metabolism, diffusion and other signaling pathways. The systemic circulation must still supply the pressure needed for increased local conductance to produce useful flow. No amount of local dilation can sustain normal perfusion if the upstream pressure gradient has disappeared.

10. Myogenic autoregulation: pressure itself changes resistance

Blood flow can be regulated even when local metabolic demand has not changed. If arterial pressure increases, a passive vascular bed with constant resistance receives more blood. Many organs, however, adjust resistance in response to the change in perfusion pressure and thereby limit the change in flow. This phenomenon is called blood flow autoregulation. It refers to the tendency to maintain relatively stable flow across a range of perfusion pressures at a specified metabolic state. It is not a claim that flow is fixed under all physiological conditions.

The word autoregulation is sometimes used too loosely to describe any feedback involving blood pressure. In this chapter, pressure autoregulation is a property of the regional vascular bed. The baroreceptor reflex, by contrast, is a centrally integrated mechanism that changes cardiac and vascular activity in response to altered arterial stretch. A denervated or isolated organ can exhibit local autoregulation even when its neural connections to the central nervous system have been interrupted. The two kinds of regulation may operate together in an intact animal, but their anatomical organisation and controlled variables are different.

One mechanism contributing to autoregulation is the myogenic response, so named because it arises from vascular smooth muscle itself. When transmural pressure rises, the vessel wall is initially distended and smooth muscle cells experience mechanical stress. Mechanosensitive signaling can lead to depolarisation, increased calcium entry through voltage-dependent channels and increased active contractile force. The resulting constriction opposes the initial distension and increases vascular resistance. A reduction in transmural pressure can elicit an opposite adjustment, allowing the vessel to dilate when the active tone decreases. These responses tend to stabilise regional perfusion when driving pressure changes.

The observation that vessels respond actively to changes in pressure is often associated with the classical Bayliss effect. Its significance lies in the distinction between passive stretching and active tone. Immediately after a pressure increase, a vessel may widen because the mechanical force on its wall has increased. After the active response develops, its diameter may decrease relative to the initial distended state and, in a sufficiently responsive vessel, may even fall below its original diameter. A time-resolved experiment is necessary to distinguish these phases. A single diameter measurement after the response has stabilised conceals the initial mechanical event.

A useful way to compare these processes is to imagine two isolated vessel preparations. Both are exposed to the same sudden increase in intraluminal pressure while external pressure remains constant. One vessel retains normal smooth muscle signaling. The other is rendered passive by conditions that remove active contractile tone. The passive vessel expands according to its wall mechanics, whereas the active vessel may develop delayed constriction. Comparing active and passive pressure-diameter relations reveals the contribution of vascular smooth muscle. The comparison does not require changes in whole-body sympathetic activity or circulating hormones, provided the preparations are adequately isolated.

A metabolic mechanism can also contribute to pressure autoregulation. If perfusion pressure decreases while metabolic demand remains unchanged, the initial reduction in flow can diminish oxygen delivery and reduce the washout of local metabolites. The resulting chemical changes can favour vasodilation. When flow recovers, improved supply and washout reduce the stimulus. If perfusion pressure increases, enhanced delivery and washout may reduce local vasodilator drive and permit greater tone. The myogenic and metabolic explanations are therefore not mutually exclusive. Their relative importance differs between vascular beds and conditions.

Consider a hypothetical organ with a constant venous pressure of 10 mmHg. When arterial pressure is 90 mmHg and flow is 80 mL/min, the calculated resistance is 80 divided by 80, or 1 mmHg·min/mL. If arterial pressure rises to 110 mmHg, a passive bed of unchanged resistance would receive 100 mL/min. To maintain a flow of 80 mL/min, effective resistance must increase to 100 divided by 80, or 1.25 mmHg·min/mL. Autoregulation in this example requires a 25 percent increase in resistance. It is the effective resistance of the whole bed, not necessarily a 25 percent increase in smooth muscle force or a corresponding change in every vessel radius.

A graph of mean organ flow against perfusion pressure makes the limits of autoregulation visible. At low pressure, resistance vessels may already be near their maximum dilation. Further reduction in driving pressure cannot be offset by an adequate reduction in resistance, so flow falls. At intermediate pressures, resistance changes partly oppose changes in pressure and the curve is relatively flat. At high pressure, the capacity for further vasoconstriction is finite and flow may rise as pressure rises. A real autoregulatory plateau is often sloped rather than perfectly horizontal, and its range differs between organs and between individuals.

It is possible to compare the degree of regulation with a dimensionless local slope. Let A equal the fractional change in flow divided by the fractional change in perfusion pressure for a specified pressure step. A value near zero indicates little steady flow change relative to pressure change; a value near one is consistent with a bed of constant resistance for small changes under comparable conditions. Intermediate values indicate partial regulation. For large changes or nonlinearity, the result depends on the starting point and the direction of the step, so this ratio should be treated as a simple teaching index rather than a universal physiological constant.

Suppose pressure difference rises from 80 to 100 mmHg while flow increases from 80 to 84 mL/min after stabilisation. The fractional pressure increase is 20/80 = 0.25, and the fractional flow increase is 4/80 = 0.05. The ratio is 0.20. The response therefore limits, but does not eliminate, the effect of pressure on flow. The corresponding resistance at the new condition is 100/84, approximately 1.19 mmHg·min/mL, compared with the initial value of 1.00. This calculation offers two consistent views of the same result: resistance has increased, and the flow response is smaller than predicted for a fixed resistor.

The pressure-flow curve is also conditional on metabolic demand. When an organ becomes more active, the regulated flow level may increase, producing a different curve. Autoregulation is the ability to buffer changes in flow when pressure changes at a given demand; active hyperaemia is the increase in flow when demand rises. A graph showing a higher plateau during exercise does not contradict autoregulation. It indicates that the regional system has adjusted the relationship between pressure and resistance to serve a different physiological state.

11. Active and reactive hyperaemia: distinguish the stimulus and the time course

The word hyperaemia refers to an increased blood supply to a tissue. Active hyperaemia, also called functional hyperaemia, occurs when tissue activity increases and local blood flow rises to meet that activity. Reactive hyperaemia is the transient increase in flow after a period of reduced or interrupted perfusion. The two phenomena can involve some of the same vascular mechanisms, but the initiating conditions and temporal patterns differ. Identifying these differences prevents errors when interpreting flow graphs.

In active hyperaemia, the initiating change is an increase in metabolic activity. A contracting skeletal muscle consumes more ATP, and its demands for oxygen and substrate increase. Local vasoactive signaling adjusts resistance, while systemic responses help sustain pressure and deliver additional cardiac output. Flow may be transiently impeded during individual strong contractions because the muscle compresses its vessels. Between contractions, when the external compressive force decreases, flow can increase markedly. The mean flow over several contraction-relaxation cycles is more informative than an instantaneous measurement when estimating total delivery.

At the beginning of a sustained workload, metabolic demand may rise before flow has reached its new level. Local signals and mechanical factors then contribute to the developing hyperaemia. As the activity continues, flow and extraction tend toward a new balance with consumption. When activity stops, the chemical and mechanical signals do not all disappear instantaneously, so flow may take time to return to its original resting level. This gradual recovery should not be confused automatically with reactive hyperaemia; there need not have been a preceding interruption of arterial inflow.

In reactive hyperaemia, the initiating event is a period of reduced flow. During a brief arterial occlusion, tissue metabolism can continue using available substrates and oxygen stores, but the supply of new oxygen and the removal of metabolites are reduced. The lower pressure downstream of the occlusion also favours myogenic dilation. When inflow is restored, the vascular bed remains transiently dilated, so flow can exceed its pre-occlusion value. As oxygen supply resumes, metabolites are removed and vascular tone recovers, the excess flow declines.

A longer interruption of perfusion often produces a larger or more prolonged hyperaemic response over a physiological range, but the relationship is not unlimited. Severe or prolonged ischaemia can damage vascular and tissue function, altering recovery. The magnitude of the response also depends on baseline flow, perfusion pressure, vascular reserve, tissue temperature and metabolism. Peak flow alone therefore cannot quantify all of the physiological consequences of the interruption.

An important distinction is the difference between peak excess flow and the integrated excess volume delivered after release. Suppose baseline flow is 4 mL/s and flow is completely interrupted for 10 seconds. The flow deficit relative to baseline is 4 multiplied by 10, or 40 mL of blood. After release, suppose the excess above baseline starts at 8 mL/s and decreases approximately linearly to zero over 10 seconds. The integrated excess is the area of a triangle: one half multiplied by 10 seconds multiplied by 8 mL/s, which equals 40 mL. In this hypothetical example the excess blood volume equals the earlier flow deficit. It does not follow that the oxygen deficit has been exactly repaid, because arterial content, extraction and consumption may differ during and after occlusion.

If the observed peak flow is high but lasts only briefly, the integrated excess can be small. Conversely, a moderate excess sustained for longer may represent greater total additional flow. The distinction between height and area under a curve applies broadly to physiology, including hormone exposure, ventilation and neural firing. For reactive hyperaemia, it also helps distinguish the time course of vascular recovery from a single measurement of vascular reserve. A proper graph labels baseline, the duration of reduced flow and the recovery phase, and states whether the vertical axis represents flow or conductance.

An experimental comparison of active and reactive hyperaemia might use a perfused limb in which arterial pressure and flow can be monitored. In one condition the muscle performs a controlled workload while the supply remains open. In a second condition the resting limb experiences a brief reversible reduction of inflow. A time-matched control establishes the baseline drift of the preparation. Measuring venous oxygen content and local vascular resistance in addition to flow helps determine whether the changes reflect altered delivery, demand or driving pressure. Comparing these conditions reveals that increased tissue activity and interruption of supply are distinct disturbances even when both ultimately recruit vasodilator mechanisms.

It is also possible for the two responses to overlap. During intense rhythmic activity, mechanical compression intermittently reduces flow while metabolic demand is elevated. During recovery, the measured hyperaemia may reflect a combination of residual metabolic signals, transient oxygen deficit and altered vascular tone. The appropriate conclusion depends on the timing and nature of the perturbation. Physiological terminology is most useful when it clarifies the causal sequence, rather than when every tracing is forced into one exclusive category.

12. Why different organs regulate their perfusion differently

The idea of a systemic circulation can give the misleading impression that all organs are served by identical vascular beds. In fact, each organ faces a particular problem of matching delivery to demand. The heart continuously performs mechanical work, the brain has a high requirement for stable energy supply, the kidney receives a large flow in order to filter plasma, and the skin participates in temperature regulation. These distinct functions explain why no single chemical or neural influence has the same importance throughout the circulation.

Coronary circulation is especially instructive because the heart is both the source of systemic pressure and an organ dependent on that pressure. Blood enters the coronary arteries from the aortic root. During left ventricular contraction, the pressure within the ventricular wall compresses intramyocardial vessels. Compression is especially important in deeper layers of the left ventricular myocardium, where the surrounding tissue pressure can become high. Even while aortic pressure is elevated during systole, resistance to coronary inflow through these regions can increase. Much of left ventricular coronary perfusion consequently occurs during diastole, when muscular compression decreases. The right ventricle has a different phasic pattern because its systolic pressure and wall compression are ordinarily lower.

This temporal arrangement is an exception to the simple intuition that peak aortic pressure must coincide with peak blood flow through every organ. Coronary flow depends on the instantaneous effective driving pressure and on the compressive state of its vessels. The aortic diastolic pressure minus left ventricular end-diastolic pressure provides a useful approximate indicator of the pressure available for left ventricular myocardial perfusion, especially in the subendocardium. It is not an exact measurement of the pressure gradient across every coronary microvessel, because intramyocardial and venous pressures, vessel resistance and the duration of diastole also matter.

Suppose a hypothetical animal has an aortic diastolic pressure of 80 mmHg and a left ventricular end-diastolic pressure of 10 mmHg. The simplified pressure difference is 70 mmHg. If the aortic diastolic pressure falls to 60 mmHg while ventricular end-diastolic pressure rises to 20 mmHg, the estimated difference becomes 40 mmHg. This is a decrease of 30/70, approximately 43 percent. The calculation alone cannot predict the exact decrease in flow, because coronary resistance may fall in compensation. It does identify a substantial change in the mechanical conditions under which coronary autoregulation must operate.

Cardiac muscle extracts a relatively large fraction of the oxygen delivered to it even at rest. Consequently, when cardiac work increases, there is less scope for increasing extraction than in many resting skeletal muscle beds. Additional coronary flow is a major means of meeting the increased oxygen requirement. A rise in heart rate, ventricular pressure or contractile strength can increase myocardial oxygen demand. Local metabolic vasodilation helps increase coronary flow, provided the driving pressure and available vascular reserve are sufficient. A beta-adrenergic signal may therefore increase myocardial work directly and increase coronary flow indirectly by increasing the demand for oxygen.

This relationship complicates the effect of a very high heart rate. Faster beating may increase the number of contractions per minute, and the higher workload may increase oxygen demand. At the same time, the duration of the cardiac cycle decreases, with a substantial reduction in diastolic time at high rates. A shortened opportunity for left ventricular perfusion can become disadvantageous, particularly if the coronary bed has already recruited much of its vasodilator reserve. An increase in heart rate is not therefore an unconditionally beneficial response to every haemodynamic disturbance.

The concept of coronary flow reserve describes the ratio of maximal achievable coronary flow to resting flow under specified conditions. If resting flow is 80 mL/min and maximal flow under a standard vasodilating condition is 240 mL/min, the reserve ratio is 3. If resting flow increases to 120 mL/min while maximal achievable flow stays at 240 mL/min, the ratio falls to 2. This reduction need not mean the maximum flow capacity itself decreased; the baseline workload may have consumed more of the available reserve. These are hypothetical teaching numbers, but the distinction between the numerator and denominator is important whenever a ratio is used as a physiological index.

The brain also depends on continuous oxygen delivery, and its perfusion is particularly sensitive to changes in arterial carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide crosses biological membranes readily and influences extracellular acid-base conditions; increases in arterial carbon dioxide generally favour cerebral vasodilation. Local neural activity can increase flow to the activated region, a phenomenon known as neurovascular coupling. This is distinct from pressure autoregulation, which buffers the influence of perfusion pressure at a given activity level. Both contribute to preserving cerebral function. During a rapid fall in arterial pressure, however, autoregulation may be too slow or reach its lower limit, and cerebral flow can fall. The fact that cerebral circulation is autoregulated does not make the brain immune to systemic hypotension.

The kidney receives blood not merely to satisfy its own metabolic demands but also to support filtration and regulation of extracellular fluid composition. Its vascular bed uses myogenic responses and tubuloglomerular feedback to regulate renal blood flow and filtration over a range of pressures. The two variables are related but not identical: the distribution of resistance between afferent and efferent arterioles influences glomerular capillary pressure. During major circulatory stress, sympathetic and endocrine vasoconstrictor influences can alter renal perfusion and filtration despite intrinsic autoregulation. The details of filtration and tubular feedback are reserved for Subunit 7.F; here the important lesson is that maintaining systemic pressure and maintaining unchanged renal flow are not always compatible short-term objectives.

The skin provides a different example because blood flow to the body surface strongly influences heat exchange. Cutaneous perfusion can rise to support heat dissipation or fall to conserve heat. During haemodynamic stress, the body may reduce skin perfusion even when local tissue metabolism has not changed appreciably. Conversely, during prolonged exercise in a warm environment, the need to supply active muscles and the need to transfer heat to the skin can place simultaneous demands on cardiac output. The thermoregulatory mechanisms are treated in Subunit 7.G, but the cardiovascular conflict illustrates why an organ's optimal flow depends on more than its oxygen consumption.

Finally, pulmonary circulation cannot simply be grouped with these systemic beds. Its pressure is generated by the right ventricle, and its regional distribution is coordinated with ventilation. A local reduction in alveolar oxygen can increase pulmonary vascular tone, as discussed previously, whereas in many systemic organs low oxygen availability favours dilation. This contrast anticipates the next subunit on respiratory physiology, where ventilation and perfusion must be considered together. The general lesson is to predict flow from the specific physiological role of the bed, its driving pressure, its available resistance reserve and the local signals that govern it.

13. Exercise: simultaneous regulation of pressure and metabolic delivery

During dynamic exercise, a large mass of skeletal muscle may increase its oxygen consumption. To satisfy that demand, the cardiovascular system must increase delivery while preserving an arterial pressure adequate for other organs. The problem can be understood by returning to the equation relating mean arterial pressure to cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Active muscle vasodilation tends to reduce systemic resistance. If cardiac output remained fixed and no other beds altered their conductance, the pressure difference across the systemic circulation would decrease. The integrated response therefore combines a rise in output with a redistribution of flow.

At the onset of exercise, neural signals associated with voluntary motor activity can modify cardiovascular control before metabolites have accumulated substantially. This contribution is often described as central command. Feedback from contracting muscle also influences autonomic responses. Withdrawal of cardiac vagal influence and increased sympathetic drive can accelerate heart rate, while beta-adrenergic stimulation increases contractility. These changes enhance the pumping response. The arterial baroreflex does not simply switch off during exercise; its operating characteristics are adjusted so that it continues buffering pressure disturbances around the prevailing exercise conditions.

At the periphery, rhythmic skeletal muscle contraction can compress veins and, with the help of competent venous valves, promote return toward the heart. Respiratory movements can also assist venous return by modifying intrathoracic and abdominal pressures. These pumps do not generate new blood volume; they help redistribute blood and support filling during activity. Sympathetic venoconstriction in some vascular regions can contribute to the same goal. Adequate preload, greater contractility and a suitable heart rate together allow a rise in cardiac output. At high rates or under conditions of reduced circulating volume, the rise in stroke volume may be limited.

The local chemical response in contracting muscle develops alongside this neural and mechanical response. Metabolic signals increase vascular conductance in the regions where demand is elevated. Functional sympatholysis permits local vasodilation to coexist with increased systemic sympathetic activity. This regional attenuation of sympathetic vasoconstriction is important because it would be inefficient to reduce sympathetic outflow globally whenever one group of muscles required more blood. Global withdrawal could compromise pressure control and reduce pumping support. By modifying the effectiveness of local vasoconstrictor signals, the active muscle participates in deciding how systemic output is distributed.

The distinction between local conductance and total systemic conductance is particularly useful for numerical problems. Suppose the pressure difference across the systemic circulation is 90 mmHg at rest and cardiac output is 5 L/min. The calculated systemic resistance is 18 mmHg·min/L. During a hypothetical moderate exercise state, cardiac output rises to 12 L/min and systemic resistance falls to 8 mmHg·min/L. The resulting pressure difference is 96 mmHg. Thus the pressure rises modestly even though total resistance is less than half its resting value, because output has increased sufficiently. If the same fall in systemic resistance occurred while output stayed at 5 L/min, the calculated pressure difference would instead be only 40 mmHg. This counterfactual makes the need for coordination explicit.

Regional distribution can also be examined with the conservation of flow. At rest, suppose skeletal muscle receives 1.0 L/min of a total cardiac output of 5 L/min. The muscle fraction is 20 percent. During the hypothetical exercise condition, it receives 7.0 L/min of a total 12 L/min, or approximately 58.3 percent. The remaining organs together receive 5 L/min rather than the previous 4 L/min. An increased fraction of flow to muscle therefore does not necessarily mean the absolute flow to every other tissue has decreased. In a real animal, particular nonworking vascular beds may receive less flow, others similar flow and some more. Both absolute flow and fraction of total output are needed to interpret redistribution.

The heart and respiratory muscles are themselves active tissues during exercise. Coronary flow must increase to support cardiac work, and the respiratory muscles need additional oxygen to sustain increased ventilation. Heat production may create a further requirement for skin perfusion. In moderate dynamic exercise, the increase in cardiac output often permits several regional flows to increase while pressure is maintained. In strenuous whole-body exercise, the combined potential vasodilation of working muscle and other beds may exceed the pumping capacity available at a tolerable arterial pressure. Sympathetic restraint of conductance can then help protect pressure, though it may limit flow to active tissues. Functional sympatholysis should not be interpreted as unlimited exemption from systemic constraints.

Different exercise modes can produce different haemodynamic patterns. During sustained static contraction, muscle compresses vessels continuously rather than allowing repeated periods of relaxation. Mechanical impedance of local flow and reflex sympathetic responses can be substantial. The relation between arterial pressure, output and resistance may therefore differ from that during rhythmic exercise involving a large muscle mass. Examination questions should specify workload, contraction pattern and the measured variable. There is no single direction of change for every haemodynamic quantity in every form of exercise.

The immediate post-exercise period offers another test of integrated reasoning. When rhythmic contractions stop, the skeletal muscle pump becomes less effective, while vasodilator influences in the recently active muscle may persist for a time. Cardiac output and sympathetic activity also adjust toward the resting state. If systemic resistance and venous return have not yet recovered while pumping support diminishes, arterial pressure can transiently fall. This does not require a new loss of total blood volume, although sweating and fluid loss can amplify the challenge. The time course of recovery is governed by the differing speeds at which neural, mechanical, metabolic and fluid-balance mechanisms change.

The experimental study of exercise regulation benefits from measuring multiple variables together. For example, Doppler-based flow measurements in a limb can be combined with arterial pressure to estimate vascular conductance; oxygen uptake and blood oxygen content can be measured to assess delivery and extraction. A rise in limb flow accompanied by unchanged systemic pressure implies increased regional conductance if downstream pressure is approximately unchanged. If flow rises only because perfusion pressure rises, the same conclusion cannot be drawn. Comparing inactive and active limbs during the same exercise condition can help identify regional mechanisms, though the conditions of muscle recruitment and perfusion pressure still need to be considered. The strongest interpretation follows from integrating flow, pressure, cardiac output and metabolic demand rather than attributing a whole-body response to one hormone.

14. Standing upright: redistribution without initial blood loss

An animal that changes from a horizontal to a vertical posture creates a gravitational pressure gradient along its body. In humans, the pressure exerted by a column of blood increases with depth below a chosen reference level. The hydrostatic relation for a stationary fluid column is ΔP = ρgh, where ρ is fluid density, g is gravitational acceleration, and h is the vertical height difference. In SI units, density is in kilograms per cubic metre, acceleration in metres per second squared and height in metres. Pressure is obtained in pascals. This expression describes a hydrostatic contribution, not the entire pressure pattern of a flowing vascular network.

For an illustrative blood density of 1,060 kg/m³ and a height of 0.40 m, the hydrostatic pressure difference is approximately 1,060 × 9.81 × 0.40 = 4,159 Pa. Dividing by 133.322 Pa per mmHg gives about 31.2 mmHg. A point 40 centimetres below the reference level has a positive hydrostatic contribution of this magnitude; a point equally far above has a negative contribution. These calculations neglect the complexities of vascular valves, flow, vessel wall deformation and varying tissue pressures. They explain why a blood pressure value measured at one height is not interchangeable with a value measured at another without considering the reference level.

The gravitational disturbance is particularly important in compliant veins of the lower body and abdominal regions. Their transmural pressure can increase, and their capacity to hold blood changes. Immediately after standing, a greater fraction of the existing blood volume may occupy these regions. Central venous filling and ventricular preload decline. By the Frank–Starling relationship, a reduction in filling tends to reduce stroke volume unless other influences offset it. Cardiac output therefore initially tends to decline. Unlike haemorrhage, the initial event is redistribution of blood rather than removal of blood from the circulation.

Arterial and cardiopulmonary stretch-sensitive receptors respond to the resulting changes in pressure and filling. Reduced arterial baroreceptor activity leads to reflex enhancement of sympathetic cardiovascular activity and withdrawal of cardiac vagal influence. Heart rate may rise and contractility can increase. Arteriolar constriction in appropriate vascular beds supports systemic resistance. Venous constriction can help support central filling. The adrenal medulla may reinforce sympathetic signaling through circulating catecholamines. The neural response is fast enough to oppose the transient pressure disturbance before changes in kidney excretion could materially replenish volume.

The relationship between these compensations is easier to see using a hypothetical numerical state. Suppose cardiac output initially falls from 5.0 to 4.0 L/min while the starting systemic resistance is 18 mmHg·min/L. Neglecting right atrial pressure for this example, mean arterial pressure would fall from 90 to 72 mmHg if resistance stayed constant. A rise in resistance to 22.5 mmHg·min/L would restore the calculated pressure to 90 mmHg at the lower output. This restored pressure would not establish that total flow has recovered; cardiac output remains 20 percent below the starting value. Some organs may preserve perfusion by local regulation while others receive less flow. The same measured pressure can therefore conceal different circulatory states.

Muscle movement improves the effectiveness of venous return during upright activity. When leg muscles contract, they compress veins; appropriately oriented valves favour movement toward the heart. When the muscles relax, venous segments can fill again. Quiet standing provides less rhythmic assistance than walking. The respiratory pump also influences venous return, depending on the changes in thoracic and abdominal pressures. These mechanisms illustrate that the gravitational problem can be managed in part through mechanical actions without changing the total volume of circulating fluid.

During prolonged upright posture, additional processes become important. Increased hydrostatic pressure in dependent microvascular beds can favour filtration of fluid toward the interstitium, and lymphatic return helps remove accumulated interstitial fluid. If central filling remains reduced, renal and endocrine responses can promote fluid conservation. Reduced effective arterial filling can stimulate renin and, under appropriate conditions, vasopressin; reduced atrial stretch may reduce ANP release. These responses are not necessary to explain the very first few seconds of compensation, and their magnitude varies with the severity and duration of the challenge.

An experimental comparison of lying, standing and passive head-up tilt illustrates how the initiating disturbance can be separated from active movement. Measuring beat-to-beat pressure, heart rate and an estimate of stroke volume can reveal the temporal sequence of compensation. Passive tilt reduces the contribution of voluntarily activated leg muscles relative to active standing, but differences in the maneuvers and the measurement reference level must be considered. A pressure trace alone cannot establish that neural signaling, a change in stroke volume or a change in vascular resistance was responsible. Simultaneous measurements narrow the range of possible explanations.

When compensation is inadequate, the pressure available for cerebral perfusion can fall, particularly because the brain is above the heart in the upright position. Local cerebral autoregulation can buffer the disturbance within its operating limits, but cannot guarantee normal flow when pressure is too low or changes too rapidly. This is a physiological example of how regional and systemic regulation depend on one another. Local dilation is useful only while a sufficient pressure gradient remains available. The mechanisms described here explain the circulatory response to posture, not the diagnosis or treatment of any particular disorder.

15. Haemorrhage: restoring pressure is not the same as restoring oxygen delivery

Haemorrhage presents a more fundamental challenge than posture change because blood has actually left the circulation. The loss includes plasma and erythrocytes, reducing the amount of fluid available to fill the vascular system and the total mass of oxygen-carrying haemoglobin. The initial consequences depend on the magnitude and rate of loss, the preceding circulatory state and the available compensatory reserve. A small reduction in circulating volume can be opposed effectively; a large or continuing reduction may overwhelm the same mechanisms.

A reduction in blood volume lowers the pressure-generating volume within the systemic circulation and tends to decrease the driving pressure for venous return. Ventricular filling and stroke volume fall. If cardiac output decreases sufficiently, arterial pressure falls unless resistance rises. Arterial and cardiopulmonary reflexes respond to altered stretch, leading to increased sympathetic activity. Catecholamines support cardiac function and promote vasoconstriction in appropriate vascular beds. In this context an increase in vascular resistance is compensatory because it helps preserve the pressure gradient required by vital organs.

Local metabolic regulation modifies the resulting distribution of blood. The brain and myocardium have particularly important requirements for continuous oxygen delivery, and local dilation can help defend their perfusion. Other vascular beds may experience reduced flow as systemic constrictor influences increase. No organ is guaranteed adequate perfusion during sufficiently severe volume loss. If arterial pressure drops below the local autoregulatory range, even a maximally dilated bed cannot preserve its earlier flow. The description of redistribution toward vital organs refers to relative priorities and regulatory tendencies, not a complete exclusion of hypoperfusion in those organs.

Endocrine responses extend the compensatory process. Reduced renal perfusion, altered tubular salt delivery and sympathetic stimulation can increase renin release. Angiotensin II supports vascular tone and promotes aldosterone secretion. Vasopressin release increases during sufficiently strong volume-related stimulation, contributing to water conservation and, at higher concentrations, vascular support. Reduced atrial stretch may decrease ANP secretion. These responses help conserve the remaining fluid and support effective filling as water and sodium become available. They do not immediately replace lost erythrocytes.

There is also a fluid exchange response that does not require external intake. If capillary hydrostatic pressure falls, the balance of forces across the microvascular barrier changes, and transient absorption of interstitial fluid into the circulation can occur in some vascular beds. This process contributes to early plasma-volume restoration. The endothelial glycocalyx and the local protein and pressure gradients influence the actual response; the older idea that every capillary continuously absorbs fluid at its venous end is not a universal rule. Lymphatic return remains important for interstitial fluid balance. Fluid entering the vascular compartment can dilute the remaining erythrocytes and plasma constituents, so haemoglobin concentration may fall as the fluid volume recovers.

This leads to a classic interpretation problem: immediately after proportional loss of whole blood, the haematocrit of the remaining, well-mixed blood may be relatively unchanged even though the total number of circulating erythrocytes is lower. After fluid redistribution or replacement without erythrocytes, the haematocrit can decline. The concentration is therefore not a direct instantaneous measure of the amount of blood lost. To infer oxygen transport capacity, one must distinguish the total mass of haemoglobin, its concentration per unit blood volume, saturation and blood flow.

Suppose a hypothetical animal begins with a cardiac output of 5 L/min and arterial oxygen content of 200 mL O₂/L of blood. Its systemic oxygen delivery is 1,000 mL O₂/min. After volume loss, assume cardiac output falls to 3.5 L/min while arterial oxygen content remains 200 mL/L. Delivery becomes 700 mL/min even though the oxygen content per litre is unchanged. If later dilution reduces arterial oxygen content to 160 mL/L while output recovers to 4.5 L/min, delivery becomes 720 mL/min. The second condition has a substantially higher flow than the first, but delivery is only slightly higher because oxygen content has fallen. These are illustrative numbers, not thresholds for clinical decisions.

At a given oxygen consumption, a reduction in delivery can initially be offset by increased extraction. If consumption is 250 mL O₂/min and delivery is 1,000, the whole-body extraction fraction is 0.25. If delivery falls to 500 while consumption remains 250, extraction would need to increase to 0.50. This simple mass balance explains how metabolic function can temporarily be maintained despite a large reduction in flow. Extraction cannot increase without limit, and different tissues have unequal extraction reserves. Once the capacity for extraction and local redistribution is exceeded, oxygen uptake can become limited by supply. Anaerobic metabolic contributions may increase, and tissue function may deteriorate.

Compensatory mechanisms can themselves encounter physiological trade-offs. Strong vasoconstriction can support arterial pressure but reduce local perfusion. Tachycardia can support cardiac output but can shorten ventricular filling and coronary perfusion time. Retaining sodium and water can support volume but cannot restore erythrocyte mass. The preservation of one variable does not necessarily demonstrate restoration of the entire circulatory system. With progressive severe hypoperfusion, impaired cellular function can weaken the very pump and vascular control mechanisms needed for recovery. This is an example of how normally protective negative feedback can be overwhelmed by a disturbance and followed by self-reinforcing deterioration.

Longer-term recovery requires restoration of lost blood constituents, not simply movement of fluid. The regulation of erythropoiesis, discussed in blood physiology, responds to oxygen-related signals and operates over a substantially longer period than acute vascular reflexes. Renal sodium and water control helps restore extracellular fluid balance. The distinction between immediate pressure support, later volume conservation and eventual restoration of oxygen-carrying capacity is essential to interpreting the time course of haemorrhage. It also reinforces the broader principle that cardiovascular homeostasis involves several linked variables rather than a single pressure reading.

16. Long-term pressure regulation and the limits of homeostasis

Fast cardiovascular reflexes can keep arterial pressure within a useful range during transient disturbances, but the circulation cannot maintain a stable state indefinitely if the amount of extracellular fluid continuously increases or decreases. Long-term regulation therefore depends on the balance between intake and excretion of sodium and water. At a steady state, total sodium intake must equal total sodium loss, and water intake and metabolic production must equal water loss. If intake persistently exceeds loss, the relevant body stores increase. A transient pressure change can alter excretion and thereby influence the subsequent volume state.

Pressure natriuresis is the tendency for increased renal perfusion pressure to promote sodium excretion under appropriate conditions. Pressure diuresis refers to a corresponding tendency to increase water excretion. These mechanisms link arterial pressure to extracellular fluid balance. If pressure increases and sodium excretion exceeds intake, sodium stores tend to fall; the associated volume changes can reduce filling and influence arterial pressure. Hormones and renal sympathetic activity alter the relationship between pressure and sodium excretion, so the kidney cannot be represented as an unchanging pressure-operated valve. Its excretory behaviour depends on the wider physiological state.

A graph of sodium excretion against arterial pressure helps distinguish a transient change in pressure from a change in the steady-state pressure required for balance. Draw a horizontal line representing a fixed daily sodium intake. The intersection of that line with an illustrative renal excretion curve is a pressure at which intake equals excretion. If the excretion curve shifts to the right, a higher pressure is required to excrete the same amount of sodium under those conditions. The new equilibrium may therefore occur at higher arterial pressure. This graphical reasoning is useful for understanding one important mechanism of sustained hypertension, but it does not imply that all causes of hypertension are identical.

Suppose the daily sodium intake in an illustrative model is 120 mmol. At one pressure the excretion rate is 100 mmol/day, giving a positive balance of 20 mmol/day. If the difference remains constant for three days, net accumulation is 60 mmol. In a real feedback system the difference would not necessarily remain constant: extracellular volume, hormones, pressure and excretion would change as sodium accumulated. The calculation is a short-interval bookkeeping example rather than a prediction of the final pressure. A conversion from sodium retention to arterial pressure would require additional information about water balance, vascular compliance, cardiac function and resistance.

Long-term arterial pressure can be influenced by vascular structure as well as fluid balance. Persistent changes in resistance vessel lumen dimensions, wall composition and smooth muscle function can affect systemic resistance. Large artery stiffness can modify the pressure change generated during ventricular ejection and thereby alter pulse pressure. Endothelial signaling may be impaired in some forms of vascular dysfunction, changing the balance between dilation and constriction. Neural and endocrine mechanisms can contribute to the development or maintenance of high pressure, and their interactions with renal excretion are important. An elevated pressure is a physiological outcome with multiple possible determinants, not direct evidence for a particular hormonal abnormality.

Baroreceptors can adjust their operating properties during sustained changes in arterial pressure. Resetting does not mean the reflex ceases to exist, nor that it has no influence on longer-term regulation. It means that the relationship between pressure and reflex output may adapt to the prevailing state. Long-term pressure regulation involves renal, neural, endocrine and vascular mechanisms that influence one another. A student should avoid an artificial division in which nerves operate only for seconds and hormones only for days. Hormonal vascular effects can be rapid, neural influences can persist and renal regulation accumulates through time.

These distinctions matter in interpreting a hypothetical animal with an elevated resting arterial pressure. If cardiac output is unchanged and right atrial pressure is negligible, higher pressure implies higher calculated systemic resistance. If output is elevated at unchanged resistance, the same pressure could result from a different haemodynamic state. If both quantities change, the product determines the pressure difference. None of these measurements alone establishes the underlying cause of the elevation. Renal sodium handling, receptor signaling, vascular structural changes and autonomic activity would need to be examined to distinguish mechanisms. The physiological interpretation should remain separate from clinical classification and treatment.

The chapter's central theme is now complete. Neural mechanisms maintain the ability of the system to respond rapidly to changing conditions. Endocrine mechanisms link vascular tone and cardiac performance to the state of salt and water balance. Endothelial and local metabolic mechanisms help individual organs match perfusion to their function. These controllers can reinforce or oppose each other depending on the disturbance. The circulation must supply oxygen and remove metabolic products, but it can do so only when the respiratory system provides adequate gas exchange. The next subunit examines the complementary problem of bringing oxygen into the body and removing carbon dioxide, completing the link between external gas exchange and tissue perfusion.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All catecholamines constrict every vessel.

Correct concept: Receptor distribution and concentration determine net effects; β2-mediated relaxation can oppose α1 contraction.

Why it is confused: Students memorise the sympathetic pressor response as a universal local effect.

Examination approach: A selective β2 blockade can reveal constriction; identify the receptor and tissue before predicting direction.

2. cAMP always relaxes muscle.

Correct concept: Vascular β2 signaling often relaxes, but cardiac β1 signaling increases contractility.

Why it is confused: A second messenger is confused with a fixed whole-cell outcome.

Examination approach: Compare the cell-specific effector machinery, not only the messenger.

3. Renin directly makes aldosterone.

Correct concept: Renin produces angiotensin I from angiotensinogen; angiotensin II stimulates adrenal aldosterone.

Why it is confused: The hormone cascade is remembered as an undifferentiated acronym.

Examination approach: Trace substrate, enzyme, intermediate, receptor and target separately.

4. ACE is found only in the lungs.

Correct concept: Pulmonary endothelium is a prominent site, but ACE is distributed in other tissues.

Why it is confused: Prominent expression is mistaken for exclusivity.

Examination approach: Do not infer loss of all conversion from removal of a single site.

5. Higher angiotensin II concentration always means stronger AT1 action.

Correct concept: Receptor blockade and feedback can increase circulating peptide while reducing receptor signaling.

Why it is confused: Concentration is treated as equivalent to biological action.

Examination approach: Separate ligand abundance from receptor availability and downstream function.

6. Aldosterone and vasopressin have identical direct renal effects.

Correct concept: Aldosterone favours sodium transport; vasopressin V2 increases collecting duct water permeability.

Why it is confused: Both can conserve fluid, obscuring their distinct targets.

Examination approach: Predict sodium and water responses separately.

7. Vasopressin requires high osmolality in every case.

Correct concept: Strong volume-related stimulation can increase secretion even at unchanged osmolality.

Why it is confused: The osmotic stimulus is taught before nonosmotic control.

Examination approach: In haemorrhage consider effective filling as well as tonicity.

8. ANP is a renal enzyme released after low renal pressure.

Correct concept: ANP is a cardiac peptide associated with atrial stretch.

Why it is confused: ANP and renin are grouped together as volume hormones.

Examination approach: Identify the signal source and direction of feedback.

9. NO and ANP activate the same guanylyl cyclase protein.

Correct concept: NO activates soluble guanylyl cyclase; ANP activates a receptor guanylyl cyclase.

Why it is confused: Both raise cGMP, so the generating enzymes are conflated.

Examination approach: Use selective inhibition to distinguish the upstream steps.

10. Endothelin always contracts vessels independent of receptor location.

Correct concept: Smooth muscle ETA commonly contracts; endothelial ETB can promote relaxation and clearance.

Why it is confused: A strong typical effect is mistaken for a universal one.

Examination approach: Check whether the receptor is on endothelium or smooth muscle.

11. Autoregulation is a centrally mediated baroreflex.

Correct concept: Local pressure-flow regulation can persist in isolated or denervated preparations.

Why it is confused: Both mechanisms oppose effects of pressure changes.

Examination approach: Ask whether the controlled variable is regional flow or systemic pressure and whether the CNS is required.

12. Autoregulation maintains one fixed flow at all pressures and workloads.

Correct concept: Flow buffering operates over finite ranges and depends on demand.

Why it is confused: A schematic plateau is mistaken for an unlimited horizontal line.

Examination approach: Inspect operating pressure, workload and reserve.

13. Reactive hyperaemia means flow is high during an occlusion.

Correct concept: The overshoot occurs after inflow is restored to a previously underperfused bed.

Why it is confused: The response is attributed to the time at which local signals accumulate rather than the time flow can occur.

Examination approach: Mark the occlusion and release times before reading the trace.

14. Low oxygen always causes vasodilation.

Correct concept: Many systemic beds dilate; local alveolar hypoxia constricts pulmonary resistance vessels.

Why it is confused: Systemic metabolic regulation is generalised to the gas exchange bed.

Examination approach: Identify the physiological role of the circulation.

15. Standing causes immediate loss of blood from the body.

Correct concept: Initial orthostatic change redistributes blood, lowering central filling.

Why it is confused: Reduced central volume is confused with reduced total volume.

Examination approach: Trace venous pooling and preload before invoking fluid loss.

16. Normal pressure guarantees normal perfusion after haemorrhage.

Correct concept: Increased resistance may restore pressure while output and oxygen delivery remain low.

Why it is confused: Pressure is easier to measure than flow and is used as a surrogate.

Examination approach: Calculate CO, resistance and oxygen delivery separately.

17. Coronary inflow must peak with aortic systolic pressure.

Correct concept: Left ventricular compression limits intramyocardial inflow during systole; diastolic perfusion predominates.

Why it is confused: The pressure source is considered without extravascular compression.

Examination approach: Include downstream tissue pressure and diastolic duration.

Part B — conceptual and mechanistic foundations

1. Which quantity most directly determines mean flow through an organ at a specified pressure difference?

  1. Arterial oxygen saturation
  2. Heart rate alone
  3. Effective vascular resistance
  4. Total erythrocyte mass
Answer and explanation

C. Effective vascular resistance

Flow equals pressure difference divided by resistance. Oxygen saturation and erythrocyte mass affect oxygen delivery, not the hydraulic flow relationship directly; heart rate contributes to systemic pumping but is not the organ resistance.

2. Which adrenergic receptor most directly mediates increased contractility of ventricular cardiomyocytes?

  1. α1
  2. β2 in vascular muscle
  3. β1
  4. V2
Answer and explanation

C. β1

Cardiac β1 receptors stimulate Gs–cAMP signaling and enhance calcium-dependent contraction. α1 is associated with vascular contraction, vascular β2 with relaxation, and V2 with renal water transport.

3. Which pairing correctly identifies renin's substrate?

  1. Renin–ACE
  2. Renin–angiotensinogen
  3. Renin–aldosterone
  4. Renin–angiotensin II
Answer and explanation

B. Renin–angiotensinogen

Renin cleaves liver-derived angiotensinogen to produce angiotensin I. ACE converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II; aldosterone is a downstream adrenal hormone, not the substrate.

4. Where is aldosterone produced?

  1. Juxtaglomerular granular cells
  2. Adrenal zona glomerulosa
  3. Atrial myocardium
  4. Posterior pituitary
Answer and explanation

B. Adrenal zona glomerulosa

Zona glomerulosa cells synthesise aldosterone in response to angiotensin II and potassium. The other sites are associated with vasopressin release, renin and ANP respectively.

5. Which input can stimulate renin secretion?

  1. Increased atrial ANP alone
  2. Activation of vascular β2 receptors alone
  3. Reduced renal perfusion pressure
  4. Increased NaCl delivery to macula densa
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced renal perfusion pressure

Reduced renal perfusion is one stimulus; reduced macula densa salt delivery and renal β1 stimulation are others. ANP generally inhibits renin, and increased macula densa salt delivery opposes the low-delivery signal.

6. Which vasopressin receptor is associated with collecting duct water permeability?

  1. V1a
  2. AT1
  3. ETA
  4. V2
Answer and explanation

D. V2

V2 activates Gs and promotes apical AQP2 insertion in principal cells. V1a is associated with vasoconstriction; AT1 and ETA respond to angiotensin II and endothelin.

7. Vasopressin is primarily synthesised in which structure?

  1. Anterior pituitary
  2. Renal cortex
  3. Adrenal medulla
  4. Hypothalamus
Answer and explanation

D. Hypothalamus

Hypothalamic magnocellular neurons synthesise vasopressin; axonal terminals in the posterior pituitary release it. Neither adrenal nor renal cells are its principal source.

8. Which change is the most direct stimulus for ANP release?

  1. Increased atrial stretch
  2. Reduced ventricular contractility regardless of filling
  3. Activation of renal V2
  4. Decreased plasma sodium alone
Answer and explanation

A. Increased atrial stretch

ANP is released in response to atrial stretch. Plasma sodium and contractility can affect filling indirectly but do not replace stretch as the relevant stimulus.

9. Which enzyme directly responds to endothelial nitric oxide in vascular smooth muscle?

  1. Renin
  2. ACE
  3. Adenylyl cyclase
  4. Soluble guanylyl cyclase
Answer and explanation

D. Soluble guanylyl cyclase

NO activates soluble guanylyl cyclase to generate cGMP. ANP activates a receptor-associated membrane guanylyl cyclase; adenylyl cyclase produces cAMP.

10. Which mediator generally inhibits platelet aggregation and promotes vascular relaxation?

  1. Angiotensin II via AT1
  2. Prostacyclin
  3. Thromboxane A2
  4. Endothelin via ETA
Answer and explanation

B. Prostacyclin

Prostacyclin activates IP receptors and raises cAMP in relevant cells. Thromboxane promotes platelet aggregation; AT1 and ETA commonly increase smooth muscle tone.

11. Which receptor-location combination can promote endothelial release of NO in response to endothelin?

  1. Smooth muscle ETA
  2. Renal V2
  3. Cardiac β1
  4. Endothelial ETB
Answer and explanation

D. Endothelial ETB

Endothelial ETB can support NO and prostacyclin release and endothelin clearance. Smooth muscle ETA and some smooth muscle ETB receptors instead favour contraction.

12. Which statement describes the myogenic response to a rise in transmural pressure?

  1. Blood volume is immediately increased
  2. Active smooth muscle contraction may follow initial distension
  3. Smooth muscle must relax throughout
  4. Afferent baroreceptor firing is required
Answer and explanation

B. Active smooth muscle contraction may follow initial distension

The response can occur in isolated vessels and tends to oppose excessive distension through increased tone. It is distinct from central baroreflexes and does not require increased blood volume.

13. Active hyperaemia is initiated primarily by what?

  1. Interruption and restoration of inflow
  2. Increased tissue metabolic activity
  3. A fall in ANP
  4. Increased venous pooling
Answer and explanation

B. Increased tissue metabolic activity

Active hyperaemia accompanies increased tissue activity. Reactive hyperaemia follows a period of reduced perfusion. Neither venous pooling nor ANP defines active hyperaemia.

14. Which event defines reactive hyperaemia?

  1. A rise in systemic resistance during standing
  2. Persistently reduced flow during exercise
  3. Transient flow overshoot after restored perfusion
  4. Increased sodium excretion
Answer and explanation

C. Transient flow overshoot after restored perfusion

After a brief flow deficit, the vascular bed can remain dilated when inflow returns, producing an overshoot. Exercise hyperaemia instead follows increased demand.

15. What is a characteristic effect of local alveolar hypoxia on pulmonary resistance vessels?

  1. Vasoconstriction
  2. Systemic arteriolar contraction only
  3. No change in any mammal
  4. Universal nitric oxide-mediated dilation
Answer and explanation

A. Vasoconstriction

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction helps redistribute perfusion toward better ventilated alveoli. Systemic tissue hypoxia often promotes dilation, illustrating organ-specific regulation.

16. Why does left ventricular coronary inflow occur predominantly in diastole?

  1. Myocardial metabolism stops in systole
  2. Myocardial compression of intramural vessels is relieved
  3. Coronary arteries contain one-way cardiac valves
  4. Aortic pressure is always zero in systole
Answer and explanation

B. Myocardial compression of intramural vessels is relieved

Systolic contraction compresses intramyocardial vessels, especially in deeper left ventricular layers. Diastole relieves compression while aortic pressure persists. Metabolism continues throughout the cycle.

17. What initially reduces stroke volume on standing?

  1. Instant aldosterone withdrawal
  2. Immediate increase in coronary reserve
  3. Loss of total erythrocyte mass
  4. Redistribution of venous blood away from central filling
Answer and explanation

D. Redistribution of venous blood away from central filling

Gravity promotes dependent venous pooling, decreasing preload. The initial event is redistribution rather than blood loss; endocrine volume adjustment is slower.

18. Which process directly restores erythrocyte mass over longer recovery after blood loss?

  1. Erythropoiesis
  2. AQP2 insertion
  3. Arteriolar constriction
  4. ANP secretion
Answer and explanation

A. Erythropoiesis

Erythropoiesis produces red blood cells. Arteriolar constriction supports pressure and AQP2 conserves water but neither replaces erythrocytes; ANP generally promotes sodium loss.

19. Which equation includes right atrial pressure in the mean systemic hydraulic relationship?

  1. RAP = MAP × CO
  2. MAP − RAP = CO × SVR
  3. MAP = SVR/CO
  4. MAP = HR × EF
Answer and explanation

B. MAP − RAP = CO × SVR

The upstream-to-downstream pressure difference equals flow times effective resistance. Heart rate and ejection fraction alone cannot determine arterial pressure.

20. Which process links a persistent increase in renal perfusion pressure to increased sodium loss?

  1. Myogenic pacemaking
  2. Reactive hyperaemia
  3. Pressure natriuresis
  4. Functional sympatholysis
Answer and explanation

C. Pressure natriuresis

Pressure natriuresis describes the tendency toward greater renal sodium excretion at higher pressure, modulated by neurohumoral and renal factors. The other processes regulate vascular tone or cardiac rhythm.

Part C — analytical, experimental and quantitative reasoning

21. A systemic organ's arterial and venous pressures are initially 100 and 10 mmHg, and flow is 90 mL/min. After a local intervention its arterial pressure is 82 mmHg, venous pressure remains 10 and flow is 120. What is the new effective resistance?

  1. 1.20 mmHg·min/mL
  2. 0.60 mmHg·min/mL
  3. 1.50 mmHg·min/mL
  4. 0.75 mmHg·min/mL
Answer and explanation

B. 0.60 mmHg·min/mL

The new pressure gradient is 72 mmHg; 72/120 = 0.60. Initial resistance was 90/90 = 1.00. Flow increased despite lower arterial pressure because effective resistance fell; using arterial pressure alone would ignore the downstream pressure.

22. An isolated arteriole's radius becomes 0.75 of baseline at fixed pressure difference, viscosity and length. Under ideal Poiseuille assumptions, what fraction of baseline flow remains?

  1. 0.316
  2. 0.5625
  3. 1.78
  4. 0.75
Answer and explanation

A. 0.316

Flow scales with r⁴, so 0.75⁴ = 0.3164. The 0.5625 alternative represents r² rather than r⁴. The calculation applies to the ideal tube, not necessarily an entire branching organ network.

23. A flow-controlled isolated vessel narrows from radius r to r/2 with viscosity unchanged. How does ideal wall shear change?

  1. Doubles
  2. Increases sixteenfold
  3. Halves
  4. Increases eightfold
Answer and explanation

D. Increases eightfold

At imposed Q, shear is proportional to 1/r³, so halving radius increases shear eightfold. The sixteenfold factor corresponds to the ideal resistance change; the result would differ under imposed pressure gradient.

24. In a pressure-controlled rigid ideal tube, radius doubles while axial pressure gradient and viscosity stay fixed. How does wall shear change?

  1. Unchanged
  2. Falls eightfold
  3. Increases sixteenfold
  4. Doubles
Answer and explanation

D. Doubles

Substitute Poiseuille flow Q proportional to r⁴ into shear proportional to Q/r³. Shear then scales with radius. This differs from the imposed-flow scenario, showing why the boundary condition matters.

25. Two artery preparations constrict equally to a standard agonist. Acetylcholine relaxes only preparation X; an NO donor relaxes both. Which inference is best supported?

  1. Y lacks functional smooth muscle guanylyl cyclase
  2. X has no vascular smooth muscle
  3. Y has impaired endothelial-to-smooth-muscle signaling upstream of the NO response
  4. Acetylcholine always contracts intact arteries
Answer and explanation

C. Y has impaired endothelial-to-smooth-muscle signaling upstream of the NO response

Y retains donor-induced relaxation and contractile viability. The selective loss of acetylcholine relaxation supports a defect upstream of the NO-sensitive effector; it does not uniquely prove a particular lesion without further testing.

26. A selective inhibitor of soluble guanylyl cyclase abolishes NO-donor relaxation but ANP still increases cGMP. Why?

  1. ANP activates a membrane receptor guanylyl cyclase
  2. NO produces only cAMP
  3. ANP is nitric oxide
  4. ANP acts through MLCK alone
Answer and explanation

A. ANP activates a membrane receptor guanylyl cyclase

ANP's NPR-A has intrinsic guanylyl cyclase activity, unlike the NO-sensitive soluble enzyme. Preserved ANP responsiveness is consistent with distinct routes to cGMP.

27. An isolated vessel relaxes to low-dose epinephrine. A selective β2 antagonist reveals contraction at the same concentration. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Epinephrine only acts on β1
  2. The antagonist removed all smooth muscle calcium
  3. Relaxant β2 and constrictor influences coexist
  4. Every intact organ must constrict after epinephrine
Answer and explanation

C. Relaxant β2 and constrictor influences coexist

Removing the β2-mediated relaxing component can expose a simultaneous constrictor influence. The observation does not determine every in vivo response because other receptors, reflexes and tissue conditions differ.

28. An intact animal receives a predominantly α1-activating agonist. Pressure rises and heart rate falls. The isolated sinoatrial preparation does not slow. What most plausibly explains the intact response?

  1. Reduced atrial natriuresis
  2. Baroreflex-mediated cardiac slowing
  3. Direct V2 inhibition
  4. Failure of coronary diastolic perfusion in every beat
Answer and explanation

B. Baroreflex-mediated cardiac slowing

The pressure rise can increase arterial stretch and reflex vagal influence, slowing the heart. Lack of slowing in isolated pacemaker tissue argues against a required direct negative chronotropic action.

29. A renal β1-selective intervention reduces stimulated renin release during sympathetic activation, while circulating aldosterone still rises after direct angiotensin II exposure. Which conclusion follows?

  1. The adrenal downstream response remains possible despite interruption of one renin input
  2. Aldosterone is renin's direct substrate
  3. The adrenal cortex synthesises angiotensinogen instead of the liver
  4. ACE cannot function
Answer and explanation

A. The adrenal downstream response remains possible despite interruption of one renin input

The intervention interrupts an upstream renal signaling route; exogenous angiotensin II bypasses renin and can still stimulate adrenal aldosterone. The result does not establish that every renin stimulus is blocked.

30. During selective AT1 blockade, measured angiotensin II increases while vascular AT1-dependent constriction falls. Which explanation is consistent with feedback?

  1. The receptor blocker is an AT1 agonist
  2. Vascular constriction must rise with concentration
  3. Reduced AT1 feedback permits increased renin despite reduced receptor action
  4. Angiotensin II has ceased to exist
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced AT1 feedback permits increased renin despite reduced receptor action

Blocking the receptor can remove negative feedback on renin and increase circulating peptide, while reducing AT1 signaling. Hormone concentration is not equivalent to receptor-mediated effect.

31. Two equal volume deficits are produced, one by mainly water loss, the other by acute proportional whole-blood removal. Which statement is most justified?

  1. Vasopressin can rise only in the first
  2. Vasopressin may rise in both through different combinations of osmotic and volume inputs
  3. Both must raise plasma osmolality equally
  4. ANP must increase in both
Answer and explanation

B. Vasopressin may rise in both through different combinations of osmotic and volume inputs

Water loss can provide osmotic and volume signals; haemorrhage can provide a strong nonosmotic stimulus even without initial hyperosmolality. ANP tends to fall if atrial stretch falls.

32. V2 signaling is selectively disabled while V1a signaling is preserved. Which response to vasopressin is most directly impaired?

  1. AQP2-dependent collecting duct water permeability
  2. Angiotensin I cleavage
  3. Vascular smooth muscle contraction
  4. ANP synthesis
Answer and explanation

A. AQP2-dependent collecting duct water permeability

V2 regulates AQP2 trafficking; preserved V1a can still support vasoconstriction. Renin–angiotensin processing and ANP synthesis are not the direct V2 effector pathway.

33. An organ receives 150 mL/min of blood with arterial content 0.20 and venous content 0.12 mL O₂/mL. What is oxygen uptake?

  1. 18 mL O₂/min
  2. 0.08 mL O₂/min
  3. 12 mL O₂/min
  4. 30 mL O₂/min
Answer and explanation

C. 12 mL O₂/min

Fick balance gives 150 × (0.20−0.12) = 12 mL/min. Thirty is delivery, not uptake; eighteen is venous oxygen outflow, and 0.08 is content difference, not a rate.

34. An organ consumes 20 mL O₂/min at flow 100 mL/min and arterial content 0.25 mL/mL. Its flow decreases to 80 mL/min with unchanged arterial content. What would be required to sustain the same uptake?

  1. Venous content of 0.05
  2. A negative arterial content
  3. Complete extraction, venous content 0
  4. Venous content of 0.25
Answer and explanation

C. Complete extraction, venous content 0

Required content difference is 20/80 = 0.25, equal to arterial content; venous content would have to be zero. This is a theoretical limit and normally not a viable sustained physiological state.

35. At fixed metabolic demand, perfusion pressure rises from 60 to 90 mmHg and flow rises from 100 to 110 mL/min. What is the fractional flow-change to pressure-change ratio?

  1. 0.5
  2. 1.0
  3. 2.0
  4. 0.20
Answer and explanation

D. 0.20

Fractional pressure change = 30/60 = 0.5; fractional flow change = 10/100 = 0.1; ratio = 0.1/0.5 = 0.20. The small positive ratio indicates partial autoregulation rather than perfect constancy or a fixed-resistance bed.

36. An isolated artery is subjected to a pressure step. It first expands, then narrows below its initial diameter. Under passive conditions it expands and stays wider. Which mechanism is supported?

  1. ANP release from the artery explains all responses
  2. Rapid erythropoiesis
  3. Central arterial baroreflex is necessary
  4. Active myogenic contraction
Answer and explanation

D. Active myogenic contraction

Delayed narrowing in the active preparation contrasted with passive expansion supports a smooth muscle response to mechanical stress. Isolation excludes the need for a central baroreflex, although it does not identify every molecular sensor.

37. Baseline flow is 3 mL/s. A complete 12 s occlusion is followed by triangular excess flow with peak 9 mL/s above baseline and duration 8 s. Compare missing and excess blood volumes.

  1. Deficit 12 mL, excess 36 mL
  2. Both equal 24 mL
  3. Deficit 36 mL, excess 72 mL
  4. Both equal 36 mL
Answer and explanation

D. Both equal 36 mL

Deficit = 3×12 = 36 mL. Excess area = 0.5×9×8 = 36 mL. Equal blood volumes do not prove equal oxygen balance because extraction and demand may differ.

38. At rest coronary flow is 100 mL/min and maximal achievable flow is 300. During a higher workload baseline flow becomes 150 while maximum remains 300. What changes?

  1. Reserve decreases from 3 to 2 without a change in maximum
  2. Reserve increases from 2 to 3
  3. Maximal coronary flow halves
  4. Reserve is unchanged at 3
Answer and explanation

A. Reserve decreases from 3 to 2 without a change in maximum

The reserve ratio is maximal divided by resting or baseline flow at the stated state: 300/100 = 3 and 300/150 = 2. The denominator increased; maximum did not decrease.

39. Aortic diastolic pressure drops from 85 to 70 mmHg while LV end-diastolic pressure rises from 10 to 25. What happens to the simplified diastolic coronary pressure difference?

  1. It stays 75 mmHg
  2. It rises by 15 mmHg
  3. It falls from 95 to 45 mmHg
  4. It falls from 75 to 45 mmHg
Answer and explanation

D. It falls from 75 to 45 mmHg

The differences are 85−10 = 75 and 70−25 = 45 mmHg. The 30 mmHg fall is not an exact prediction of flow because coronary resistance, compression and timing can also change.

40. During dynamic exercise, cardiac output rises from 5 to 10 L/min while SVR falls from 18 to 10 mmHg·min/L. Neglect RAP. What happens to MAP?

  1. 90 to 100 mmHg
  2. 90 to 90 mmHg
  3. 180 to 100 mmHg
  4. 90 to 50 mmHg
Answer and explanation

A. 90 to 100 mmHg

The products are 5×18 = 90 and 10×10 = 100 mmHg. A fall in systemic resistance does not necessarily lower arterial pressure when cardiac output increases.

41. A hypothetical animal's muscle flow rises from 1 L/min of a total 5 L/min at rest to 6 L/min of a total 10 L/min during exercise. Which conclusion is correct?

  1. Muscle fraction rises from 20% to 60%; aggregate other-organ flow remains 4 L/min
  2. Muscle fraction remains 20%
  3. Aggregate other-organ flow becomes 6 L/min
  4. All other organs individually lose half their flow
Answer and explanation

A. Muscle fraction rises from 20% to 60%; aggregate other-organ flow remains 4 L/min

The fractions are 1/5 and 6/10. Nonmuscle flows sum to 5−1 = 4 and 10−6 = 4 L/min. The aggregate does not establish the response of each individual organ.

42. After standing, stroke volume drops while heart rate increases from 70 to 84 beats/min. If stroke volume drops from 70 to 50 mL, what happens to cardiac output?

  1. It falls to 0.42 L/min
  2. It rises from 4.9 to 5.88 L/min
  3. It falls from 4.9 to 4.2 L/min
  4. It stays 4.9 L/min
Answer and explanation

C. It falls from 4.9 to 4.2 L/min

Initial output is 70×70 = 4,900 mL/min; final is 84×50 = 4,200 mL/min. Tachycardia only partially offsets the lower stroke volume.

43. After proportional blood loss, arterial oxygen content remains 200 mL/L but CO falls from 5 to 3 L/min. Later CO reaches 4 L/min with content 150 mL/L. Compare oxygen delivery in the two post-loss states.

  1. 1,000 then 600 mL/min
  2. 600 then 800 mL/min
  3. 300 then 150 mL/min
  4. 600 then 600 mL/min
Answer and explanation

D. 600 then 600 mL/min

Delivery is flow times arterial content: 3×200 = 600 and 4×150 = 600. Later flow improves but dilution lowers content; delivery need not improve in parallel with flow.

44. An animal has arterial pressure 90 mmHg with output 5 L/min. After a disturbance, pressure is restored to 90 with output only 3 L/min, RAP negligible. Which conclusion is correct?

  1. Total oxygen delivery is guaranteed normal
  2. Calculated SVR rises from 18 to 30 mmHg·min/L
  3. SVR is unchanged
  4. All tissue flows must be normal
Answer and explanation

B. Calculated SVR rises from 18 to 30 mmHg·min/L

Resistance is 90/5 = 18 before and 90/3 = 30 after. The same pressure can conceal a large reduction in systemic flow; oxygen delivery also depends on oxygen content and regional distribution.

45. On a sodium excretion versus pressure graph, a fixed intake intersects the original curve at 95 mmHg and a right-shifted curve at 120. What does the shift imply under the model?

  1. The heart can no longer pump
  2. The arterial baroreflex immediately ceases
  3. A higher pressure is required for sodium balance at the same intake
  4. Sodium intake must be zero
Answer and explanation

C. A higher pressure is required for sodium balance at the same intake

The equilibrium is where excretion equals intake. A rightward shift means the same excretion rate requires higher pressure, without specifying a single cause or implying total absence of neural control.

46. Figure 10 shows a baseline flow line and two schematic traces. In the lower panel, flow remains low during the shaded interval and then rises above baseline on release. Which observation would most directly distinguish a change in resistance from a rise in driving pressure at release?

  1. Simultaneous measurement of the arterial-to-venous pressure difference and flow
  2. Counting ventricular beats without measuring flow
  3. Measuring arterial oxygen saturation alone
  4. Measurement of plasma ANP alone
Answer and explanation

A. Simultaneous measurement of the arterial-to-venous pressure difference and flow

The hydraulic relationship permits calculation of effective resistance from pressure gradient and flow. The flow overshoot alone cannot establish how much of the change is due to resistance versus pressure. Other measures do not determine the local pressure-flow relation.

47. Use the hypothetical dataset in Section 10. Compare the regulating bed's effective resistance at 60 and 120 mmHg. Which result follows?

  1. 1.27 and 0.76 mmHg·min/mL respectively
  2. 0.79 and 1.32 mmHg·min/mL respectively
  3. 76 and 91 mmHg·min/mL respectively
  4. Both exactly 1.00 mmHg·min/mL
Answer and explanation

B. 0.79 and 1.32 mmHg·min/mL respectively

Using R = ΔP/Q, 60/76 = 0.789 and 120/91 = 1.319. Resistance rises as pressure increases, limiting the change in flow. The reciprocal values are conductances rather than resistances.

SET-style integrated practice

48. Assertion: Renin is an enzyme. Reason: It cleaves angiotensinogen to form angiotensin I.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

A. Both true and reason explains assertion

The catalytic cleavage is the defining functional evidence that renin is a protease. Angiotensin I is the peptide product, not renin's receptor ligand.

49. Assertion: A fall in arterial pressure always reduces the flow through every organ by the same percentage. Reason: Local resistance may change independently between organs.

  1. Assertion false; reason true
  2. Both false
  3. Both true and reason explains assertion
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

A. Assertion false; reason true

Organ beds are not fixed identical resistors. Local autoregulation and systemic redistribution alter resistance differently, so the assertion is false while the reason is true.

50. Which statements are correct? I. ANP can inhibit renin. II. Aldosterone directly inserts AQP2 through V2 receptors. III. Endothelial NO can relax smooth muscle. IV. Reactive hyperaemia can follow a temporary flow interruption.

  1. II and IV
  2. I, II, III and IV
  3. I and II
  4. I, III and IV
Answer and explanation

D. I, III and IV

I, III and IV describe valid mechanisms. II confuses aldosterone-dependent sodium transport with vasopressin V2 signaling and AQP2 trafficking.

51. Match the physiological stimulus to its most immediate local response: raised transmural pressure in an autoregulating arteriole; increased active muscle metabolism; low local alveolar oxygen.

  1. Dilation, constriction, dilation
  2. Active constriction, dilation, pulmonary constriction
  3. Dilation, dilation, constriction
  4. Constriction, constriction, dilation
Answer and explanation

B. Active constriction, dilation, pulmonary constriction

Myogenic tone opposes pressure distension; active muscle metabolic signaling favours dilation; hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is a distinct gas-exchange adaptation.

52. A study reports normal blood pressure after haemorrhage but reduced tissue oxygen uptake. Give the best explanation.

  1. Pressure can be maintained by increased resistance despite reduced flow and oxygen delivery
  2. Normal pressure proves normal cardiac output
  3. ANP directly transports oxygen
  4. All erythrocytes must have recovered
Answer and explanation

A. Pressure can be maintained by increased resistance despite reduced flow and oxygen delivery

Restored pressure does not guarantee output, regional perfusion or arterial oxygen content. The correct explanation follows the hydraulic and oxygen mass-balance relationships.

53. Consider three statements about early compensation after acute proportional blood loss. I. Cardiac output may fall while arterial oxygen content per unit volume initially remains similar. II. Vasopressin cannot rise unless osmolality rises. III. Arteriolar constriction may preserve pressure without restoring total output. Which are correct?

  1. I, II and III
  2. I and II
  3. I and III
  4. II only
Answer and explanation

C. I and III

I and III are consistent with hydraulic and oxygen-delivery relationships. II ignores nonosmotic stimulation of vasopressin by marked reduction in effective circulating volume. Normal pressure and normal oxygen content do not individually establish adequate oxygen delivery.

Revision and mastery

Core synthesis

Cardiovascular homeostasis is the coordinated defence of arterial pressure, effective circulating volume and regional oxygen delivery. Fast sympathetic and baroreflex adjustments modify pumping and vascular resistance. RAAS and vasopressin support pressure and volume during underfilling; ANP opposes volume expansion. Endothelial and tissue-local signals regulate the resistance of individual vascular beds. These control pathways operate together and have finite reserve.
