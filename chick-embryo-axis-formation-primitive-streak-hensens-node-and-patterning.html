1. From a circular blastoderm to a bilateral body

A newly laid fertilized chicken egg contains a small, living embryonic disc above a very large yolk reserve. Although the disc appears nearly circular from above, the bird that emerges from the egg will have a definite head and tail, dorsal and ventral surfaces, and two sides that are similar externally but differ in the disposition of internal organs. Development must establish these relationships while simultaneously reorganizing cells into tissue layers. The initial geometry therefore poses a problem quite different from simply producing a larger collection of cells. A nearly planar tissue must acquire a coordinate system, and cells must interpret this system while moving relative to their neighbors.

An axis is a directed spatial relationship, not a structure that one can necessarily dissect out of an early embryo. The anterior–posterior axis distinguishes the prospective head from the tail. The dorsal–ventral axis distinguishes the future back from the belly. The left–right axis is defined relative to the other two, and the two sides are not biologically interchangeable once asymmetric developmental programs have begun. A midline is the central plane separating the two sides of the body. Anterior and posterior locations can be recognized in an early chick before the final outline of the head exists. Conversely, an anatomical midline becomes progressively more conspicuous as the primitive streak and later the axial mesoderm form.

Pattern formation and morphogenesis are related but separable processes. A cell may begin to express molecular markers characteristic of a region without yet occupying its final anatomical location. Conversely, tissue movement may reposition a cell without immediately changing its molecular identity. To distinguish these possibilities, developmental biologists combine observations of tissue geometry with fate mapping, gene expression studies and experimental manipulation. A successful account of chick axis formation must explain not only where a tissue originates but also why it adopts a particular identity and how its location changes as the embryo elongates.

The chick is particularly valuable because its embryo develops on an accessible surface. Investigators can observe the dorsal aspect, label small groups of cells, exchange tissues between embryos and subsequently identify the descendants of the labeled cells. The planar organization makes spatial relationships relatively easy to follow, but it introduces a difficulty of interpretation: a photograph is usually a projection of several layers. Two labeled cells at the same apparent position in a top view may actually lie at different depths and be members of different tissues. A transverse section or an optical reconstruction can resolve that ambiguity.

In 1951, Viktor Hamburger and Howard Hamilton established a widely used staging series for chick embryos. Their approach organizes development by observable features rather than treating hours of incubation as an exact developmental clock. Incubation duration is useful for selecting specimens, but embryos with the same incubation history need not be identical. For an experiment that compares the size of a streak or the number of somites, developmental stage should be assessed independently. An embryo that appears abnormal merely because it is younger than its control cannot establish a requirement for a particular patterning gene. The pre-streak disc is also commonly described using the earlier Eyal-Giladi and Kochav staging system; these stages must not be confused with Hamburger–Hamilton stage numbers.

Coordinates, staging and the regulative embryo

The broad geometry of an avian egg can be understood by distinguishing the position of the embryo from the origin of its coordinates. The germinal disc occupies a restricted region of the egg, so the animal–vegetal organization of the egg already establishes a direction through its thickness. This direction does not by itself specify where a future head will lie within the plane of the disc. To establish anterior and posterior, a difference must arise around the circumference or within the epithelial sheet. A useful representation therefore involves two coordinate systems: a vertical coordinate relative to the yolk and a planar coordinate along the surface. Confusing them would lead to an incorrect comparison with the amphibian embryo, where the distribution of yolk and the geometry of the gastrula are quite different.

The timing of early chick development is also important. Fertilization and many cleavage divisions take place while the egg remains within the maternal reproductive tract. The embryo has already reached a multicellular stage when an ordinarily fertilized egg is laid. Incubation does not start development from a one-cell zygote. If a student compares the number of cells in a newly laid chick egg with the number in an externally fertilized amphibian egg immediately after fertilization, the comparison confounds developmental stage with species. The same caution applies when considering environmental factors such as temperature: a lower temperature may alter the developmental rate without necessarily producing a specific failure of axis identity.

There are two different reasons for describing the chick as a regulative system. First, the prospective developmental properties of an early tissue cannot always be identified with the fate of every cell in an intact embryo. Second, interactions among portions of an embryonic field can allow an altered arrangement to generate a coherent developmental axis. The concept is best appreciated by comparing a fate map with a transplantation test. A fate map answers where cells usually end up if left in their normal situation. A transplantation experiment asks what the cells can do under changed conditions. A cell that normally contributes to non-neural ectoderm might still be responsive to neural induction at an earlier time; a fate map alone cannot tell us whether that possibility remains.

One can think of the early blastoderm as a field with spatially distributed competence rather than as a collection of cells, each carrying a permanently assigned final address. A field has boundaries within which certain interactions can produce a coherent outcome. Its boundaries can change as tissues mature, and a new pattern may arise if the normal signaling relationships are disturbed. This idea helps explain why transplantation of a relatively small posterior marginal region can have an effect extending beyond the transplanted material. The graft does not need to contain a miniature copy of the final organism. It can establish conditions under which the host tissue executes a developmental program.

Historical work in chick embryos also illustrates the difference between a morphological description and a causal explanation. Early embryologists could identify the first elongated structure in an otherwise flat disc by following changes in shape. Only later experimental analyses could assign developmental properties to that region. Molecular studies add another layer by connecting a tissue's inductive activity to specific signaling systems. Each kind of evidence remains useful. A molecular marker without an anatomical reference can be difficult to interpret, and an anatomical description without manipulation cannot tell us which signal produced the structure. A complete explanation uses the complementary strengths of all three approaches.

Physical orientation and developmental time

The early avian axis is also an example of a developmental system influenced by physical conditions outside the embryo. Classical work on the orientation of the developing blastoderm during egg passage and rotation examined whether the future posterior side was related to the direction of gravity. The important inference was that a planar polarity can be biased by the physical orientation of the developing egg before a stable posterior domain is established. This is not equivalent to saying that gravity remains the instructive signal for every subsequent cell-fate decision. Once an asymmetry in signaling and tissue organization has formed, the embryo can maintain and elaborate that asymmetry through its own interactions.

The distinction between a bias and a determinant is particularly important in comparing species. In amphibians, cortical rotation after fertilization redistributes determinants associated with dorsal specification. It would be misleading to transplant that entire mechanism into the chick merely because both animals establish vertebrate axes. In the chick, the relation between embryonic surface orientation, local posterior inductive activity and restriction of competing axes must be considered in the planar geometry of the blastoderm. A common developmental objective does not imply a common cellular route to achieving it.

A hypothetical orientation experiment illustrates the logic. Before polarity becomes stable, groups of viable blastoderms are maintained in different orientations relative to gravity; after a defined developmental interval, the posterior region is identified with an appropriate molecular marker and the subsequent streak position is recorded. A correlation between orientation and the initial posterior domain would be evidence of an orienting influence. If embryos rotated after establishing a posterior domain retain their original streak orientation, the comparison would also suggest a stage-dependent stabilization of polarity. The two groups must be matched for developmental stage and handling. Without this control, a difference in axis position could be due to deformation or selective loss of cells rather than an instructive effect of orientation.

Morphological staging supplies a second independent coordinate, this time in developmental time. At Hamburger–Hamilton stage 2 the early streak is recognizable; at stage 3 it has extended further; at stage 4 it has reached its definitive length and node morphology is evident. Subsequent stages display a head process, head fold and then somites. Stage 7 is associated with the first somite pair. This sequence is a practical framework for asking which processes occurred before a phenotype became visible. The stage numbers should never be interpreted as hours, and the time required to pass between stages is not assumed to be constant.

If a set of embryos exposed to a perturbation has a shorter streak at eighteen hours of incubation, there are at least two competing interpretations. The perturbation may specifically impair streak elongation, or it may slow the whole developmental program. Comparing other stage markers, including the extent of differentiation and the morphology of neighboring tissues, helps decide between these possibilities. The most convincing evidence of a specific morphogenetic defect would be an abnormal streak in embryos that otherwise have progressed to the expected stage. This logic recurs in experiments on somite number and on the later appearance of asymmetric genes.

Evidence and further reading: Hamburger and Hamilton, 1951 · Gilbert, Developmental Biology

2. The blastoderm: geometry, layers and regional identities

Bird eggs contain such a large quantity of yolk that the early cleavage furrows are restricted to a cytoplasmic region at the animal pole. Cleavage is therefore meroblastic and discoidal: the entire yolk is not partitioned into blastomeres. The resulting embryonic disc is called the blastoderm. During its formation, a space separates the central cells from the yolk, producing a relatively translucent central region known as the area pellucida. The surrounding area opaca appears darker because of its association with the underlying yolk. The contrast is useful for recognizing territories but does not by itself establish their future cell fates.

The embryonic body arises principally within the area pellucida. Extraembryonic tissues support its development, providing the interface through which nutrients and gases can eventually be exchanged. The boundary between regions is therefore more than a visual landmark. It separates cellular environments with different developmental responsibilities. The marginal zone lies around the periphery of the area pellucida, and its posterior portion is an important source of early patterning activity. It should be distinguished from the broad area opaca and from the primitive streak, which is a later structure within the developing embryonic field.

Epiblast and lower-layer identities

At pre-streak stages the epiblast is the upper epithelial tissue. Its cells are joined to their neighbors and possess apical and basal surfaces. Beneath it lies the lower layer, which includes the developing hypoblast. Cells contributing to the hypoblast initially occur in groups, and lower-layer populations are subsequently rearranged as development proceeds. The two layers are close enough to exchange molecular signals but differ substantially in their developmental contributions. In particular, the epiblast is the source of the definitive embryonic germ layers. It is an error to identify the original hypoblast with all of the future gut endoderm merely because both occupy a lower position at different times.

The definitive endoderm is established when epiblast-derived cells ingress and enter the lower layer, displacing or intercalating among pre-existing lower-layer cells. Hypoblast descendants contribute predominantly to extraembryonic tissues. This relationship provides a classic demonstration that anatomical position alone does not define lineage. Imagine marking a group of hypoblast cells before the streak forms and later detecting a different population of unlabeled cells within the definitive gut endoderm. The later lower-layer position would not justify concluding that the originally marked cells became gut. The distinction can be resolved by using stable lineage labels, examining an appropriate later stage and tracing the cells in sections.

Koller's sickle is a crescent-shaped thickened region associated with the posterior border of the area pellucida. Its appearance indicates that a posterior region is becoming distinguishable before the streak has extended. The posterior marginal zone, Koller's sickle and the posterior epiblast are adjacent but not identical territories. Their relationships are best understood by mapping cells, rather than by treating every posterior structure as an alternative name for an organizer. A tissue can influence organizer formation without itself becoming the final organizer.

The epiblast also has an important physical property: its epithelial architecture permits coordinated deformation. A group of cells can change its contacts, intercalate and move toward the midline while remaining part of the sheet. Later some cells leave that sheet. Interpreting these two kinds of movement as one process obscures the sequence of avian gastrulation. The streak begins through rearrangements within the epiblast; at the developing streak, cells also cross from the epithelial surface to underlying positions. These movements accomplish distinct geometrical tasks.

The layer terminology is easiest to understand if we follow an imaginary cell from the beginning rather than memorize a cross-section. At the pre-streak stage, a cell in the upper sheet may be located close to the posterior marginal zone. Its current location gives it exposure to certain signaling molecules, and its epithelial neighbors constrain how it can move. Later it may travel toward the developing streak, change its relationship with the epithelial sheet and enter an underlying region. Its descendant could become mesoderm or definitive endoderm depending on its history and timing of ingression. The cell does not become hypoblast simply because it now lies below the epiblast. Hypoblast names a developmental population with a specific history, not an arbitrary lower coordinate.

There are at least three useful distinctions among the lower-layer populations. Some early hypoblast cells form islands that eventually contribute to a more continuous sheet. Other cells moving forward from the posterior region contribute to the lower layer and are often described in the context of the endoblast. Definitive embryonic endoderm subsequently appears as cells from the epiblast enter and reorganize this region. In an ordinary introductory diagram, only an upper and a lower line may be shown, because the aim is to distinguish depth. In a lineage problem, however, two lines are insufficient unless their origin and the stage of the diagram are specified. A lower layer drawn at one developmental time cannot be assumed to have the same cell composition as a lower layer drawn at a later time.

The posterior marginal zone is frequently presented in textbooks as a small crescent near the posterior edge. That representation is useful for highlighting its influence but can obscure the organization of the entire marginal ring. Competent marginal epiblast is present outside the posterior region, and signals characteristic of the margin can provide a context in which posterior signals act. Therefore, a difference between posterior and nonposterior regions need not arise from entirely unrelated molecular pathways. One territory may provide a factor that is already present more broadly, while another provides a localized partner or removes an inhibitor. This arrangement makes combinatorial signaling particularly useful for developmental patterning.

Consider an experiment that asks whether an anatomical boundary is also a functional boundary. A researcher compares grafts from the posterior margin, lateral margin and central area pellucida, using recipient embryos matched for developmental stage. The grafts are similar in size and placed at equivalent locations. If only the posterior marginal graft reliably induces a secondary streak, the result supports a localized inductive property. If lateral marginal tissue can induce a streak after receiving a specific experimental signal, the result further indicates that the tissue retained the capacity to respond even though it did not normally initiate a streak. Neither observation is equivalent to demonstrating that the central epiblast has no developmental potential whatsoever.

A careful evaluation of a graft phenotype requires attention to the amount and condition of the transplanted tissue. A much larger posterior graft could deliver more of many signaling factors or alter local mechanical geometry. A smaller control graft might produce no response for reasons unrelated to its origin. Tissue viability is especially important when comparing regions attached to the yolk to regions separated from it. The experimental controls should match as many physical variables as possible while varying only the developmental identity that is being tested. This is how a map of an embryonic surface becomes a map of causal relationships.

The geometry of a cell sheet also places constraints on signal propagation. A secreted protein can move through extracellular fluid, bind receptors on the nearest cells, interact with extracellular matrix and be removed by degradation or internalization. Its range is therefore not equivalent to the length of the entire embryonic disc. If a graft affects a large territory, one possibility is that its signal acts over that distance directly. Another is that the first responding cells produce further signals, extending the response through a relay. Distinguishing these mechanisms requires measurements at successive times or perturbation of the responding population, rather than merely measuring the final size of the induced structure.

Lineage evidence and tissue boundaries

The problem of distinguishing cell origin from current location became experimentally tractable through labeling methods. An ideal lineage label is retained by descendants, remains detectable over the relevant period and does not alter normal cell behavior. A dye introduced into one population can be diluted during cell division, while a diffusible label may spread independently of the cells that originally received it. A genetically encoded fluorescent reporter provides another way to follow cells, although its expression and detection also require controls. The classical quail–chick combination provides a particularly instructive example: donor cells can be distinguished from host cells by species-associated nuclear characteristics, allowing the investigator to determine whether a tissue is donor derived in a mixed embryo.

A lineage study of the lower layer can be designed around an explicit time-dependent question. At the first observation, one population is marked in the hypoblast and a separate population in the epiblast. After the streak has formed, the experimenter examines the labeled descendants in the lower layer and later endodermal tissues. If epiblast-derived cells occupy the definitive gut territory while hypoblast-derived cells are found predominantly in extraembryonic positions, the results establish a replacement of developmental populations rather than a simple maturation of all original lower-layer cells into gut. The initial labeling must be selective; accidental staining of the epiblast during hypoblast labeling would otherwise produce misleading apparent contributions.

The significance of this lineage difference is not limited to nomenclature. An extraembryonic population can have an essential instructive role in development of an embryonic tissue without being its cellular ancestor. In the chick, the hypoblast provides an especially clear example because its effect on streak positioning can be separated from its predominantly extraembryonic fate. This distinction generalizes to many developmental systems: a tissue that organizes its neighbor is not necessarily the tissue from which that neighbor descends.

For the same reason, a fate map and an expression map should be drawn using different conventions. A fate map places labels at the original positions of cells and describes their later descendants. An expression map shows the spatial domain in which a gene or protein is detected at the observation time. When a gene is expressed by several developmental populations, the expression map cannot substitute for a lineage experiment. If a labeled cell moves out of the original expression domain but continues to contribute to a new structure, a lineage map records that movement even if the original marker is no longer expressed. This difference is a major source of incorrect interpretations in examinations involving colored embryonic territories.

Dorsal–ventral organization should be distinguished from the apical–basal polarity of any individual epithelial cell. In the early disc the upper epiblast faces away from the yolk, while lower-layer populations lie toward it. As gastrulation constructs mesoderm and definitive endoderm, the embryo establishes a tissue arrangement in which neural ectoderm and later the neural tube are dorsal to axial mesoderm and gut endoderm. Subsequent folding converts these initially layered relationships into the three-dimensional arrangement of the body. Local signals refine dorsal and ventral identities within particular tissues. For instance, ventral neural and somitic patterning depends on interactions with axial mesoderm, whereas dorsal tissues receive other signals. The embryonic dorsal–ventral axis is therefore not identical to the simple apical–basal coordinate of the pre-streak epiblast. It is a developing relationship among tissues, elaborated at different times and scales.

3. Posterior induction and restriction to a single axis

The first obvious manifestation of anterior–posterior polarity is not the formation of a head. It is the establishment of a posterior region from which the streak will arise. In a regulative embryo, the posterior domain must be favored over other potentially competent territories. Posterior identity involves both positive induction and the restriction of equivalent responses elsewhere. If only a positive signal mattered, any cell exposed to a sufficient amount might initiate an additional axis, and a reproducible single body plan would be difficult to explain.

Experimental transplantation of posterior marginal zone tissue has helped separate the inducing region from the cells that execute gastrulation. When a piece of posterior marginal zone is moved to an appropriate ectopic position in a competent early blastoderm, a new streak can be induced. A central question is whether the additional streak is made from transplanted cells or recruited from the host. Labeling the donor tissue and identifying the origin of streak cells addresses that question. Evidence that a host-derived streak develops under the influence of the graft supports a role for posterior marginal tissue in organizer induction, distinct from the later organizer activity of the node itself. The experiments of Bachvarova and colleagues and other investigators established this distinction in chick embryos.

Cooperative induction and restriction

A useful molecular framework begins with Vg1, a member of the transforming growth factor beta superfamily, in a posterior signaling context. Wnt activity in the marginal region cooperates with Vg1-related activity, promoting the expression and action of Nodal-related signals in adjacent epiblast. Nodal ligands are secreted signaling proteins. They activate receptor complexes that ultimately regulate transcription through intracellular Smad proteins. Wnt signaling changes the transcriptional activity of responsive cells through a different signaling system. Their cooperation gives a cell information about both its location and the signaling environment. The details of generic receptor signaling belong to Unit 4; the important developmental principle is that a signal need not have the same outcome everywhere in the blastoderm.

The lower layer contributes a restriction on this positive induction. The hypoblast produces Cerberus, a secreted antagonist with activity against several signaling systems, including Nodal. In the chick context studied by Bertocchini and Stern, antagonism of Nodal is especially important for inhibiting premature or misplaced primitive streak formation. As lower-layer territories are rearranged and the hypoblast is displaced anteriorly by endoblast, the inhibitory environment changes. The posterior epiblast can then express its streak-forming potential under appropriate inductive conditions.

The inhibitory model does not imply that the hypoblast directly supplies all organizer cells or that removal of inhibition automatically causes every cell to form mesoderm. A cell still requires positive signals and appropriate competence. The distinction is analogous to removing a brake from a vehicle: it does not create the forward driving force. In a biological experiment, inhibiting Cerberus and independently blocking Nodal provides a means of testing the relationship. If extra streaks after removal of the hypoblast depend on Nodal activity, simultaneous inhibition of Nodal should reduce that response. Such a result supports the proposed pathway order, provided that the inhibitor does not simply kill the tissue.

A simple thought experiment makes the importance of regulation clear. Consider a pre-streak disc with competent marginal cells, positive signaling at the posterior margin and inhibition over much of the rest of the disc. Increasing positive signaling in an anterior region might have little effect while the inhibitor is present. Removing inhibition may uncover ectopic competence, particularly where a positive signal can act. Introducing the positive signal after the tissue has lost competence might again produce no streak. These outcomes are not contradictory; they arise because induction is conditional on time, location and responsiveness.

The development of a single axis can be stabilized by more than one inhibitory event. Evidence from studies of early chick polarity suggests that inhibitory processes act at successive stages, including hypoblast-derived Cerberus and later signals from the streak itself. The role of each antagonist should therefore be placed in its developmental time window. The early absence of multiple axes is not proof that all nonposterior cells are permanently incapable of forming one. A result obtained after the streak is established may reflect restriction by an already existing embryonic axis rather than the initial mechanism that selected its position.

In considering the relationship between Vg1, Wnt and Nodal, it is useful to separate the identity of a ligand from the state of its responding cell. A secreted ligand is a molecular source of information, but it can influence a cell only if the cell has the necessary receptors and intracellular response machinery. Even cells expressing a receptor may respond differently because of prior transcriptional states. A cell's ability to activate a particular gene in response to a signal may depend on transcription factors that were produced before the signal arrived. Thus a posterior region can be selected by the combination of ligand availability, competence and the absence of antagonism.

A ligand that binds a receptor can also be intercepted before reaching it. Cerberus provides an example of extracellular control. As an antagonist of Nodal-related activity it changes the availability of effective inductive signaling, rather than acting as a transcription factor inside every cell that it influences. This distinction matters experimentally. If a cell is exposed to the same concentration of Nodal in two conditions but only one condition contains an extracellular antagonist, the total ligand concentration need not correspond to the concentration capable of activating receptors. An antibody assay that detects ligand protein may therefore give a different picture from a reporter of downstream pathway activity.

Dynamic signaling and experimental predictions

The movement of the hypoblast adds a dynamic element to this regulation. If the lower-layer cells that express an antagonist are displaced, the location of inhibition shifts even if the antagonist-producing cells do not immediately alter their gene expression. The embryo can then change its signaling field through tissue rearrangement. This is an example of a feedback relationship between morphogenesis and pattern formation: signals regulate developmental movement, and movement changes the spatial relationship among signaling sources and responsive cells. A static map of gene expression at one stage cannot describe the complete regulatory sequence.

To analyze the evidence for an inhibitory mechanism, imagine a series of four hypothetical conditions. In the first, a pre-streak blastoderm is left undisturbed and produces one correctly positioned streak. In the second, the hypoblast is removed and extra streaks appear. In the third, the hypoblast is removed but a replacement lower layer capable of producing the normal inhibitory factor is provided. The frequency of extra streaks is reduced. In the fourth, a lower layer that is viable but unable to provide that factor fails to rescue the phenotype. Together, these observations would connect a tissue-level manipulation with a molecular hypothesis more convincingly than removal alone. The experiment must still establish that the replacement tissue is in the correct position and that other properties of the two replacement layers are comparable.

A different question concerns the relationship between the inductive factors. If Vg1 can initiate ectopic axis formation only within a Wnt-competent context, the response should depend on the presence of both activities. In a factorial experiment one can compare no added signal, each signal individually, and both signals together. A strong response only in the combined condition suggests cooperation. It does not prove a particular biochemical interaction between the two ligand proteins. The cooperation may occur at the level of transcriptional targets, tissue competence or a sequence in which one pathway changes the cellular response to the other. Examination problems often ask the student to infer more than the data justify. The correct conclusion is typically that both inputs are required in the tested condition, followed by a separate experiment to establish the physical mechanism.

Developmental inhibition also explains a familiar feature of regulative embryos: the consequences of perturbation depend on whether the original axis remains present. A prospective streak-inducing fragment placed far from an already established streak may behave differently from the same fragment placed in a younger embryo. In the older embryo, inhibition associated with the existing axis can restrict initiation of an extra one. A finding that a graft is effective only before a particular stage need not mean that the donor has changed. It could reflect changes in the host environment. Reciprocal experiments using donors and hosts of different stages are therefore informative: they separate the developmental state of the inducer from the developmental state of the responding field.

A simple diffusion model clarifies why the spatial distribution of an antagonist matters. If a secreted signaling protein is produced at a localized source and undergoes diffusion and removal, its concentration can decline away from the source. In a simplified one-dimensional steady state, with diffusion coefficient D and first-order removal rate k, the characteristic distance is the square root of D divided by k. D has dimensions of area per time and k has dimensions of inverse time, so the characteristic distance has units of length. A higher removal rate reduces the characteristic distance, whereas a greater diffusion coefficient increases it. The model is not a measured description of Nodal propagation in the chick, because actual tissue geometry, binding, feedback and signaling relays complicate the process. Its purpose is to explain why identical rates of ligand synthesis need not generate identical ranges of biological response.

An extracellular antagonist introduces an additional complication: what matters for receptor activation may be the amount of available ligand rather than the total amount present. A high concentration of antagonist near a particular embryonic region can lower the effective signaling activity there even if the amount of ligand synthesized by its source remains unchanged. A pathway reporter that measures the cellular response can distinguish this effect from a simple reduction in ligand expression. The concept also predicts that changing the spatial distribution of the antagonist can change the response field without changing its total production. A moving population of hypoblast cells therefore provides a plausible way to alter the position and extent of inhibition.

For a beginner, it is useful to separate three experiments that might all produce a second primitive streak. The first moves a source of inductive activity to a new location. The second removes an inhibitory tissue while leaving the normal positive signals in place. The third changes the competence of a group of epiblast cells so that they now respond to a previously ineffective signal. All three can generate a similar anatomical endpoint, yet the causal changes are different. A secondary streak is a phenotype, not the name of a molecular mechanism. The mechanism must be inferred from the particular intervention and verified through additional readouts.

The analysis of cooperative signals can likewise be formalized using a qualitative response table. If Wnt activity alone produces little streak-inducing response in a tested marginal territory, and Vg1 activity alone produces little response where Wnt signaling is effectively absent, a robust combined response indicates that the two activities cooperate. This can be represented as a conditional requirement rather than a claim that the two proteins directly bind each other. Skromne and Stern's work on the chick illustrates the developmental importance of this cooperation. To decide which signal acts upstream of another in a particular branch of the response, one would need a perturbation that bypasses the suspected upstream event and restores the downstream activity.

One further point is that a molecular inhibitor may play both a spatial and a temporal role. Early inhibitory signals restrict the possibility of initiation in inappropriate locations; subsequent signals can prevent competing initiation events after a streak has formed. The time when a tissue becomes refractory to an inductive graft is therefore informative, but it cannot be explained simply by assuming that all cells have irreversibly differentiated. A temporary inhibitory environment and a permanent loss of developmental potential can produce similar negative responses in a single test. Removing the inhibitory influence and reassessing competence helps distinguish the two possibilities.

4. Primitive streak: formation, direction and cell movement

The primitive streak is an elongated midline structure that appears in the posterior part of the epiblast and extends toward the anterior. Its extension is commonly accompanied by the convergence of cells toward the forming axis, together with coordinated movements within the epithelial sheet. It is a transient developmental structure rather than the future spinal cord or vertebral column. Its importance lies in establishing where cells enter deeper layers and how these cells are distributed along the emerging body axis.

At the fully extended streak, Hensen's node occupies its anterior end. A depression at the node is called the primitive pit, while a longitudinal depression along much of the streak is called the primitive groove. These names describe morphology, not the developmental identities of all cells nearby. A dorsal diagram often places the node at the top, the streak extending toward the bottom and the prospective anterior region beyond the node. The same embryo can be drawn in a different orientation, so a student should locate its anterior end using labeled anatomical features instead of assuming that the top of every figure is anterior.

Epithelial rearrangement and ingression

Primitive streak formation and ingression can be separated conceptually. Before ingression, epithelial cells retain an arrangement with junctions and polarity. To move into a deeper tissue, many cells alter their adhesions and cytoskeletal organization, cross the basal boundary of the epithelium and adopt motile characteristics. This transition is an example of an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, although the precise sequence and extent vary among cell populations. The ability to migrate depends on intracellular forces and interactions with the extracellular matrix. Actin dynamics and ATP-dependent myosin activity can change cell shape, while adhesions provide the traction necessary for movement.

The molecular signals coordinating these changes include FGF signaling and transcriptional regulators of adhesion. In developmental terms, however, it is crucial to distinguish specification of mesoderm from physical departure of mesodermal precursors from the epiblast. A cell can express an early mesodermal marker yet remain in an abnormal location if its migratory machinery is defective. The reverse problem can also occur: a cell may move but adopt an inappropriate identity because the inductive signal that normally specifies its fate has been disrupted. A useful experimental design therefore combines position and molecular identity as separate readouts.

Cells enter the embryo at different regions and at different times along the streak. Early cells entering near the anterior streak and node contribute to axial and anterior mesendodermal structures; other streak territories contribute to paraxial, intermediate and lateral mesoderm. This general map is not a rigid table of destinations valid for every stage. The streak changes shape, cells move into and through it, and the fate of cells at a particular position can change over time. A fate map is an observation of the normal descendants of cells at a specified stage, not necessarily a statement about an irreversible restriction of their developmental potential.

Brachyury is a transcription factor associated with mesodermal development and expressed in the streak and later axial territories. Detecting Brachyury helps identify cells involved in mesodermal development, but expression of the marker alone does not prove that every positive cell will become notochord. Different tissues share aspects of their transcriptional programs, and a marker can be expressed transiently. For a mechanistic experiment, combine a marker with the position of the labeled cells and observations of their subsequent differentiation. This avoids treating a map of messenger RNA abundance as a complete lineage map.

A streak is often drawn as if it were a narrow line inserted into a previously static sheet. The living tissue is more dynamic. Before the definitive streak is visible, cells undergo coordinated movements within the epiblast. Their trajectories depend on the behavior of surrounding cells, so a large-scale pattern of movement can emerge from changes in junctions and the forces generated locally. As cells approach the midline, the tissue may thicken and become more densely organized. An observer comparing two fixed specimens cannot reliably identify whether a thickened region resulted from proliferation, local accumulation of migrating cells or a change in cell shape. Direct observation of cell trajectories provides the relevant evidence.

An epithelial cell maintains contacts with adjacent cells through adhesion complexes that connect to its cortical cytoskeleton. During ingression, changes in these contacts allow cells to separate from the surface sheet. The basal extracellular matrix must also permit passage. Once below the sheet, mesenchymal cells can extend processes and move relative to nearby cells and matrix. The term epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition summarizes this combination of altered polarity, adhesion and motility. It should not imply that every cell instantaneously switches from one perfectly defined state to another. Cells can pass through intermediate arrangements, and those intermediates are precisely what a good mechanistic explanation should acknowledge.

Mechanical force is necessary because a cell does not move merely by changing the expression of a fate marker. Actin polymerization can contribute to protrusion, and the ATP hydrolysis cycle of myosin converts chemical energy into mechanical work. Adhesions transmit forces across cell boundaries and between cells and extracellular matrix. The direction in which a population moves depends on the balance of local forces and constraints. For example, a cell exerting traction against a substrate may move its body in one direction while exerting an opposing force on the substrate. The developmental significance is that a change in tissue geometry can be generated by active cellular processes rather than by passive displacement alone.

There is no requirement for the epiblast to behave as a uniform solid. Cells can rearrange their relative positions, and the sheet can deform in a way that changes its outline without breaking apart everywhere. At the streak, ingression changes the topology by removing individual cells from the epithelial arrangement. These two kinds of movement are complementary. Convergence supplies cells to a region where ingression occurs, and ingression transfers the cells into the internal compartments. If ingression were normal but convergence failed, the streak might receive too few cells. If convergence were normal but ingression failed, cells might accumulate at the midline without building appropriate internal tissues.

This distinction suggests a useful imaging experiment. Label cells in a small lateral epiblast territory, observe their movement toward the streak, and then determine whether they cross into the deeper tissue. A perturbation that prevents labeled cells from reaching the streak would affect the first part of the trajectory; a perturbation that allows arrival but prevents departure would affect the second. In a separate assay, examine mesodermal gene expression. An absence of labeled cells below the epiblast is not enough to conclude that mesodermal identity was never specified. A marker analysis provides the additional information needed to distinguish altered pattern from altered movement.

The primitive streak provides a spatial sorting mechanism because the entry position and the developmental time influence the destination of cells. For example, prospective axial cells and prospective lateral plate cells do not normally follow identical trajectories from the same entry domain at the same moment. Their differences can reflect signaling history as well as the physical routes available after ingression. The correct interpretation of a fate map must therefore specify the stage at which labeling occurred. A cell labeled near the anterior end of an early streak and one labeled at the same apparent position later may have different descendants. The location of the streak itself changes relative to the embryo, and the cell populations occupying it also change.

Directional cell flow and planar polarity

The term polonaise movements describes a striking pattern observed in the chick epiblast before and during early streak formation: two broad counter-rotating cell flows meet in the posterior midline region. The trajectories help deliver cells into the forming axis. They can be visualized by following labeled cells in living embryos, rather than inferred from the distribution of cells in a single preserved specimen. Such tissue-level flows and the local processes that shape the streak are related, but one should not assume that all changes in the streak are explained by a single movement pattern.

Planar cell polarity refers to organization of cells within the plane of an epithelium, distinct from the apical–basal polarity perpendicular to that plane. This distinction is useful because elongating a streak requires directionally biased rearrangement of cells in the sheet. In the chick, work by Voiculescu and colleagues connected FGF signaling from underlying tissues with the expression of components of Wnt-associated planar polarity pathways in the region of intercalation. Experimental studies also indicate that cell division, cell shape changes and ingression contribute to the complex flow and deformation of the epiblast. The broad examination-level conclusion is that oriented rearrangement is essential to understanding streak morphogenesis; it is not appropriate to claim that one molecular pathway uniquely accounts for every observed cell trajectory.

A concrete way to interpret intercalation is to imagine a patch initially two rows deep and six columns wide. If some cells exchange positions with their neighbors in an ordered manner, the patch may become narrower and extend in the perpendicular direction without a corresponding increase in the total number of cells. In actual tissue the cell arrangement is not a perfect rectangular lattice, but the example shows why mediolateral intercalation can contribute to longitudinal extension. A measurement of cell number alone would miss this mechanism. An analysis of cell neighbor relationships before and after movement is more directly informative.

The contrast between the two polarity systems also helps distinguish phenotypes. Disruption of epithelial apical–basal organization may impair the ability of cells to maintain a coherent sheet or to leave it in a controlled way. Disruption of planar organization can alter the orientation of rearrangements while cells remain epithelial. Although these processes interact, they are not the same. A mutant or inhibitor that changes the shape of the streak without completely abolishing mesodermal specification may therefore affect tissue mechanics rather than the earliest induction of mesodermal identity.

Tissue movement can be quantified without importing specialist mathematical models. If an individual cell travels 120 micrometers over thirty minutes, its mean speed along the measured trajectory is four micrometers per minute. If its net displacement is only sixty micrometers because the path curves, the magnitude of its average velocity is two micrometers per minute. Mean speed and average velocity are different quantities. In a rotating epiblast flow, a cell can travel a substantial distance while its net displacement is modest. Thus a straight arrow between its initial and final positions does not accurately describe every feature of its path. This is relevant when a developmental phenotype is reported as altered movement speed rather than altered destination.

5. Hensen's node: organizing capacity and evidence

The node is a specialized territory at the anterior end of the fully extended streak. It is a source of axial mesendoderm and participates in the organization of the surrounding embryo. The central developmental question is not whether the node possesses a recognizable shape, but whether it has the ability to instruct other embryonic cells. A region can become a normal tissue without having this capacity, so descriptive observation alone cannot establish its organizer identity.

The classical logic of the organizer experiment was established in amphibians by Spemann and Mangold and was extended to avian development by Waddington. The relevant experimental principle is heterotopic transplantation: tissue from one embryonic position is placed at a different position, and the response of neighboring host cells is followed. A successful graft of organizer tissue can elicit organized axial structures at the new location. Donor cells contribute to some axial tissues, whereas the surrounding host can supply induced neural and other tissues. Therefore, a new axis is evidence of coordinated interaction rather than merely the survival and enlargement of a transplanted fragment.

To distinguish these explanations, a modern experiment can employ genetically or fluorescently distinguishable donor and recipient cells. Quail–chick chimeras historically provided a valuable way to follow donor contributions through recognizable nuclear features. In a conceptual graft experiment, suppose that a donor node gives rise to a central notochord-like region while much of the ectopic neural tissue is host derived. The donor contribution demonstrates the ability of the node to produce axial mesoderm, and the host contribution demonstrates induction. Neither conclusion follows from the appearance of a second ridge alone. A ridge could reflect injury, crowding or an incompletely organized response.

Graft controls and neural induction

The appropriate comparison is not simply a graft versus an untouched embryo. Transplanting a matched nonorganizer tissue controls for tissue insertion and wound healing. A sham operation controls for the physical manipulation, and the developmental stage of the host must be recorded because competence changes with time. If the donor is viable but the host has passed the responsive period, an absence of induction does not establish that the donor lacks organizer activity. Conversely, if a donor dies after transplantation, absence of induction cannot identify the particular signal that would normally have acted.

The organizer influences neural specification in part through secreted factors, including Chordin and Noggin, which antagonize BMP activity. BMP signaling normally participates in non-neural ectodermal and other patterning programs, depending on stage and context. Its reduction in a competent region can favor neural development, but in the chick neural induction requires coordinated inputs rather than a universal single-step BMP-off switch. FGF signaling, timing and the prior state of the ectoderm contribute to the response. The distinction between a signal being necessary in a tested setting and being sufficient in isolation is especially important for interpreting neural induction experiments.

A comparison with the amphibian dorsal lip reveals an important evolutionary principle. Both the chick node and the amphibian organizer have axial mesodermal contributions and instructive activity toward neighboring tissue. Their anatomical settings are different: the amphibian organizer is associated with the dorsal blastopore lip, whereas the chick organizer is associated with the anterior end of an elongated streak. The similarity lies in developmental function and conserved interactions, not identical geometry. Likewise, posterior marginal zone induction in the chick has a functional resemblance to the early inductive role of the amphibian Nieuwkoop center, but this does not mean that their lineages, location or molecular regulation are precisely equivalent.

Necessity, sufficiency and comparative evidence

The organizer concept becomes more precise when separated into three kinds of experimental evidence. A tissue's normal contribution can be investigated by fate mapping, its capacity to induce can be investigated by transplantation, and its requirement for a process can be investigated by removal or disruption. These are not interchangeable experiments. The observation that a node graft produces an ectopic axis demonstrates a form of sufficiency under the conditions of the graft; it does not by itself show that no other embryonic population could provide equivalent inductive activity. Conversely, a removal experiment that produces a defect may reflect loss of several cell types and signals at once.

The avian system is particularly well suited to these distinctions because its tissues can be followed in both planar and sectional views. Suppose an ectopic node graft produces a patterned region containing neural cells, a central axial structure and paired mesodermal blocks. A student should first ask whether the structures are identified anatomically or molecularly and whether their lineages have been traced. Only then can the outcome be interpreted as evidence for organizer activity. A secondary axis that consists of a collection of donor cells with no ordered host response does not provide the same evidence as a graft that produces an integrated combination of donor and host tissues.

An organizer is not expected to maintain the same instructive properties throughout development. The node is a changing population of cells in an embryo undergoing extensive morphogenesis. A graft taken at an early stage may contain populations with different normal fates and signaling activities from a graft taken at a later stage. Its ability to induce anterior versus more posterior neural identities can also depend on the stage and context of the responding tissue. Therefore, the statement that the node induces neural development should not be expanded into the claim that any node tissue, transplanted anywhere and at any time, will induce a complete head and trunk. Such a claim is neither required by the organizer concept nor justified by the classical graft logic.

To study which organizer signals are necessary, a researcher may selectively reduce a candidate activity while preserving the viability of donor tissue. If the host neural response is lost, a requirement for that activity is supported. The conclusion becomes stronger if an independent way of restoring the activity rescues the response. If no defect occurs after disrupting one candidate, there are several possibilities: the candidate may not be needed under these conditions, another factor may compensate, or the perturbation may not have eliminated the relevant activity. Negative results are informative only when the effectiveness of the manipulation is demonstrated.

The relationship between BMP and neural induction provides a good illustration. Secreted antagonists can bind or interfere with BMP ligands and reduce effective receptor activation. The induced neural response nevertheless depends on the history and competence of the responding epiblast. In an experimental setting where prior signals have established responsiveness, BMP antagonism may be essential for maintaining a neural program. In a different tissue lacking the earlier inputs, inhibiting BMP may fail to initiate that program. Thus apparently different experimental conclusions can arise from the use of different stages or different definitions of the response. A neural marker detected soon after treatment and a fully organized neural epithelium observed later are not equivalent outcomes.

There is an additional distinction between organizing a tissue and assigning a regional identity within it. The induction of neural ectoderm establishes a broad tissue program; later signals distinguish regions along the anterior–posterior and dorsal–ventral directions. The organizer and its derivatives participate in this broader process, but they do not encode every detail of the future nervous system in a single initial signal. This is why the present chapter treats neural induction as evidence of organizer activity and leaves the detailed specification of neuronal classes to the later chapter. The distinction keeps the causal chain clear: first establish a body axis and relevant tissue relationships, then explain how those tissues acquire their finer identities.

The value of classical organizer transplantation can be extended through reciprocal tissue combinations. A prospective responding ectoderm can be isolated from the influence of an axial signal and assessed for its developmental tendency; in a parallel condition it is placed next to organizer tissue. If a neural response occurs preferentially in the combination, the evidence supports an inductive interaction. The isolated tissue is a control for its intrinsic specification at the stage of the experiment. If the isolated tissue also becomes neural, it may already have received signals before separation. The experiment would then need to use a younger population or a more precise definition of the developmental response.

A related logic applies to ablation and rescue. Removing a node region can interfere with normal axis development, but the operation may simultaneously alter cell supply, signaling and tissue mechanics. Restoring a candidate molecular signal without replacing the removed tissue may recover one aspect of the phenotype but not the complete axis. Such a partial rescue is valuable because it reveals which component of organizer function can be attributed to that signal. It is not a failure of the experiment merely because the entire embryo is not restored; the outcome must be interpreted in relation to the question being asked.

The amphibian and avian comparisons become stronger if one identifies parallel functional tests. In the classic amphibian graft, a donor dorsal lip was associated with an induced secondary axis in host territory. In avian organizer studies, a tissue from the leading streak region could produce an analogous inductive effect under appropriate conditions. In both cases it is the organized response of neighboring cells that justifies the organizer concept. The precise pattern of donor contribution and the full spectrum of induced tissues depend on donor stage and host context. A superficial matching exercise that equates an anatomical groove with a blastopore would miss this experimental basis for comparison.

The posterior marginal zone and Hensen's node can be placed in the same causal sequence without conflating their properties. A marginal-zone transplant that elicits a largely host-derived new streak demonstrates the ability to induce the emergence of an organizer-related structure. A node transplant that induces surrounding tissues while contributing axial cells demonstrates the later organizer activity. These are sequentially related developmental operations. The first helps establish where the axis-organizing region will form, while the second helps pattern tissues around the emerging axis. A question asking which region corresponds functionally to the amphibian Nieuwkoop center is therefore different from one asking which region corresponds functionally to the amphibian dorsal lip.

It is also useful to identify the limits of the analogy with Drosophila. The fly embryo uses maternal and zygotic patterning programs to establish positional information, but it does not form an avian primitive streak or vertebrate organizer. In the chick, extensive movement of cells accompanies the construction of an axial tissue that influences its neighbors. Both organisms demonstrate the importance of position-dependent gene regulation and interactions among developmental territories, but their cell organization and morphogenetic strategies differ. The comparison should be used to reveal general principles rather than to search for a one-to-one equivalent of every embryonic structure.

6. Regression of the node and construction of the notochord

The primitive streak initially extends toward the prospective head region, but it subsequently regresses toward the posterior as the axis elongates. This is not a reversal of the entire developmental program. While the posterior growth region continues to supply new tissues, earlier axial structures remain anterior to it and differentiate. The relative position of the node changes because several tissue movements and growth processes act together. A sequence of fixed images can reveal the changing location of the node, but cell tracing is needed to determine which cells move with it and which leave it.

Axial tissues and notochord signals

Axial mesoderm extending from the node forms the notochord and related anterior midline structures in the appropriate temporal sequence. The notochord is a rod-like midline structure with mechanical and signaling roles. It establishes a central reference for the developing vertebrate trunk and provides signals that influence the adjacent neural tube and paraxial mesoderm. It should not be confused with the neural tube, which forms from ectoderm above it, or with the future vertebral bodies, which arise mainly from sclerotome derived from somites.

As the body becomes longer, convergence and extension rearrange tissues. Convergence narrows the tissue mediolaterally; extension increases its longitudinal dimension. Intercalation of cells can produce both changes even without a proportional increase in cell number. This is a geometrical explanation for why shape change is not identical to growth. A labeled rectangular group of cells may become narrower and longer while retaining approximately the same area if the thickness is stable. An increase in length alone therefore does not demonstrate cell proliferation.

A simple measurement can distinguish two possible explanations for axial elongation. Record the length, width and cell density of a marked tissue region at the beginning and end of an observation period. If the length doubles while width halves and density remains similar, the observations are compatible with rearrangement at approximately constant cell number in a planar region. If both length and width increase at similar density, proliferation or recruitment becomes more plausible. Neither conclusion is definitive unless the experiment also accounts for thickness, cell loss and movement into or out of the marked area.

The notochord secretes Sonic hedgehog, which acts on neighboring tissues. In the adjacent neural tube, signals from the notochord and subsequently the floor plate participate in ventral patterning. In the somite, Shh helps specify ventromedial sclerotome. This creates a useful link between the construction of an anatomical axis and its signaling function. A notochord defect can disrupt the development of tissue that is not itself derived from the node. The defect may therefore be non-cell-autonomous. Detailed neural cell identities are treated separately in the neuronal differentiation chapter.

Dynamic cell populations and axial growth

A fate map of the node is not a map of a stationary anatomical object. It records the descendants of cells located in a changing structure at a specified stage. During axial elongation, some cells leave the node or anterior streak and become incorporated into axial tissues, while other cells participate in continued posterior development. Consequently, a label introduced into the node can generate a distribution that extends along the embryonic axis. The resulting pattern depends on when the label was introduced, which cells were labeled and how long the embryo developed before it was examined.

Anterior axial mesendoderm and the trunk notochord are related but should not be treated as identical territories. Cells emerging from early organizer regions contribute to anterior midline structures, including prechordal mesendoderm, while later contributions are important for the elongating notochord. Prechordal tissue is associated with the region beneath the developing forebrain and participates in its patterning. The notochord lies along the developing trunk axis. This temporal change in the nature of axial contributions helps explain why early and late organizer grafts need not produce the same spectrum of induced structures.

A transverse section through the developing trunk reveals the importance of tissue adjacency. The neural tube is dorsal, the notochord lies ventral to it, the somites are paired to either side, and the endoderm occupies a more ventral position. The notochord is therefore well placed to influence both neural and mesodermal populations. It does not physically turn into all of the neighboring tissues that respond to its signals. When a gene necessary for axial mesoderm formation is disrupted, several surrounding structures can become abnormal because their inductive environment has changed. The resulting multi-tissue phenotype is not evidence that those structures share an identical lineage.

The distinction between a source and a target of a signal is especially useful for interpreting mosaic experiments. If a signaling defect is restricted to notochord cells, adjacent otherwise normal neural or somitic cells may develop abnormally. This is a non-cell-autonomous consequence, because the genotype of the target tissue need not be altered for its phenotype to change. If the defect instead prevents receptor function only in a subset of responding somite cells, those cells may fail to respond despite a normal signal source. Comparing the two mosaics separates a failure of signal production from a failure of reception.

The mechanical and signaling roles of the notochord should also be distinguished. In an early embryo, the midline architecture provides structural organization and a physical reference for neighboring tissue movements. At the same time, molecules released by axial cells influence gene expression in surrounding populations. A manipulation that removes the notochord eliminates both influences and may also disturb the geometry of the embryo. An experiment using a localized signal source in the absence of an intact notochord may restore some molecular responses without restoring the complete physical architecture. Rescue of a molecular marker is therefore not equivalent to rescue of normal body shape.

Axial elongation reflects the relationship between the supply of new cells and the rearrangement of existing tissues. If a posterior population continues to generate paraxial mesoderm while previously generated tissue becomes segmented, the total length of the patterned region increases. Its final form depends on how quickly cells are supplied, how they move and how they acquire boundaries. A reduction in posterior cell supply could shorten the embryo even if individual somite formation events occur at normal intervals. A defect in convergence and extension could alter proportions without directly affecting the segmentation oscillator. This separation of causal processes becomes important when comparing embryos with superficially similar short-axis phenotypes.

A careful interpretation of axial tissues also requires a distinction between the notochord and the head process visible at early stages. A head process extending anteriorly from the node is an early axial structure; its presence is one of the features used to stage chick development. The anterior axial tissues subsequently differentiate and influence the developing head, whereas the elongated trunk notochord is associated with the future vertebral axis. A morphological staging description of a head process is not a claim that the whole adult head derives from it. The neighboring ectoderm and mesoderm contribute many structures in response to additional developmental interactions.

When the head fold becomes visible, the previously planar embryonic field is beginning to acquire more complex three-dimensional relationships. Folding changes the positions of tissue layers and their contacts. A section through the head at this time cannot be interpreted using the same simple flat geometry that describes the pre-streak blastoderm. This is another reason to specify the plane of section, orientation and stage when discussing a developmental image. A structure that appears anterior to another in one projection may occupy a different relative position when viewed in a transverse section.

The distinction between axial mesoderm and the axial skeleton is important because they are causally connected yet lineage-distinct. The notochord establishes a central signaling environment around which sclerotomal cells organize. Sclerotomal populations contribute to the vertebral column, but their cells descend from somites, not from the notochord. In a fate-mapping problem, labeling the notochord would therefore not be expected to label all vertebral skeletal cells. A notochord perturbation can nevertheless alter their development through changes in the local signals and structural organization. This is a clear example of why lineage and developmental dependence must be assessed independently.

The posterior end of the elongating embryo supplies cells that continue to contribute to axial and paraxial tissues. At this level of explanation, the tail bud is the later posterior growth region rather than a miniature finished tail. Its role in continuing axial development does not imply that all cells of the regressing streak have the same fate or remain indefinitely undifferentiated. The formation of posterior structures depends on a balance between continued production of cells, their exit into appropriate tissues and the progressive maturation of those tissues. A phenotype with a normal anterior region but truncated posterior structures is compatible with a failure of continued axial extension after initial polarity was established.

7. Somitogenesis: segmentation in space and time

On either side of the notochord and neural axis lies paraxial mesoderm. Newly generated posterior paraxial mesoderm initially lacks obvious transverse boundaries and is called presomitic mesoderm. As development proceeds, paired blocks called somites form at its anterior end. The newly formed blocks are relatively anterior to the remaining unsegmented tissue but posterior to older somites. The sequence proceeds from head toward tail: the segmentation front advances posteriorly in relation to established anterior body structures.

A somite is not merely a cluster of mesodermal cells. It is a patterned unit with organized boundaries and an epithelial arrangement that is subsequently remodeled. Somites ultimately contribute to skeletal muscle, the axial skeleton and aspects of the dermis. The ventromedial portion becomes sclerotome in response to signals including Shh, while dorsal portions form the dermomyotome, with dermal and muscle-forming populations. These fate choices depend on signals from neighboring structures, including the neural tube, notochord and surface ectoderm. An isolated somite may therefore differentiate differently from one that retains its normal surroundings.

Clock and wavefront

Segmentation requires a way to repeat a pattern at regular intervals while the tissue grows. The clock-and-wavefront model, originally proposed by Cooke and Zeeman, separates these requirements into a temporal oscillator and a spatial boundary of competence or maturation. The clock provides recurring molecular states in presomitic cells. The wavefront defines where those states can be converted into a stable segment boundary. Both are necessary to explain regular segment spacing in a lengthening tissue: a clock alone has no fixed place to produce a boundary, while a maturation boundary alone supplies no repeated timing.

Palmeirim and colleagues demonstrated in 1997 that the chick gene c-hairy1 displays rhythmic expression in presomitic mesoderm. A cycle of this expression is associated with the time required for formation of a somite pair, approximately ninety minutes under the conditions studied. Spatial waves of messenger RNA expression are visible at successive stages. These waves should not be interpreted as physical waves of cells migrating bodily from posterior to anterior. Neighboring cells can change their transcriptional state at different phases, producing an apparent movement of a domain of expression even while their trajectories differ from that domain's trajectory.

Notch-mediated communication participates in coordination of segmentation oscillations and boundary formation. It helps neighboring cells maintain ordered relationships in the context of the oscillatory network. FGF and Wnt-related activity tends to maintain posterior immature presomitic characteristics, while more anterior tissue is in a different signaling environment. Retinoic acid is associated with anterior maturation in standard vertebrate models; its precise roles are context dependent. As tissue matures and relative signaling levels change, oscillatory information is translated into a stable spatial pattern.

Evidence from FGF manipulations in chick shows that the position of a prospective somite boundary can be shifted by changing FGF signaling. A region exposed to excessive posteriorizing FGF activity can retain an immature character in a place where segmentation would otherwise occur. This experiment distinguishes a maturation mechanism from a hypothesis that the position of every boundary is permanently fixed when cells first enter the mesoderm. The molecular clock and the moving maturation domain are coordinated, but a perturbation of one need not alter the period of the other in the same way.

A simplified quantitative model can make these distinctions precise. Let T denote the interval between successive boundary formation events in hours, and v the rate at which a maturation front advances relative to the already segmented axis in millimeters per hour. If v is approximately constant for an interval T, the distance traveled during one cycle is v multiplied by T. Thus the approximate axial length of a segment is L = vT. This is a kinematic approximation, not a biochemical law of oscillation. It assumes that the front speed is expressed in the chosen tissue reference frame, ignores subsequent compaction and assumes that the clock and maturation front remain coupled.

If the period increases while front velocity is unchanged, the model predicts larger spacing. If front velocity decreases at a constant period, it predicts smaller spacing. An experiment in which both change cannot be interpreted from somite size alone. For example, a twofold decrease in speed combined with a twofold increase in period would leave the predicted spacing unchanged despite altered underlying dynamics. Independent measurements of period and position are therefore required. Paired somites represent a left and a right block at each axial level; a count of ten pairs means ten segmented axial levels, not twenty.

Somitogenesis is a useful example of how a developing tissue combines timing with position. An early presomitic cell is not already enclosed within a visible somite. It participates in a tissue whose gene expression changes over time and whose maturation state depends on position. A periodic molecular program can create repeated opportunities for boundary formation, but those opportunities must be restricted to an appropriate region. If the same oscillator were converted into an epithelial boundary simultaneously throughout the entire posterior mesoderm, the embryo would not generate the orderly sequence of segments observed in normal development.

The clock-and-wavefront framework provides a way to understand this restriction without supposing that the embryo counts individual cells. The oscillator marks recurrent phases of a transcriptional program, while a maturation front moves relative to the tissue as the body elongates. Only cells in an appropriate region are competent to convert an oscillator phase into a stable boundary. Subsequent cells experience the equivalent combination of conditions after a further cycle. The model thereby links the periodicity of gene expression to the spatial periodicity of anatomy. It does not require every gene in the tissue to oscillate or every cell to have precisely the same instantaneous phase.

One must distinguish oscillator period from the propagation speed of an expression wave. Imagine several neighboring cells whose gene expression follows similar cycles but reaches its maximum at slightly different times. A map at one moment shows the maximum in one region, and a later map shows it farther anteriorly. The apparent wave is a consequence of the phase relationship among cells. Its speed can be different from the physical movement of those cells. To measure the period of an oscillator, repeated observations of the same cells or a carefully validated temporal comparison are needed. To measure the velocity of a maturation front, one follows the position of a molecular or anatomical maturation boundary in a defined reference frame.

An important feature of the chick segmentation evidence is that a molecular oscillation was linked to an anatomical rhythm. A spatially periodic pattern observed at a single time could be generated by a fixed arrangement of signaling regions. Repeated changes over the period associated with somite formation provide a different kind of evidence. Cultured pieces of presomitic tissue compared at different elapsed times help demonstrate the temporal progression of expression states. Such comparisons need controls for the developmental stage and condition of the explanted tissue, because a damaged tissue might show declining expression that superficially resembles part of an oscillation.

The maturation environment is likewise an experimentally testable concept. The posterior presomitic mesoderm expresses factors such as FGF8 associated with maintaining immature characteristics. Cells farther anteriorly experience a different effective signaling environment. Manipulating FGF activity in a restricted region can change where a future boundary becomes established. This is not the same intervention as changing the frequency of the underlying oscillator. When the resulting somites differ in size, an experiment should examine both the timing of successive boundary formation events and their position. If only the location changes, a spatial maturation effect is supported. If the interval changes, a temporal effect must also be considered.

The physical separation of a somite also requires a local morphological response. A prospective boundary marked by gene expression must be translated into differences in cell adhesion, shape and organization. Somite epithelialization is a cellular process that makes the newly formed unit distinguishable from the surrounding mesoderm. Failure of that process could leave periodic molecular domains without sharply defined anatomical blocks. Conversely, an unusual tissue fold could resemble a boundary without representing the normal molecular segmentation program. These alternatives motivate the combination of imaging and gene-expression markers in studies of somite development.

Once a somite forms, its cells acquire additional positional information. Ventromedial cells are exposed to signals from the notochord and the ventral neural tube, while dorsally placed cells encounter a different set of signals from neighboring ectoderm and neural tissue. The early epithelial somite is consequently remodeled into populations with different developmental functions. The sclerotome contributes to axial skeletal structures, whereas myogenic and dermal populations originate in the dorsal part. A somite is thus both a segmental unit and a field of internal cell differentiation. Knowing its number along the axis does not by itself reveal the identity of every cell within it.

Segment number and regional identity are also distinct. Two adjacent somites may both have normal boundaries but differ in positional identity along the body. Hox genes participate in regional specification of the vertebrate axis. The chick experiments that displaced segmentation boundaries while examining Hox expression connected aspects of axial identity to the segmentation process. The conceptual point is that a segmentation defect can change the placement of structural boundaries without necessarily altering every aspect of regional identity. Conversely, a defect in a regional identity gene could alter the developmental character of a segment without eliminating the rhythmic formation of segments. The corresponding distinction in Drosophila was developed in the previous segmentation chapter, although the mechanisms of somite formation are not the same as insect segmentation.

Boundary execution and tissue differentiation

The transition from a periodic molecular state to a morphological boundary can be understood more concretely through the chick transcription factor cMeso-1, related to mammalian Mesp2. Expression of this factor in a restricted region of the anterior presomitic mesoderm is associated with prospective segmentation boundaries. Work on chick somite development has linked cMeso-1 activity to expression of the receptor EphA4 near a forming boundary and to interaction with ephrin molecules on adjacent cells. Eph receptors and ephrin ligands are cell-associated signaling partners, making them particularly suitable for generating local differences at an interface between two populations. Their function in this context helps explain why a boundary can form at a specific cell–cell interface rather than across the whole presomitic tissue.

An examination question may ask what happens if a periodic molecular pattern remains intact but Eph–ephrin-mediated boundary formation is disrupted. A plausible prediction is that the embryo could retain some periodic expression domains while failing to form normal physical clefts between somites. This is a different phenotype from an oscillator that ceases to cycle or from a maturation front that is displaced. The precise outcome of a real mutation depends on the gene, developmental stage and possible compensation, but the conceptual separation is well founded. The pathway links a temporal pattern to a spatial arrangement and then to a physical boundary.

The earliest formed somite consists of cells organized around an internal space or core, with an epithelial arrangement evident in its outer region. As the somite matures, the ventral region loses epithelial organization and produces migrating sclerotomal cells. These cells move toward the central axial structures and participate in skeletal development. Dorsal cells retain epithelial organization for a time as the dermomyotome. Myogenic cells subsequently contribute to the skeletal musculature, while other populations contribute to dermal tissues. The fact that an epithelial structure can later give rise to migratory populations illustrates that epithelialization during segmentation is not an irreversible endpoint.

Signals from the dorsal neural tube and surface ectoderm, including Wnt-associated activity, contribute to dorsal somitic patterning. Signals from the ventral axial region, notably Shh, contribute to ventral sclerotomal specification. The somite thus interprets information from its surrounding structures in the dorsal–ventral direction after it has acquired its position along the anterior–posterior axis. A perturbation that changes the relative exposure to these signals could alter the balance of somitic derivatives without necessarily changing the initial number of segments. A developmental structure can have multiple independently regulated coordinates.

The distinction between local pattern and whole-embryo growth can be tested with an explant. Consider paraxial mesoderm isolated at a defined stage, with one group cultured adjacent to an axial signal source and another without that source. If the first group shows stronger expression of a sclerotomal marker, the result supports an inductive contribution from the axial environment. It does not establish that the signal source is sufficient for every skeletal feature. Additional conditions may be necessary for migration, cartilage formation and later bone development. The most appropriate interpretation stays at the developmental level directly measured by the experiment.

Left and right somites also illustrate the need for coordination. In a normal chick, a new pair of blocks forms at approximately the same axial level on opposite sides. Each side contains cells capable of participating in an oscillatory program, so bilateral organization requires their temporal and spatial progressions to remain suitably aligned. A difference in the maturation environment on one side could shift the position of a boundary there without changing the period measured on the opposite side. Thus an asymmetric pair is not necessarily evidence that a single organism-wide oscillator has stopped. Comparing local molecular phase, boundary position and timing on both sides helps distinguish a coordination defect from a general arrest of segmentation.

8. Left–right information and the midline

The external body plan of a young vertebrate appears nearly bilaterally symmetric, yet the disposition of organs later becomes consistently asymmetric. The symmetry-breaking problem is to convert left and right from geometrically equivalent directions into distinguishable molecular environments. This process takes place within the coordinate system already established by the anterior–posterior and dorsal–ventral axes. Leftness is not a simple property of the observer's left side of a diagram, because changing the viewing direction reverses the apparent sides.

Asymmetric molecular signals

Hensen's node is a critical region in early chick left–right patterning. Studies of gene expression revealed that Sonic hedgehog, initially more broadly expressed at the node, becomes left-biased at an appropriate stage, and that Nodal-related expression develops in the left lateral plate mesoderm. The asymmetric signaling program eventually includes Pitx2, a transcription factor associated with left-sided identity in several developing organs. These signals represent stages in a developmental cascade, not three interchangeable markers. Expression of the upstream signal near the node and of a downstream factor in lateral mesoderm are spatially and functionally distinct observations.

Work by Levin and colleagues identified asymmetric molecular relationships through expression mapping and perturbation. Subsequent live-imaging work by Gros and colleagues demonstrated asymmetric cell movements around the chick node and showed their importance for establishing the asymmetric expression patterns. This is a significant species-specific distinction from a common account of the mouse embryo in which motile cilia produce a directional fluid flow in the node region. A conserved downstream left-sided signaling program does not require an identical upstream symmetry-breaking mechanism in every vertebrate.

The developing midline also helps prevent left-sided developmental information from spreading inappropriately across the axis. A defect in midline structure or signaling may consequently generate bilateral or disorganized expression in a lateral plate marker. However, an embryo with bilateral Nodal expression cannot be assumed to possess two complete left organs. Different organs respond to the signaling pathway at different times and through distinct local morphogenetic programs. Similarly, reversal of one organ does not prove that every internal organ is reversed. Situs describes the arrangement of organs, and heterotaxy refers to abnormal or discordant arrangements.

An examination question may report loss of left-sided Nodal in an embryo with an apparently normal number of somites. This observation supports disruption of the left–right patterning program without demonstrating that the segmentation clock has failed. Conversely, asymmetric somite sizes could indicate disruption of bilateral segmentation coordination without necessarily proving reversal of organ laterality. The two mechanisms share an embryonic environment and can interact indirectly, but their principal readouts differ. A strong mechanistic interpretation distinguishes temporal segmentation, spatial midline formation and the induction of lateralized organ identity.

Physical symmetry breaking and experimental tests

The distinction between an initial symmetry-breaking event and a later patterning signal deserves special attention. At the beginning of a left–right pathway, a small difference between two otherwise similar sides must be generated or revealed. Later events can amplify that difference and transmit it to distant tissues. A gene that is expressed only on the left at a later stage may be crucial for organ development without being the earliest source of the left–right difference. Identifying an asymmetric marker therefore establishes a particular stage of the pathway but does not automatically establish the origin of asymmetry.

The asymmetric movements described at the chick node provide a mechanism for converting initially more symmetric patterns into spatially different domains. Cell movements can redistribute cells that express a particular signal, bringing them into new relationships with neighboring tissues. This process links mechanics and gene expression. It is different in principle from an explanation based on cilia-driven directional fluid movement within a specialized cavity. The comparison between chick and mouse is valuable because it shows that similar downstream developmental outcomes can arise from different upstream mechanisms. Evolutionary conservation of the output of a regulatory pathway does not require complete conservation of its initial physical implementation.

A test of whether cell movement is necessary must distinguish a failure of symmetry breaking from a general failure of development. Suppose a perturbation that disrupts cytoskeletal movement also prevents the primitive streak from forming. The laterality phenotype would then be hard to interpret because the tissue responsible for transmitting left–right information is itself missing. A more informative experiment would preserve recognizable stage-appropriate node and streak structures while selectively disrupting the relevant asymmetric movement and examining left–right gene expression. Time-lapse images help establish that the cell movements are altered before the molecular asymmetry fails. The result can support a causal relationship without proving that cytoskeletal activity is the sole determinant of laterality.

The information transmitted from the node does not simply diffuse across the whole embryo until every cell has the same identity. Lateral plate mesoderm constitutes a responding territory, and the developing midline helps maintain separation between the two sides. The response of the left lateral plate is shaped by both upstream signals and the competence of its cells. Pitx2, as a downstream transcription factor, contributes to the interpretation of laterality information in developing organs. The distinction between early signal, responding territory and downstream transcriptional program makes it possible to interpret defects at different levels of the cascade.

A useful experiment would compare three readouts in the same staged embryo: asymmetry of an early node-associated signal, expression of Nodal-related genes in lateral plate mesoderm and expression of a later target such as Pitx2. If the node-associated asymmetry remains but the lateral plate response is absent, the defect could involve transmission or reception of information. If the lateral plate response is normal but Pitx2 expression is abnormal, the defect may lie farther downstream. If all three readouts lose their asymmetry, an upstream defect becomes plausible. These observations locate a likely level of failure, but a selective rescue or an independent perturbation is needed to establish a specific causal molecule.

The assessment of organ laterality occurs later than these early molecular events. It should therefore be related to the molecular state without being equated with it. An altered left–right gene expression pattern is a developmental warning that can change the probabilities of particular organ arrangements. It is not a deterministic statement about the complete anatomy of every embryo. Even within one organism, organs may have different sensitivities and developmental windows. This explains why the strongest conclusions often come from comparing a series of molecular markers and subsequent morphological outcomes in a population, rather than treating one marker as a complete representation of laterality.

The earliest molecular asymmetries in chick have been studied by comparing the expression of genes on both sides of the node and by altering the distribution of signaling activities. The early classical studies placed Shh and Nodal-related activity in a sequence leading toward left-sided development. Later studies of tissue orientation and cell trajectories refined this model by distinguishing the location where asymmetric expression is first observed from the origin of the positional information. This history illustrates an important feature of scientific explanation: a pathway can be correctly identified at one level while the physical mechanism that establishes its initial asymmetry remains to be resolved.

An embryo with a reversed left–right expression pattern has not necessarily lost anterior–posterior polarity. The two directions are related through the embryonic coordinate system but can be perturbed separately. If the head and tail develop in normal positions and somites are formed in a normal sequence, while left-sided molecular markers appear on the right, the evidence favors a specific laterality defect. In contrast, an embryo lacking a recognizable midline and possessing severely disrupted axial tissues does not supply a clean test of left–right patterning. Its lateralization phenotype might be secondary to loss of the coordinate system itself.

For a population-level examination problem, suppose a treatment produces fifty embryos with the normal lateral plate pattern, twenty-five with reversed expression, fifteen with expression on both sides and ten with no detectable expression. These four classes should be reported separately rather than combined into a single assertion of complete reversal. The overall abnormal fraction is one half, but the fraction with a reversed marker is one quarter. Bilateral and absent expression are different developmental outcomes. A comparison with a control group could reveal that the treatment reduces the reliability of laterality specification without establishing a consistent opposite direction. Such a distinction is essential when evaluating data about symmetry breaking.

Finally, the chick model offers an instructive transition from large embryonic fields to smaller organ-forming fields. In the chick, inductive interactions can alter the position of an entire body axis or the identity of one side of the embryo. In the next curriculum node, the nematode vulva provides an example in which the same principles of competence, induction and communication among adjacent cells generate a small, precisely ordered set of cell fates. The geometry and signals are different, but the experimental reasoning remains continuous: identify the source, identify the responder, change one interaction and measure which developmental outcome follows.

The practical importance of a stage-matched chick embryo model extends beyond description of normal development. Embryonic tissues are useful for examining the consequences of altered developmental signals because investigators can relate molecular changes to visible structures at defined stages. In developmental toxicology, for example, a general delay in growth must be distinguished from a specific effect on axis organization or organ position. A treatment applied before streak formation may have different consequences from one applied during somitogenesis, even if the exposure is otherwise similar. In poultry embryology, temperature and incubation conditions affect developmental timing and survival; morphological staging helps avoid interpreting all delayed development as a defect in axis patterning. These applications depend on the same principles of stage control, tissue viability and causal interpretation that underlie the classical organizer experiments. They do not imply that manipulating a single pathway is sufficient to correct an abnormal embryo or that results can be transferred unchanged to other species.

4. Consider statements: I. The primitive streak extends anteriorly before regression. II. The notochord is derived from ectoderm. III. Somites form from anterior PSM. IV. Left-sided Nodal is a segmentation clock gene. Which are correct?

  1. I and II
  2. II and IV
  3. III and IV
  4. I and III
Answer and reasoning

D. I and III are correct. The notochord is axial mesoderm, and Nodal's asymmetric lateral plate expression concerns laterality rather than the segmentation oscillator.

5. A diffusion-removal teaching model has D = 100 μm²/min, k = 0.04 min⁻¹. Which characteristic length follows from √(D/k)?

  1. 50 μm
  2. 2500 μm
  3. 4 μm
  4. 0.0004 μm
Answer and reasoning

A. D/k = 2500 μm²; its square root is 50 μm. The second answer omits the square root, while the other answers misapply the ratio or units. This is a generic model, not a measured chick value.

6. Which combination of readouts best distinguishes a failure of planar rearrangement from a failure of fate specification?

  1. Egg color and yolk mass
  2. Somite number after hatching only
  3. Cell trajectories, neighbor changes and a lineage-appropriate molecular marker
  4. One fixed photograph of the egg shell
Answer and reasoning

C. Movement and neighbor exchange report morphology; marker expression reports aspects of specification. The other readouts are not specific enough to distinguish the mechanisms.

7. A perturbation produces 12 normal, 6 right-sided, 4 bilateral and 2 absent lateral plate expression patterns. What fraction has specifically reversed expression?

  1. 50%
  2. 25%
  3. 75%
  4. 16.7%
Answer and reasoning

B. There are 24 embryos and 6 reversed, so 6/24 = 25%. Fifty percent is total abnormal; 16.7% is bilateral; 75% is not the reversed fraction.

8. Which experiment best tests whether axial Shh acts on adjacent somitic cells rather than being required only within the notochord?

  1. Count only cells in the yolk
  2. Remove the entire epiblast
  3. Change the temperature without measuring stage
  4. Compare normal and receptor-deficient target cells next to a normal axial source
Answer and reasoning

D. A target-specific receptor perturbation tests response to a normal source. The other manipulations fail to separate source function from target competence.

9. A developmental clock completes 5 cycles over 7.5 h. Under a constant-rate model with a speed of 0.10 mm/h, how far does the maturation front move per cycle?

  1. 0.75 mm
  2. 0.15 mm
  3. 1.5 mm
  4. 0.05 mm
Answer and reasoning

B. T = 7.5/5 = 1.5 h; displacement per cycle = 0.10 × 1.5 = 0.15 mm. 0.75 mm is total displacement, 1.5 is the period in hours, and 0.05 mm is an incorrect division.

10. Assertion: Similar downstream left-right signals in chick and mouse prove that the initiating physical mechanism is identical. Reason: Different species can conserve a downstream pathway while differing in upstream symmetry breaking.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and reasoning

C. Conservation of downstream signaling does not establish identical physical initiation. Chick perinodal cell movements and the mouse cilia-associated flow model illustrate the distinction.

11. In Figure 14, which prediction follows when the front speed decreases from 0.12 to 0.08 mm/h while the period remains 1.5 h?

  1. Spacing decreases from 0.18 to 0.12 mm.
  2. Spacing increases from 0.12 to 0.18 mm.
  3. Spacing remains 0.18 mm.
  4. The clock period must become 0.08 h.
Answer and reasoning

A. Read the two lines at the same horizontal coordinate. The faster front moves 0.18 mm per cycle and the slower one moves 0.12 mm. B reverses the comparison, C ignores velocity, and D confuses velocity with time.

12. In Figure 4, the third dorsal view shows a node at the anterior streak tip. If a viable labeled node graft is placed in a competent host and induces an extra axial structure, which observation most specifically demonstrates induction?

  1. The donor label remains detectable in a few donor cells.
  2. The graft grows without any change in surrounding host tissue.
  3. Unlabeled host cells form an organized neural region adjacent to labeled axial donor cells.
  4. The host yolk increases in volume.
Answer and reasoning

C. The lineage distinction identifies a patterned host response to the transplanted tissue. Donor survival alone (A), donor growth alone (B) and yolk volume (D) do not establish induction.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Streak equals spinal cord. The streak is a temporary ingression and patterning structure; the neural tube arises from ectoderm. A midline drawing can make the two look interchangeable. NET may ask the origin of the spinal cord.
  2. Hypoblast equals definitive endoderm. Epiblast-derived cells supply definitive embryonic endoderm; hypoblast is chiefly extraembryonic. Both occupy lower positions at different times. A lineage-label question tests ancestry rather than depth.
  3. Posterior marginal zone equals node. The marginal zone helps induce a streak and organizer; the node is at the anterior streak tip. Both have inductive functions. A graft question distinguishes organizer induction from organizer action.
  4. Node lies at the posterior streak end. The definitive node occupies the anterior tip. The streak begins posteriorly, leading to a reversal of orientation. Label anterior and posterior before interpreting diagrams.
  5. Chick node is the amphibian dorsal lip anatomically. The two have related organizing functions but different embryonic geometries. Comparative textbooks emphasize homology of function. A matching question may deliberately conflate structures.
  6. All secondary axis tissues come from donor. Induced host tissues can combine with donor axial cells. The graft is visible before the host response. A donor-label experiment tests the distinction.
  7. Expression marker equals fate map. A marker identifies current expression; a lineage tracer follows descendants. Both use colored tissue images. Part C may show a transient marker in several lineages.
  8. Specification necessarily causes immediate movement. Identity and morphogenesis can be uncoupled. Cells often change gene expression and move together. A marker-positive cell trapped in epiblast tests the separation.
  9. Streak regression reverses development. The streak shortens as established axial tissue remains and further tissue is added posteriorly. Regression sounds like loss of progress. Stage sequences ask which direction new tissue is added.
  10. Notochord becomes all vertebrae. Vertebral skeletal tissues originate mainly from sclerotome; axial signals influence them. Both lie centrally. A fate map versus ablation comparison distinguishes origin and dependence.
  11. Lateral plate makes somites. Somites originate from paraxial mesoderm. Both are mesodermal but have distinct positions. Transverse section questions test the distinction.
  12. c-hairy1 expression wave is a wave of cells. Changing phase can produce moving expression patterns without matching cell trajectories. Fixed images show moving-looking bands. Time-lapse lineage analysis tests physical movement.
  13. Clock alone fixes somite size. Spacing depends on timing and front movement; boundary remodeling adds complications. One cycle is associated with one pair. A numerical question changes period and velocity independently.
  14. One somite pair equals two axial levels. Two blocks at one level form a pair. Counts sometimes refer to blocks and sometimes pairs. Convert units before calculating.
  15. Chick uses the same initiating ciliary flow mechanism as mouse. Chick evidence supports asymmetric cell movements around Hensen's node. Downstream left-sided signals are conserved. Comparative questions may test upstream diversity.

Revision and active recall

Integrated high-yield revision

Posterior marginal signaling establishes a streak-forming domain in competent epiblast. Hypoblast antagonism restricts inappropriate induction. Coordinated epiblast movement and ingression construct the body axis; the definitive node organizes adjacent tissues and contributes axial mesendoderm. During regression, axial tissues extend while posterior development continues. Paired somites form at the anterior edge of PSM through coupling of a periodic clock, spatial maturation and boundary execution. Left–right information is transmitted from asymmetric perinodal activity to lateral plate and downstream transcriptional programs.

Essential relationships

  • Approximate somite spacing: L = vT, with a consistent reference frame and time unit.
  • Somite pairs = individual blocks / 2 when all blocks are paired.
  • Average speed = path length/time; magnitude of mean velocity = displacement/time.
  • For a simplified diffusion-removal model, characteristic length = √(D/k); do not confuse this model with measured chick signal ranges.
