1. Why chromosomes need more than nucleosomes

A human chromosome is not simply a compact version of a short DNA molecule. It is a very long, flexible molecular chain whose sequence must remain intact while its spatial arrangement changes repeatedly. The same chromosome that appears as a sharply defined rod during metaphase is much less easily distinguished from its neighbours by conventional staining during interphase. The DNA sequence has not been replaced. The physical organisation of that sequence has changed. Understanding how this happens requires separating three problems: reducing the extension of DNA, organising its position relative to other DNA segments, and keeping replicated copies identifiable as separate structures until they must be distributed to new cells.

The preceding chapter introduces the nucleosome, a particle in which DNA is wrapped around a histone octamer. This arrangement already reduces the spatial extension of DNA, but a chain of thousands of nucleosomes is still a long polymer. If every particle merely remained in a line, many chromosome-sized chains would compete for space in a nucleus. If they all collapsed into an immovable mass, enzymes would have difficulty reaching selected DNA sites and replicated chains could be difficult to separate. Higher-order organisation therefore has a dual purpose: it packs the genome and arranges the packed material in a mechanically useful way. It cannot be understood solely as making DNA smaller.

In this chapter a polymer means a long chain built from repeating structural units, and chromatin means the DNA with its associated proteins and other components. A chromosome is a physically organised genomic unit; in a typical unreplicated eukaryotic nuclear chromosome there is one continuous double-stranded DNA molecule. After replication there are two sister chromatids, each containing its own double-stranded DNA molecule. A condensed chromosome does not contain several independent DNA molecules merely because its surface seems granular or because several loops are visible. Following the continuous DNA contour is a useful way of preventing that mistake.

Packing can change at many scales. A local nucleosome pair can change orientation, a region can bend back on itself, distant sequences can come close, and an entire chromatid can become shorter and thicker. These changes are compatible with one another, but none automatically implies the others. An experimentally observed contact between two genes is not sufficient evidence that their whole chromosome has condensed. Similarly, a short metaphase chromosome need not have an identical local fiber structure along its entire length. The goal is to link molecular interactions to higher-order geometry without treating a diagram as a literal photograph.

2. DNA contour length and the meaning of compaction

The starting measurement is the contour length: the distance measured along the molecular path, as if an otherwise flexible DNA double helix were gently straightened without stretching its bonds. For ordinary B-form DNA, the mean rise along the helical axis is approximately 0.34 nanometres per base pair. If a molecule contains N base pairs, its contour length L is approximately N × 0.34 nm. This relationship comes from adding the axial rise contributed by each successive base-pair step. It assumes predominantly B-form DNA and neglects variations in local structure. One micrometre is 1,000 nm, and one metre is 1,000,000,000 nm.

Consider a chromosome with 150 million base pairs. Its naked DNA contour is approximately 150,000,000 × 0.34 nm = 51,000,000 nm = 51,000 µm = 5.1 cm. If its metaphase chromatid is 6 µm long along its axis, the ratio of DNA contour to chromatid axial length is 51,000/6 = 8,500. This is a linear packing ratio. It does not say that DNA is shortened chemically by 8,500 times. The contour remains present in folds, turns and loops. It also does not mean that the chromatid diameter is 8,500 times that of DNA or that chromatin occupies 1/8,500 of its original volume. Length, diameter, volume and molecular concentration answer different questions.

A human haploid nuclear genome of about 3.2 billion base pairs has a B-DNA contour near 1.09 m. A typical diploid G1 cell therefore has roughly 2.18 m of nuclear DNA, distributed across its chromosomes. In G2, after replication, it has approximately twice that amount. Those estimates depend on genome size and cell-cycle state, and exclude mitochondrial DNA. Dividing the entire 2.18 m by a nuclear diameter gives a striking scale comparison, but not a measured packing ratio of a single chromosome, because many chromosomes occupy different trajectories through the nucleus. A sensible quantitative answer must name both lengths being compared.

DNA concentration is another measure. If a defined volume contains a fixed number of base pairs, reducing that volume increases the number of base pairs per unit volume. Two domains of identical DNA content may have different local concentrations if one occupies less space. Conversely, an apparently smaller region can contain fewer base pairs rather than more densely packed DNA. Imaging intensity per volume is useful only when staining, optical conditions and DNA content are controlled. The later experimental questions will use this distinction to separate observations from inferences.

3. From a nucleosome to a connected chromatin array

A nucleosome core contains approximately 147 base pairs wrapped around two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. The DNA makes roughly 1.7 left-handed turns around the protein core. Adjacent particles are connected by linker DNA, which is part of the same continuous molecule. Histone H1, when present, associates near DNA entry and exit and with linker DNA; it is not one of the eight core histones. The nucleosome repeat length is the average number of base pairs associated with one repeat of the array, including core DNA and the intervening linker. In an idealised array with a repeat length of 200 bp, the mean linker length is 53 bp. Actual linkers vary between regions, cell types and organisms.

Imagine an extended array seen from the side. Each particle appears as a bead, with linker DNA providing the connections. This is often called the 10- or 11-nm fiber, referring to the approximate diameter of the nucleosome particles, not the distance between beads or the contour length of the DNA associated with one bead. Extended arrays are particularly apparent under conditions that reduce internucleosomal attraction. In a nucleus, linker DNA is not necessarily stretched straight, and nucleosomes need not sit at uniform intervals or with uniform orientation. The term fiber here describes an interconnected chain, not an insistence on a perfectly regular cylinder.

For an illustrative array of 60,000 bp with a uniform 200-bp repeat, the number of repeats is 60,000/200 = 300. Of the DNA, 300 × 147 = 44,100 bp is associated with the cores and 15,900 bp is assigned to linkers in the repeating-unit approximation. The core-associated fraction is 147/200 = 0.735, or 73.5%. An actual linear array has end effects, and a short DNA fragment may not contain a whole number of repeats. The model is most useful when a long regularly spaced region is considered. Changing the repeat length to 180 bp increases the predicted number of nucleosomes per kilobase and reduces mean linker length to 33 bp. It does not directly tell us which array will occupy less volume in a real cell because geometry, histone occupancy and interactions also matter.

Why does the connecting DNA matter so much? Entry and exit directions set the orientation from which the next nucleosome can be reached. A change in linker length can alter the relative orientation of adjacent particles because the DNA helix rotates along its axis. Linkers also bend; their flexibility allows particles distant along the chain to approach one another. Thus nucleosome packing is partly a geometrical problem. The number of core particles, their positions and the paths of their linkers all contribute. Identical histone composition does not guarantee identical higher-order organisation.

The nuclease ladder introduced in the preceding chapter provides evidence for a repeating structural unit. It does not independently establish the way nucleosomes fold in three dimensions. Cutting exposed linker DNA yields fragments around multiples of the repeat length, whether the original chain was arranged in a regular fiber or an irregular cluster. The correct conclusion is that nuclease protection reports how DNA is protected along its contour. A separate spatial measurement is required to establish how the protected units are arranged.

4. Physical interactions that bring nucleosomes together

DNA is negatively charged because its phosphate groups are ionised under ordinary cellular conditions. Two naked DNA segments repel each other electrostatically when close together. Histones contain many positively charged amino acid residues, especially lysine and arginine, which contribute to interactions with DNA and reduce the effective repulsion between DNA segments. Wrapping DNA around histones also changes the geometry of the polymer. Packing is not the complete elimination of negative charge; a nucleosome remains a complex charged particle with unevenly distributed binding surfaces.

Dissolved ions screen electrostatic interactions. Screening means that the electric influence of a charge becomes less effective at a distance because surrounding mobile ions rearrange. Raising the concentration of suitable ions from very low levels can favour nucleosome association in reconstituted arrays by reducing repulsion. Divalent cations can be particularly effective. But it would be misleading to state that any increase in salt always produces greater compaction. At sufficiently high ionic strength, protein–DNA interactions themselves can be weakened, and different ionic conditions alter different interactions. A laboratory salt titration has to be interpreted over the range tested, not extrapolated to all possible concentrations.

Core histones possess flexible terminal tails that extend beyond the compact histone fold. These tails can interact with DNA on other nucleosomes and with negatively charged surfaces on neighbouring histone cores. One well-studied example is the interaction between the H4 tail and the acidic surface patch formed largely by H2A and H2B. Such contacts can stabilise particular particle arrangements, but they are reversible rather than covalent links. Removing a tail or altering its charge can weaken some modes of packing without disassembling every core particle. The student should distinguish the interaction holding DNA on a single octamer from interactions connecting separate nucleosomes.

Histone H1 changes how the entering and exiting DNA are positioned and can stabilise association within many nucleosome arrays. Its abundance and binding dynamics vary. H1 depletion can therefore make some chromatin less compact or alter its linker arrangement, but it is not a universal switch between two fixed fiber diameters. In a cell, other proteins and neighbouring chromatin can compensate partially. H1 also does not substitute for a missing core histone: the structural tasks are different. A useful prediction is that loss of a linker-associated protein may change array-level architecture while core-sized DNA protection remains detectable.

At the physical level there is competition between interaction energy and conformational freedom. Many attractive contacts favour association, whereas a flexible chain has more possible configurations when not tightly constrained. A compact state can be favourable under particular conditions without every local contact being fixed. This is why chromatin can be both stable enough to organise a genome and dynamic enough to respond to changing conditions. Passive local association should be distinguished from active ATP-dependent rearrangements by structural proteins, which will be considered below.

An experimental comparison of otherwise identical arrays can isolate some of these contributions. One can compare particles with or without H1, or with intact and altered tails, keeping DNA length, nucleosome number and ionic composition comparable. If a preparation becomes less extended when H1 is added but the core DNA fragment remains the same size, the result supports a change in array folding rather than an increase in the quantity of DNA wrapped on each core. It cannot establish the sole molecular contact responsible for the change, nor prove that identical structures exist throughout a living nucleus.

A further physical variable is the stiffness of the DNA contour. A polymer resists bending over short distances, although it can fold extensively over longer ones. The persistence length is a measure of how far the direction of a polymer tends to remain correlated along its contour. For naked double-stranded DNA it is often approximated as about 50 nm under ordinary physiological salt conditions, corresponding to roughly 150 bp; it is condition dependent. Wrapping around a nucleosome therefore imposes substantial bending on a relatively short DNA segment. Favorable histone–DNA contacts help pay that energetic cost. The flexibility of a chromatin array is not identical to the stiffness of naked DNA: linkers, particle rotation and local interactions alter the range of possible conformations. A mutation that changes the ease with which one segment bends might influence local folding even if the number of histones is unchanged. This is another reason to avoid predicting chromosome width solely from nucleosome count.

Histone charge changes are also relevant to packing, but their effects should be described carefully. Acetylation of a lysine side chain removes its usual positive charge. In some histone tails this weakens particular electrostatic contacts and can favour more accessible arrangements. In addition, an altered tail can recruit or fail to recruit specific binding proteins. Thus a change in packing after acetylation cannot always be explained by charge alone. The chemical modifications and their roles in gene regulation receive fuller treatment elsewhere; here the structural point is that small changes at the nucleosome surface can propagate into different interparticle arrangements without changing the DNA sequence.

5. The classical 30-nm fiber: solenoid and zigzag

In 1976, Finch and Klug reported a solenoidal superstructure of isolated chromatin, supported by electron microscopy and diffraction observations. Their interpretation placed nucleosomes along a helix and implicated H1 in stabilising the folded arrangement. Electron microscopy of isolated or reconstituted chromatin under selected conditions revealed thicker fibers than extended nucleosome arrays. A diameter near 30 nm became the basis of a widely taught packaging model. The central geometrical question was whether consecutive nucleosomes follow a continuous helical path or whether the chain crosses from one side of the fiber to another. Both models attempt to explain how nucleosomes can occupy a much shorter axial distance than they would in an extended array.

In the classical one-start solenoid model, consecutive nucleosomes follow a single helical trajectory around a common axis. In a schematic depiction, nucleosomes 1, 2, 3 and so on are successive positions around the same winding path. Linker DNA bends to connect these neighbours. A frequently quoted textbook idealisation places approximately six nucleosomes per turn of the helix. That number is a characteristic of a model, not a universal biological constant. A different pitch, linker length or particle orientation changes the prediction. The helix referred to here is the path of the nucleosome array, not the double helix of the DNA itself.

In two-start zigzag models, consecutive nucleosomes tend to alternate between opposite stacks or sides. Linkers cross the interior of the arrangement, often in a relatively straight configuration in idealised drawings. Nucleosome i can lie near i+2 in one stack even though i+1 connects them along the DNA contour. The phrase two-start refers to the two intertwined stacks of nucleosomes, not two separate DNA molecules. Tracing the linkers from particle 1 to particle 2 and then to particle 3 reveals a single connected chain. Several variations of the two-start model exist, and both bent linkers and different crossing angles are possible.

The key distinction is spatial neighbourhood versus sequence neighbourhood. In a simplified solenoid, consecutively numbered particles form neighbouring positions around a turn. In a simplified zigzag, particles separated by one intervening particle may stack next to one another. If a diagram shows two rows of nucleosomes, the correct interpretation requires following the numbered linkers rather than assuming that each row is a separate molecule. Conversely, seeing close i and i+2 contacts does not alone establish the precise global geometry, because irregular arrays can create the same local contact.

Worked geometry illustrates the limits of classical dimensions. Suppose an ideal solenoid contains six nucleosomes per turn with a repeat length of 200 bp and an axial pitch of 12 nm. One turn would contain 1,200 bp of DNA, whose naked contour is 408 nm. The ideal axial packing ratio for this model is 408/12 = 34. For 60 turns, the model contains 72,000 bp in 720 nm of axial length. Neither six nor 12 is a required constant for native chromatin; the pitch has been chosen for this teaching calculation. If the pitch doubles with identical DNA content per turn, the axial packing ratio halves. Knowing a fiber diameter of 30 nm alone is insufficient to compute its axial packing ratio: the pitch and DNA content per axial repeat must also be known.

The traditional sequence of drawings often progresses from a 2-nm DNA double helix to an approximately 10-nm nucleosomal array, a proposed 30-nm folded fiber, thicker looped domains, a roughly 700-nm chromatid and a roughly 1,400-nm replicated metaphase chromosome. Some textbook diagrams label an intermediate looped level around 300 nm. These are schematic dimensions of different objects, not universal thresholds and not multipliers for DNA length. In particular, 700 nm describes a typical illustrative chromatid width, not its axial length. The 1,400-nm value is a side-by-side representation of two chromatids. Neither width specifies the DNA content. In an examination asking explicitly for the classical order, recognise that hierarchy; in a question asking about evidence from intact cells, evaluate the limitations of each proposed intermediate.

6. Why a universal regular 30-nm fiber is not supported

A model formed from an isolated specimen must be tested in the environment to which it is being applied. Regular nucleosome fibers can be produced in selected experimental conditions, especially when arrays have relatively uniform repeat length and composition. A nucleus, however, contains arrays with variable linkers, diverse histone compositions, transiently bound proteins, crowded surroundings and regions engaged in different cellular tasks. There is no reason in principle for every local arrangement to converge on the same periodic geometry. The question is therefore not whether a regular folded fiber can exist, but whether it is the obligatory intermediate throughout ordinary eukaryotic chromosomes.

A major difficulty with conventional microscopy is distinguishing DNA-containing structures from other nuclear material of similar electron density. Preparation can also disturb packing: extracting chromatin removes its normal surroundings, and fixing or dehydrating a specimen may change spatial relationships. Researchers can improve confidence by comparing different preparation conditions and by using approaches that identify chromatin more specifically. A structural pattern that is stable across complementary methods is more convincing than one seen only after a particular extraction.

Ou and colleagues reported in 2017 a DNA-selective electron microscopy and tomography approach called ChromEMT. Electron tomography reconstructs a three-dimensional volume from images taken at different viewing angles. Their study visualised chromatin in the investigated interphase and mitotic cells as irregular chains with local diameters in a range of roughly 5–24 nm, packed at differing local concentrations. These observations did not show the pervasive regular 30-nm cylinder expected from a rigid universal hierarchy. The finding supports a picture in which variable local arrangements and packing densities contribute to organisation across scales. It does not imply that every chromatin segment in every organism has the same 5–24 nm structure or that regular fibers are impossible in specialised conditions.

One can reconcile the classical and modern descriptions by separating possible local structures from required global organisation. Solenoid and zigzag diagrams describe geometrically conceivable arrangements and have support from particular experimental systems. Irregular arrays may contain local contacts resembling features of those models without maintaining a long periodic fiber. A chromosome can be compacted through changes in contact frequency, bending and loop organisation without every chain passing through a uniform 30-nm intermediate. This also explains why it remains useful to learn the older diagrams while treating their numerical labels with care.

The evidence changes how a student should answer apparent contradictions. Suppose an isolated array yields a 30-nm fiber while in situ tomography of the source tissue mainly reveals irregular chromatin. These results are not necessarily mutually exclusive. The first demonstrates that the array can adopt a regular structure under the conditions of isolation. The second provides information about its organisation in the cellular sample studied. A claim about native organisation must privilege appropriately controlled observations made in that context. Likewise, absence of a distinct 30-nm fiber in one experiment does not demonstrate absence of nucleosomes. The evidence for nucleosomes comes from independent observations and remains valid.

The practical lesson is to avoid replacing one rigid dogma with another. Chromatin folding is heterogeneous. Some local contacts are favoured, and proteins actively organise larger structures, yet a chromosome can remain ordered at larger scales even when the local polymer is irregular. A road map can be organised into districts without every street running parallel; similarly, a loop-rich chromosome can have a recognisable axis without uniform nucleosome spacing or orientation. The analogy concerns scale of organisation only; it is not an explanation of the molecular forces.

7. Loop domains connect distant portions of the same DNA molecule

A loop is formed when two positions on a continuous DNA or chromatin chain are brought near each other while the intervening chain extends away. The positions at which the loop begins and ends along the contour are distinct genomic coordinates. They can be separated by thousands of base pairs while lying close together in space. This distinction between genomic distance and spatial distance is fundamental. A physical loop does not necessarily create a circular DNA molecule: unless the DNA backbone is covalently joined, the overall molecule retains its original continuity and termini.

Loop formation changes which regions are near one another and can concentrate the chain into a smaller axial extent. In a schematic radial arrangement, the bases of several loops lie near a common chromosome axis and the intervening chromatin projects outward. A loop may itself contain many nucleosomes arranged irregularly. It does not need to be a preformed 30-nm fiber. Adjacent loops may be of different lengths, and in an actual chromosome the boundaries and contacts can change. A cartoon showing identical loops along a line should be read as an explanatory model, not evidence of equally spaced, permanently anchored domains.

Why is a loop more useful than an undifferentiated collapse? If many parts of a long chromosome interact without constraint, contacts can form between far-separated regions and between different molecules. This may create difficulties when chromosomes must be individually distinguished and moved. Organising segments into loops associated with an axis can favour contacts within local portions of a chromatid and reduce its axial extension while retaining the continuous order of its DNA. This structural principle can help create an elongated body rather than a random aggregate. It is not a guarantee that all crossings or entanglements disappear.

The number of loops depends on both DNA content and mean loop size, if a simple non-overlapping tiling model is assumed. A 90 Mb unreplicated chromosome divided into 150 kb loops would have 90,000 kb/150 kb = 600 loops. If the mean loop size changes to 75 kb while the entire DNA length remains assigned to nonoverlapping loops, the estimate rises to 1,200. This reasoning is bookkeeping, not a prediction that doubling the number of loops necessarily doubles chromosomal compaction. Nested loops, shared bases, variable spacing, overlap and unlooped segments violate the simple tiling assumption. The calculation is useful because it makes clear what can and cannot be deduced from an average size.

Some contacts arise because a flexible chain bends and thermal motion brings two segments together. Other contacts are stabilised by protein interactions. Structural maintenance complexes can actively generate loops by consuming ATP. The mere existence of a loop does not identify which of these mechanisms produced it. To assign a role to a particular protein, one needs perturbation evidence or a controlled reconstruction that measures the formation of loops. The following sections explain these different kinds of evidence.

8. From chromosome halos to the modern chromosome axis

Condensed chromosomes were once depicted as thick, continuously coiled fibers. A different possibility emerged when researchers asked whether nonhistone proteins preserve larger-scale organisation even if much of the histone-associated packing is removed. In a classical 1977 study, Paulson and Laemmli examined metaphase chromosomes depleted of histones. Electron micrographs showed a central residual structure with a halo of extended DNA loops. The appearance suggested that the DNA had been held in loop-like arrangements associated with a central protein-rich structure.

The experimental logic is easier to understand if the word scaffold is treated operationally. The investigators could remove particular classes of chromosomal proteins and observe what remained. If the entire shape depended only on nucleosomal coiling, extensive histone removal might be expected to erase any residual axial arrangement. The persistence of a chromosome-shaped core with extended DNA around it supported the existence of nonhistone contributions to chromosome-scale organisation. A comparison to untreated chromosomes, verification of histone removal, and confirmation that the extended material is DNA are important checks when evaluating this type of evidence.

What the observation cannot establish is equally important. Extraction changes the normal chemical environment, and a remaining structure may be stabilised or rearranged during preparation. A protein-rich residue does not prove that an identical immobile skeleton existed in the living chromosome. Nor does the appearance of DNA loops demonstrate that each loop is attached permanently to a specific DNA sequence. The original observation supports the scaffold concept, while more modern experiments support dynamic interactions and chromosome-associated axes. A careful answer should not reject the classical evidence, but should state the inference at the appropriate strength.

The terms chromosome scaffold and nuclear matrix also need separation. A metaphase chromosome scaffold refers to chromosome-associated material in a condensed chromosome, often operationally defined by extraction. A nuclear matrix refers to proposed or isolated structural material of the interphase nucleus more broadly. The nuclear lamina is a distinct peripheral protein network. Contacts between chromatin and the lamina may influence nuclear positioning, but a chromosome axis is not simply a piece of nuclear lamina extending through each chromosome. They have different locations and functions. Structural models that invoke scaffold- or matrix-associated regions should be understood with the limitations of the experimental definitions.

Among important proteins associated with chromosome axes are condensins and topoisomerase II. Condensins can organise intramolecular chromatin loops and shape the chromatid. Topoisomerase II can pass one DNA duplex through a transient break in another duplex, followed by resealing, allowing changes in DNA entanglement. This action is distinct from simply drawing the chain into a loop. Chromosomal condensation and removal of entanglement therefore cooperate but are not interchangeable processes. Detailed strand-passage chemistry belongs to DNA topology and replication; the relevant lesson here is that mechanical organisation of a long chain requires both spatial folding and management of its crossings.

A chromosome axis is best understood as a protein-enriched organisation of chromatin rather than a hollow rod into which DNA is inserted. The chromatin chain itself contributes to the architecture. Protein complexes bind, release and change relationships over time. In a schematic bottlebrush model, many loops extend outward from a central region, while their bases contribute to an axial organisation. Such a model accounts for a rod-shaped object without requiring a rigid solid backbone. The word axis refers to an organisational centre, not to a second DNA molecule running down the chromosome.

9. SMC complexes: structural machines rather than histones

Structural maintenance of chromosomes proteins, abbreviated SMC proteins, form a family of large ATP-dependent chromosome-associated complexes. Their structural design is particularly well suited to connecting or rearranging segments of DNA. Each SMC polypeptide folds back on itself to form a long coiled-coil arm, with a hinge region at one end and an ATPase head formed by the amino- and carboxy-terminal regions at the other end. Two SMC molecules associate at their hinges. A non-SMC protein of the kleisin family connects their head regions, with additional associated proteins providing regulation and interactions. This produces a ring-like architecture with flexible arms. A ring-like drawing is a simplified representation; it does not imply that every interaction with DNA occurs by the same topological route.

Two important SMC complexes in eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes are cohesin and condensin. Cohesin commonly contains SMC1 and SMC3 together with a kleisin and associated proteins. Condensin contains SMC2 and SMC4 together with a different set of non-SMC subunits. In many vertebrate cells two condensin complexes, condensin I and condensin II, share SMC2 and SMC4 but differ in their regulatory components. This molecular distinction is useful because an experiment that disrupts one complex does not necessarily disrupt the other. Both classes use ATP, yet they have different major structural responsibilities.

Cohesin is especially important for sister chromatid cohesion, the physical association of two replicated copies, and for organisation of loops within individual interphase chromatids. Condensin has major roles in assembling and maintaining the shape of condensed chromosomes. In animal cells cohesin-dependent looping is prominent in interphase, while condensin-dependent loop organisation is prominent during mitotic condensation. These are broad biological patterns rather than absolute statements about every stage or organism. In particular, cohesion and intramolecular looping are two related but distinct functions of cohesin. If a diagram shows two DNA molecules held together, it illustrates cohesion; if it shows two segments of one DNA contour brought together, it illustrates a loop.

Why is ATP necessary for active rearrangement? Thermal fluctuations cause DNA and bound proteins to change positions, but such fluctuations by themselves do not sustain a directional cycle of work. ATP binding and hydrolysis can be coupled to conformational changes in an SMC complex. Repeated cycles allow the complex to alter its contacts with DNA and change the amount of chain captured in a loop. The exact molecular stepping mechanism varies between complexes and remains more specialised than is necessary here. The exam-relevant principle is that a protein can use chemical energy to organise DNA geometry without cutting the DNA backbone or synthesising new DNA.

Histones and SMC complexes should not be placed in the same category simply because both associate with chromatin. Histones create the basic repeated DNA-wrapping unit. SMC complexes act at a larger organisational scale through dynamic contacts and rearrangements. Depleting condensin may leave nucleosome cores intact while severely affecting chromosome shape. Depleting core histones directly compromises the basic packaging particles. The effects can influence one another indirectly, but the different primary roles are the starting point for interpreting a perturbation.

10. Loop extrusion: a causal model and the evidence supporting it

In a loop-extrusion model, an SMC complex binds a DNA or chromatin chain and increases the amount of contour held within a loop. Initially two nearby portions of the chain are engaged. As the complex cycles through DNA-binding configurations, more DNA is drawn into the loop and the intervening contour extends outward. Continued action enlarges the loop until the complex releases, encounters a constraint or reaches a condition in which further movement is unfavourable. The base can change its genomic coordinates over time. No new base pairs are produced; DNA outside the growing loop is redistributed into it.

A simplified single-sided model holds one DNA position relatively fixed while DNA is reeled in from the other side. A two-sided representation shows both flanking portions contributing to growth. These drawings differ in the motion relative to the DNA contour, not in the number of DNA molecules. Experiments have observed different extrusion behaviour depending on the complex and conditions; yeast condensin in a classical purified single-molecule experiment showed one-sided extrusion. It is not appropriate to assume a universal directionality for every SMC complex. The student needs to recognise how loop growth changes the distribution of chain length.

Ganji and colleagues in 2018 provided direct experimental support by observing fluorescent DNA molecules with purified yeast condensin in real time. In the presence of ATP, DNA loops developed and increased in size. This is stronger evidence for active extrusion than a single static image of a loop, because it resolves the process of loop growth over time. Comparing conditions with and without ATP or a functional protein complex helps distinguish energy-dependent growth from spontaneous bending or aggregation. Comparable experiments with human cohesin later supported loop-extrusion activity for that complex as well.

The purified assay establishes that the protein system can perform this operation under the tested conditions. It cannot by itself prove the exact size, lifetime, number or orientation of loops in a crowded intact chromosome. Naked DNA in a controlled assay differs from chromatin with nucleosomes and other DNA-binding proteins. In vivo perturbation evidence and structural observations are therefore needed to relate the elementary activity to chromosome-scale organisation. These complementary observations support the general model without requiring every detail of native folding to be identical to a simplified assay.

A quantitative example can be used without learning specialist motor kinetics. In an illustrative constant-rate experiment a loop grows from 2 kb to 26 kb in 12 seconds. Its net rate of contour capture is (26 − 2) kb/12 s = 2 kb/s. If 40 kb of available DNA remains outside the loop and the same rate continues without release, at most another 20 seconds would be needed to capture that amount. This is not a measured universal condensin speed. If both sides contribute, the total growth rate is the sum of contributions; for equal rates in this example each side contributes 1 kb/s. For strictly one-sided growth the active side contributes the full 2 kb/s. The total loop growth alone cannot distinguish these scenarios. One must observe marker positions on each flank.

The structural consequence of repeated loop formation is important. Multiple complexes can generate arrays of loops whose bases are organised near an axis. The contour is then distributed laterally away from the axis, producing shortening along it. The interaction between neighbouring loops and proteins can shape thickness and stiffness. This accounts for an important step between local packing and chromosome-wide morphology. It is not a complete mathematical description of chromosome mechanics, which depends on multiple proteins and cellular conditions.

In the illustrative graph below, the axes measure elapsed time and loop size. A horizontal trace indicates no net change, not necessarily an absence of molecular motion. A rising trace reports net growth. The slope from 4 to 8 seconds in the positive-ATP trace is (18 − 10)/4 = 2 kb/s, matching the whole-trace average. A curved or plateauing trace would indicate a changing rate or limited growth; it would not by itself specify whether ATP depletion, a barrier, dissociation or another factor caused the change. Replicated traces and matched controls are necessary to interpret that change.

11. How interphase chromatin becomes a condensed chromosome

During interphase, most eukaryotic chromosomes occupy extended but organised regions of the nucleus. The cell carries out transcription, DNA replication when appropriate, and repair. These activities require access to selected DNA sequences. Chromatin can form locally compact regions without the whole chromosome becoming a short discrete metaphase rod. After replication, sister copies must be held together while their individual DNA molecules remain distinct. Cohesin contributes to this association, but association is not equivalent to fusing the two backbones.

As a typical animal cell enters mitosis, higher-order chromosome organisation changes substantially. Condensin-dependent loop arrangements contribute to progressive shortening and individualisation of chromatids. In vertebrate cells condensin II is already nuclear during interphase and acts early in mitotic chromosome organisation; condensin I gains broad access to chromosomes after nuclear-envelope breakdown in open mitosis. Their contributions to axial and lateral packing are coordinated. This description is useful for understanding structure, but exact activation pathways, phosphorylation and cell-cycle timing are reserved for Subunit 2.D.

If many chromatin loops are arranged along an axis, local folding reduces the distance along that axis occupied by a given length of DNA. Organising loops rather than merely sticking distant segments together also helps distinguish neighbouring chromatids. Topoisomerase II assists in resolving DNA crossings and catenations, which are topological links between DNA molecules, or analogous entanglements in long chromosomes constrained by their organisation. Compact shape does not mean all entanglements have been removed. Failure of topological resolution can leave bridges between separating chromosomes even if they appear condensed.

At metaphase, a replicated chromosome typically consists of two closely associated compact chromatids. Its appearance is a result of both intrachromatid organisation and remaining interchromatid cohesion. If cohesion is impaired, the sister structures may separate prematurely even if each is relatively compact. If condensin function is impaired, individual chromatids can be abnormally shaped or less well organised despite DNA synthesis having occurred normally. Therefore a single microscopy image must be interpreted with an awareness of which structural relationship is disturbed.

After chromosome distribution, the highly condensed mitotic form is not maintained indefinitely. In the daughter nuclei the chromatin reorganises into an interphase arrangement, with changes in protein association, loop organisation and DNA accessibility. Nucleosomes remain central to the packaging. The sequence of the DNA remains the same in an ordinary faithful division even though morphology differs. This reversibility demonstrates that a chromosome is not defined by a particular width or shape.

Many familiar images come from animal metaphase preparations. Plants and other eukaryotes also condense chromosomes but differ in details such as nuclear-envelope behaviour, chromosome sizes and the timing of associated proteins. Some fungi undergo closed mitosis, so the animal-cell sequence of envelope breakdown and protein access is not universal. Specialised cells also exhibit exceptional chromosomal structures, as examined below. A good general answer identifies the biological context and separates broad structural principles from organism-specific timing.

12. Interphase organisation: chromosome territories and spatial proximity

When interphase chromatin is stained uniformly, individual chromosomes may be hard to distinguish. Does that mean all DNA is thoroughly mixed throughout the nucleus? Chromosome painting experiments answered this question by using fluorescent probes complementary to sequences distributed along a chosen chromosome. In fluorescence in situ hybridisation, or FISH, the probes hybridise to target DNA within a preserved cellular preparation and identify the spatial distribution of the target. If each chromosome occupied the entire nucleus uniformly, signals for different chromosomes would be expected to spread broadly through the same space. Instead, whole-chromosome painting often reveals preferentially occupied volumes called territories.

A territory is a region preferentially occupied by a particular chromosome. It has no surrounding lipid membrane and does not constitute a new organelle. Its boundaries can be irregular, and chromatin from different chromosomes may interpenetrate or approach one another. Some loci extend from a territory or occupy positions near its boundary. The word territory indicates a statistical spatial organisation rather than complete isolation. A single two-dimensional optical section also cannot establish the full three-dimensional shape of the territory, since portions may lie above or below that section.

The location of chromatin inside a nucleus is influenced by its interactions with nuclear structures and with other chromatin segments. The nuclear lamina can associate with certain domains, and regions near nucleoli can have characteristic organisations. A gene's location is not determined solely by its chromosome number. Different cell types and even different individual nuclei can have different arrangements. Gene-rich regions often differ from gene-poor regions in their typical positioning and accessibility, but these relationships are not absolute. Detailed regulatory and chromatin-state mechanisms belong to the subsequent euchromatin/heterochromatin chapter and Unit 3.

Territory organisation helps explain why distances along DNA do not map simply onto distances in the nucleus. Two sites separated by many megabases on one chromosome may become nearby because of folding. Two sites on different chromosomes may also become close at territory interfaces. Conversely, consecutive genes can be separated in three-dimensional space if the intervening chain is extended. Spatial proximity can facilitate interactions, but it does not demonstrate that an interaction occurred or that two genes were expressed together. A comparison of contact patterns with functional measurements is needed for such an inference.

Consider an illustrative FISH analysis of 100 nuclei. In 60 nuclei two labelled loci are separated by less than 0.4 µm; in 40 they are farther apart. After depletion of a candidate structural protein, the corresponding counts are 25 and 75. The fraction of close observations falls from 0.60 to 0.25, a decrease of 35 percentage points, or about 58.3% relative to the original fraction. This supports a change in spatial association under the experiment's conditions. A matched control, comparable DNA content and preservation quality, and independent repetitions would strengthen the conclusion. The result does not establish that every close observation represented direct molecular binding, nor that the chromosome territories disappeared.

Chromatin domains within a chromosome also describe preferred local organisation. At exam level it is enough to recognise that DNA segments can form groups of contacts more frequently with one another than with some surrounding sequences. Cohesin-driven loops and other interactions can contribute to such patterns. Contact frequency is a population or time-dependent measure, not a photograph of permanent loops in all nuclei. Detailed genomic contact-mapping technologies and specialised domain models lie outside this chapter. The essential concept is that local heterogeneity, chromatin loops and chromosome territories can coexist at successively larger scales.

13. Special chromosomes reveal that packing is not a single fixed architecture

Some of the clearest demonstrations of chromosome organisation came from cells whose chromosomes are unusually large or visibly extended. Lampbrush chromosomes are characteristic of the growing oocytes of many animals, especially well-studied amphibian species. Along an axis are numerous compact chromomeres, with prominent lateral loops extending outward. These loops contain extended chromatin associated with transcription and newly made RNA-containing material. Their appearance makes the distinction between condensed axial regions and extended looped regions easy to see.

The lampbrush example demonstrates that a chromosome can preserve an identifiable organisation while particular regions are extended. The loops are not independent chromosomes, and their visible thickness is not a direct measurement of bare DNA diameter; associated transcripts and proteins contribute to the structure. A comparison of an extended transcriptionally active loop with a more compact region helps explain the connection between architecture and accessibility. It would be incorrect, however, to use the existence of these specialised loops as proof that every interphase chromosome has visible lampbrush morphology or that all loops in mitotic chromosomes are transcriptionally active.

Polytene chromosomes provide a different example. They arise in certain cells, notably the salivary glands of many dipteran insects, through repeated DNA replication without ordinary chromosome separation. Many aligned chromatid copies create a large structure with reproducible bands and interbands. Local expansions called puffs are associated with increased transcription in particular regions. The large appearance results substantially from increased DNA copy number and aligned copies, whereas a puff reflects local structural expansion and activity. These are distinct reasons for an enlarged chromosomal region.

Imagine two bands of a polytene chromosome with equal numbers of aligned copies, but one develops a puff after a developmental signal. A reasonable prediction is that the puffy region has altered local chromatin organisation and often enhanced RNA synthesis. It does not follow that its underlying DNA has been newly replicated relative to the adjacent band. By contrast, comparing a polytene chromosome with an ordinary single chromatid involves a difference in the number of DNA copies. Observations of width must therefore be interpreted together with knowledge of DNA content.

Together, lampbrush and polytene chromosomes provide visible evidence that large-scale organisation and local accessibility can vary without requiring a universal compact fiber throughout. They also remind us that morphology is influenced by cell type and biological function. The later chromosome architecture chapter will examine structural landmarks common to ordinary chromosomes, while the next chromatin-state chapter will develop the distinctions between less compact and more compact chromatin regions.

14. Interpreting experimental perturbations without overclaiming

In an examination, experimental questions often give a perturbation and several possible conclusions. The first task is to identify what was changed and what was actually measured. Suppose a researcher removes histone H1 from an isolated nucleosome array and observes a larger end-to-end distance while the core-sized DNA fragments are unchanged. This supports a change in array-level packing with retained nucleosomal protection. It does not prove that all H1 was removed from every native chromosome or that condensin function was affected. A rescue in which H1 is restored under otherwise matched conditions would help connect the change to H1 itself.

Now consider cells in which condensin function is impaired. Chromosomes appear elongated and irregular at mitosis, yet measurements of DNA content show that replication has completed. The defect is consistent with altered chromosome-scale folding rather than insufficient DNA synthesis. If nucleosome protection is also similar to control, there is further evidence that the basic particles remain present. These measurements distinguish levels of organisation. They do not determine exactly which loop geometry was altered; one would need complementary structural observations to test that more specific claim.

A different phenotype occurs if cohesin-dependent sister association is impaired. If each replicated chromatid forms a compact structure but the sisters separate too early, the principal observation concerns the association between copies rather than compaction within each copy. This does not exclude additional effects of cohesin depletion on interphase loops. The immediate interpretation depends on the measured property and the phase examined. A well-designed comparison measures both individual chromatid shape and sister separation rather than treating all chromosome abnormalities as identical.

Topoisomerase II perturbation presents a third case. Replicated DNA molecules may remain entangled, leading to segregation bridges even if substantial condensation has occurred. An intervention that alters DNA topology can therefore affect the successful individualisation of chromosomes without directly removing histone cores. The appearance of a bridge alone is not a unique diagnostic of topoisomerase II loss; DNA damage, replication problems and other defects may also produce bridges. A causal claim requires appropriate controls, a specific perturbation and ideally rescue or complementary measurements.

Imaging also has interpretive limits. A brighter region in a DNA-stained image might contain more DNA in the optical section, have higher DNA concentration, or be affected by staining and instrument settings. A smaller territory might reflect a different cell-cycle stage or change in nuclear volume. Measuring volume in three dimensions, using matched staining, controlling DNA content and comparing multiple nuclei helps distinguish these alternatives. The design principle is simple: isolate a structural variable and measure it at the scale where the hypothesis makes a prediction.

These experimental approaches complement one another. Nuclease protection reports a repeating local DNA–protein organisation. Electron microscopy and tomography reveal local structure and spatial density. Histone-depletion preparations reveal residual organisation under extraction conditions. Chromosome painting identifies broad spatial distributions. Time-resolved single-molecule assays directly reveal loop growth in controlled systems. No single method answers all structural questions. When apparently conflicting results are obtained, ask whether the methods measured the same length scale, whether the sample preparation was comparable and whether the conclusion exceeded the observation.

15. Quantitative integration: length, density and loop accounting

Several useful calculations follow directly from the structural relationships rather than from memorising specialised formulas. Begin with a 120 Mb chromosome in a G1 cell. Its DNA contour is 120 × 10⁶ × 0.34 nm = 40.8 × 10⁶ nm = 40,800 µm. If the average repeat length is 200 bp and all DNA is assigned to repeating nucleosomal units, its estimated repeat count is 600,000. If 147 bp of each repeat is associated with a core, there are 88.2 Mb of core-associated DNA and 31.8 Mb of linker-assigned DNA. The two amounts sum to 120 Mb, providing an independent mass-balance check.

Suppose this chromosome after replication has two equivalent chromatids, each with a mean axial length of 8 µm during metaphase. The linear packing ratio for one chromatid is 40,800/8 = 5,100. There are now about 1.2 million nucleosomal repeats across both chromatids if the original repeat-length assumption remains valid. The DNA contour of the replicated chromosome is 81,600 µm, but one should not divide that total by the length of a single chromatid and report it as the single-chromatid packing ratio. The replicated chromosome contains two DNA molecules, not one twice as long. This is a common source of factor-of-two errors.

A separate illustrative calculation concerns spatial concentration. A 12 Mb domain occupies 4 µm³ in condition A and 2.5 µm³ in condition B, with unchanged DNA content. The average DNA density rises from 12/4 = 3 Mb/µm³ to 12/2.5 = 4.8 Mb/µm³, a factor of 1.6. The volume decreases by (4 − 2.5)/4 = 0.375, or 37.5%, whereas density increases by 60%. The percentages are not equal because density is inversely proportional to volume at fixed DNA content. If DNA content instead doubled while volume doubled, density would be unchanged. This reasoning avoids conflating visible size with packing.

An idealised loop model provides a third calculation. Let a 120 Mb chromatid contain disjoint loops averaging 100 kb, and suppose their bases are uniformly distributed along an 8 µm axis. There would be 1,200 loops, so the mean axial length per loop base is 8 µm/1,200 = 0.00667 µm, or approximately 6.67 nm. This is a projected average spacing, not a measurement of physical centre-to-centre separations among proteins, and not necessarily a feasible arrangement of a single row of loop bases. Real loops may be nested, bases may be radially distributed, and a chromosome axis is not a one-dimensional rail with one binding site per repeat. The value illustrates why simplistic uniform drawings should not be interpreted literally.

Finally, consider a hypothetical chromosome that shortens from 16 µm to 8 µm while DNA content stays constant. Its axial packing ratio doubles. If its cross-sectional area simultaneously doubles, its approximate cylindrical volume remains constant (volume = area × length). Under that approximation its mean DNA concentration does not change. It has become shorter and thicker without necessarily becoming denser. This example captures the central distinction between axial compaction and volumetric compaction. A student who can state the assumptions and identify the missing measurements is better prepared for an experimental question than one who only remembers the usual sequence of textbook fiber diameters.

16. Integrating the levels of organisation and their limitations

The chromosome is one continuous DNA molecule per unreplicated chromatid, organised at several interacting scales. At the local scale, DNA wrapping around histone cores and linker geometry establish a flexible array. Electrostatic screening, histone tails and linker-associated proteins influence how nucleosomes approach one another. Some conditions permit regular folded arrays; many native cellular contexts show variable local packing. This is the physical starting point, not the final architecture.

At a larger scale, folding brings distant DNA segments together into loops and domains. Some contacts are transient outcomes of polymer motion and others are stabilised by proteins. ATP-dependent SMC complexes can actively rearrange DNA contour into loops. Cohesin and condensin have overlapping broad abilities to organise DNA but different dominant structural roles, including cohesion between replicated copies and formation of condensed chromosome architecture. Topoisomerase II helps manage DNA crossings. These functions cooperate rather than replace each other. A failure at one level may be diagnosed using measurements that remain normal at another level.

At the chromosome and nuclear scales, arrays of loops contribute to chromosome shape and extended chromosomes occupy territories during interphase. During mitosis, chromosomes become individually recognisable, with organised chromatids. The same sequence can assume different three-dimensional arrangements. Specialised lampbrush and polytene chromosomes demonstrate how cell type, transcription and DNA copy number can affect visible architecture. None of these examples licenses a universal rule about all chromosomes.

A few tempting shortcuts should now be recognisably incorrect. Chromatin is not synonymous with heterochromatin. Chromosomes are present in interphase even when their individual boundaries are difficult to see with ordinary staining. A loop is not necessarily circular DNA. The classical 30-nm fiber is not mandatory in all native chromatin. Cohesin does not simply perform the same task as condensin, and neither is a core histone. A residual scaffold after extraction does not establish an immutable skeleton in living cells. Increased linear compaction does not by itself establish increased DNA density. These distinctions are often the basis of questions that reverse a condition or present two apparently contradictory observations.

The next chapter moves from packing principles to the structural landmarks of chromosomes. A centromere, a telomere, a chromatid and a chromosome arm are defined by their positions or structural relationships, not by a particular fiber diameter. Having learned how DNA contour is organised into chromatin and chromosome-scale structures, the student can now understand why these landmarks remain identifiable even as a chromosome changes its degree of packing. The following chapter on euchromatin and heterochromatin then examines regional differences in organisation and accessibility in greater detail.

17. Part B — Original conceptual and assertion–reason practice

1. Which quantity is approximately 0.34 nm for B-DNA?

  1. Axial rise per base pair
  2. Nucleosome diameter
  3. Linker length
  4. Chromatid width
Answer and explanation

A. The rise is measured along the DNA helical axis. The other quantities describe larger-scale structures or variable DNA lengths.

2. Which protein is not a core histone?

  1. H2A
  2. H2B
  3. H1
  4. H4
Answer and explanation

C. H1 is linker associated. H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 form the octamer.

3. A 200-bp repeat contains 147 bp of core DNA. The ideal linker is:

  1. 147 bp
  2. 53 bp
  3. 200 bp
  4. 347 bp
Answer and explanation

B. Subtract the core from the repeat: 53 bp. The other values confuse the whole repeat, core or their sum.

4. Which feature is most characteristic of a simplified two-start zigzag?

  1. Two independent DNA molecules
  2. Alternation of consecutive particles between stacks
  3. Absence of linker DNA
  4. Covalently joined circular DNA
Answer and explanation

B. The two stacks contain particles of the same chain. Linkers connect them and no covalent circularisation is required.

5. Which statement about 30-nm fibers is defensible?

  1. All living chromosomes contain them throughout
  2. They never form under any conditions
  3. They can form in particular preparations but are not an obligatory native intermediate
  4. They are identical to naked DNA
Answer and explanation

C. Regular fibers are observed in selected systems, not a universal native requirement. Naked DNA is a different structural level.

6. A chromosome loop is defined by:

  1. Newly synthesised DNA
  2. A necessarily circular DNA molecule
  3. Spatial proximity of two contour positions with intervening chain extending away
  4. Loss of histones from every base
Answer and explanation

C. Loop geometry does not require replication, covalent closure or complete histone loss.

7. The primary classic structural association of cohesin is:

  1. Sister chromatid cohesion
  2. Core DNA wrapping
  3. DNA strand passage
  4. Formation of ribosomes
Answer and explanation

A. Cohesin maintains sister association and also organises loops. Histones wrap DNA; topoisomerases alter topology; ribosome assembly is unrelated.

8. Condensin typically contains:

  1. SMC1 and SMC3
  2. SMC2 and SMC4
  3. H2A and H2B only
  4. Tubulin dimers
Answer and explanation

B. SMC2 and SMC4 are condensin core SMC subunits. SMC1/3 are cohesin, histones form nucleosomes, and tubulin forms microtubules.

9. What does chromosome painting directly reveal?

  1. All DNA–protein binding events
  2. DNA sequence at single-base resolution
  3. Preferential spatial distribution of labelled chromosome DNA
  4. A membrane surrounding each chromosome
Answer and explanation

C. Hybridisation identifies the distribution of targeted sequences. It neither resolves all binding events nor establishes membranes.

10. Which special chromosome displays conspicuous lateral transcription-associated loops in growing oocytes?

  1. Lampbrush
  2. Ordinary metaphase
  3. Bacterial nucleoid
  4. Polytene band only
Answer and explanation

A. Lampbrush chromosomes exhibit lateral loops. Polytene chromosomes have bands and puffs; the other structures lack this defining morphology.

11. Assertion: a chromosome can become shorter without losing DNA. Reason: the DNA contour can be redistributed into folds and loops.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Folding reduces axial extension while preserving contour length. This directly explains shortening.

12. Assertion: a 30-nm fiber is obligatory in every native chromosome. Reason: some reconstituted arrays form regular fibers.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. A possible structure under selected conditions does not establish a universal requirement in cells.

18. Part C — Original experimental and quantitative practice

1. A G1 chromosome has 100 Mb of DNA. One metaphase sister chromatid is 5 µm long. Find its axial packing ratio.

  1. 340
  2. 3,400
  3. 6,800
  4. 13,600
Answer and explanation

C. 100 × 10⁶ × 0.34 nm = 34,000 µm; divide by 5 = 6,800. 13,600 doubles DNA incorrectly; the smaller alternatives misconvert units.

2. Two replicated chromatids each have 80 Mb. At 200 bp per repeat, how many repeats are estimated across both?

  1. 200,000
  2. 400,000
  3. 800,000
  4. 1,600,000
Answer and explanation

C. 160 × 10⁶/200 = 800,000. 400,000 is for one chromatid; the other values introduce unnecessary factors.

3. A 10 Mb domain shrinks from 5 to 2 µm³ without DNA loss. What happens to mean DNA density?

  1. Decreases 60%
  2. Increases 150%
  3. Increases 60%
  4. Unchanged
Answer and explanation

B. Initial density 2, final 5 Mb/µm³. Relative gain (5−2)/2 = 150%. The 60% is the volume decrease, not the density increase.

4. A reconstituted array retains its nuclease ladder after H1 removal but becomes extended. Which inference is strongest?

  1. Core particles may remain while higher-order packing changes
  2. All core DNA is lost
  3. Every chromosomal loop is abolished in vivo
  4. DNA content has doubled
Answer and explanation

A. Local protection and extension measure different levels. Neither complete core loss, native loop abolition nor DNA replication is demonstrated.

5. An in situ reconstruction shows irregular chains; isolated chromatin from comparable cells forms thick regular fibers. Which explanation fits?

  1. One must be fraudulent
  2. Regular fibers can be condition dependent
  3. Nucleosomes cannot exist
  4. DNA sequence must have changed
Answer and explanation

B. Preparation can alter structural arrangement. Both observations can be valid without sequence change or rejection of nucleosomes.

6. A hypothetical 60 Mb chromatid is assigned to disjoint 120 kb loops. Number of loops?

  1. 50
  2. 500
  3. 5,000
  4. 72
Answer and explanation

B. 60,000 kb/120 kb = 500. The other values reflect unit or operation errors; nested loops would require a different model.

7. A loop grows from 5 to 29 kb in 8 s. If the two sides contribute equally, the rate per side is:

  1. 1.5 kb/s
  2. 3 kb/s
  3. 4.25 kb/s
  4. 12 kb/s
Answer and explanation

A. Net growth = 24/8 = 3 kb/s, split equally into 1.5. 3 is total, 4.25 is unrelated to the net change and 12 omits time.

8. A purified protein generates growing loops with ATP but not without ATP. What is supported?

  1. All native loops have the same size
  2. The protein can mediate ATP-dependent loop growth under tested conditions
  3. The protein synthesises DNA
  4. All genomic contacts require this protein
Answer and explanation

B. The assay demonstrates an activity under controlled conditions. It does not establish universal native geometry, DNA synthesis or exclusive responsibility.

9. A mitotic chromosome is abnormally long but DNA content and nucleosome protection are normal. Which defect is most directly consistent?

  1. Failure to copy DNA
  2. Altered higher-order condensin-dependent organisation
  3. Complete histone loss
  4. Absence of DNA itself
Answer and explanation

B. The preserved content and protection argue against replication or core loss. Chromosome shape implicates higher-order organisation, but further tests are needed for specificity.

10. A cell has compact individual chromatids that separate prematurely. Which relationship is primarily disturbed?

  1. Intra-core DNA wrapping
  2. Sister association
  3. DNA contour rise
  4. Chromosome painting probe specificity
Answer and explanation

B. Premature separation concerns the relationship between copies, consistent with cohesion impairment. Individual compaction need not be lost.

11. DNA bridges persist despite visible condensation. Which process may be defective?

  1. Topological resolution by topoisomerase II
  2. DNA base pairing in every nucleosome
  3. Histone H1 synthesis alone
  4. All chromosome painting
Answer and explanation

A. Topoisomerase II helps resolve entanglements. Bridges can have other causes; the observations do not uniquely diagnose the enzyme or support the other claims.

12. In a FISH experiment close loci decline from 80/100 to 40/100 after perturbation. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Close fraction drops by 40 percentage points, a 50% relative decrease
  2. Close fraction drops by 50 percentage points
  3. All loci are now permanently separated
  4. The DNA is necessarily deleted
Answer and explanation

A. 0.8 to 0.4 is a difference of 0.4 and relative drop 0.4/0.8 = 50%. It measures proximity frequency, not permanent separation or deletion.

13. A chromosome shortens twofold while its cross-sectional area doubles. Assume a cylinder and fixed DNA content. Mean DNA concentration:

  1. Doubles
  2. Halves
  3. Stays the same
  4. Increases fourfold
Answer and explanation

C. Volume = area × length is unchanged. Since DNA is fixed, concentration stays constant; only axial packing doubles.

14. An extraction reveals DNA loops around a resistant protein core. Which additional claim would exceed the evidence?

  1. Nonhistone proteins contribute to residual organisation
  2. DNA retains some loop organisation after treatment
  3. An identical immutable scaffold exists in all living nuclei
  4. The extraction conditions matter
Answer and explanation

C. The residue is an operational observation; its presence does not prove a universal static native structure. The other claims are compatible with the experiment.

15. A polytene chromosome develops a local puff with unchanged DNA copy number. Which prediction is reasonable?

  1. Local chromatin becomes more extended and often transcriptionally active
  2. Entire chromosome replicates again
  3. The DNA is necessarily deleted
  4. Every other band becomes a centromere
Answer and explanation

A. A puff reflects local organisation associated with transcription, not necessarily DNA synthesis, deletion or centromere formation.

16. In a solenoid model with 5 nucleosomes per turn, 180 bp per repeat and pitch 15 nm, estimate axial packing ratio.

  1. 6
  2. 20.4
  3. 60
  4. 306
Answer and explanation

B. DNA contour per turn = 5×180×0.34 = 306 nm; divide by 15 = 20.4. 306 is a length; 60 is bp/nm and omits conversion; 6 is unrelated.

17. In the illustrative loop-growth graph, the +ATP trace reaches 10 kb at 4 s and 18 kb at 8 s. The −ATP trace stays at 2 kb. What is the most defensible inference?

  1. The net loop growth rate from 4 to 8 s is 2 kb/s, and the control shows no net growth.
  2. ATP creates new DNA at 2 kb/s.
  3. The graph proves that every native loop is 18 kb long.
  4. The horizontal control proves that DNA never undergoes thermal fluctuations.
Answer and explanation

A. Subtract the sizes and divide by the interval: (18 − 10)/4 = 2 kb/s. This reports redistribution of contour, not synthesis (B); a synthetic trace cannot establish universal native sizes (C), and no net growth does not exclude fluctuations (D).
