1. The packaging problem and selective gene expression

The DNA of a eukaryotic nucleus is both an information store and a physical molecule. The sequence of bases specifies the information that can be copied into RNA, while the arrangement of the DNA with associated proteins determines whether a particular segment can participate in that copying process. The distinction becomes apparent when comparing two differentiated cells of the same organism. A muscle cell and a neuron may possess essentially the same inherited genes, yet each uses only a particular subset extensively. Their differences cannot be explained simply by a list of genes present in their nuclei. We need to ask how the cellular machinery recognizes and gains access to appropriate segments of DNA and why other segments are less available.

A promoter is a DNA region at which the transcription machinery assembles to initiate RNA synthesis. A transcription factor is a protein that recognizes a DNA sequence or helps regulate the assembly and activity of that machinery. On a purified, protein-free DNA molecule, a factor may find its recognition sequence by molecular encounters. Within the nucleus the same recognition sequence may face towards a histone surface or be covered by another protein. Its chemical identity has not changed, but its availability as a binding substrate has changed. A recognition motif is therefore a potential binding site, not a guarantee of actual binding.

Packaging is not an accidental obstruction that a cell must simply remove. DNA needs protection, organization and controlled allocation within the small volume of the nucleus. Packaging also provides an additional level at which inappropriate expression can be prevented. For example, a promoter embedded in an unfavorable nucleosome arrangement may produce little RNA even if a weak activating factor occasionally encounters its recognition sequence. Selectively modifying this local environment can permit expression without exposing the entire genome. The advantage of the system is precisely that DNA can remain organized while particular regions become available at particular times.

Chromatin is the composite material consisting of chromosomal DNA, histones and associated proteins. Its organization varies over a chromosome and can change through time. The term chromatin accessibility refers to the opportunity for other molecules to interact with DNA in its packaged state. It is an operational concept: what is accessible to a small nuclease may not be equally accessible to a large transcription complex. A chromosomal region can allow a factor to bind without allowing all the steps required for productive RNA synthesis. Thus accessibility is related to transcriptional competence but is not identical to transcriptional activity.

We can distinguish at least three barriers to expression. First, a sequence may be buried or otherwise unavailable to a transcription factor. Second, a factor may bind but lack the partners needed to recruit or activate RNA polymerase. Third, an assembled polymerase may fail to proceed efficiently into elongation. Chromatin regulation can affect each barrier. A nucleosome across a promoter may impair initiation, whereas nucleosomes in the gene body present physical obstacles to the moving enzyme. An accessible enhancer can contain the binding sites that allow regulatory proteins to influence a distant promoter, but opening that enhancer is not equivalent to transcribing the associated gene.

The earlier study of gene-specific activators and repressors established that regulatory proteins can recognize cis-regulatory DNA sequences. Chromatin extends that framework rather than replacing it. A DNA-bound activator may recruit enzymes that reposition histones or change their chemistry. A repressor may recruit an enzyme that removes an activating modification or establishes a local arrangement less favorable for initiation. The effect depends on where the regulatory protein binds, which partners it recruits, and the existing condition of the chromatin. A single factor does not necessarily carry an intrinsic universal label of activation or repression independent of the cellular setting.

An important consequence is that a change in gene expression need not involve a mutation in the DNA sequence. The number, placement and chemical state of histones can differ while every nucleotide remains unchanged. Such changes can be relatively rapid and reversible. Some contribute to stable expression programs, but the ability to modify chromatin is not by itself proof of heritable epigenetic memory. Demonstrating persistence through cell division requires additional evidence. This chapter concerns the local molecular mechanisms that change access and transcription; later chapters develop DNA methylation and inheritance of regulatory states separately.

A useful prediction follows from the distinction between DNA sequence and its packaging. If a regulatory recognition sequence is unaltered but a chromatin enzyme is inactivated, binding by the factor could decrease even though the factor itself is normal. A purified DNA-binding assay might consequently show no defect. In the intact nucleus, the same factor could fail to occupy its target. This apparent disagreement between experiments becomes intelligible when the naked DNA assay is recognized as a test of sequence recognition and the nuclear measurement as a test of recognition in a physical and regulatory context.

2. How the chromatin model emerged

The recognition that chromatin changes during gene activity predates the modern molecular explanation. In microscopy, some nuclear regions stain more densely than others. Euchromatin and heterochromatin were originally distinguished using such cytological differences, not by sequencing their histone modifications. A striking example is provided by the giant polytene chromosomes in certain insect tissues, where locally expanded regions called puffs can accompany increased RNA synthesis. The DNA is not replaced at a puff; its packaging and molecular associations change. This observation suggested that the physical arrangement of chromosomes is related to gene use.

Microscopy alone could not identify the molecular cause. A chromosomal segment might become less condensed because transcription has already started, or transcription might begin because condensation has diminished. Both processes might also be triggered independently by a regulatory signal. This distinction between association and cause is central to experimental biology. It motivated studies in which isolated components of chromatin could be manipulated and their effects on transcription measured. A structural observation is often the beginning of a causal investigation, not its conclusion.

In the 1960s Vincent Allfrey and colleagues connected chemical modification of histones with RNA synthesis. Acetylation and methylation showed that chromosomal proteins were chemically variable rather than uniform structural materials. The connection between histone acetylation and active gene expression challenged the idea that transcription could occur only where histones had been completely removed. Nevertheless, an association of acetylation with increased RNA synthesis did not identify the enzyme responsible or establish the direction of causality at an individual gene. A molecular explanation required the identification of the relevant catalytic and regulatory proteins.

Nuclease digestion provided another line of investigation. When chromatin was exposed to an enzyme capable of cutting DNA, a repeating pattern of protected DNA fragments could be detected. The regularity suggested a repeated structural unit shielding part of the DNA from enzyme attack. Together with microscopy and structural studies this work led to the nucleosome model. The regulatory significance of the model is that a gene is not merely packed inside an indiscriminate mass of protein. Individual nucleosomes occupy definable portions of its DNA, making the arrangement of protected and accessible segments experimentally tractable.

Yeast genetics supplied a complementary route to understanding chromatin regulation. Mutants defective in the switching of mating type or use of particular carbon sources helped identify SWI and SNF genes. These genes were not simply alternative names for the DNA-binding proteins that recognized every affected promoter. Their products formed part of a regulatory machinery with a broader capacity to alter chromatin. Biochemical studies subsequently linked the machinery to ATP-dependent changes in nucleosome organization. The genetic and biochemical lines of evidence supported the conclusion that transcription can require an active process for changing the DNA–histone substrate.

A particularly important biochemical result came from Brownell and colleagues in 1996. Histone acetyltransferase A isolated from Tetrahymena was found to be related to Gcn5, a yeast transcriptional regulator. Here, an enzyme that chemically modifies histones was linked to a protein already understood through transcriptional genetics. The result was significant because it connected a long-recognized correlation between acetylation and expression with a plausible molecular mechanism. Subsequent work on Gcn5-containing complexes and other acetyltransferases showed how regulatory proteins can bring chromatin-modifying activities to selected genes.

The two discoveries established complementary principles. ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling alters the arrangement or composition of the DNA–histone complex. Histone modification alters the chemical properties and interaction potential of the associated histone proteins. A cell can use both processes at the same gene and can coordinate their recruitment through sequence-specific regulators. In some contexts a modification promotes binding of a remodeling protein; in others remodeling permits the modification enzyme to act. Neither process can be reduced to a single invariant sequence of events.

The histone code hypothesis articulated by Strahl and Allis in 2000 proposed that combinations of histone modifications could provide information interpreted by chromatin-associated proteins. The analogy to a code is useful because the position and identity of a modification matter. It is limited because the relationship between a mark and the eventual expression level is not as fixed as the relation between an ordinary sense codon and its encoded amino acid. Histone modifications participate in a network of molecular recognition, catalytic activities and local conditions. The same modification can be present at sites with different expression outputs.

Taken together, the historical evidence suggests a hierarchy of experimental strength. Cytological differences establish that chromosomal organization varies. Nuclease protection and structure establish the existence of a physical substrate for local regulation. Genetic loss-of-function experiments identify factors necessary for particular phenotypes. Purification and biochemical reconstitution test what an identified factor can do directly. The strongest explanation usually combines these observations and also considers alternative mechanisms. The scientific story of chromatin is therefore one of increasing causal resolution, from visible chromosome behavior to molecular events.

3. The nucleosome as a regulatory substrate

A nucleosome contains a histone octamer and about 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around it. The octamer contains two copies of each of four core histones, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Histone H1 can associate with linker DNA and nucleosome entry and exit regions, but it is not one of the eight core histones. This distinction is necessary because altering H1 binding and removing a core octamer are different physical events. We will use the nucleosome structure from Unit 2 as the starting point for reasoning about regulation, rather than reconstructing all aspects of chromosome architecture here.

The DNA backbone contains negatively charged phosphate groups, whereas many histone residues, particularly lysine and arginine, contribute positive charge at physiological pH. Electrostatic attraction contributes substantially to DNA binding and is complemented by multiple contacts between DNA and histone surfaces. Wrapping also bends DNA and constrains its movement. The stability of a nucleosome reflects the combined effect of DNA deformation, favorable protein–DNA contacts, protein–protein interactions and the surrounding ionic environment. It cannot be described accurately by one electrostatic interaction alone.

DNA is wrapped around the histone octamer in a superhelical path. A binding site near the point where DNA enters or leaves the particle may become transiently exposed when terminal DNA unwraps. A site near the dyad, the central region of the wrapped DNA, generally requires greater disruption of histone–DNA contacts to become exposed by this mechanism. Thus two copies of an identical transcription-factor motif inserted at different positions in a nucleosome need not have the same effective accessibility. This is an example of a structural constraint acting on otherwise identical sequence information.

The histone proteins possess segments extending outward from the structured core. These segments, frequently called histone tails, include residues that can be modified after protein synthesis. A chemical modification can alter the charge or shape of a residue, affect an interaction between chromatin components, or supply a recognition surface for another protein. Modifications can occur at sites outside the protruding tails as well, so the tails are an important example and not a complete definition of histone modification. The position of each modified residue is relevant to interpreting its effect.

Linker DNA connects neighboring nucleosome cores. Its length and the arrangement of nucleosomes can influence the degree to which local arrays are packed. If an activator recruits a complex that slides a nucleosome away from a promoter, a DNA recognition site can become available without changing the total number of histones in the local array. Conversely, removal of a nucleosome can increase the amount of exposed DNA. These mechanisms are experimentally distinguishable in principle: measuring total histone abundance alone cannot reveal the precise position at which remaining nucleosomes are located.

The term positioning describes the location of a nucleosome relative to the DNA sequence. Its rotational setting describes which side of the DNA helix faces inward or outward at a given location. The term occupancy describes how frequently a sequence is covered by nucleosomes in the population or during an observation interval. A region may have high occupancy but poorly defined positioning if different nucleosomes cover it in different registers. Another region may have lower occupancy but a well-defined position whenever a particle is present. These ideas are often confused because both affect signals obtained from DNA protection experiments.

A nucleosome repeat length is the average distance between equivalent positions on successive nucleosomes, including the DNA associated with the core and intervening linker. Consider an ideal regular array with repeat length 190 base pairs and 147 base pairs wrapped around each core. The average linker length is 43 base pairs in this model. Ten such repeats occupy 1900 base pairs and contain 1470 core-associated base pairs. Approximately 77.4 percent of the repeat DNA is associated with cores. These numbers describe a geometric average, not an experimentally measured fraction of DNA unavailable to factors: some wrapped DNA can become transiently exposed and other proteins may occupy linker regions.

Because the DNA itself varies in sequence and bendability, certain nucleosome positions are energetically more favorable than others. In cells these intrinsic preferences are only one influence. Sequence-specific factors can exclude or stabilize nearby particles; ATP-dependent remodeling can move them away from preferred locations; ongoing transcription can disturb them; and histone deposition can alter occupancy. A position observed in a nucleus is therefore not necessarily the lowest-energy state of a purified DNA and histone system. The cell continually exchanges energy and materials with its environment.

The physical substrate is consequently dynamic even before an external stimulus is considered. Histone–DNA contacts fluctuate, regulatory proteins bind and dissociate, and histones can be exchanged. Chromatin accessibility should be understood as the outcome of these competing processes rather than an all-or-none condition in which DNA is either permanently exposed or permanently hidden. The practical question is whether a particular regulator has enough opportunity to bind and whether its binding survives long enough to affect the transcription machinery.
