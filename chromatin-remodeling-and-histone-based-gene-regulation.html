1. The packaging problem and selective gene expression

The DNA of a eukaryotic nucleus is both an information store and a physical molecule. The sequence of bases specifies the information that can be copied into RNA, while the arrangement of the DNA with associated proteins determines whether a particular segment can participate in that copying process. The distinction becomes apparent when comparing two differentiated cells of the same organism. A muscle cell and a neuron may possess essentially the same inherited genes, yet each uses only a particular subset extensively. Their differences cannot be explained simply by a list of genes present in their nuclei. We need to ask how the cellular machinery recognizes and gains access to appropriate segments of DNA and why other segments are less available.

A promoter is a DNA region at which the transcription machinery assembles to initiate RNA synthesis. A transcription factor is a protein that recognizes a DNA sequence or helps regulate the assembly and activity of that machinery. On a purified, protein-free DNA molecule, a factor may find its recognition sequence by molecular encounters. Within the nucleus the same recognition sequence may face towards a histone surface or be covered by another protein. Its chemical identity has not changed, but its availability as a binding substrate has changed. A recognition motif is therefore a potential binding site, not a guarantee of actual binding.

Packaging is not an accidental obstruction that a cell must simply remove. DNA needs protection, organization and controlled allocation within the small volume of the nucleus. Packaging also provides an additional level at which inappropriate expression can be prevented. For example, a promoter embedded in an unfavorable nucleosome arrangement may produce little RNA even if a weak activating factor occasionally encounters its recognition sequence. Selectively modifying this local environment can permit expression without exposing the entire genome. The advantage of the system is precisely that DNA can remain organized while particular regions become available at particular times.

Chromatin is the composite material consisting of chromosomal DNA, histones and associated proteins. Its organization varies over a chromosome and can change through time. The term chromatin accessibility refers to the opportunity for other molecules to interact with DNA in its packaged state. It is an operational concept: what is accessible to a small nuclease may not be equally accessible to a large transcription complex. A chromosomal region can allow a factor to bind without allowing all the steps required for productive RNA synthesis. Thus accessibility is related to transcriptional competence but is not identical to transcriptional activity.

We can distinguish at least three barriers to expression. First, a sequence may be buried or otherwise unavailable to a transcription factor. Second, a factor may bind but lack the partners needed to recruit or activate RNA polymerase. Third, an assembled polymerase may fail to proceed efficiently into elongation. Chromatin regulation can affect each barrier. A nucleosome across a promoter may impair initiation, whereas nucleosomes in the gene body present physical obstacles to the moving enzyme. An accessible enhancer can contain the binding sites that allow regulatory proteins to influence a distant promoter, but opening that enhancer is not equivalent to transcribing the associated gene.

The earlier study of gene-specific activators and repressors established that regulatory proteins can recognize cis-regulatory DNA sequences. Chromatin extends that framework rather than replacing it. A DNA-bound activator may recruit enzymes that reposition histones or change their chemistry. A repressor may recruit an enzyme that removes an activating modification or establishes a local arrangement less favorable for initiation. The effect depends on where the regulatory protein binds, which partners it recruits, and the existing condition of the chromatin. A single factor does not necessarily carry an intrinsic universal label of activation or repression independent of the cellular setting.

An important consequence is that a change in gene expression need not involve a mutation in the DNA sequence. The number, placement and chemical state of histones can differ while every nucleotide remains unchanged. Such changes can be relatively rapid and reversible. Some contribute to stable expression programs, but the ability to modify chromatin is not by itself proof of heritable epigenetic memory. Demonstrating persistence through cell division requires additional evidence. This chapter concerns the local molecular mechanisms that change access and transcription; later chapters develop DNA methylation and inheritance of regulatory states separately.

A useful prediction follows from the distinction between DNA sequence and its packaging. If a regulatory recognition sequence is unaltered but a chromatin enzyme is inactivated, binding by the factor could decrease even though the factor itself is normal. A purified DNA-binding assay might consequently show no defect. In the intact nucleus, the same factor could fail to occupy its target. This apparent disagreement between experiments becomes intelligible when the naked DNA assay is recognized as a test of sequence recognition and the nuclear measurement as a test of recognition in a physical and regulatory context.

2. How the chromatin model emerged

The recognition that chromatin changes during gene activity predates the modern molecular explanation. In microscopy, some nuclear regions stain more densely than others. Euchromatin and heterochromatin were originally distinguished using such cytological differences, not by sequencing their histone modifications. A striking example is provided by the giant polytene chromosomes in certain insect tissues, where locally expanded regions called puffs can accompany increased RNA synthesis. The DNA is not replaced at a puff; its packaging and molecular associations change. This observation suggested that the physical arrangement of chromosomes is related to gene use.

Microscopy alone could not identify the molecular cause. A chromosomal segment might become less condensed because transcription has already started, or transcription might begin because condensation has diminished. Both processes might also be triggered independently by a regulatory signal. This distinction between association and cause is central to experimental biology. It motivated studies in which isolated components of chromatin could be manipulated and their effects on transcription measured. A structural observation is often the beginning of a causal investigation, not its conclusion.

In the 1960s Vincent Allfrey and colleagues connected chemical modification of histones with RNA synthesis. Acetylation and methylation showed that chromosomal proteins were chemically variable rather than uniform structural materials. The connection between histone acetylation and active gene expression challenged the idea that transcription could occur only where histones had been completely removed. Nevertheless, an association of acetylation with increased RNA synthesis did not identify the enzyme responsible or establish the direction of causality at an individual gene. A molecular explanation required the identification of the relevant catalytic and regulatory proteins.

Nuclease digestion provided another line of investigation. When chromatin was exposed to an enzyme capable of cutting DNA, a repeating pattern of protected DNA fragments could be detected. The regularity suggested a repeated structural unit shielding part of the DNA from enzyme attack. Together with microscopy and structural studies this work led to the nucleosome model. The regulatory significance of the model is that a gene is not merely packed inside an indiscriminate mass of protein. Individual nucleosomes occupy definable portions of its DNA, making the arrangement of protected and accessible segments experimentally tractable.

Yeast genetics supplied a complementary route to understanding chromatin regulation. Mutants defective in the switching of mating type or use of particular carbon sources helped identify SWI and SNF genes. These genes were not simply alternative names for the DNA-binding proteins that recognized every affected promoter. Their products formed part of a regulatory machinery with a broader capacity to alter chromatin. Biochemical studies subsequently linked the machinery to ATP-dependent changes in nucleosome organization. The genetic and biochemical lines of evidence supported the conclusion that transcription can require an active process for changing the DNA–histone substrate.

A particularly important biochemical result came from Brownell and colleagues in 1996. Histone acetyltransferase A isolated from Tetrahymena was found to be related to Gcn5, a yeast transcriptional regulator. Here, an enzyme that chemically modifies histones was linked to a protein already understood through transcriptional genetics. The result was significant because it connected a long-recognized correlation between acetylation and expression with a plausible molecular mechanism. Subsequent work on Gcn5-containing complexes and other acetyltransferases showed how regulatory proteins can bring chromatin-modifying activities to selected genes.

The two discoveries established complementary principles. ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling alters the arrangement or composition of the DNA–histone complex. Histone modification alters the chemical properties and interaction potential of the associated histone proteins. A cell can use both processes at the same gene and can coordinate their recruitment through sequence-specific regulators. In some contexts a modification promotes binding of a remodeling protein; in others remodeling permits the modification enzyme to act. Neither process can be reduced to a single invariant sequence of events.

The histone code hypothesis articulated by Strahl and Allis in 2000 proposed that combinations of histone modifications could provide information interpreted by chromatin-associated proteins. The analogy to a code is useful because the position and identity of a modification matter. It is limited because the relationship between a mark and the eventual expression level is not as fixed as the relation between an ordinary sense codon and its encoded amino acid. Histone modifications participate in a network of molecular recognition, catalytic activities and local conditions. The same modification can be present at sites with different expression outputs.

Taken together, the historical evidence suggests a hierarchy of experimental strength. Cytological differences establish that chromosomal organization varies. Nuclease protection and structure establish the existence of a physical substrate for local regulation. Genetic loss-of-function experiments identify factors necessary for particular phenotypes. Purification and biochemical reconstitution test what an identified factor can do directly. The strongest explanation usually combines these observations and also considers alternative mechanisms. The scientific story of chromatin is therefore one of increasing causal resolution, from visible chromosome behavior to molecular events.

3. The nucleosome as a regulatory substrate

A nucleosome contains a histone octamer and about 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around it. The octamer contains two copies of each of four core histones, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Histone H1 can associate with linker DNA and nucleosome entry and exit regions, but it is not one of the eight core histones. This distinction is necessary because altering H1 binding and removing a core octamer are different physical events. We will use the nucleosome structure from Unit 2 as the starting point for reasoning about regulation, rather than reconstructing all aspects of chromosome architecture here.

The DNA backbone contains negatively charged phosphate groups, whereas many histone residues, particularly lysine and arginine, contribute positive charge at physiological pH. Electrostatic attraction contributes substantially to DNA binding and is complemented by multiple contacts between DNA and histone surfaces. Wrapping also bends DNA and constrains its movement. The stability of a nucleosome reflects the combined effect of DNA deformation, favorable protein–DNA contacts, protein–protein interactions and the surrounding ionic environment. It cannot be described accurately by one electrostatic interaction alone.

DNA is wrapped around the histone octamer in a superhelical path. A binding site near the point where DNA enters or leaves the particle may become transiently exposed when terminal DNA unwraps. A site near the dyad, the central region of the wrapped DNA, generally requires greater disruption of histone–DNA contacts to become exposed by this mechanism. Thus two copies of an identical transcription-factor motif inserted at different positions in a nucleosome need not have the same effective accessibility. This is an example of a structural constraint acting on otherwise identical sequence information.

The histone proteins possess segments extending outward from the structured core. These segments, frequently called histone tails, include residues that can be modified after protein synthesis. A chemical modification can alter the charge or shape of a residue, affect an interaction between chromatin components, or supply a recognition surface for another protein. Modifications can occur at sites outside the protruding tails as well, so the tails are an important example and not a complete definition of histone modification. The position of each modified residue is relevant to interpreting its effect.

Linker DNA connects neighboring nucleosome cores. Its length and the arrangement of nucleosomes can influence the degree to which local arrays are packed. If an activator recruits a complex that slides a nucleosome away from a promoter, a DNA recognition site can become available without changing the total number of histones in the local array. Conversely, removal of a nucleosome can increase the amount of exposed DNA. These mechanisms are experimentally distinguishable in principle: measuring total histone abundance alone cannot reveal the precise position at which remaining nucleosomes are located.

The term positioning describes the location of a nucleosome relative to the DNA sequence. Its rotational setting describes which side of the DNA helix faces inward or outward at a given location. The term occupancy describes how frequently a sequence is covered by nucleosomes in the population or during an observation interval. A region may have high occupancy but poorly defined positioning if different nucleosomes cover it in different registers. Another region may have lower occupancy but a well-defined position whenever a particle is present. These ideas are often confused because both affect signals obtained from DNA protection experiments.

A nucleosome repeat length is the average distance between equivalent positions on successive nucleosomes, including the DNA associated with the core and intervening linker. Consider an ideal regular array with repeat length 190 base pairs and 147 base pairs wrapped around each core. The average linker length is 43 base pairs in this model. Ten such repeats occupy 1900 base pairs and contain 1470 core-associated base pairs. Approximately 77.4 percent of the repeat DNA is associated with cores. These numbers describe a geometric average, not an experimentally measured fraction of DNA unavailable to factors: some wrapped DNA can become transiently exposed and other proteins may occupy linker regions.

Because the DNA itself varies in sequence and bendability, certain nucleosome positions are energetically more favorable than others. In cells these intrinsic preferences are only one influence. Sequence-specific factors can exclude or stabilize nearby particles; ATP-dependent remodeling can move them away from preferred locations; ongoing transcription can disturb them; and histone deposition can alter occupancy. A position observed in a nucleus is therefore not necessarily the lowest-energy state of a purified DNA and histone system. The cell continually exchanges energy and materials with its environment.

The physical substrate is consequently dynamic even before an external stimulus is considered. Histone–DNA contacts fluctuate, regulatory proteins bind and dissociate, and histones can be exchanged. Chromatin accessibility should be understood as the outcome of these competing processes rather than an all-or-none condition in which DNA is either permanently exposed or permanently hidden. The practical question is whether a particular regulator has enough opportunity to bind and whether its binding survives long enough to affect the transcription machinery.

4. Accessibility, occupancy and the probability of binding

When a protein binds to a recognition sequence in chromatin, the first physical requirement is that its DNA-contacting surface can approach the required bases. Consider a regulatory site close to the edge of a nucleosome. Thermal motion can weaken some histone–DNA contacts temporarily, exposing enough DNA to permit recognition. When the factor binds, it can stabilize the exposed configuration because returning to the original wrapped state would require disruption of the new factor–DNA interaction. A factor can therefore influence chromatin organization even before it recruits a catalytic chromatin enzyme.

This idea can be described using a simplified equilibrium model. Suppose a site exists in two states, closed and open, and only the open state can bind a factor. Let Gclosed and Gopen be the free energies of the two states in the absence of the factor. Define ΔG as Gopen minus Gclosed. At equilibrium the ratio of probabilities of open and closed states is exp(−ΔG/RT), where R is the gas constant and T is the absolute temperature. The open fraction is consequently 1/[1 + exp(ΔG/RT)]. The result follows by writing the ratio as Popen/(1 − Popen) and rearranging. This is a model of passive fluctuations, not a universal formula for ATP-driven chromatin.

For a hypothetical site with ΔG = 5.8 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K, RT is approximately 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹. The exponent is 2.34 and the open fraction is about 0.088. Reducing the free-energy difference to 2.9 kJ mol⁻¹ gives an exponent of 1.17 and an open fraction of approximately 0.237. The change in opening probability is about 2.7-fold, despite only a modest change in the free-energy difference. Small changes to physical interactions can therefore generate substantial changes in the opportunity for binding. The numbers are illustrative teaching values, not measured nucleosome free energies.

A second step is binding to the exposed recognition sequence. Suppose a transcription factor has a free concentration F, its dissociation constant for the open site is Kd, and the exposed and unexposed states can be approximated as a fixed mixture. Among exposed sites, the equilibrium fraction occupied by the factor is F/(F + Kd). If accessibility is represented by a, the simple approximation for overall occupancy is aF/(F + Kd). For a = 0.2 and F = Kd, the fraction is 0.1; for a = 0.8 with the same concentration and affinity, it becomes 0.4. Under these assumptions, altered access produces a fourfold increase in binding without changing the sequence recognition constant of the factor.

The fixed-mixture assumption can fail because factor binding itself can shift the wrapping equilibrium. If the closed state has statistical weight 1, the open unbound state has weight L = exp(−ΔG/RT), and the open bound state has weight LF/Kd, then the normalized probability of the bound state is LF/Kd divided by 1 + L + LF/Kd. In this model a sufficiently high concentration of factor can favor the exposed bound configuration. The two formulations answer different experimental questions: one approximates a fixed accessible subpopulation, whereas the other explicitly allows binding to redistribute equilibrium states. Neither includes ATP-dependent work or multiple interacting factors.

A nucleosome can also create a restriction that depends on orientation. An outward-facing DNA surface may be approachable even while other surfaces remain constrained. A change in position by several base pairs can change the direction in which a motif faces relative to the histone octamer. Consequently, moving a nucleosome a short distance can alter factor binding without necessarily uncovering the entire motif in the usual sense. This is why the concepts of positioning and accessibility cannot be used interchangeably. A nucleotide protection pattern is informative but may not capture the geometry required by a particular factor.

Some factors are unusually capable of recognizing motifs within nucleosomal DNA. Such factors are often described as pioneer transcription factors when they can engage relatively inaccessible chromatin and promote the subsequent establishment of a regulatory region accessible to other proteins. They do not all bind every nucleosome with equal efficiency. The sequence, rotational orientation, histone contacts and cell-specific partners matter. In certain circumstances a pioneer factor recognizes only part of its usual motif while the remainder remains constrained by packaging. In others the interaction is assisted by transient DNA exposure.

Consider an enhancer that contains two motifs, one for a pioneer factor and one for a conventional activator. Initially both lie in a region with low accessibility. The pioneer factor can engage its own motif, recruit additional proteins and make the neighboring site more available. The conventional activator subsequently binds and helps influence the promoter. Inactivation of the pioneer factor may leave the conventional activator fully functional on naked DNA while greatly reducing its binding at the enhancer. Inactivation of the conventional activator may leave enhancer accessibility relatively high but reduce the final expression response. This pair of predictions distinguishes the functions of the two factors in a hypothetical sequential model.

Factors can cooperate without making direct protein–protein contact. If binding of one factor increases local exposure of the DNA recognized by another, the second factor gains a higher probability of binding. The nucleosome itself provides the physical coupling. This is distinct from direct cooperativity, in which protein surfaces interact and stabilize the joint complex. Both mechanisms can occur together. If the cooperative effect disappears on naked DNA, competition with nucleosomes is one plausible explanation, but the result does not rule out all other forms of interaction. The geometry and experimental design determine which conclusion is defensible.

The local environment extends beyond the single particle. Adjacent nucleosomes can contact one another, linker-associated proteins can stabilize compact arrangements, and proteins bound at nearby sequences can promote or obstruct encounters with the target. Accessibility measurements are consequently averages over a collection of possible structures. A twofold difference between conditions might reflect a shift in the fraction of cells in an open state, a change in the frequency of transient openings within each cell, or a combination of both. Bulk measurements generally cannot resolve those explanations by themselves.

A further distinction concerns the difference between binding and transcriptional throughput. A highly accessible promoter may remain inactive if its activator is absent. A promoter bound by a transcription factor may produce little RNA if the factor lacks a coactivator. A promoter occupied by RNA polymerase may not release that enzyme efficiently into productive transcription. If an experiment shows that factor binding increases after a chromatin change but RNA synthesis does not, the result does not refute increased accessibility; it suggests that another step is limiting. This logic will recur throughout the chapter.

5. ATP-dependent remodeling: moving a physical barrier

Passive fluctuations allow molecules to sample different configurations, but cells also need controlled changes that do not depend entirely on the spontaneous exposure of DNA. ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers are protein complexes that use chemical energy from ATP to alter the placement, stability or composition of nucleosomes. The catalytic subunit generally belongs to the Snf2 family of ATPases. It interacts with DNA and converts energy released during its nucleotide cycle into changes in the DNA–protein substrate. Associated subunits determine many aspects of substrate recognition, regulation and cellular targeting.

These ATPases are related to helicase-like motors, but the appropriate regulatory model is DNA translocation rather than obligatory separation of the double helix into single strands. A motor can move duplex DNA relative to the histone surface while the octamer remains associated with the substrate. Small local disruptions of contacts permit DNA to move along the histone surface. Repeated catalytic cycles can accumulate a change in the position of the DNA relative to the octamer. The precise microscopic intermediates differ among remodeling complexes and are not required to predict their basic regulatory outcomes.

An elementary catalytic cycle begins when a remodeling complex encounters an appropriate substrate. DNA and histone features help stabilize the bound enzyme; some complexes also require suitable linker lengths or additional recognition partners. ATP binding changes the conformation of the motor. Hydrolysis and product release support progression through the catalytic cycle, with coordinated contacts allowing the enzyme to generate directed movement or rearrangement of the substrate. The association may persist for several cycles or end after a smaller change, depending on the enzyme and conditions. This sequence describes the general energy-coupling principle rather than asserting an identical order of every conformational event for all families.

Consider a promoter with a nucleosome centered across its essential recognition sequence. A remodeler bound to that particle can reposition it along adjacent DNA. If the nucleosome moves far enough away, the promoter becomes accessible to a transcription factor or general initiation machinery. The total amount of histone in the local region can remain approximately constant. The important difference is the overlap between nucleosome-bound DNA and the regulatory sequence. A nuclease protection assay may therefore reveal a shift in protected fragments even if a measurement of total histone protein detects no change.

Sliding is not the only possible outcome. In nucleosome eviction the histone core, or parts of it during disassembly, is removed from its original DNA association. This can produce a region with lower occupancy. Histone exchange is another outcome: a histone or histone dimer is replaced, often with the aid of specific chaperones or associated exchange activities. The DNA may remain in nucleosomal form while the molecular properties of the particle change. These outcomes must be distinguished from chemical modification, in which a covalently attached group on a histone is added or removed without necessarily changing the position or identity of the histone molecule.

The consequences of sliding depend entirely on direction and context. Movement away from a transcription start region may facilitate initiation, whereas movement into a critical regulatory region can impede it. An ATP-dependent enzyme is therefore not intrinsically a gene activator simply because it remodels chromatin. Some remodeling complexes organize regular arrays and contribute to repression or suppression of inappropriate initiation. Others can support activated transcription at a target. The same family can contain complexes with distinct cellular functions, and a single complex can have different consequences at different loci.

ATP hydrolysis provides a way to maintain arrangements that would not be favored by a passive equilibrium of purified DNA and histones. The cellular free energy of ATP hydrolysis depends on concentrations of ATP, ADP and inorganic phosphate. In the standard transformed biochemical convention the standard free-energy change is about −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹; the actual cellular value can be more negative. A motor couples only part of that free-energy change to useful mechanical or conformational work, with some dissipated into the environment. It is incorrect to assume that the ATP energy is entirely converted into breaking one particular histone–DNA bond.

As an idealized energy-accounting example, suppose the magnitude of the hydrolysis free energy is 50 kJ mol⁻¹ under stated conditions and a motor hydrolyzes 12 ATP molecules per completed event. The available chemical free energy per mole of such events is 12 × 50 = 600 kJ. If a model assigns 25 percent of this energy to useful work, the work is 150 kJ mol⁻¹ of events. Per single event this is approximately 2.49 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, obtained by dividing 150,000 J mol⁻¹ by Avogadro's constant. These assumptions do not establish the ATP stoichiometry or efficiency of any real remodeler. Their purpose is to distinguish an energy budget from measured mechanistic behavior.

ATP dependence can be tested by comparing nucleosome positioning in reactions with and without hydrolyzable ATP. If a purified complex moves a nucleosome only when hydrolyzable ATP is present, ATP-dependent remodeling is a plausible interpretation. However, ATP could influence other components if the preparation contains multiple enzymes. A catalytic mutation in the motor and a control showing that substrate binding persists help isolate the role of motor activity. A nonhydrolyzable ATP analogue may be useful in distinguishing nucleotide binding from hydrolysis, although different analogues can produce different conformations or affect binding differently.

Loss of ATP-dependent remodeling in an intact cell need not cause all nucleosomes to become stationary. Passive fluctuations continue, and other remodelers may remain active. Transcription itself can alter chromatin, as can histone deposition and exchange. A selective mutation in one ATPase can therefore change a specific subset of promoters while leaving many other loci relatively unaffected. Conversely, depleting ATP globally interferes with many essential reactions besides chromatin remodeling, including transcription-related and metabolic processes. A reduction in transcription after global ATP depletion is not sufficient evidence for a specific chromatin mechanism.

The relation between motor activity and targeting is another essential distinction. A complex may remodel a generic nucleosome normally in a purified assay but fail to regulate a particular gene because it cannot interact with the appropriate transcription factor. A mutant catalytic subunit may still be recruited to the promoter but fail to move its nucleosomes. These two mutations can produce similar transcriptional phenotypes with different occupancy patterns of the remodeler itself. An experiment that separately measures complex recruitment, nucleosome location and nascent RNA can distinguish the targeting defect from the catalytic defect.

6. Four major families of chromatin remodelers

The classification of ATP-dependent remodelers into major families reflects relationships among their motor proteins and associated functional domains. SWI/SNF, ISWI, CHD and INO80-related remodelers are the four conventional families encountered in standard accounts. They share a capacity to couple ATP-dependent changes in conformation to the nucleosomal substrate, but differ in their usual outcomes, interactions and targeting mechanisms. Family names should guide recognition of representative functions, not encourage the assumption that every member behaves identically in every tissue or organism.

SWI/SNF takes its name from genetic studies of switching and sucrose nonfermentation in budding yeast. Related complexes in other eukaryotes include mammalian BAF and PBAF assemblies. Representative SWI/SNF complexes can alter nucleosome positioning and can promote eviction under suitable circumstances. At many genes this helps expose promoters or enhancers and supports the action of transcriptional activators. Their activity depends not only on the ATPase but also on accessory proteins that influence recruitment and interaction with the chromatin substrate.

A useful model is an activator that binds a regulatory element adjacent to a nucleosome-covered promoter and recruits SWI/SNF. The motor rearranges the nucleosomal DNA so that the basal transcriptional machinery can engage more effectively. A mutation removing the activator's recruitment domain may prevent this local change while preserving its ability to recognize naked DNA. A mutation that inactivates the SWI/SNF ATPase could instead allow recruitment without productive movement. If the promoter is made accessible through an independent intervention, either defect might be partly bypassed, depending on whether additional coactivator functions are also missing.

ISWI, the imitation SWI family, contains complexes well known for nucleosome sliding and spacing. A spacing enzyme acts on a substrate that may have unequal linker lengths and adjusts the position of nucleosomes relative to adjacent DNA. In a regular array the distribution of linker lengths can become narrower even if the total number of nucleosomes stays constant. This role can support chromatin assembly and organization following replication. At individual genes ISWI-family complexes can also contribute to regulatory changes, so spacing should be regarded as a representative biochemical function rather than a universal transcriptional sign.

Consider four nucleosome cores arranged over a linear DNA segment with successive linker lengths of 15, 70 and 20 base pairs. If a spacing activity redistributes the cores to produce approximately 35 base pairs between each pair of neighbors while preserving the positions of the outer boundaries in an idealized model, the amount of core-associated DNA has not changed. The distances between internal positions have become more uniform. An assay sensitive to regular spacing could report a stronger periodic signal despite an unchanged histone count. The outcome cannot be inferred from average linker length alone because the initial and final averages are both 35 base pairs.

CHD proteins are named for chromodomain helicase DNA-binding features. This family includes complexes that can help organize nucleosomes and support transcriptional processes, as well as members of repressive assemblies. CHD1 in yeast is an important example associated with transcription and organization of gene-body nucleosomes. CHD3 and CHD4 in animals provide examples of remodeling subunits in NuRD, a complex linking ATP-dependent nucleosome remodeling with histone deacetylation. These contrasting examples are especially useful because they refute the claim that all members of one remodeler family necessarily have an activating or repressive output.

The INO80-related family includes complexes involved in nucleosome remodeling and histone variant dynamics. The yeast SWR1 complex provides a classic example of ATP-dependent replacement of canonical H2A–H2B dimers with dimers containing H2A.Z and H2B. H2A.Z can occur near promoters and participate in regulation, but its presence is not an unconditional indication of gene activation. INO80-family activities also contribute to other processes involving chromatin. For this chapter the central idea is that exchanging a histone variant can change the behavior of a nucleosome without removing all histones from the DNA.

Histone variants and chemical histone modifications are different categories. A variant is an alternative protein sequence encoded by a histone gene; an acetylated or methylated form is a chemically modified state of a protein molecule. A variant may itself carry covalent modifications. Thus exchange changes which protein molecule is part of the chromatin substrate, whereas an acetyltransferase changes a chemical feature of an existing protein. A regulatory phenotype caused by failure to deposit H2A.Z should not be described as failure to acetylate H2A, even when both processes influence promoter activity.

Comparative reasoning about the families is strongest when based on what an enzyme does to a defined substrate. A position shift without loss of histone can indicate sliding. More uniform distances between nucleosomes suggest spacing. Replacement of labeled canonical histone with a variant suggests exchange, provided controls exclude independent deposition onto naked DNA. Increased exposure of DNA accompanied by loss of the core suggests eviction. More than one family can produce a given outcome, however, so a biochemical result may identify a process without uniquely identifying a family. Genetic evidence or direct identification of complex components is needed to establish the enzyme involved.

Accessory subunits are particularly relevant when interpreting cell-specific outcomes. Two complexes can possess related ATPase catalytic domains but use different recognition proteins. One may be recruited by an activator and another by a repressor; one may engage a modified histone tail and another a characteristic DNA or linker configuration. This diversification allows evolution to reuse the basic motor mechanism in different regulatory systems. It also explains why loss of an accessory subunit can affect a smaller set of genes than loss of a shared catalytic subunit, although the exact phenotype depends on how the complex is assembled and whether other subunits compensate.

The four families provide a useful conceptual framework for examination problems, but they must be connected to the physical and chemical state of the substrate rather than treated as four disconnected names. When a question specifies nucleosome movement, regular spacing, replacement by a histone variant or coordinated deacetylation, identify the actual change being measured first. Then select the representative complex consistent with that change, taking account of additional information provided by the question. If several complexes are plausible, the correct scientific response is to identify the missing discriminating evidence rather than invent a unique assignment.

7. Histone acetylation: changing charge and molecular recognition

Histone acetylation is the covalent addition of an acetyl group to an appropriate amino group. In the context of gene regulation the most familiar substrates are the side-chain amino groups of lysine residues in histones. Lysine contains a terminal amino group that is normally protonated and positively charged under physiological conditions. Attachment of an acetyl group forms an amide, whose nitrogen is not positively charged in the same way. This chemical transformation removes one positive charge from the modified lysine side chain. It does not remove the lysine residue, alter the histone gene sequence or cleave the DNA backbone.

A histone acetyltransferase, often abbreviated HAT and more generally referred to as a lysine acetyltransferase, transfers an acetyl group from acetyl coenzyme A to a lysine. The products are acetylated lysine and coenzyme A. Acetyl-CoA supplies the activated acetyl group through its thioester linkage. A histone deacetylase, abbreviated HDAC, catalyzes removal of the acetyl modification. Different deacetylase classes achieve this chemistry through different mechanisms. Classical metal-dependent HDACs use hydrolysis, whereas the sirtuin family uses NAD⁺ in a distinct deacetylation reaction. Acetylation and deacetylation can therefore be regulated independently through enzyme recruitment and substrate availability.

The first regulatory consequence of acetylation is a change in electrostatics. The positively charged side chains of histones can interact with the negatively charged DNA backbone and with charged regions of neighboring nucleosomes. Neutralization of selected lysine side chains can change these interactions and the extent to which local chromatin adopts compact arrangements. Histone H4 lysine 16 acetylation is a particularly instructive example because it can reduce interactions between nucleosomes in defined arrays. This illustrates how a modification on one particle can change the behavior of a group of particles without requiring all nucleosomes to dissociate.

It would nevertheless be incorrect to reason that every acetylation event necessarily causes the entire nucleosome to fall apart. An octamer makes numerous contacts with DNA and contains many residues that are not affected by acetylating one site. Even a highly acetylated chromatin region can retain nucleosomes. Individual sites vary in their contributions to structural interactions, and some changes influence transcription primarily by altering protein recognition rather than by producing a large electrostatic effect. The term open chromatin therefore should not be interpreted as histone-free DNA throughout the region.

The second regulatory consequence is the creation of recognition surfaces for acetyllysine-binding proteins. A bromodomain is a protein domain that can recognize an acetylated lysine in a suitable sequence and structural context. Bromodomain-containing proteins include components of transcriptional regulatory complexes and some remodeling complexes. Recognition can help retain these proteins at selected chromatin regions. Thus the initial covalent modification can be translated into recruitment of an additional regulatory activity. The resulting transcriptional change may depend more strongly on the recruited protein than on the direct physical effect of charge neutralization.

For instance, suppose an activator recruits an acetyltransferase to a particular enhancer. Acetylated histones accumulate locally because the rate of addition exceeds the rate of removal. A bromodomain-containing co-regulator is then recruited more efficiently. If that protein supports productive transcription, the acetylation contributes indirectly to increased RNA synthesis. Mutating the bromodomain might reduce the transcriptional response while leaving acetylation high. In this hypothetical case, measuring the modification alone would suggest that the first step of the pathway remains functional, but it would not indicate that the entire regulatory pathway succeeds.

Representative acetyltransferases include Gcn5 in yeast and p300/CBP in animals. Gcn5-containing complexes provide a useful connection between gene-specific regulatory proteins and catalytic modification of histones. p300 and CBP are coactivators that possess acetyltransferase activity and can participate in transcriptional regulation at selected enhancers and promoters. They also act on substrates other than histones. Therefore, a change in transcription following inhibition of a p300/CBP catalytic activity cannot automatically be attributed to a particular histone modification. The possible effects on nonhistone regulatory proteins must be considered.

The location of an acetylated residue carries additional information. H3K27ac denotes acetylation of lysine 27 on histone H3. H3K9ac denotes acetylation of lysine 9 on histone H3. These are frequently encountered marks in descriptions of active chromatin. H3K27ac is commonly enriched at active enhancers and promoters, whereas the exact distributions of histone acetylation depend on the regulatory system. A student should understand the notation: H3 identifies the histone, K identifies lysine, the number is its residue position, and ac identifies the acetyl modification. The number does not refer to a position on DNA.

A deacetylase can counteract an acetyltransferase by removing its product, but it does not necessarily act at the same sites or at the same time. Repressors can recruit HDAC-containing complexes to regulatory regions. Local removal of acetyl groups can reduce the binding opportunities of some acetyllysine readers and favor a chromatin environment less compatible with transcription. A particular HDAC can also help maintain the appropriate chromatin organization in an actively transcribed gene body, where unrestricted initiation from internal sequences would be undesirable. Hence the usual association of histone acetylation with activation does not make every deacetylase a universal inhibitor of all useful transcription.

Inhibition of an HDAC illustrates the importance of rate balance. If acetyltransferases continue to act while deacetylation decreases, the fraction of acetylated substrates may rise. The resulting effects on individual genes depend on their original regulatory states and on which proteins respond to the modifications. Some may become more active, some may remain unchanged, and some may become less active indirectly because another regulatory gene has changed expression. A global increase in acetylated histone abundance is not evidence that every promoter has become more active. Locus-specific measurements are needed for the stronger claim.

Chemical substitutions in a histone provide a common teaching model. Replacing lysine with arginine can preserve a positively charged side chain while preventing acetylation at the original lysine position. Replacing lysine with glutamine gives an uncharged side chain and is sometimes used as a rough approximation to acetylated lysine. Neither substitution reproduces the native modified state exactly. Arginine and lysine differ in shape and interactions, and glutamine does not display the complete acetyllysine chemical group recognized by a bromodomain. Consequently, a phenotype of a K-to-Q substitution cannot by itself prove that the natural acetylation recruits a reader.

The dependence of acetylation on acetyl-CoA creates a plausible connection between metabolic conditions and gene regulation. A cell must have the relevant metabolic substrate and enzyme, and the enzyme must encounter suitable lysines. Increasing acetyl-CoA availability may influence acetylation in some settings, but a single measurement of cellular acetyl-CoA cannot identify which promoter will respond. Local recruitment, compartmentalization, competing metabolic reactions and deacetylation remain relevant. The biological principle is that metabolism supplies chemical resources for regulation, while the regulatory system determines where and how those resources are used.

8. Histone methylation: residue, degree and context

Methylation of histones is another form of covalent protein modification. Histone lysine methyltransferases transfer methyl groups to suitable lysine residues, generally using S-adenosylmethionine, abbreviated SAM, as the methyl donor. S-adenosylhomocysteine is produced during the transfer reaction. Lysine side chains can acquire one, two or three methyl groups, giving mono-, di- and trimethylation. Histone arginine residues can also be methylated, but the principal regulatory examples in this chapter concern lysine modifications. The chemical identities of the modified substrates must remain distinct when interpreting examination questions.

Unlike acetylation, lysine methylation does not generally neutralize the positive charge of lysine. The chemical addition changes the shape, hydrophobic features and interaction potential of the side chain while retaining a positive charge under usual physiological conditions. Trimethyllysine is a quaternary ammonium group with a permanent positive charge. It follows that methylation cannot be assigned the same general electrostatic mechanism as acetylation. Its effects on gene regulation often involve recognition by proteins sensitive to the position and degree of methylation.

In H3K4me3, the designation me3 identifies trimethylation of lysine 4 on histone H3. H3K4me1 refers to monomethylation of the same residue, not to methylation of another histone or to a modification on the first copy of H3 in the nucleosome. Because the octamer contains two H3 molecules, a particular nucleosome could in principle contain distinct modification states on those copies. Measurements of an average modification abundance do not normally specify the full chemical state of every nucleosome. Interpreting signals requires attention to the resolution of the measurement.

H3K4me3 is a widely encountered mark enriched near transcription start sites of active genes. Methyltransferase complexes related to Set1/COMPASS provide representative writers of H3K4 methylation. Certain protein domains can recognize H3K4 methylation and contribute to interactions with transcription-associated machinery. However, H3K4me3 may be deposited or maintained as a consequence of transcription-associated processes as well as contributing to transcription. The observation of the mark near a promoter therefore does not establish by itself that the mark was the initial activating cause.

H3K4me1 is frequently associated with enhancer regions in mammalian cells. Some active enhancers also carry H3K27ac, whereas an enhancer with H3K4me1 but relatively little H3K27ac may have a different regulatory status. This distinction is helpful in comparing chromatin profiles, but neither mark is a unique biochemical definition of an enhancer. A functional enhancer must influence a target promoter in an appropriate experimental or biological setting. A segment bearing a typical enhancer-associated modification may fail to activate a reporter if essential transcription factors or promoter contacts are missing.

H3K36me3 is often enriched within the bodies of actively transcribed genes. In budding yeast, Set2 is coupled to elongating RNA polymerase II and deposits H3K36 methylation during transcription. The mark can help recruit a deacetylase complex, Rpd3S, that limits inappropriate initiation from internal gene regions. This example is important because the mark is associated with active gene-body transcription while its downstream effect can include repression of undesirable local transcription initiation. Describing H3K36me3 as simply activating without identifying the process being measured misses the biological meaning of the mark.

H3K9me3 is characteristic of many forms of repressive heterochromatin. Histone methyltransferases of the SUV39 family provide well-known examples of writers of this mark in animals. Heterochromatin protein 1, commonly called HP1, has a chromodomain that recognizes H3K9 methylation in appropriate contexts. Its other interactions can support local organization and recruitment of additional factors. This provides a molecular explanation for how a covalent modification can contribute to a chromatin environment that is relatively unfavorable for many transcriptional activities. It does not imply that every methylated nucleosome becomes physically impenetrable.

The recognition of H3K9 methylation by HP1 illustrates how local chromatin regulation can become self-reinforcing. A methylated histone recruits a protein capable of participating in an interaction network that favors recruitment or stabilization of methyltransferase activity at nearby chromatin. Newly modified neighboring nucleosomes can supply additional recognition sites. Such feedback can help maintain or extend a local regulatory domain. Its action is limited by opposing enzymes, sequence-bound proteins, changes in substrate and other features that restrict inappropriate expansion into adjacent regions. The molecular feedback is not by itself proof that the entire domain will be transmitted faithfully to daughter cells.

H3K27me3 is another important repressive mark but belongs to a distinguishable regulatory system. Polycomb repressive complex 2, or PRC2, contains a catalytic subunit such as EZH2 that can methylate H3K27 in animals. Polycomb-related regulation contributes to the controlled repression of developmental genes. Other Polycomb complexes participate in the organization of repressed chromatin and can catalyze other modifications, including histone H2A monoubiquitylation in representative PRC1 complexes. A question asking which complex writes H3K27me3 should not be answered with the name of the reader HP1 merely because both are associated with gene repression.

H3K27 acetylation and H3K27 methylation modify the same lysine side chain. The residue cannot simultaneously carry an acetyl group and a trimethyllysine state. Switching the modification of a particular lysine requires removal of the existing incompatible group or replacement of the modified histone before the alternative state can be established. At the scale of a gene region, however, a mixture of nucleosomes with different modification states is possible. Observing both H3K27ac and H3K27me3 in a tissue sample is therefore not a chemical contradiction. They may be found at different loci, different nucleosomes, different histone copies or different cells.

A frequently discussed example of combined regulatory marks is the bivalent promoter, which can display enrichment of both H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 in certain developmental settings. These modifications occupy different lysine residues, and their association can occur within a chromatin region without requiring simultaneous incompatible modifications at one side chain. A promoter carrying both marks may have low transcription despite possessing a mark often associated with activity. Such a state demonstrates why identifying one mark is not sufficient to predict a unique output. Bulk measurements also require care before assigning the two marks to the same nucleosome or the same cell.

Histone methylation is not irreversible. Lysine demethylases can remove methyl groups from appropriate substrates. The enzyme LSD1 is a classical example that uses an FAD-dependent reaction and can demethylate mono- and dimethylated lysine substrates but not trimethyllysine through that mechanism. JmjC-domain demethylases use oxygen, ferrous iron and 2-oxoglutarate in oxidative reactions, and suitable members can act on trimethylated lysines. The enzymes differ in their site and degree specificity. A question about loss of one demethylase should specify its substrate rather than assume that all histone methylation increases uniformly.

The distinction between histone methylation and DNA methylation is essential. DNA methylation modifies a nucleotide base, commonly cytosine in the eukaryotic examples considered in the next chapter. Histone methylation modifies an amino acid residue in a chromosomal protein. The two processes can interact because proteins recruited by one type of mark may influence the establishment of the other. They remain separate chemical transformations with different substrates, enzymes and immediate recognition mechanisms. An increase in H3K9me3 cannot by itself establish that the DNA cytosines at that locus are methylated.

The general conclusion is not that methylation has no predictive value, but that the identity of the residue and its biological context are part of the prediction. H3K4me3, H3K36me3, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are useful landmarks because they are repeatedly associated with characteristic regulatory environments. Their presence becomes more informative when combined with knowledge of promoter or gene-body position, transcriptional activity, histone occupancy and the relevant reader and writer proteins. It is this combination of information that allows a mechanistic interpretation rather than a memorized binary label.

9. Writers, erasers and readers as a regulatory system

The terms writer, eraser and reader describe three functional roles in chromatin regulation. A writer catalyzes establishment of a particular chemical modification. An eraser removes or reverses that modification through an appropriate chemical reaction. A reader recognizes the modified substrate and can influence a downstream process by binding or recruiting additional proteins. These terms identify functions rather than rigid classes of whole proteins. A multi-subunit complex can contain more than one role, and a single protein can combine catalytic and recognition domains.

A writer does not necessarily select a unique gene through its own catalytic site. The active site recognizes a chemical substrate, such as a histone lysine in an appropriate local sequence. Other parts of the protein or complex can interact with sequence-specific transcription factors, existing chromatin features or additional regulators that guide it to a particular chromosomal region. A catalytic mutation can thus prevent writing at every site reached by the enzyme, whereas a mutation in a targeting interface might leave the enzyme active in vitro but reduce writing at a specific set of genes. The difference is analogous to the distinction between substrate specificity and cellular localization.

An eraser can determine how long a modification survives after a writer has departed. The abundance of a mark is not simply a record of how much writer is present. It reflects the balance of addition, removal and replacement of the modified protein. Even if the writer activity remains constant, increased eraser activity or deposition of unmodified histones can reduce the fraction of modified sites. Conversely, reducing an eraser can increase a mark without increased recruitment of its writer. An experiment that measures the final mark alone cannot distinguish those kinetic alternatives.

The reader provides a bridge between a modification and another function. Bromodomains commonly recognize acetylated lysine, while chromodomains and certain PHD fingers provide important examples of methylation-sensitive recognition domains. Not every member of a domain family recognizes the same modification. For instance, the H3K9 methylation recognition characteristic of HP1 should not be assigned to every chromodomain-containing protein. The sequence surrounding the modified residue, the degree of methylation and contacts with other parts of the substrate can all influence affinity.

Imagine an enhancer where a writer, an eraser and a reader are all present. If the writer is inactivated, the relevant modification may decay as the eraser continues to work and the histones turn over. Reader occupancy may subsequently decrease because the binding substrate is reduced. If the reader is inactivated instead, the modification may remain abundant while the downstream transcriptional response fails. If the eraser is selectively inhibited, the mark may increase, but the response may saturate if the reader is already fully occupied. These distinguishable outcomes provide a rational basis for interpreting genetic and biochemical perturbations.

The language of histone modifications also includes interactions between different marks. A modification can alter the ability of another enzyme to recognize a nearby residue. Alternatively, a protein may contain separate interaction domains that preferentially engage a combination of chemical features. Multiple weak interactions can collectively increase retention at a chromatin substrate, whereas an incompatible feature can weaken an otherwise favorable interaction. These mechanisms explain why the histone code hypothesis emphasizes combinations and context rather than treating each mark as an independent instruction with a predetermined transcriptional output.

Regulatory interpretation requires distinguishing recognition from catalysis. A bromodomain-containing protein may bind acetylated histones without acetylating them. Conversely, a catalytic acetyltransferase may act on a histone even if it lacks a reader for the product. When a complex possesses both functions, a preexisting modification may stabilize the writer-containing complex and promote further local modification. This is a possible form of feedback. The catalytic activity still requires suitable substrates and cofactors, and the reader interaction does not substitute for those chemical requirements.

A local feedback loop can produce a strong response from a small initial difference. Suppose a sequence-specific factor recruits a writer to an enhancer. The resulting mark recruits a reader-containing complex that also helps retain the writer or recruits additional copies of the same activity. Local writing may then increase faster than it would through the initial transcription factor alone. Opposing erasers and limited substrate prevent indefinite accumulation. A positive feedback loop of this kind can support persistence, yet it need not produce two stable states under all conditions. Bistability depends on the quantitative properties of the feedback and competing removal processes.

One can investigate the order of events by comparing loss-of-function and rescue experiments. A catalytically inactive writer that still binds its normal targeting partner can test whether writing is necessary after recruitment. A wild-type reader and a recognition-defective reader can be compared at equal expression levels to test the importance of mark recognition. A downstream regulatory activity artificially localized to the DNA may partly bypass the need for the reader. Such a bypass would support the proposed recruitment role but would not establish that all endogenous functions of the reader have been restored.

A decisive lesson is that a chromatin mark is neither an autonomous regulatory agent nor a mere decorative label. Its chemical state influences interactions in a particular molecular environment. Some marks participate causally in recruiting and stabilizing regulators, while others may primarily record the activity of an associated pathway in a given experiment. A single mark can even fulfill different roles at different loci. The most reliable approach is to identify what writes it, what removes it, which proteins recognize it, what processes those proteins control and which experimental observations establish the direction of causality.

10. Chromatin states and the organization of transcription

Euchromatin and heterochromatin describe broad patterns of chromosome organization. Euchromatin tends to be relatively dispersed during interphase and contains many genes capable of transcription. Heterochromatin tends to be more compact, with regulatory conditions that suppress many forms of transcription. The words originated in cytology and must not be reduced to a single histone modification. A segment classified as euchromatic can contain inactive genes, and heterochromatin can support specialized transcription where the relevant machinery is present. The difference concerns characteristic molecular environments rather than absolute prohibitions.

Constitutive heterochromatin is typically associated with repetitive regions, including many pericentromeric and telomeric regions in eukaryotes. These regions often possess molecular systems that maintain a relatively compact and transcriptionally restricted state. In many animal systems H3K9 methylation and HP1-related interactions contribute to this organization. Their functions include protecting genome stability by limiting inappropriate use of repetitive sequences. The exact distribution of H3K9 methylation and its partners differs across organisms and chromosome regions. A repeated sequence need not be silent merely because it is repetitive.

Facultative heterochromatin develops at particular loci or chromosome regions in a condition-dependent manner. A gene may need to remain inactive in one lineage but be transcribed in another. Polycomb-associated chromatin provides an important example of a regulated repressive environment at developmental genes. H3K27me3 is often associated with this state. The distinction from constitutive heterochromatin is useful because a facultatively repressed region has the potential to adopt a different regulatory state in an appropriate context. This does not imply that the transition occurs rapidly or without coordinated regulatory signals.

Consider two chromosomal regions that both exhibit relatively low transcription. One contains a gene whose activator is absent, but its promoter is accessible. The other is within a repressed chromatin domain containing high H3K9me3 and low measured accessibility. Supplying the activator might be sufficient to raise transcription at the first region, while the second may require additional chromatin changes. Their equal RNA abundance in the original conditions does not indicate equal regulatory states. The consequences of a perturbation expose the differences between these mechanisms.

A chromatin domain also has boundaries. A self-reinforcing molecular process can favor modification of neighboring nucleosomes, but expansion cannot be allowed to occur indefinitely into every nearby gene. Sequence-specific proteins, competing chromatin modifications, local transcription and other chromosomal organizational features can limit propagation. A boundary mutation can therefore change expression of a nearby gene without altering its promoter sequence. The phenotype depends on the position of the gene relative to the affected domain and on whether the regulatory environment can actually extend across the former boundary.

The arrangement of chromatin around an active gene provides a useful reference. Many active promoters contain a region of relatively low nucleosome occupancy around the transcription start site, flanked by positioned nucleosomes. Enhancers may likewise exhibit an accessible core containing regulatory factors, with modified nucleosomes on either side. Nucleosomes in transcribed gene bodies can persist even during active transcription. The polymerase and associated factors negotiate these obstacles, and chromatin is reorganized or restored as transcription proceeds. A diagram showing an entire active gene stripped of histones would therefore be a poor general model.

The first downstream nucleosome at a promoter can affect the transition from initiation into elongation. The promoter may be exposed but a nucleosome within the early transcribed region can influence polymerase progression. A change in gene expression may consequently arise from a nucleosome outside the binding site of the initiating factor. This is a reason to distinguish promoter access from productive transcript generation. In a transcription assay that measures only short initiation products, a defect in elongation could be missed.

A gene body also needs a mechanism for avoiding transcription from accidental internal promoter-like sequences. When polymerase passes through chromatin, histone contacts are disturbed and accessibility can transiently increase. Restoring appropriate histone organization helps limit inappropriate initiation after the polymerase has passed. The yeast Set2/Rpd3S example illustrates the coordination of transcription-associated histone methylation with local deacetylation. It is not contradictory that an actively expressed gene can require a locally repressive process within its body. The useful outcome is faithful initiation from the correct promoter and production of the appropriate RNA.

Chromatin changes can have different durations. A transient extracellular signal may recruit an acetyltransferase and alter access for a short interval, after which the original state is restored. A developmental regulator may establish a longer-lasting modification pattern through interactions with feedback systems. A stable expression difference can persist for a period even after the original signal is removed, but persistence during one cell's lifetime is not equivalent to transmission across DNA replication and cell division. The later study of epigenetic memory will explicitly address that additional requirement.

Eukaryotes share the general use of nucleosomes and regulated chromatin, while individual complexes and patterns differ. Yeast provides experimentally tractable examples of remodeling and transcription-associated methylation. Animals use related catalytic machinery within complex developmental programs. Plants likewise use ATP-dependent chromatin regulation and histone modifications to control tissue-specific and environmental responses, but the distributions and partnerships of some modifications differ. It would be inappropriate to assume that every chromatin mark has the same distribution in all three groups. Most bacteria, in contrast, organize DNA using nucleoid-associated proteins rather than the canonical eukaryotic histone octamer. Archaeal histones provide a separate comparative case and should not be equated wholesale with the full eukaryotic regulatory machinery.

The biological advantage of a flexible packaging system is the coordination of genome protection with selective access. In a multicellular organism, different cell types can retain the same DNA sequence while presenting different local regulatory environments to transcription factors. In a single-celled organism, nutrient changes can recruit regulatory activities to new gene sets without requiring permanent changes to DNA. In both situations the relevant information includes the factor repertoire and its interactions with chromatin, not the histone state alone. A complete explanation must connect the physical organization to the biological demand for a particular expression program.

11. Coordinated activation and repression at a selected gene

We can now connect sequence-specific recognition, covalent modification and ATP-dependent remodeling within a single model. Imagine a eukaryotic gene required after a change in the cellular environment. Its promoter is initially partly covered by a nucleosome, and an adjacent regulatory element is accessible enough for a signal-responsive transcription factor to bind. The factor becomes active in response to the signal and interacts with a coactivator. The coactivator may recruit an acetyltransferase, a remodeling complex or both. These activities can increase the likelihood that additional DNA-binding proteins and basal transcription machinery engage the promoter.

In one possible order of events, the activator first recruits an acetyltransferase that modifies nearby histones. The modifications stabilize a bromodomain-containing remodeler or associated protein. Remodeling repositions a particle that previously overlapped an essential promoter sequence. General transcription factors can then assemble more efficiently and facilitate RNA polymerase II initiation. This is a useful causal model because each arrow represents a defined interaction or catalytic activity. It does not state that acetylation must always precede remodeling. Other genes may require an initial remodeling event to make suitable histone or DNA surfaces available.

If the activating transcription factor is removed, both recruitment and transcription can decrease. If its activation domain is specifically deleted but DNA recognition is preserved, factor binding may persist while acetylation and nucleosome movement decrease. If the acetyltransferase is catalytically inactivated, the factor can still recruit it but the relevant chemical product may not accumulate. If the remodeler is catalytically inactivated, the mark may remain while promoter accessibility is reduced. These expected outcomes assume a simple pathway without redundant enzymes and provide testable differences rather than universal phenotypes.

A repressive pathway can be constructed using the same logic. A DNA-binding repressor recognizes a regulatory element and recruits a corepressor containing a deacetylase. Loss of the relevant acetylation can reduce binding of coactivators or favor closer interactions between chromatin particles. A remodeling complex may also establish an arrangement that impedes the initiation machinery. If the repressor instead recruits a methyltransferase, the product may attract a different set of regulatory proteins. The term corepressor does not imply that the protein recognizes a unique DNA sequence itself; it may be targeted through the repressor that supplies the sequence recognition.

Activation and repression can compete at the same region. A signal-responsive activator may recruit a writer while a constitutively present repressor recruits an eraser. The observed modification abundance is then determined by both rates. If the activator concentration increases but the eraser activity is very high, the chemical change may be modest. If the eraser is inhibited, a smaller amount of activator may produce a more substantial change. A gene's apparent sensitivity to a signal can therefore be altered by changes in the chromatin-regulatory environment even when the signal receptor and DNA-binding specificity are unchanged.

Chromatin also contributes to the specificity of enhancer function. A factor present in multiple cell types may only engage a particular enhancer where its motif is accessible. In one cell the enhancer may contain a suitable pioneer factor and regulatory partners; in another it may be embedded in a repressed nucleosome arrangement. The same concentration of the signal-responsive factor could consequently produce different outcomes. This is one molecular route by which a broadly distributed signaling pathway can generate a cell-specific transcriptional response. The accessibility state is part of the response logic rather than simply a reflection of the DNA motif.

Regulatory proteins frequently influence each other's retention on chromatin. A transcription factor can recruit a modifier, and the modification can stabilize a reader-containing complex that in turn helps retain the transcription factor. Such cooperation need not involve a direct contact between every member of the assembly. It can arise because each event improves the conditions for the next. Removing one component may disrupt the stability of several others, making it difficult to infer the initial recruitment order from a single endpoint measurement. Time-dependent observations and targeted mutations help separate sequential dependencies from simultaneous stabilization.

Another possibility is that transcription itself recruits a chromatin enzyme. An RNA polymerase engaged in elongation can provide a platform for factors that deposit or remove modifications within a gene. An increase in a transcription-associated mark after gene activation could therefore reflect the movement of polymerase rather than a preceding cause of that movement. A treatment that blocks productive elongation while preserving initial factor binding can help distinguish these alternatives, although direct drug effects and secondary consequences must be considered. The presence of an apparently activating mark at a locus does not specify whether it belongs upstream or downstream in the causal chain.

A gene may contain several regulatory elements contributing to its expression. If one enhancer loses accessibility but another remains functional, transcription may decline only partially. If two enhancers act redundantly, deletion of either one may produce little change despite a substantial effect of deleting both. A chromatin regulator recruited by one enhancer might therefore appear dispensable in a particular condition but become important under another stimulus. The resulting expression level reflects a network of inputs. Predicting the exact magnitude of a response requires knowledge of how those inputs combine, not just identification of the affected modification.

The simplest way to interpret a perturbation is to follow the pathway in terms of measurable molecular states. Determine whether the regulator is present and whether it binds the appropriate site. Determine whether the expected modification or physical rearrangement occurs. Determine whether the promoter becomes competent for initiation and whether productive RNA synthesis increases. Finally distinguish nascent RNA from steady-state RNA and protein output. Each measurement answers a different question, and changes at later steps cannot automatically be attributed to an earlier step. This structured reasoning is more reliable than using chromatin marks as labels for expression states.

12. Quantitative balance of writing, erasing and accessibility

A useful mathematical model treats a chosen histone modification as a reversible process affecting a defined set of sites. Let x be the fraction of sites bearing the modification, so 0 ≤ x ≤ 1. Assume that every unmodified site can receive the mark with an effective first-order rate constant kw and every modified site can lose it with an effective rate constant ke. The model assumes a fixed total number of sites, constant conditions and independent equivalent sites. Writing increases x at the rate kw(1 − x), whereas erasing decreases x at the rate kex. The net change is dx/dt = kw(1 − x) − kex.

At steady state dx/dt is zero. Rearranging kw = (kw + ke)x gives xss = kw/(kw + ke). This result describes a balance of rates, not a condition in which the enzymes stop working. The forward and reverse fluxes remain equal and can both be substantial. The constants have units of inverse time, so the steady fraction is dimensionless. The fraction is sensitive to the ratio of the two constants, while the rate of approach to steady state depends on their sum. Two systems with the same modification fraction may thus differ in turnover.

Suppose kw = 0.30 min⁻¹ and ke = 0.10 min⁻¹. The steady fraction is 0.30/0.40 = 0.75. If a deacetylase inhibitor reduces ke to 0.025 min⁻¹ while leaving kw unchanged, the new steady fraction is 0.30/0.325 ≈ 0.923. The absolute increase is approximately 0.173, or 17.3 percentage points. It is not a 17.3 percent relative increase: the relative change is 0.173/0.75, approximately 23.1 percent. Correctly distinguishing percentages from percentage points matters when experimental data are reported as modified fractions.

The time course can be obtained by subtracting the steady state from x. Let y = x − xss. The equation becomes dy/dt = −(kw + ke)y, whose solution is y(t) = y(0)exp[−(kw + ke)t]. Therefore x(t) = xss + [x(0) − xss]exp[−(kw + ke)t]. The half-time for the difference from steady state is ln(2)/(kw + ke). For the original rates it is 0.693/0.40 ≈ 1.73 minutes, whereas under inhibition it becomes 0.693/0.325 ≈ 2.13 minutes. The larger eventual modification level is accompanied by a somewhat slower relaxation toward that new level in this particular model.

Histone replacement can be included as an additional loss route if modified histones are replaced with unmodified molecules. Let kdil be the effective first-order replacement rate. The model then predicts xss = kw/(kw + ke + kdil). If kw = 0.30 min⁻¹, ke = 0.10 min⁻¹ and kdil = 0.20 min⁻¹, the new steady fraction is 0.50 rather than 0.75. This change occurs without increasing eraser activity. The model clarifies why a decrease in a mark after a perturbation might arise from histone turnover rather than direct chemical removal. Replacement by histones already carrying the mark would require a different term in the equation.

A related approach can describe switching between two accessibility states. Let kopen be the effective rate of changing from closed to open and kclose the reverse rate. The stationary open fraction in a two-state kinetic model is kopen/(kopen + kclose). Suppose a remodeler increases kopen from 0.02 to 0.10 s⁻¹ while kclose remains 0.08 s⁻¹. The open fraction increases from 0.20 to approximately 0.556. This is a 2.78-fold increase in the model. Unlike the thermal equilibrium expression earlier in the chapter, this kinetic formulation can be used phenomenologically for a system with energy-driven transitions. The effective rates do not establish the underlying ATP stoichiometry.

If transcription requires the open state and proceeds at rate ron when open, with negligible synthesis when closed, the average synthesis rate is ron times the open fraction. With ron = 18 transcripts per minute, the two fractions predict average rates of 3.6 and 10 transcripts per minute. The units remain transcripts per minute because the fraction is dimensionless. The model could fail if an opening must persist for a minimum duration before initiation, if the transcription machinery changes the opening rates, or if the regulator alters the initiation rate within the open state as well as the fraction of time spent open.

Quantitative models are useful precisely because they make assumptions visible. A measured change in accessibility is not necessarily a direct measure of open probability, since an experimental signal can depend on cutting efficiency or population composition. A normalized histone modification signal is not necessarily an absolute modified fraction. Before substituting measured values into a mathematical formula, determine whether the experiment estimates the corresponding variable. These simplified models are teaching tools for comparing mechanisms and predicting tendencies, not claims that real chromatin obeys one universal two-state or first-order law.

13. Experiments that distinguish access, position, modification and transcription

An experimental investigation of chromatin begins with a distinction between the biological question and the quantity being measured. Does a treatment expose a particular DNA sequence? Does it reposition nucleosomes, change their occupancy, alter histone chemistry or influence transcription after initiation? A single experimental signal rarely answers all these questions. A strong design measures several complementary properties and includes a comparison in which an alternative explanation can be recognized.

Micrococcal nuclease, often abbreviated MNase, can digest DNA in chromatin, with linker DNA generally more susceptible than DNA protected by nucleosome cores. Under suitable digestion conditions, protected fragments approximately corresponding to mononucleosomal DNA can be recovered. In a defined reconstituted substrate, the positions of protected regions can reveal how nucleosomes are arranged. If a remodeler shifts a protected region from one side of a promoter to the other, the pattern supports repositioning. If the original protected region disappears without a corresponding new region, eviction is a possibility, but the observation must be interpreted with controls for digestion and alternative protection by proteins.

MNase digestion is not a direct count of histone molecules. The enzyme has sequence preferences, and the degree of digestion affects which fragments survive. An overdigested sample may lose fragments that would otherwise reveal protection, creating an apparent reduction in occupancy. A meaningful comparison therefore uses comparable conditions and preferably examines a range of digestion levels. A naked DNA control can help identify sequence-related differences in cleavage. A complementary histone measurement helps distinguish reduced protection caused by changes in particle organization from genuine loss of histone association.

DNase I hypersensitivity provides a related but distinct approach. Regions that are relatively accessible to the nuclease can show enhanced cleavage compared with more protected regions. If a signal-responsive enhancer becomes hypersensitive following stimulation, the result supports a change in accessibility. It does not prove that the DNA is permanently devoid of nucleosomes, nor does it directly identify the responsible remodeling complex. The observed sensitivity is an operational measurement of enzyme access under the experimental conditions. Interpretation requires attention to local DNA sequence, associated proteins and the condition of the sample.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which a promoter has normalized nuclease cleavage signals of 0.15 before stimulation and 0.60 after stimulation. A neighboring constitutively accessible control region changes from 0.78 to 0.80. The promoter signal has increased fourfold, while the control changes little. This pattern is consistent with a local accessibility change rather than a uniform fourfold difference in digestion across the whole sample. It does not establish that exactly 60 percent of promoters are open, because the signal has not been calibrated as an absolute probability. That distinction prevents an inappropriate application of the quantitative models developed earlier.

A restriction enzyme can be used in a more focused reconstituted system if its recognition site is deliberately positioned near the nucleosome edge or center. Compare cleavage of the same site on naked DNA and on DNA assembled into a nucleosome. If the nucleosomal substrate is cleaved more slowly, its association with the histone core restricts access under those conditions. Addition of a purified remodeling enzyme can test whether access increases in the presence of ATP. A catalytic mutant, a reaction lacking ATP and a control containing the same DNA without histones help separate the role of the remodeler from other influences on enzyme activity.

A chromatin immunoprecipitation experiment addresses association of a protein or histone modification with a selected genomic region. Chromatin is prepared in a suitable manner, often involving crosslinking for particular applications, and fragmented. An antibody is used to enrich material associated with the target protein or modification. The abundance of a chosen DNA sequence in the enriched material is then measured, for example by a targeted PCR-based assay. The result indicates enrichment of the target-associated chromatin relative to suitable controls. It does not provide a direct structural image or independently prove that the target caused a transcriptional change.

Antibody specificity is critical. An antibody directed against H3K27ac must distinguish that modification from other chemical states sufficiently well for the intended inference. A nonspecific immunoglobulin or other appropriate negative control helps estimate background association. An input sample representing chromatin before enrichment helps account for variation in the starting material. A positive reference locus can test whether the assay detects the expected target. A measurement of total H3 at the same locus is particularly valuable when histone occupancy could change between conditions.

Suppose a hypothetical assay produces a normalized H3K27ac signal of 12 units and a total H3 signal of 24 units before treatment. After treatment these signals are 9 and 12 units respectively. The raw acetylation signal has declined by 25 percent. However, the ratio of acetylation signal to total H3 rises from 0.50 to 0.75, an increase of 50 percent relative to the original ratio. Under an assumption that signals are proportional and comparable across conditions, the result is consistent with increased acetylation per remaining histone despite a decrease in overall histone occupancy. The ratios are relative indices rather than directly measured absolute fractions of acetylated H3 molecules.

The example demonstrates why a decline in modification enrichment can have two different meanings. The same number of histones might carry a smaller proportion of the mark, or the total number of histones at the locus might have declined. If both quantities change, the raw mark signal combines their effects. Normalization to total histone is useful for investigating the chemical state of the remaining substrate, while the absolute normalized mark signal may still matter for the total number of available reader-binding sites. Neither quantity should automatically replace the other.

Transcription itself can be measured through newly synthesized RNA or through the steady abundance of a particular RNA. Nascent RNA is especially useful for examining a proposed transcriptional mechanism because it more directly reflects ongoing synthesis. Steady-state RNA also depends on processing and degradation. For example, if a chromatin treatment doubles RNA synthesis but simultaneously doubles the effective loss rate, a simple steady-state model predicts no change in mature RNA abundance. A conclusion that transcription has not changed merely because mature RNA is constant would be incorrect under those conditions.

The most direct biochemical test of remodeling uses a defined DNA–histone substrate and an identified remodeling complex. An investigator can compare the positions or accessibility of a nucleosome before and after addition of the enzyme. A reaction containing hydrolyzable ATP can be compared with controls lacking ATP or containing a catalytically impaired enzyme. A similar logic applies to histone acetylation: a defined histone substrate, an appropriate acetyl donor and an active acetyltransferase allow a direct test of chemical modification. Subsequent measurements of compaction, factor binding or transcription can reveal consequences of the modification.

Reconstitution offers a powerful form of causal control because many cellular pathways are absent. If a defined acetylated histone array differs from an otherwise comparable unmodified array, the observed difference can be attributed more narrowly to the specified chemical change. The limitation is that such a substrate does not reproduce all features of a nucleus. A result showing that a modification can weaken array compaction demonstrates a physical capability; it does not prove that this is the dominant mechanism at every endogenous locus. Combining a defined assay with cellular perturbations is therefore especially informative.

A reporter experiment can test the functional importance of a regulatory element in a particular chromatin context. One can compare identical reporter coding sequences controlled by a wild-type enhancer and a mutated enhancer that lacks a transcription-factor recognition site. When comparing integrated reporters, their genomic location matters because the surrounding chromatin can influence expression. Ideally the constructs occupy equivalent sites or sufficient independent controls are used. A plasmid-based reporter may behave differently from an integrated construct, since its packaging and nuclear environment need not resemble the endogenous locus. A functional reporter result should not be equated uncritically with the behavior of the original gene.

Finally, time-course experiments can distinguish candidate orders of regulatory events. Suppose factor binding is detected early, histone acetylation rises next, promoter accessibility subsequently increases and nascent RNA rises last. This pattern is compatible with a sequential mechanism. It is not sufficient to prove that each preceding event is required, because several events could be independent responses to the same stimulus. A catalytic mutation in the writer that prevents acetylation while preserving factor binding provides a more specific test. A rescue with the wild-type enzyme and an appropriate inactive control can further strengthen the causal inference. Scientific reasoning proceeds from timing to necessity and, where possible, to tests of sufficiency.

14. Predicting perturbations and integrating biological outcomes

The preceding mechanisms explain why a regulatory mutation can change gene expression even when the DNA coding sequence of the affected gene remains normal. For example, a mutation in a remodeling complex may leave the target gene intact but change accessibility of its promoter. Because the complex can act at multiple loci, the mutation may produce a broad expression phenotype. A mutation of a cis-regulatory recognition site, in contrast, can prevent recruitment of the complex at that particular DNA copy while leaving its activity at other genes largely unaffected. This distinction connects chromatin regulation to the cis–trans reasoning used earlier in the unit.

An instructive pair of perturbations concerns a writer and its reader. Assume a promoter requires histone acetylation to recruit a coactivator. In cells lacking the writer's catalytic activity, both the mark and recruitment decline. In cells lacking the reader's recognition activity, the mark remains abundant but recruitment fails. If both mutants reduce transcription to a similar level, the RNA measurement alone cannot identify the defective step. A measurement of the mark discriminates them. The prediction depends on the assumption that the reader does not also contribute substantially to maintenance of the mark through feedback.

The same logic applies to remodeling. Suppose a promoter remains nucleosome-occupied in an ATPase mutant, even though the normal activator and the remodeling complex are both detected at the region. This pattern supports a defect after targeting, consistent with loss of catalytic function. If the complex is not detected at the promoter in a different mutant but is active on a purified nucleosome, the more likely defect concerns recruitment or retention. Rescue with wild-type protein is helpful, but rescue with variants that separate catalytic and targeting functions can provide a more specific conclusion.

One should not expect an identical response at every gene after removing a chromatin enzyme. Different loci can rely on different combinations of remodelers and modifiers. Some promoters may already be highly accessible and therefore require little additional remodeling for a particular stimulus. Others may have a critical motif deeply constrained by nucleosomal organization. Redundant enzymes may compensate at one region but not another. Consequently, a relatively selective expression phenotype does not refute a general biochemical function of the mutated complex. The specificity emerges from the relationship between the shared molecular activity and the individual chromatin context.

Chromatin regulation also helps explain why a cell can change its physiological behavior in response to environmental information. A signal-responsive transcription factor may establish a temporary regulatory state at genes required during stress. When the signal declines, recruitment of writers or remodelers can diminish, allowing opposing activities to restore the original state. In a differentiating tissue, combinations of factors may instead stabilize a longer-lasting set of active and inactive regions. These mechanisms allow gene expression to reflect developmental history as well as immediate signals, without implying that every short-term chromatin change is inherited.

The relevance to biotechnology and disease follows from the same molecular principles. A transgene introduced at different chromosomal locations may exhibit different expression because its promoter encounters distinct regulatory environments. A loss-of-function alteration in a chromatin-regulatory protein can disrupt expression programs controlling proliferation or differentiation. Enzymes that modify histones can be pharmacological targets, but their inhibition may influence many genes and nonhistone substrates. The proper scientific inference is that a specific molecular perturbation can alter multiple regulatory pathways; it is not that every disease associated with chromatin can be corrected by uniformly increasing acetylation or removing methylation.

A useful integrative exercise is to imagine a gene with high H3K4me3, high promoter accessibility and little mature RNA. Several explanations remain possible: a required activator may be absent, the polymerase may fail to enter productive elongation, or newly made RNA may be removed rapidly. Measuring only the chromatin profile does not distinguish those options. If nascent transcription is also high, a post-transcriptional explanation becomes more plausible. If initiation products are high but full-length nascent transcripts are low, a defect in productive progression deserves attention. Each additional observation restricts the possible mechanisms.

The reverse situation can also occur. A gene with abundant steady-state RNA may have become transcriptionally inactive only recently. Its previously synthesized RNA can persist while promoter accessibility and activating marks decline. A chromatin measurement made at this point describes the current regulatory environment, whereas the RNA pool partly reflects past synthesis. This time lag connects the present chapter to the earlier quantitative treatment of RNA turnover. Examination questions that compare chromatin and expression measurements should therefore specify the time after perturbation and the type of RNA being measured whenever the distinction affects the answer.

The essential framework is to move from physical substrate to regulatory action and from regulatory action to a measured output. First identify whether the perturbation changes a DNA sequence, a histone modification, nucleosome organization or the activity of an associated protein. Then identify the expected immediate molecular consequence. Finally ask which subsequent steps are required for a change in RNA or protein abundance and what experiment can distinguish that model from its alternatives. This reasoning avoids attributing every expression phenotype to a generic open or closed state. It also prepares the transition to DNA methylation, where a different chemical substrate supplies another layer of regulation that can interact with the chromatin mechanisms established here.

Part B — Conceptual practice

Original practice questions; not previous-year examination questions.

1. Which property of a nucleosome is described by occupancy?

  1. The number of DNA base pairs between adjacent nucleosome dyads
  2. The translational register of the core on the DNA sequence
  3. The frequency with which a DNA segment is covered by a nucleosome
  4. The rotational exposure of the DNA major groove
Answer and explanation

C. The frequency with which a DNA segment is covered by a nucleosome

Occupancy is a fraction of molecules or time covered. The distance between dyads measures repeat length, the translational register describes position, and the major-groove orientation describes rotational setting.

2. Which component is not part of the canonical histone octamer?

  1. H1
  2. H2A
  3. H3
  4. H4
Answer and explanation

A. H1

H1 is a linker-associated histone; two copies of each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 make the core octamer.

3. What is the immediate chemical consequence of acetylating a lysine side chain?

  1. Increased positive charge on the lysine side chain
  2. Addition of a methyl group without a charge change
  3. Neutralization of its usual positive charge
  4. Formation of a negatively charged phosphoserine residue
Answer and explanation

C. Neutralization of its usual positive charge

An acetyl group forms a neutral amide at the side-chain nitrogen. Methylation generally retains positive charge; phosphorylation changes a different chemical group and is not this acetylation reaction.

4. What supplies the transferred acetyl group for a histone acetyltransferase?

  1. ATP as the acetyl donor
  2. Acetyl-CoA
  3. S-adenosylmethionine
  4. NAD⁺ as the acetyl donor
Answer and explanation

B. Acetyl-CoA

Acetyl-CoA is the acetyl donor. SAM transfers methyl groups; NAD⁺ participates in sirtuin deacetylation; ATP can power remodeling but does not supply the acetyl group.

5. Which domain commonly recognizes acetyllysine?

  1. Chromodomain
  2. Bromodomain
  3. SET catalytic domain
  4. Histone-fold domain
Answer and explanation

B. Bromodomain

Bromodomains bind acetyllysine in suitable contexts. Chromodomains may recognize methylated lysines; SET domains catalyze many methylation reactions; the histone fold supports core histone organization.

6. Which mark is commonly enriched at active transcription start sites?

  1. H3K9me3
  2. H3K4me3
  3. H3K27me3
  4. H3K36me3 concentrated in the gene body
Answer and explanation

B. H3K4me3

H3K4me3 is frequently found near active promoters; the other marks are more often associated with repressive environments. None is a universal indicator.

7. Which protein provides a classic reader of H3K9 methylation in heterochromatin?

  1. HP1
  2. PRC2
  3. SWR1
  4. Gcn5
Answer and explanation

A. HP1

HP1 recognizes H3K9 methylation through its chromodomain. PRC2 writes H3K27 methylation, SWR1 exchanges histone variants, and Gcn5 is an acetyltransferase.

8. Which catalytic complex establishes H3K27 methylation in the representative animal Polycomb system?

  1. PRC2
  2. PRC1
  3. NuRD
  4. COMPASS/Set1
Answer and explanation

A. PRC2

PRC2 contains the H3K27 methyltransferase activity. PRC1 can monoubiquitylate H2A, NuRD combines remodeling and deacetylation, and COMPASS/Set1 writes H3K4 methylation.

9. Which statement correctly describes histone lysine trimethylation?

  1. It neutralizes the positive charge exactly as acetylation does
  2. It adds a negatively charged group to the lysine
  3. It retains a positive charge and can alter protein recognition
  4. It changes a cytosine base rather than a protein residue
Answer and explanation

C. It retains a positive charge and can alter protein recognition

Trimethyllysine is positively charged. Unlike acetylation, this chemistry does not neutralize the side-chain charge; it neither adds a negative phosphate group nor modifies DNA cytosine.

10. What is a representative activity of ISWI-family complexes?

  1. Histone lysine acetylation
  2. H3K27 trimethylation
  3. Nucleosome sliding and spacing
  4. Selective H2A.Z deposition as its defining activity
Answer and explanation

C. Nucleosome sliding and spacing

ISWI-family motors use ATP to organize nucleosomes, often spacing arrays. Acetylation and methylation require different enzymes, while H2A.Z exchange is a representative SWR1-family function.

11. What distinguishes histone variant exchange from histone methylation?

  1. Exchange adds a covalent group to an unchanged H2A molecule
  2. Exchange changes the protein molecule incorporated in the nucleosome
  3. Histone methylation alters the encoded amino acid sequence of H3
  4. Variant exchange necessarily removes all histones permanently from DNA
Answer and explanation

B. Exchange changes the protein molecule incorporated in the nucleosome

A variant is a different histone protein sequence. Methylation changes a covalent group on an existing protein, not its gene sequence.

12. Which statement best describes a pioneer factor?

  1. It always binds every wrapped motif without restriction
  2. It can engage some relatively inaccessible or nucleosomal sites and assist subsequent accessibility
  3. It is the catalytic ATPase of every remodeler
  4. It directly synthesizes all nascent RNA
Answer and explanation

B. It can engage some relatively inaccessible or nucleosomal sites and assist subsequent accessibility

Pioneer factors can engage selected nucleosomal sites, but their ability is context dependent; they are not synonymous with remodelers or RNA polymerases.

13. Which histone mark is characteristically associated with elongating transcription across gene bodies?

  1. H3K36me3
  2. H3K4me3 at promoter-proximal nucleosomes
  3. H3K27me3 at Polycomb-repressed loci
  4. H3K9me3 at many constitutive heterochromatin regions
Answer and explanation

A. H3K36me3

H3K36me3 frequently tracks active transcription across gene bodies. The others do not supply this characteristic association.

14. Which statement about H3K27ac and H3K27me3 is correct?

  1. The same lysine side chain cannot carry both states simultaneously
  2. They can coexist on the same lysine because acetyl and methyl groups bind different atoms
  3. H3K27ac is a DNA modification while H3K27me3 is a protein modification
  4. Their coexistence in a mixed sample establishes both on every H3 molecule
Answer and explanation

A. The same lysine side chain cannot carry both states simultaneously

Acetylation and trimethylation are incompatible chemical states of one lysine. Different H3 copies or cells can carry different states.

15. What is a representative biochemical property of LSD1?

  1. It can remove trimethyllysine by its FAD-dependent oxidation mechanism
  2. It catalyzes H3K27 methylation from SAM
  3. It is the ATPase responsible for nucleosome spacing
  4. It can demethylate suitable mono- and dimethyllysine substrates
Answer and explanation

D. It can demethylate suitable mono- and dimethyllysine substrates

LSD1's FAD-dependent chemistry acts on suitable lower methylation states, not trimethyllysine by that mechanism. PRC2 provides representative H3K27 methyltransferase activity; ISWI provides representative spacing activity.

16. What does DNase I hypersensitivity most directly suggest?

  1. An absolute measure of nucleosome occupancy independent of cleavage kinetics
  2. The identity of the chromatin remodeling enzyme at the site
  3. Relatively increased access of the nuclease to DNA
  4. The amount of full-length RNA made from the associated promoter
Answer and explanation

C. Relatively increased access of the nuclease to DNA

Hypersensitivity measures relative nuclease access. Cleavage does not directly establish absolute nucleosome occupancy, causal enzyme identity or transcriptional output.

17. Which description of euchromatin is most accurate?

  1. A region containing no histones
  2. A region where every gene must be active
  3. A generally less compact chromatin environment that can contain both active and inactive genes
  4. A region chemically defined solely by H3K4me3
Answer and explanation

C. A generally less compact chromatin environment that can contain both active and inactive genes

Euchromatin is a broad organizational category. Histones remain, activity varies by gene, and a single mark does not define every instance.

18. Which observation most directly supports ATP-dependent sliding rather than eviction?

  1. All histones disappear with no shift in position
  2. Mature RNA decays at an increased rate
  3. A lysine acquires an acetyl group
  4. A protected DNA interval shifts while histone association is retained
Answer and explanation

D. A protected DNA interval shifts while histone association is retained

Sliding moves nucleosomes relative to DNA while retaining a particle. Loss of histones instead favors eviction; the other observations concern separate processes.

19. What best distinguishes a chromatin writer from a reader?

  1. A writer catalyzes modification; a reader recognizes the modified substrate
  2. Both necessarily methylate the same lysine
  3. A reader must hydrolyze ATP to remove marks
  4. A writer must recognize a DNA sequence directly
Answer and explanation

A. A writer catalyzes modification; a reader recognizes the modified substrate

The functional distinction is catalysis versus recognition. Targeting and ATP hydrolysis are not defining requirements.

20. Which statement correctly distinguishes DNA methylation from histone methylation?

  1. Both methylation reactions use an amino acid residue as their immediate substrate
  2. Their immediate substrates are DNA bases and histone amino acids, respectively
  3. Both methylation reactions target cytosine bases in DNA
  4. Histone and DNA methylation cannot influence each other's recruitment
Answer and explanation

B. Their immediate substrates are DNA bases and histone amino acids, respectively

The substrates are chemically distinct: a nucleotide base versus a protein side chain. Both types of marks may influence recruitment of regulators of the other, so their distinction does not imply independence.

Part C — Analytical practice

Original practice questions; not previous-year examination questions.

21. Two identical motifs are positioned near the edge and dyad of otherwise matched nucleosomes. The same factor binds the edge site more efficiently. What is the best explanation?

  1. The motif at the dyad must have changed its nucleotide sequence
  2. Every nucleosome edge is devoid of histones
  3. Transient edge unwrapping makes the first motif more accessible
  4. The factor necessarily has a different intrinsic affinity for naked DNA at the two motifs
Answer and explanation

C. Transient edge unwrapping makes the first motif more accessible

With matched sequences, the geometry of packaging is a plausible cause. Intrinsic naked-DNA affinity need not change; edge sites are not permanently histone free.

22. Two ideal arrays have repeat lengths of 190 and 200 bp and identical 147-bp cores. Which pair of linker lengths distinguishes the arrays?

  1. 337 bp and 347 bp
  2. 43 bp and 53 bp
  3. 147 bp and 147 bp
  4. 53 bp and 43 bp
Answer and explanation

B. 43 bp and 53 bp

Subtract the common 147-bp core length from each repeat: 190 − 147 = 43 bp and 200 − 147 = 53 bp. The 10-bp difference comes from linkers under the stated assumptions, not a change in core size.

23. In a fixed-accessibility mixture, F = Kd. Accessibility falls from 0.8 to 0.2. What happens to predicted overall factor occupancy?

  1. It falls from 0.40 to 0.10
  2. It falls from 0.80 to 0.20
  3. It remains 0.50
  4. It rises from 0.10 to 0.40
Answer and explanation

A. It falls from 0.40 to 0.10

Conditional binding is F/(F+Kd) = 0.5. Multiplying by each accessible fraction yields 0.4 and 0.1. The result assumes accessibility is externally fixed.

24. A recruited ATPase-dead remodeler binds the same promoter as wild type but fails to shift protected fragments. What defect is most directly implicated?

  1. Catalytic remodeling after recruitment
  2. Failure of the activator to bind its motif
  3. Loss of the promoter DNA sequence
  4. Increased degradation of mature RNA as the direct cause of the missing shift
Answer and explanation

A. Catalytic remodeling after recruitment

Recruitment persists but physical remodeling fails, favoring a motor defect. The other explanations are inconsistent with or do not explain the measured recruitment and protection pattern.

25. An array retains four histone cores after treatment. Linker lengths change from 15, 70, 20 bp to 35, 35, 35 bp. What changed?

  1. Mean linker length increased threefold
  2. Core occupancy necessarily doubled
  3. Linker-length variability decreased; mean length stayed 35 bp
  4. Variant exchange is demonstrated
Answer and explanation

C. Linker-length variability decreased; mean length stayed 35 bp

Both linker sums equal 105 bp and the mean is 35. Only regularity is established; neither a histone-count increase nor variant identity follows.

26. A histone writer mutant leaves its recruitment intact but reduces its mark and RNA. An independent reader mutant leaves the mark high but reduces RNA. What model fits best?

  1. The reader is necessarily the sole enzyme writing the mark
  2. The mark is irrelevant to all transcription
  3. Writer catalysis establishes a mark that the reader uses downstream
  4. Both mutations remove the DNA recognition motif
Answer and explanation

C. Writer catalysis establishes a mark that the reader uses downstream

The distinct marker patterns separate mark establishment from interpretation. A downstream reader model fits; absolute claims about every gene or all other mechanisms are unwarranted.

27. A nuclease cleavage signal increases from 0.15 to 0.60 at a promoter, but a control locus remains similar. What can be inferred?

  1. Relative promoter accessibility increased fourfold under the assay conditions
  2. Exactly 60% of cells contain a fully open promoter
  3. Total histone H3 is necessarily zero
  4. The promoter must contain four times as many DNA molecules
Answer and explanation

A. Relative promoter accessibility increased fourfold under the assay conditions

The signal ratio is 4, consistent with increased access. Without calibration it is not an absolute open-cell fraction; occupancy and DNA copy number need separate measurements.

28. A targeted ChIP assay shows mark/H3 signals of 12/24 before and 9/12 after treatment. How does the normalized mark-to-H3 index change?

  1. 0.50 to 0.75, a 25% relative increase
  2. 0.75 to 0.50
  3. 12 to 9 proves lower modification per remaining histone
  4. 0.50 to 0.75, a 50% relative increase
Answer and explanation

D. 0.50 to 0.75, a 50% relative increase

The ratios are 0.5 and 0.75; (0.75−0.5)/0.5 = 0.5. The raw mark decline does not determine the per-H3 index.

29. Assume kw = 0.30 min⁻¹ and ke = 0.10 min⁻¹. What is the steady modified fraction?

  1. 0.40
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.75
  4. 3.00
Answer and explanation

C. 0.75

At steady state x = kw/(kw+ke) = 0.30/0.40. The sum is a rate constant, 0.25 is the unmodified fraction, and 3 is a ratio rather than a fraction.

30. If ke is reduced to 0.025 min⁻¹ while kw remains 0.30 min⁻¹, what is the model's steady fraction?

  1. Approximately 0.077
  2. 0.325
  3. 0.75
  4. Approximately 0.923
Answer and explanation

D. Approximately 0.923

0.30/0.325 ≈ 0.923. The complementary fraction is 0.077; 0.325 is the relaxation rate and 0.75 the previous steady fraction.

31. Two systems have (kw,ke) = (0.3,0.1) and (0.6,0.2) min⁻¹. How do they compare?

  1. Second has twice the steady fraction
  2. Same steady fraction; the second relaxes twice as fast
  3. First relaxes twice as fast
  4. Both rate and fraction necessarily differ
Answer and explanation

B. Same steady fraction; the second relaxes twice as fast

Both ratios equal 0.75. The sum of rate constants doubles from 0.4 to 0.8 min⁻¹, so the relaxation half-time halves.

32. With kw = 0.30, ke = 0.10 and unmodified-histone replacement kdil = 0.20 min⁻¹, what is the steady modified fraction?

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.60
  3. 0.20
  4. 0.50
Answer and explanation

D. 0.50

The loss term includes erasing and replacement. 0.30/(0.30+0.10+0.20) = 0.50; ignoring replacement yields 0.75.

33. In a two-state model kopen = 0.10 s⁻¹, kclose = 0.08 s⁻¹ and ron = 18 transcripts/min. What average synthesis rate is predicted if the closed state is silent?

  1. 18 transcripts/min
  2. 1.8 transcripts/min
  3. 10 transcripts/min
  4. 0.18 transcripts/min
Answer and explanation

C. 10 transcripts/min

The open fraction is 0.10/0.18 = 5/9, so 18 × 5/9 = 10. Multiplying by a rate constant rather than a dimensionless fraction is invalid.

34. A loss of Set2 in yeast causes elevated acetylation inside some gene bodies and new short RNAs beginning within them. What interpretation is strongest?

  1. Set2 must be the HAT for every promoter
  2. Failure of transcription-associated methylation and deacetylation permits cryptic initiation
  3. H3K36 methylation normally forbids all productive transcription
  4. The short RNAs necessarily originate from the normal promoter by accelerated translation
Answer and explanation

B. Failure of transcription-associated methylation and deacetylation permits cryptic initiation

Set2-dependent H3K36 methylation can support Rpd3S-mediated suppression of internal starts. The data are consistent with a failure of this process, not global inhibition of transcription.

35. A promoter is accessible and H3K4me3-rich, but full-length nascent RNA is low. Which result most strongly suggests an elongation defect?

  1. No transcription-factor binding anywhere in the cell
  2. High promoter-proximal initiation products and low downstream nascent RNA
  3. A decrease in mature protein with no RNA measurements
  4. An increase in an unrelated heterochromatin mark
Answer and explanation

B. High promoter-proximal initiation products and low downstream nascent RNA

Initiation products with poor downstream output suggest a post-initiation restriction. Other results fail to localize the limiting step to elongation.

36. After an HDAC inhibitor, total acetylation rises but gene Y falls in expression. Which explanation is scientifically valid?

  1. The increase in acetylation is chemically impossible
  2. Every gene must increase transcription when global acetylation rises
  3. HDAC inhibition changes every DNA sequence
  4. An indirect transcriptional response or locus-specific regulatory effect can lower Y
Answer and explanation

D. An indirect transcriptional response or locus-specific regulatory effect can lower Y

Global marks average across loci; indirect effects and nonhistone substrates also matter. The observation does not contradict the usual association with activity.

37. A K-to-Q histone mutant mimics a charge-neutral phenotype but fails to recruit a bromodomain protein. Why?

  1. Glutamine does not reproduce the complete acetyllysine recognition surface
  2. Glutamine is a trimethylated lysine
  3. Bromodomains always recognize DNA cytosine instead
  4. The mutant must have the same charge as unmodified lysine
Answer and explanation

A. Glutamine does not reproduce the complete acetyllysine recognition surface

Charge similarity is not chemical identity. K-to-Q is an imperfect acetylation mimic, especially for modification-specific binding.

38. Two identical reporter genes are integrated at different loci and differ in expression. What should be tested before concluding their promoter sequences differ?

  1. Only the genetic code of the reporter protein
  2. Local chromatin accessibility and integration-site context
  3. Only the ribosomal RNA sequence
  4. Whether one reporter uses different universal codons without sequencing
Answer and explanation

B. Local chromatin accessibility and integration-site context

Position effects can alter expression despite identical reporter DNA. Measuring local accessibility and matching genomic integration sites addresses the alternative.

39. A factor binds an enhancer before acetylation and RNA increases. Writer catalytic inactivation blocks the mark and RNA but not factor binding; wild-type rescue restores both. What is supported?

  1. Temporal order alone proves the mark is sufficient for every gene
  2. The transcription factor cannot bind DNA
  3. The writer must be a DNA methyltransferase
  4. Writer activity is necessary for the full response in this tested system
Answer and explanation

D. Writer activity is necessary for the full response in this tested system

The targeted perturbation and rescue support a causal requirement after factor binding. They do not prove sufficiency or universal applicability.

40. A chromatin change doubles nascent RNA synthesis and doubles first-order RNA loss. At steady state what happens to mature RNA under a simple model?

  1. It stays unchanged
  2. It doubles
  3. It falls by half
  4. It necessarily quadruples
Answer and explanation

A. It stays unchanged

Examination traps and misconceptions

  1. Open chromatin means histone-free DNA. Accessible sites can occur within or between retained nucleosomes. The misleading shortcut confuses accessibility with eviction; compare a sliding phenotype with a histone-loss phenotype.
  2. Positioning and occupancy are identical. Position refers to location; occupancy is the frequency of coverage. A broad protection distribution can indicate variable positioning at high occupancy.
  3. All transcription factors bind any exposed motif equally. Affinity, orientation and partners matter. Identical motifs can differ in occupancy if chromatin changes their accessibility.
  4. ATP-dependent remodelers are all activators. Positioning can expose or obstruct regulatory DNA. The motor's biochemical mechanism does not fix its transcriptional sign.
  5. Remodelers must unwind both DNA strands. Duplex translocation is central to representative chromatin remodeling. Do not equate helicase-like sequence domains with obligatory strand separation.
  6. Sliding removes histones. Sliding changes location, whereas eviction removes the particle or histone components. Protection shifts with retained histones distinguish them.
  7. Histone variants are modifications. Variants have different protein sequences; modifications alter existing residues. Exchange of H2A.Z is not methylation.
  8. Acetylation always ejects the octamer. Charge neutralization changes specific interactions but does not abolish all histone–DNA contacts. Reader recruitment is an additional mechanism.
  9. Acetylation and methylation have the same charge effect. Acetylation neutralizes lysine charge; methylation generally retains it. An exam may distinguish chemistry from regulation.
  10. All histone methylation represses transcription. H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 often accompany active transcription. Residue, degree and location determine the useful interpretation.
  11. H3K27ac and H3K27me3 never occur in the same sample. They cannot occupy the same lysine simultaneously, but different histones or cells can carry either mark.
  12. A reader always writes the mark it binds. Recognition and catalysis are separate functions. Reader loss may preserve the modification but prevent a downstream response.
  13. Global acetylation predicts every gene's output. Locus-specific regulators and indirect pathways differ; measure the relevant gene rather than infer it from a global average.
  14. ChIP enrichment proves causality. Association is not necessity or sufficiency. Catalytic perturbations, time courses and rescue strengthen mechanistic inference.
  15. A stable chromatin mark automatically proves inheritance. Persistence after a signal is not evidence of transmission through cell division. Epigenetic memory requires additional evidence.
  16. Reduced raw mark signal always means lower modification per histone. Histone occupancy may fall faster than the mark signal. Compare mark/H3 indices under controlled proportionality assumptions.

Integrated revision and mastery

DNA accessibility is a physical and regulatory property, not an alternative to sequence recognition. Nucleosome organization is dynamic. ATP-dependent motors change the position, occupancy or composition of chromatin; covalent modifications change histone chemistry and recognition surfaces. Gene-specific regulators coordinate these processes to promote or restrict initiation and elongation. Histone marks provide context-dependent evidence, not a universal binary code. Compare physical accessibility, histone chemistry, regulator association and nascent RNA to test the direction of causality.

High-yield relationships

  • ATP-dependent sliding can alter accessibility without changing histone number.
  • HAT transfers acetyl groups from acetyl-CoA; HDAC removes them.
  • Histone methyltransferases generally use SAM, and demethylases have substrate specificity.
  • HP1–H3K9 methylation and PRC2–H3K27 methylation are representative but distinct repressive systems.
  • H3K36 methylation in yeast can help suppress cryptic intragenic initiation.
  • Writer, eraser and histone replacement rates together determine modification abundance.
