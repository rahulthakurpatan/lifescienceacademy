From a mixture to a measurement

Crushing a green leaf in a suitable solvent produces an extract containing many kinds of molecules. Some pigments participate directly in photosynthesis, some protect the light-harvesting apparatus, and many other substances in the extract are not pigments at all. A single absorbance measurement on the mixture cannot assign each part of the signal to a particular compound. Even if centrifugation has removed the larger fragments of tissue, the supernatant may still contain hundreds of dissolved substances. A separation is required before an individual component can be studied with confidence.

Chromatography meets this need by allowing molecules in a mixture to travel at different average speeds through a defined separation system. A moving material called the mobile phase carries the sample. Another material called the stationary phase remains in a fixed location relative to the device. A particular molecule spends some fraction of its time traveling with the mobile phase and some fraction interacting with or exploring the stationary phase. Molecules that differ in this behavior gradually become spatially separated. The separation may be observed as a pattern of spots on a plate or as a series of peaks recorded from a column.

A chromatographic separation should not be imagined as a row of barriers of progressively smaller size. That analogy is useful only for certain aspects of size exclusion and even there the pores are mainly spaces to explore, not simple gates that reject large molecules from passage through the entire device. In many other types the decisive property is the relative energetic preference for two environments. A small polar molecule may be retarded much more strongly than a large nonpolar molecule on one surface, but the same pair may reverse their order when the stationary and mobile phases are changed.

It is also essential to distinguish a phase from a component of the sample. A phase is a region with particular chemical and physical properties; an analyte is a substance being studied. In a classical liquid chromatographic system the flowing liquid is the mobile phase, while the packed particles or a chemically modified layer on them constitute the stationary environment. A sample is introduced in a small region. It does not become a third permanent phase in an ordinary dilute analytical experiment. A solvent used to carry the sample into the instrument must be considered separately from the mobile phase that subsequently transports it.

The idea of chromatography emerged from the observation that a complex mixture could be divided into colored bands when transported through a column. Early work by Mikhail Tsvet on plant pigments showed the usefulness of differential adsorption. Later developments established that visible color is not a requirement for the method. Martin and Synge developed partition chromatography, for which they received the 1952 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Their work showed how repeated distribution between two liquid environments could separate molecules with relatively subtle differences in chemical behavior. Modern instruments extend these physical principles using controlled flow, well-characterized media and sensitive detectors.

Consider two molecules that have nearly equal probabilities of occupying the mobile phase. In a single contact with the stationary material their distribution may differ only slightly. Repeated movement and equilibration nevertheless allow the difference to accumulate. A molecule that spends a little more time traveling at every stage progresses farther than its competitor. The crucial idea is the repetition of small differences in average velocity, not a requirement that one component bind absolutely and the other not at all. In fact, reversible intermediate-strength interactions often produce the most useful separations.

Within the column, a collection of chemically identical molecules does not occupy a mathematical point. Individual molecules diffuse and follow different paths. They can encounter stationary sites at different times and may enter and leave pores at different rates. The initial sample zone therefore spreads as it travels. In a successful separation the centers of two zones move apart faster than the zones become wider. This competition between differential migration and dispersion explains why a chromatogram is more informative than a simple list of elution times.

Analytical chromatography aims primarily to obtain information, such as the amount of a compound in an extract or the number of detectable components. Preparative chromatography aims to recover a material for subsequent use. The best analytical result may be a very sharp peak from a tiny injection, whereas a preparative separation may accept some band broadening to recover useful quantities. A protein preparation often requires both: a preparative run to isolate fractions and a separate analytical measurement to test purity or activity. Confusing the two objectives leads to incorrect interpretations of yield and sample loading.

A column experiment normally starts with a defined mobile phase in equilibrium with the stationary phase. A narrow sample zone enters, and movement of the mobile phase advances its components. In an elution experiment, separated components leave the column and are detected or collected. The detector records a response that may depend on absorbance, fluorescence or another measurable property. It does not generally count all molecules equally. A detector that responds to an aromatic pigment but not to a sugar may show only one peak in a sample that contains both.

The initial region of a chromatogram can include a disturbance associated with introduction of the sample solvent. It should not automatically be classified as an analyte. An unretained marker, selected to avoid stationary interactions, can help estimate the time taken for mobile phase transport through a conventional column. This time provides a reference for the additional delay caused by retention. In size exclusion, different accessible volumes have special meanings, which will be developed separately. A blank run gives a complementary estimate of signals produced by the system rather than the sample.

A peak in a chromatogram represents a detector signal across time or volume, not a direct image of the molecule. Its position provides conditional evidence of retention under the chosen method; its area provides quantitative information if response has been calibrated and the peak is adequately integrated. Its width describes dispersion. Its shape can suggest overloading or unwanted interactions, although more than one cause can produce a similar distortion. Identification, quantification and assessment of separation quality are distinct reasoning tasks that must not be collapsed into a single observation.

For a biological sample the final goal is to retain the property relevant to the question. An enzyme can have a clear chromatographic peak and yet be inactive after separation. A lipid may remain chemically present while oxidation changes its biological properties. A mixture of nucleic acid fragments can give strong absorbance but lack a desired intact species. Thus the chromatogram is often combined with an independent functional assay or orthogonal measurement. Earlier nodes introduced sample integrity and centrifugation; chromatography adds a way of discriminating among soluble constituents. Electrophoresis, developed in the next node, provides another separation principle based on migration in an electric field.

Partition and adsorption

Partition is the distribution of a solute between two environments. Suppose a molecule is dissolved in two immiscible liquids that are in contact. Some molecules occupy one liquid and others occupy the second, and at equilibrium the rates of movement in the two directions balance. For a single chemical form of the solute under dilute conditions, the ratio of its equilibrium concentrations in stationary and mobile environments is called a distribution or partition coefficient, K = C_s/C_m. This convention makes a greater K correspond to a stronger preference for the stationary phase. Other contexts may define a distribution ratio in the opposite direction, so the convention must always be checked.

The ratio describes concentrations, not the total number of molecules in the two phases. If stationary and mobile volumes are V_s and V_m, then the stationary amount is C_s V_s and mobile amount is C_m V_m. The ratio of these amounts is K V_s/V_m. If K = 4 but stationary volume is only one quarter of mobile volume, the amounts in the two environments are equal. One cannot conclude that four fifths of the molecules are stationary simply from the concentration ratio of four. The amount fraction becomes K V_s/(V_m + K V_s) when the two environments account for all analyte. This relationship will later explain retention factor in an ideal equilibrium column.

At the molecular level, partition is influenced by interactions with the surrounding molecules. Polar substances can form favorable dipole interactions or hydrogen bonds in polar solvents. Ions require adequate solvation, whereas nonpolar groups often have a lower energetic cost in less polar environments. Neither polarity nor the number of functional groups predicts every result by itself. The entire molecular structure, ionization state and composition of both environments affect the balance. The phrase like dissolves like is a useful introductory guide but is not a quantitative rule and does not replace a stated chromatographic system.

Adsorption differs from bulk partition in that solute molecules accumulate at an interface or surface. On a polar solid such as silica, surface silanol groups can interact with polar functional groups of an analyte. Mobile solvent molecules compete for these same interactions. If the mobile phase occupies the surface effectively or stabilizes the analyte in solution, a solute is more readily displaced. A solid surface provides a finite number of sites; when their occupation increases substantially the relation between adsorbed amount and mobile concentration may become nonlinear. This is why sample loading can alter retention and peak symmetry.

Adsorption is not the same term as absorption. Absorption suggests penetration into the bulk of a material, whereas adsorption concerns accumulation at an interface. Actual chromatographic media sometimes combine surface interactions with distribution into a bonded layer, so the experimental distinction is not always perfectly sharp. A method is commonly classified according to its dominant separation mechanism. The classification is useful because it predicts which changes in solvent or stationary chemistry are likely to alter selectivity, but it should not be treated as a claim that other molecular interactions have disappeared.

Normal-phase chromatography commonly employs a polar stationary environment with a relatively nonpolar mobile phase. A more polar analyte may interact more strongly with the stationary surface than a less polar analyte, and therefore may travel more slowly. Increasing the proportion of a stronger, more polar eluent commonly weakens retention in this system by competing for surface interactions and changing analyte solvation. This trend is conditional on stationary material and solvent chemistry; two compounds with particular hydrogen-bonding properties can behave differently from a simple polarity ordering.

Reversed-phase chromatography commonly uses a comparatively nonpolar stationary environment and a more polar mobile phase. A typical example is an aqueous-organic mobile phase with a bonded hydrocarbon stationary layer. Hydrophobic solutes usually retain more strongly in such a system. Increasing the proportion of an organic modifier such as acetonitrile generally reduces retention of neutral hydrophobic solutes by making the mobile phase a more favorable environment for them. It is called reversed phase because the relative polarity arrangement is reversed compared with classical normal phase, not because the flow direction is reversed.

The influence of pH follows naturally from this interpretation. Imagine a weak acid that has a neutral protonated form and a negatively charged deprotonated form. The charged form is often better solvated by water. In a conventional reversed-phase separation it may therefore retain less strongly than the neutral form. An increase in pH can shift the balance toward the ionized species and shorten retention. A weak base shows the opposite acid-base speciation trend as pH increases. The word often is important: secondary ionic interactions, ion pairing and stationary chemistry may modify retention. Buffers also help maintain reproducible speciation between injections.

A mixed extract may contain two compounds that show very different partition coefficients in one solvent system but almost identical values in another. Changing solvents is therefore not simply a means of making a run faster. It can change the relative difference between the compounds and hence selectivity. If both compounds become less retained by the same factor, their separation in time may actually become worse even though the run is shorter. If one becomes disproportionately less retained, separation could improve. The proper observation is the distance between peaks relative to their widths, not the retention time of either compound in isolation.

The assumption of rapid reversible equilibration is central to many simple calculations. If the solute changes chemical form irreversibly, aggregates, precipitates or binds so strongly that it does not elute within the observation window, the ideal distribution model does not apply. Missing material should trigger an investigation of stability, solubility and recovery instead of an immediate claim that it was absent from the original sample. Biological materials in particular can be sensitive to changes in ionic strength, solvent composition and pH. An efficient chemical separation is of little value if it destroys the property that the subsequent experiment is designed to measure.

Paper and thin-layer chromatography

A planar separation is a convenient starting point because the movement of separated components can be observed directly. In paper chromatography a strip of cellulose paper provides a supporting structure. Depending on the solvent system, water associated with the cellulose can behave as a stationary liquid, and partition between that water-rich environment and the advancing solvent contributes to the separation. Direct interactions with cellulose can also matter. It is therefore more accurate to describe the predominant mechanism in a specified experiment than to insist that every paper chromatogram represents ideal liquid-liquid partition.

A thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plate has a thin layer of stationary material on a supporting surface, which can be glass, aluminium or plastic. Classical silica TLC is usually an adsorption-dominated system. Other media, including cellulose, alumina and bonded reversed-phase layers, are used for different purposes. In a simple ascending experiment the bottom of the plate contacts the mobile solvent while the original sample spot remains above the solvent level. Capillary forces draw the solvent upward through the layer. Dissolved analytes travel with it to different extents according to their interactions with the two environments.

The original position must be known so that distances can be measured. The solvent front is marked as soon as development ends because it can become difficult to locate after evaporation. Distances are measured in the direction of development from the origin. The retardation factor, often written R_f in planar chromatography, equals the distance travelled by the center of a solute spot divided by the distance travelled by the solvent front. If a spot moves 3.2 cm while the solvent front advances 8.0 cm, R_f is 0.40. The units cancel. A compound remaining at the origin has an R_f close to zero, while a compound moving with the front has an R_f close to one.

This planar ratio must not be confused with the column retention factor k, which is not restricted to the interval from zero to one. A larger R_f denotes faster migration relative to the front, hence weaker effective retardation. A larger column k denotes stronger retardation relative to an unretained marker. Examination questions can exploit this inversion by presenting two quantities under the loose phrase retention factor. The geometry and definition in the question determine which calculation is appropriate.

Suppose a leaf extract produces a spot at 2 cm and another at 6 cm with an 8 cm solvent front. Their R_f values are 0.25 and 0.75. The second traveled farther under those conditions. It is not legitimate to identify it as chlorophyll or carotene using the two distances alone. To make an assignment one can compare appropriate standards on the same plate and inspect features such as visible color or a suitable independent spectrum. Co-migration with a standard supports an assignment but does not uniquely establish identity. Chemically distinct compounds sometimes have the same migration ratio.

The initial sample spot should be narrow relative to the final distance between spots. If a broad deposit is introduced, the separation begins with overlapping spatial distributions rather than well-defined zones. Adding more sample can also overload the stationary layer, so that the center of the spot no longer represents an ideal dilute solute. A streak extending from origin to front may indicate several mechanisms including overloading, strong surface interactions, poor solubility, or degradation. Assigning a single molecular cause from a streak without controls would be unjustified.

The composition of a mobile phase is a controllable variable. Consider two neutral pigments separated on a polar stationary layer. If the solvent is too weak to displace either analyte, both stay near the origin. A somewhat stronger solvent may move the pigments into the central region of the plate where their different interactions can produce well-separated spots. If the solvent is excessively strong, both may approach the front. This explains why the highest R_f is not the aim of TLC optimization. The aim is sufficient separation under reproducible conditions, with adequate detectability and an interpretable front.

Even a fixed solvent composition may not reproduce exactly the same behavior if conditions differ. For example, water adsorbed by silica modifies surface chemistry; evaporation changes the effective mobile phase composition; temperature affects solvent transport and partition; and a tilted or poorly wetted plate can produce an uneven front. Comparing spots developed in different chambers without controls is weaker evidence than comparing them on a common plate. A reference substance traveling with the unknown provides a check for deviations in development behavior.

In addition to visible pigments, colorless substances can be separated and visualized. Some absorb ultraviolet radiation on a fluorescent indicator plate and appear as dark zones. Others are detected through a chemical reagent or a suitable optical method. The detection chemistry defines what becomes visible. A missing spot may mean low concentration, poor detection response or overlap with another spot, not necessarily chemical absence. The limits of detection for different compounds can vary considerably.

TLC is especially useful for examining simple mixtures rapidly, comparing extracts and assessing the presence of a component before more quantitative analysis. Paper chromatography has classical teaching applications in separating amino acids and plant pigments. Two-dimensional planar separation extends the idea by developing the same sample region in one direction, changing the chromatographic environment, and then developing in a perpendicular direction. If two compounds co-migrate in the first system but differ in the second, their spots may resolve in the resulting plane. This is an example of combining selectivities rather than simply repeating the same separation.

A distinction between detection and quantification persists in planar methods. Spot intensity may be influenced by sample amount, spreading, staining response and illumination. A spot that appears twice as dark is not necessarily twice as concentrated. Quantitative planar chromatography requires controlled application, suitable standards, a validated optical measurement and attention to the calibration range. For many examination problems the numerical information consists only of migration distances; such data support relative migration reasoning, not automatic estimates of concentration.

Ion-exchange chromatography

Many biological macromolecules carry electrical charge in aqueous solution. A protein contains acidic and basic groups, and the fraction of each group in its protonated state depends on pH. Nucleic acids normally carry negative charge because of the phosphate backbone under commonly used aqueous conditions. Ion-exchange chromatography exploits reversible attraction between charged analytes and oppositely charged groups attached to a stationary matrix. It can resolve molecules of similar mass but different charge characteristics, a problem that cannot always be solved by size-based methods.

The nomenclature is easy to reverse accidentally. A cation exchanger binds positively charged ions and therefore possesses negatively charged stationary groups. An anion exchanger binds negatively charged ions and possesses positively charged stationary groups. The name refers to the mobile ions that are exchanged, not to the sign of the stationary matrix. In the stationary material, the attached groups cannot move away with the flowing solvent. Small mobile counterions balance the fixed charges. When a suitable analyte enters, it can interact with the stationary groups and displace some of these counterions.

Carboxymethyl groups are examples of weak cation-exchange functionalities; sulfonate groups are commonly used in strong cation exchangers. Diethylaminoethyl groups are examples of weak anion exchangers and quaternary ammonium groups are typical strong anion exchangers. Here strong and weak concern the pH dependence of ionization of the exchange groups. The terminology does not mean that a strong exchanger necessarily retains every protein more strongly than every weak exchanger. Actual retention depends on ligand density, accessibility, ionic environment and molecular features of the analyte.

The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which a protein has zero average net charge under the specified conditions. At pH below its pI, a protein tends to have a positive net charge; above its pI, it tends to have a negative net charge. A protein of pI 5.2 at pH 7 is generally a candidate for anion exchange, while a protein of pI 9 at pH 7 is generally a candidate for cation exchange. The calculation is qualitative: subtract the pI from the working pH and interpret the sign correctly. A positive difference indicates a tendency toward negative protein charge, not positive protein charge.

The pI does not represent the pH at which every acidic or basic group becomes neutral. A protein at its pI still contains numerous charged side chains. It may have local surface patches of either sign, and these can interact with a charged stationary material despite zero average net charge. Thus retention is not determined exclusively by the difference between pH and pI. Protein shape, exposure of surface groups, ionic strength, and the number of simultaneous interactions are relevant. The simple pI rule is most useful when an examination specifies idealized behavior and asks for the expected sign of the net charge.

Consider an illustrative mixture of three soluble proteins at pH 7. Their pI values are 4.8, 6.1 and 9.0. In an idealized anion-exchange column the first two are negatively charged and may bind, while the third is positively charged and is more likely to pass through. One might predict that the first protein binds more strongly because its pI is farther from the working pH, but that order is not guaranteed for real proteins. Different numbers of exposed charges and spatial organization can reverse the order. If asked only to identify the likely bound proteins, the pI data are sufficient; if asked for exact elution concentrations, they are not.

Ionic strength is another principal variable. An electrolyte in the mobile phase supplies charged species that compete for interactions with the exchange groups and screen electrostatic attraction. At low ionic strength, attraction between a suitably charged protein and the stationary phase may be sufficient for retention. Increasing salt concentration often weakens effective binding and promotes elution. The ions released and taken up are part of an exchange equilibrium. The effect is not simply mechanical washing away by a heavy liquid, because salt changes the molecular interactions that govern distribution.

In a salt gradient, mobile phase composition changes gradually during the experiment. Proteins with weaker effective retention typically elute at lower salt concentration than those with stronger retention, provided other conditions remain comparable. In step elution, salt concentration is changed in discrete stages. A step may release several substances together, whereas a continuous gradient can sometimes resolve smaller differences. A shallow gradient provides a wider time or volume interval over which similar binding strengths are expressed, at the cost of a longer separation and potentially more dilute collected material.

A pH change can also release an analyte by altering the charge of the protein, the exchange group or both. For example, reducing the pH of an anion-exchange system toward a protein's pI may diminish its net negative charge and lower its retention. A weak anion exchanger itself may gain positive charge on protonation, so the net effect is not always predicted by protein charge alone. Biological stability must be considered: a protein may precipitate or aggregate near its pI, and a substantial pH shift may affect folding or enzyme activity.

In a practical analytical interpretation, the detector trace is accompanied by information about mobile phase composition, often obtained through a conductivity measurement when salt changes. Conductivity is an indirect measure of the concentration and mobility of ions in solution, not a direct measurement of protein mass. A conductivity trace rising during a gradient is expected even if no protein is present. Protein peaks must therefore be distinguished from the gradient trace and any baseline changes associated with solvent composition. A blank gradient is a useful control for this purpose.

A preparative ion-exchange experiment may be designed either to capture the target or to capture contaminants. In the first strategy the target binds, unwanted material passes through, and the target is recovered by elution. In the second strategy the target is recovered in the unbound fraction while selected impurities are retained. A large flow-through peak is not necessarily a failed purification; it may be the intended product stream. A student should identify which property belongs to the desired molecule and which to the impurities before interpreting a column diagram.

There are limits to the amount of material that an exchanger can retain. When available sites are occupied, additional analyte can break through during loading. If the target appears both in the flow-through and in the later elution peak, possible explanations include capacity limitation, binding heterogeneity and unsuitable mobile phase conditions. Comparing different sample loads under otherwise identical conditions can help distinguish a capacity problem from a failure of attraction. Recovery should include both bound and unbound fractions when tracing the fate of a molecule.

Nucleic acids are another useful example of the distinction between size and charge. A negatively charged nucleic acid can interact with a positively charged exchange medium, but the actual retention depends on the amount and accessibility of charge as well as molecular conformation and the surrounding ions. One cannot assume that doubling chain length doubles retention time. The experimental quantity is a collective consequence of many interactions, not a direct reading of sequence length. The method can nevertheless be valuable in fractionating nucleic acids and other charged biological substances.

The principal lesson is that ion exchange separates on the basis of effective electrostatic interactions under a stated chemical environment. It can complement a size-based column because similar-sized molecules may differ greatly in surface charge. It can also precede size exclusion when a sample needs to be concentrated through capture and elution before size analysis. The correct sequence depends on the target, its stability and the kinds of contaminants present.

Affinity chromatography

Ion exchange distinguishes broad physicochemical properties shared by many molecules. Affinity chromatography can exploit a much more specific form of recognition. A ligand, meaning a molecule or functional group capable of binding the target, is attached to a stationary support. The desired analyte recognizes that ligand and is retained while many other substances pass through. Subsequent changes in binding conditions release the analyte. The method translates an interaction familiar from biological systems into a means of purification.

Biological recognition is based on molecular complementarity. An antibody recognizes structural and chemical features of an antigen. A carbohydrate-binding protein recognizes certain sugar motifs. An enzyme recognizes aspects of a substrate, inhibitor or cofactor. A metal-binding tag interacts with an immobilized metal ion through appropriate coordinating groups. These examples involve different types of chemistry, but they share the practical possibility of retaining a target through selective reversible binding.

The support should provide a suitable environment for ligand attachment and target accessibility. Attaching a ligand directly to a surface can obstruct an important binding region, especially when the target is a large macromolecule. A spacer can sometimes improve accessibility by separating the ligand from the support. Immobilization changes the molecular environment, so an interaction measured between free molecules in solution is not necessarily identical to the same nominal interaction when one partner is immobilized. The matrix may also attract unrelated substances nonspecifically.

The simplest model describes a soluble analyte A reversibly binding a ligand L to form AL. For a one-to-one binding equilibrium the dissociation constant K_d equals the product of free analyte and free ligand concentrations divided by the complex concentration. A lower K_d corresponds to stronger equilibrium affinity under the stated conditions. If the ligand is immobilized, a related occupancy model can illustrate the importance of free analyte concentration and accessible sites, but a chromatographic column also involves transport and flow. Retention time cannot in general be converted directly into K_d from this formula.

Suppose two proteins interact with an immobilized ligand. Protein X recognizes the binding site with higher equilibrium affinity than protein Y under a given buffer condition. X may be retained more strongly, but if access to the binding sites is kinetically limited, the observed behavior may not match the equilibrium prediction. A binding interaction has both association and dissociation rates. A rapidly flowing mobile phase may provide insufficient time for a slow association process, whereas a slow dissociation rate may make recovery difficult. Equilibrium affinity and kinetic accessibility must be considered separately.

The basic stages of an affinity separation are equilibration, sample contact, removal of unbound components, and target elution. These stages are conceptual rather than a universal laboratory recipe. During sample contact a mixture enters the region containing immobilized ligand. Some target molecules bind. During washing many substances with little affinity leave. The retained target must then be released using a suitable change in conditions. An elution strategy that preserves activity is especially important when the goal is to study the native biological function of a protein.

Competitive elution is readily understood from binding equilibria. A soluble ligand can occupy the binding region of the target, reducing its tendency to remain attached to the stationary ligand. For example, in an illustrative system a carbohydrate-binding protein may be released by a soluble sugar that competes for its recognition site. The recovered fraction can contain this soluble competitor. If subsequent experiments require measurement of the same binding site, the competitor may need to be removed before activity is interpreted. High recovery of protein does not by itself establish that the binding function has been restored.

Other elution strategies modify pH, ionic strength or another property that influences binding. These approaches may affect more than the desired ligand-target interaction. Changes in pH can disrupt protein conformation; a competing molecule can interfere with a downstream assay; and a change in ionic strength may promote or suppress unrelated interactions. A well-designed separation evaluates target integrity after elution instead of assuming that molecules leaving the column are biologically unchanged.

Affinity chromatography can produce a large purification factor in one stage when the desired protein is a minor component of a complex mixture and the ligand discriminates strongly. However, high selectivity is not absolute specificity. Proteins sharing a binding domain can co-purify. Unrelated proteins may adsorb to the matrix, and fragments of the desired protein may bind if they retain the recognition feature. A single affinity elution peak can therefore contain several molecular species. An independent assay or a second separation principle is needed to assess purity.

Consider a benign illustrative enzyme preparation. Suppose total protein falls from 100 mg to 5 mg after affinity capture while measured total activity falls from 1000 units to 750 units. The starting specific activity is 10 units per mg; the recovered specific activity is 150 units per mg. The purification factor is 15 and activity recovery is 75 percent. These are different quantities: the preparation is strongly enriched in activity per unit protein even though a quarter of the starting activity has been lost. A student should not report 15-fold purification as 15-fold recovery.

A useful experimental control is a comparable support that lacks the intended ligand. If the target is retained similarly by both supports, nonspecific matrix interactions may explain the observation. A second control is competition with a suitable soluble ligand; loss of retention under competition supports the interpretation that a recognition interaction contributes to binding. Neither control alone proves a unique binding mechanism, because the competitor could alter other solution properties and two supports may differ in surface chemistry. Their combined evidence is stronger than retention alone.

Affinity approaches may also be used to isolate antibodies, lectins, or tagged proteins from benign samples. Protein A and protein G, for instance, recognize Fc regions of many immunoglobulins, with binding characteristics dependent on species and antibody subclass. These proteins are not universal receptors for all antibodies. A tagged protein may bind an affinity support yet lack native activity if the protein is improperly folded or if the tag disrupts function. The relevant inference from purification is the presence of a binding feature, not automatic proof of correct biological structure.

The important comparison with ion exchange is that affinity selectivity depends on recognition of a particular feature rather than only the net sign of charge. An affinity column can therefore separate proteins with very similar mass and pI values if only one recognizes the immobilized ligand. Conversely, two molecules that share the same affinity feature may need an additional charge-based or size-based separation. Combining independent properties is a recurring principle in biomolecular purification.

Size-exclusion chromatography

The preceding methods depend on the presence of molecular interactions that retard analytes. Size-exclusion chromatography is distinctive because its ideal separation mechanism does not require favorable chemical binding to the stationary material. The packed medium contains porous particles. A large molecule may be unable to enter most of the internal pore space, whereas a smaller molecule may diffuse into and out of much of that space. The smaller molecule spends additional time exploring internal liquid that does not advance through the column at the same average speed as the interparticle mobile phase. The large molecule therefore usually emerges first.

This elution order is sometimes surprising because a familiar sieve retains large particles and allows small particles to pass through. A size-exclusion column is not a membrane sieve that blocks the route of larger solutes. Both large and small molecules can travel in the liquid between particles. It is the availability of additional internal paths that delays smaller molecules. The analogy with a traveler who repeatedly explores side streets is helpful for understanding the direction of the effect, but real molecular movement is governed by diffusion and accessible pore volume rather than conscious decisions about routes.

The pores of a chromatographic gel do not all need to be of identical dimensions. The range of accessible sizes depends on the structure of the material. Crosslinked polysaccharide and synthetic polymer matrices are examples of media used for size separation. A medium is chosen so that the solutes of interest fall within a useful fractionation range. If both analytes are much larger than its accessible pores, they both elute near the exclusion limit. If both can explore essentially all the internal volume, they both elute near the total accessible liquid volume. In either extreme the difference in molecular size yields little chromatographic selectivity.

The void volume V_0 denotes the interstitial volume accessible to molecules completely excluded from the pores in an idealized size-exclusion system. An appropriately large, noninteracting marker can estimate it experimentally. The total permeation volume V_t in the simplified model is the liquid volume accessible to a fully included small marker, including the interstitial and accessible internal liquid. This definition does not necessarily equal the geometric bed volume because stationary solid material also occupies space. Different conventions in textbooks and manufacturers' documentation should be checked before applying a numerical equation.

An intermediate solute elutes at a volume V_e between these limits when the assumptions of ideal exclusion are satisfied. The conventional apparent distribution parameter K_av is calculated as (V_e − V_0)/(V_t − V_0), using a consistent definition of V_t. When V_0 is 40 mL and V_t is 100 mL, an analyte eluting at 64 mL has K_av = 24/60 = 0.40. An analyte eluting at 88 mL has K_av = 48/60 = 0.80. Under ideal size-exclusion conditions the first has the greater effective hydrodynamic size. These values represent fractions of the accessible internal liquid volume in the simple idealized model; they are not fractions of molecular mass.

The quantity that governs pore access is related to the effective size of a molecule in solution, often described through its hydrodynamic radius. Molecular mass and hydrodynamic radius correlate for comparable compact globular proteins, but they are not interchangeable. An elongated protein can occupy a larger effective solution volume than a compact protein of the same molecular mass. A flexible polymer may adopt a distribution of conformations. A hydrated glycoprotein can behave differently from a nonglycosylated protein of similar mass. Consequently, size-exclusion elution alone gives an apparent size or mass relative to the chosen standards and conditions.

A classical calibration compares the elution positions of several molecules with known sizes. For many sets of globular protein standards, a plot of K_av against the logarithm of molecular mass is approximately linear over an intermediate portion of the fractionation range. The direction is negative: larger standards elute sooner and have smaller K_av. This is an empirical relationship, not a law valid for all proteins at every size. When an unknown elutes within that calibrated range, interpolation estimates its apparent molecular mass. Extrapolation outside the range is unreliable because the elution curve approaches its exclusion and inclusion limits.

Suppose illustrative standards of 20 kDa and 80 kDa have K_av values 0.70 and 0.30, respectively, and assume that the relation between K_av and log mass is linear between them. An unknown with K_av 0.50 lies halfway between these two points. Its logarithmic mass is halfway between log(20) and log(80), so its apparent mass is the geometric mean, square root of 1600, or 40 kDa. Taking the arithmetic mean of 50 kDa would be incorrect under the stated logarithmic calibration. The estimate is meaningful only if the unknown behaves comparably to the standards.

Size exclusion is widely useful for testing molecular association. If a protein whose polypeptide mass is 30 kDa appears at a position corresponding to a 60 kDa globular standard, dimerization is a plausible interpretation. It is not the only explanation. An elongated monomer, an associated nonprotein molecule, a changed hydration state or calibration differences could also alter the elution position. A denaturing electrophoretic measurement of subunit mass and a complementary native mass method can strengthen or challenge the dimer hypothesis. The logic is to compare distinct physical observables rather than repeatedly measure the same approximate property.

Buffer exchange and desalting provide another application. A large protein may be excluded from the internal pores while small ions and solvent components can explore them. The protein then emerges before the small molecules originally associated with its sample solution. If the column is equilibrated in a different compatible buffer, the protein can be recovered in that environment. This is not dialysis, although both methods exploit differences in accessible space or transport associated with molecular size. In the chromatographic case the separation is expressed as different elution volumes during flow.

An important limitation of size exclusion is dilution. A narrow sample zone spreads during passage through the medium. The target may emerge in a volume larger than the starting sample volume. Loading a large sample volume also reduces the ability to resolve nearby peaks because it increases initial zone width. This is one reason that size exclusion is frequently used as a polishing or analytical step after another method has concentrated and enriched the target. It is not necessarily the most economical first stage for a complex crude extract.

The idealized mechanism also assumes negligible chemical attraction or repulsion between solute and stationary material. If a protein adsorbs to the matrix, it may elute later than expected or fail to recover completely. Electrostatic repulsion can reduce access to pores and lead to earlier apparent elution. An observed K_av outside the range zero to one is therefore a warning that the assumed accessible-volume model, marker choice or measurement may not describe the system. Such a result should not be interpreted as a negative molecular size.

A size-exclusion chromatogram of a protein preparation may show an early broad peak, a main peak and a late smaller peak. Aggregated species are plausible contributors to the early peak, the desired monomer or oligomer may form the main peak, and smaller fragments may form a late peak. These assignments remain provisional until confirmed using appropriate controls. The relative areas also need careful interpretation: a detector at 280 nm responds to aromatic amino acid content, not directly to the number of particles, and the response of unrelated proteins can differ.

It is helpful to compare the direction of this separation with ion exchange. Increasing effective charge attraction tends to delay a protein in ion exchange, whereas increasing hydrodynamic size tends to advance a protein in ideal size exclusion. The word retained has different physical explanations in the two methods. A question giving two proteins of equal pI and distinct hydrodynamic sizes is most naturally solved using size exclusion. A question giving proteins of equal size and opposite net charge under a specified pH is more naturally solved by ion exchange. Neither method is intrinsically superior; the measurable contrast between the analytes determines the choice.

Gas chromatography

Gas chromatography differs from the liquid methods because the moving phase is a gas. The sample components must enter the gas phase and remain sufficiently stable to travel through the separation system under the chosen conditions. A gas stream, called the carrier gas, moves them through a column. The stationary environment may be a nonvolatile liquid film or a solid adsorbent. Gas-liquid chromatography uses differential distribution into a stationary liquid-like film, while gas-solid chromatography depends more directly on adsorption. The fact that the mobile phase is gaseous does not imply that the stationary phase is gaseous.

A typical gas chromatograph includes a controlled carrier-gas supply, an inlet, a column within a temperature-controlled oven, and a detector. In an analytical separation the sample is introduced into the inlet, enters the gas stream, and travels down the column. In an open tubular capillary column the stationary film coats the inner surface and the center remains open to carrier-gas flow. Packed columns provide another configuration. The long separation path and repeated interactions can distinguish molecules whose chemical properties differ only moderately.

The dominant contrast in a gas chromatographic separation depends on both volatility and interactions with the stationary phase. A more volatile compound tends to spend more time in the gas phase under comparable conditions, but a particular stationary film may preferentially interact with it. It is therefore unsafe to rank all analytes solely by their boiling points. Boiling point can provide a useful approximation for closely related compounds on a relatively nonpolar stationary film. Differences in polarity, hydrogen bonding or other interactions may change elution order when the film chemistry changes.

Temperature is especially important because it influences both vapor-phase abundance and distribution into the stationary material. At a relatively low temperature, a compound may retain for a long time; at a higher temperature it may spend more time in the mobile phase. For a mixture containing a wide range of volatilities, a temperature program can begin under conditions that separate the more volatile substances and later increase the temperature to elute less volatile ones within a reasonable period. Excessively high initial temperature may make early components elute too closely, whereas an excessively low temperature may lead to long retention of late components.

It is important to distinguish the temperature program from a solvent gradient. In GC the programmed temperature changes the physical balance between vapor and stationary environment, and can alter retention during a run. In liquid chromatography a gradient typically changes mobile solvent composition or, for biomolecular methods, ionic strength or pH. Both approaches allow elution conditions to vary with time, but they are not identical mechanisms and should not be described using interchangeable experimental variables.

Carrier-gas velocity also affects efficiency. Molecules diffuse relatively rapidly in a gas compared with many liquid environments. At very low flow velocity they have a longer time to disperse along the direction of the column; at high velocity, equilibration between mobile gas and stationary film can become incomplete. A suitable flow regime balances these effects. The precise optimal flow depends on the gas, column geometry, temperature and analyte properties, rather than one universal flow rate.

GC is well suited to sufficiently volatile and thermally stable small molecules. Biological examples include certain volatile plant compounds and suitably prepared fatty-acid derivatives. Intact proteins, long nucleic acids and many unmodified carbohydrates are not appropriate for ordinary GC because they are nonvolatile or decompose under conditions required for vaporization. Chemical derivatization can convert some small polar compounds into more volatile forms. For example, fatty acids are often examined as fatty-acid methyl esters. Derivatization changes chemical identity, and interpretation must account for reaction completeness and the response of the derivative. A chromatogram of derivatives is not a direct inventory of intact original macromolecules.

The flame ionization detector is commonly associated with GC analysis of organic compounds. Its response arises from ions produced when organic analytes enter a flame; it responds strongly to many carbon-containing substances, but response factors are not identical for all chemical structures. A thermal conductivity detector measures changes in thermal conductivity relative to carrier gas. Different detectors vary in selectivity and sensitivity. A mass spectrometer coupled to a GC column provides information about ions and fragment patterns, offering additional evidence beyond retention position. The detailed physics of mass spectrometry is reserved for the biophysical methods subunit.

If a chromatogram contains a peak that coincides with the retention time of a known volatile plant compound, the observation supports but does not establish identity. A second compound can co-elute at the same time. A mass spectrum or a comparison on a different stationary phase may strengthen the assignment. Retention indices can provide more standardized relative information than absolute time when defined reference compounds and consistent methods are used. Nevertheless, neither a retention index nor a single mass fragment is infallible evidence of structure.

Consider an illustrative analysis in which a standard gives a peak at 7.4 minutes and an unknown plant extract gives a peak at 7.4 minutes under nominally identical conditions. Addition of the standard to a portion of the extract increases the area of that peak without producing a new resolved peak. The result is consistent with the suspected component, but another compound may still lie under the combined peak. If the mass spectrum across the peak changes from leading edge to trailing edge, co-elution becomes a reasonable concern. Agreement of multiple independent measurements is stronger than agreement of retention alone.

GC can distinguish mixtures that look identical by bulk measurements, such as different distributions of volatile organic compounds in two plant samples. However, sample preparation and detector response determine which substances are observed. An apparent absence of a late-eluting compound can result from insufficient run duration, sample instability or unsuitable volatility. A peak area difference between two samples can reflect variation in extraction, injection or instrumental response as well as variation in actual concentration. Standards, blanks and replicate measurements help distinguish these possibilities.

High-performance liquid chromatography

The liquid chromatography principles studied so far can be implemented using controlled pumps and columns designed for efficient separation. High-performance liquid chromatography, commonly abbreviated HPLC, is a family of instrumental liquid chromatographic techniques rather than a single stationary chemistry. The mobile phase is a liquid delivered at a controlled flow through a column that offers substantial resistance to flow. The pump provides the pressure needed to overcome that resistance. The separation mechanism may be reversed-phase, normal-phase, ion-exchange, size-exclusion or another compatible mode.

The phrase high performance refers to efficient and reproducible separations made possible by well-characterized media and instrument control. High pressure is often involved, but pressure itself is not the selective force that distinguishes two analytes. Molecular interactions and accessible environments remain the origins of differential retention. A column can operate at high pressure and still fail to separate two analytes if their interactions with the phases are almost identical. Conversely, an appropriately chosen lower-pressure chromatography medium can provide very useful selectivity for a biological macromolecule.

An HPLC instrument includes solvent reservoirs, a pump system, an injection device, a separation column, a detector and a data system. A mixing arrangement may produce controlled changes in mobile phase composition. A guard column can help protect the analytical column from particulate material or strongly retained impurities. The instrument records the signal as a function of time. The pressure trace is also diagnostically useful because sudden increases or decreases can indicate changes in resistance, leakage or other conditions affecting the flow path. The pressure reading is not itself a chromatogram of the analyte.

A fundamental distinction is between isocratic and gradient elution. In an isocratic run the mobile phase composition supplied to the column remains constant. The retention model based on a constant distribution coefficient is most readily applied in this case. In gradient elution the composition changes during the run, usually to decrease the retention of more strongly retained analytes. A reversed-phase gradient often increases the proportion of organic modifier. An ion-exchange gradient often increases ionic strength. The retention measured in a gradient depends on the history of the changing environment and should not be interpreted as if the analyte had one constant equilibrium retention factor throughout.

Gradient changes reach the column after traveling through the volume between the mixing system and the column inlet. This volume can delay the arrival of the programmed composition. Thus two instruments using the same nominal gradient schedule may produce different retention times if their mixing and flow paths differ. For examination purposes, this illustrates a broader distinction between a programmed condition and the condition actually experienced by the sample. Reproducible chromatography requires equilibration and consistency of the instrument as well as of the chemical method.

Reversed-phase HPLC is widely used for small biomolecules and peptide analysis. A hydrophobic bonded stationary layer interacts with analytes to an extent determined by both their structures and the mobile phase composition. Peptides with differing amino acid sequences may have different retention even if their masses are similar. Ionizable side chains and terminal groups complicate a simple hydrophobicity ranking. Conditions commonly used for analytical peptide separation may disrupt the native structure of a folded protein, so a method effective for sequence-related analysis need not be appropriate for preserving enzyme function.

In contrast, aqueous biomolecular chromatography commonly uses stationary media and mobile buffers compatible with the stability of proteins or other macromolecules. The term FPLC, often expanded as fast protein liquid chromatography, is associated with instrument platforms for such work. It should not be treated as a separate molecular separation mechanism. An aqueous ion-exchange or size-exclusion separation may use different instrument configurations. The key question is the column chemistry and sample requirements, not the acronym alone.

Smaller packing particles can improve efficiency by reducing characteristic diffusion distances and differences in flow paths, but they also increase hydraulic resistance. At fixed mobile viscosity and bed geometry, the pressure required for a given velocity rises strongly as particle diameter decreases. A high-efficiency column must therefore be compatible with the pressure limits of the instrument. Changing particle size without considering pressure, flow and temperature can make a theoretical improvement unattainable. Examination problems typically isolate these relationships using other conditions held constant.

UV absorbance is a common HPLC detection principle. An analyte needs to absorb at the selected wavelength to contribute appreciably to the signal. Proteins may absorb near 280 nm due primarily to aromatic residues, whereas peptide bonds absorb at shorter ultraviolet wavelengths. A molecule with weak absorption at the selected wavelength can produce a small peak even if its concentration is substantial. Fluorescence detection can be sensitive for naturally fluorescent molecules or appropriate derivatives. Refractive-index detection responds to differences in refractive index but is especially sensitive to mobile phase composition, making many gradient applications difficult. Detector choice must follow analyte properties.

A diode-array detector can record information at several wavelengths across a chromatographic peak. If the spectral shape differs between its leading and trailing portions, more than one absorbing component may be present. Agreement of the spectral shape does not prove absolute purity because co-eluting molecules can have similar spectra. Coupling chromatography to mass spectrometry can add another dimension of evidence, but ionization response and matrix effects introduce distinct analytical considerations. The basic distinction remains that a chromatographic peak is an interval of detector response, not necessarily a single pure molecule.

Injection solvent and sample matrix can affect peak shape. If a small volume of sample enters a system in a solvent that is much stronger than the initial mobile phase, some analytes may move too far before focusing into a narrow zone. Particulate contamination can raise backpressure, while dissolved matrix components can compete for stationary sites or alter detector response. These effects can be investigated by comparing standard solutions and matrix-containing samples under controlled conditions. A good chromatographic result is a property of the entire analytical workflow rather than the column alone.

Consider a benign example in which two extracts of a plant leaf are analyzed for the same phenolic compound. A higher peak area in one extract does not establish a higher concentration in the original tissue unless sample mass, extraction volume, dilution and recovery are accounted for. It may be that one extract was concentrated more strongly before injection. The relevant calculation will be developed in the calibration section. At this stage the important lesson is that the instrument measures the injected solution; converting its response into a biological comparison requires reconstructing the sample history.

For small molecules, GC and HPLC can sometimes address similar questions, but the choice depends on volatility, thermal stability, solubility and compatibility with the detector. For a thermally unstable soluble biomolecule, a liquid method is generally more suitable. For a mixture of volatile stable compounds, gas chromatography can be particularly useful. TLC may serve as a rapid preliminary comparison, and affinity or ion-exchange separation can recover native proteins. These choices all derive from matching the physical properties of the analytes to a suitable separation environment.

Retention and selectivity

A chromatogram provides several measurable quantities that can be used to separate the effects of retention, selectivity and efficiency. We begin with an isocratic column system in which composition and flow remain constant. The retention time t_R is the interval between injection and the specified characteristic position of an analyte peak, conventionally the peak maximum for a reasonably symmetric peak. The hold-up time t_M is the time associated with transport of an appropriate unretained component. Both quantities have units of time, commonly minutes. Because every solute must traverse the mobile pathway, even an unretained substance does not reach the detector instantaneously.

The difference t_R − t_M is known as the adjusted retention time. It subtracts the baseline transport time from the total measured time. Dividing the adjusted time by the hold-up time gives the dimensionless column retention factor, k = (t_R − t_M)/t_M. If an analyte has a retention time of 6.0 minutes and a suitable marker has a hold-up time of 2.0 minutes, adjusted retention is 4.0 minutes and k is 2.0. The ratio t_R/t_M is 3.0; this is not k because it includes the unretained travel contribution.

The physical significance of k can be derived in the ideal local equilibrium model. Let a solute spend a fraction f_m of its time in the mobile phase. Its mean progress relative to the mobile phase is f_m, if its progress in the stationary phase is negligible. The time needed to traverse the column is then t_R = t_M/f_m. Substitution into the retention equation gives k = 1/f_m − 1 = (1 − f_m)/f_m. The ratio corresponds to stationary-to-mobile amount or time fraction under the model assumptions. Consequently f_m = 1/(1 + k). For k = 2 the solute spends, on average, one third of the relevant time in the mobile environment.

Using the equilibrium concentrations defined earlier, the fraction in the mobile phase is C_m V_m/(C_m V_m + C_s V_s). Replacing the concentration ratio C_s/C_m with K gives f_m = V_m/(V_m + K V_s). It follows that k = K V_s/V_m. This derivation assumes accessible stationary and mobile volumes, sufficiently rapid equilibrium, constant conditions, and no additional uncorrected instrument delay. It explains why retention can change when the relative phase volumes change even if the chemical partition coefficient is unaltered. A retention factor measured in one column cannot simply be equated with an intrinsic molecular constant.

Consider an ideal column with stationary volume 1 mL and mobile volume 4 mL. An analyte has partition coefficient K = 8 using the stationary-to-mobile concentration convention. The predicted k is 8 × 1/4 = 2. With t_M = 2 minutes, the predicted t_R is 2 × (1 + 2) = 6 minutes. A second analyte with K = 12 gives k = 3 and t_R = 8 minutes. The raw time ratio is 8/6, or about 1.33. Their relative retention in terms of k is 3/2, or 1.5. The two ratios answer different questions.

For two positively retained analytes in the same system, selectivity or separation factor alpha is commonly expressed as k_2/k_1, where compound 2 is defined as the later-eluting compound. Under that ordering alpha is at least one. Equal retention factors give alpha equal to one. Increasing efficiency cannot produce separate peak centers if the two analytes have genuinely identical retention behavior under unchanged conditions. If the selectivity is very small but not zero, increasing efficiency may help resolve them. If alpha equals one, a different chromatographic chemistry or condition is necessary.

The use of adjusted times is essential when evaluating alpha. With t_M = 2 minutes, peaks at 3 and 4 minutes have adjusted times of 1 and 2 minutes. Their alpha is 2, not 4/3. Peaks at 10 and 12 minutes have adjusted times of 8 and 10 minutes, yielding alpha = 1.25. The second pair has a greater absolute difference between peak centers but a smaller relative retention difference. Resolution still depends on peak widths, so neither the absolute difference nor alpha alone fully describes the separation.

At constant volumetric flow Q, retention volume is commonly related to retention time by V_R = Q t_R. Thus a 7-minute peak at 0.8 mL/min corresponds to 5.6 mL of delivered mobile phase. In an ideal system with unchanged chemistry and appropriate correction for extra-column volumes, changing flow rate changes retention times inversely while characteristic retention volumes may remain similar. Doubling flow from 0.8 to 1.6 mL/min would predict a time of 3.5 minutes for a 5.6 mL retention volume. Real peak positions and widths may shift if mass transfer, temperature, or pressure changes substantially.

This distinction is valuable when comparing column data recorded in different units. Fraction numbers in a preparative separation are not times or volumes unless the collection interval is known. If each fraction contains 2 mL, the centers of fractions 5 and 10 are separated by about 10 mL, regardless of the arbitrary numbering origin. If fractions are collected at constant time intervals while flow changes, their volumes may differ. The appropriate variable must be reconstructed before combining chromatograms or calculating recovery.

The ideal retention model is not a universal interpretation of every chromatogram. In gradient runs an analyte encounters changing mobile conditions and its local retention may vary substantially along the column and over time. In size exclusion the unretained reference and fully included reference are used to characterize accessible volume rather than ordinary chemical partition. In GC, gas compressibility can complicate the relation between measured outlet volumetric flow and the volume within the column. The basic k formula is useful in the textbook situations for which its reference and assumptions are defined; it should not be extended mechanically to incompatible conditions.

A change in retention that affects both peaks proportionately may leave alpha unchanged. For example, initial k values of 3 and 4 give alpha 1.33. If solvent conditions reduce both to 1.5 and 2, alpha remains 1.33 but adjusted retention times are halved at the same hold-up time. In many simple models, the contribution of retention to resolution becomes smaller as k approaches zero. Therefore faster elution may come at a cost even if the relative selectivity is maintained. A method should balance adequate retention against excessive analysis time rather than simply maximize or minimize k.

A difference in selectivity is fundamentally a difference in relative free-energy preference for the environments sampled. In an ideal distribution model the standard Gibbs energy of transfer into the stationary phase is related to the partition coefficient through −RT ln K, with consistent reference states and activities. Comparing two analytes cancels the common phase-volume ratio when alpha is calculated. A small difference in transfer free energy can therefore produce measurable selectivity across repeated equilibration events. This provides a thermodynamic explanation of how chemically similar metabolites can be resolved without forming permanent bonds.

Efficiency, peak width and resolution

A chromatographic peak has a finite width because a large collection of molecules experiences a distribution of migration histories. Two peaks that differ by only a small amount in retention time can be resolved if both are narrow, but the same retention-time difference can be inadequate if the peaks are broad. The practical ability to distinguish adjacent peaks is described by resolution. For two peaks with baseline widths w_1 and w_2, a commonly used estimate is R_s = 2(t_R2 − t_R1)/(w_1 + w_2). All time values must be expressed in the same unit. The result is dimensionless.

Imagine two symmetric peaks centered at 5.0 and 6.0 minutes with baseline widths 0.60 and 0.80 minutes. Their resolution is 2 × 1.0/(0.60 + 0.80) = 2/1.40 = 1.43. The peaks are reasonably well separated but just below the conventional approximate baseline-resolution criterion of 1.5 for similar Gaussian peaks. The criterion is a guide, not a guarantee. Strongly asymmetric peaks, unequal peak sizes or a trace component next to a much larger peak can require different considerations.

If the first and second peak positions remain at 5.0 and 6.0 minutes but each baseline width falls to 0.40 minutes, resolution becomes 2 × 1.0/0.80 = 2.50. The selectivity of the centers has not changed; the improvement is due to narrower bands. Conversely, suppose widths stay at 0.60 and 0.80 minutes but the second peak shifts to 6.5 minutes. Resolution becomes 3.0/1.4 = 2.14. In this case the relative position of the peaks has changed. Both examples show that a better chromatogram can result from changes in distinct physical properties.

Chromatographic peak widths may be defined in different ways. The width at half maximum, w_1/2, is measured at half of the height above an appropriate local baseline. For approximately Gaussian peaks, a commonly used resolution expression based on half-height widths is R_s ≈ 1.18(t_R2 − t_R1)/(w_1/2,1 + w_1/2,2). A baseline-width formula must not be applied directly to half-height widths. Mixing the two conventions generally yields an incorrect estimate. When a problem does not state a width convention, the formula and result should be interpreted with explicit assumptions.

Efficiency describes the amount of band spreading relative to the position of a peak. The theoretical plate model treats the column as if it were a sequence of equilibration stages. These plates are mathematical constructs; a chromatographic column is not literally filled with stacked trays at which each solute waits until perfect equilibrium is attained. The model provides a useful relationship between band width and retention time. For approximately Gaussian peaks, the plate number N can be estimated as 16(t_R/w_b)^2 with baseline width w_b, or 5.54(t_R/w_1/2)^2 with half-height width. The numerical constants differ because the width conventions differ.

To understand these expressions, let a Gaussian peak have standard deviation sigma in units of time. Its ideal baseline tangent width is approximately 4 sigma, and its full width at half maximum is approximately 2.355 sigma. If the plate number is expressed as (t_R/sigma)^2, then substitution of the respective widths gives factors 4 squared = 16 and 2.355 squared ≈ 5.55, conventionally written 5.54. The derivation explains why the equations are not independent empirical tricks. It also shows why the estimates become less meaningful for strongly tailing or fronting peaks.

A peak at 8.0 minutes with baseline width 0.40 minutes has N = 16(8/0.4)^2 = 16 × 400 = 6400. A peak at the same retention time with baseline width 0.80 minutes has N = 16 × 100 = 1600. Doubling the width reduces estimated efficiency fourfold. The plate height H is column length L divided by plate number N. For a 160 mm column with N = 6400, H is 0.025 mm, or 25 micrometers. A smaller H denotes greater efficiency per unit length. Comparing N alone between columns of very different lengths may therefore hide an important difference in packing performance.

The relationship between efficiency and flow velocity can be understood by considering the physical sources of dispersion. In a packed column, molecules can follow paths of unequal length around particles. Some arrive earlier simply because their trajectories are shorter. Longitudinal diffusion spreads molecules along the column axis, especially when travel time is long. Finally, finite rates of transfer between mobile and stationary environments mean that some molecules fail to maintain the equilibrium distribution expected at their current position. This mass-transfer contribution becomes important at high flow velocities.

The classical van Deemter expression summarizes these contributions as H = A + B/u + Cu, where u denotes a representative linear mobile-phase velocity. A represents a contribution associated with multiple paths, B/u expresses longitudinal diffusion, and Cu represents finite mass-transfer effects in the simplified model. The parameters depend on the chromatographic system; A has dimensions of length, B has dimensions of length squared per time, and C has dimensions of time. These dimensions ensure each term has the dimensions of plate height. An open tubular column has no packed-bed multiple-path contribution of the classical type.

At low u, B/u becomes large because molecules have more time to diffuse along the direction of separation. At high u, Cu becomes large because molecules travel farther during the time required for redistribution. The curve of H against u therefore has a minimum in the elementary model if B and C are positive. Differentiation gives dH/du = −B/u^2 + C; setting it equal to zero gives u_opt = square root of B/C. At this point the diffusion and mass-transfer contributions are equal. The A term shifts the height of the curve but does not shift its minimum if it remains independent of u.

Consider an illustrative model with A = 0.010 mm, B = 0.080 mm²/s, C = 0.020 s and u expressed in mm/s. The optimal velocity is square root of 0.080/0.020 = 2 mm/s. The resulting H is 0.010 + 0.040 + 0.040 = 0.090 mm. At 1 mm/s the height is 0.010 + 0.080 + 0.020 = 0.110 mm, and at 4 mm/s it is 0.010 + 0.020 + 0.080 = 0.110 mm. This synthetic example shows that increasing flow can improve efficiency when starting below the optimum but worsen it above the optimum.

Changing column length can improve separation but also increases analysis time and pressure demand. When plate height is constant, doubling length doubles N. Under a simplified resolution relationship the effect of N enters through its square root, so doubling length does not double resolution; it increases it by a factor of about 1.414 if selectivity and retention remain unchanged. To double resolution through efficiency alone in this idealized comparison would require four times the plate number. Changing selectivity may be much more effective for poorly separated compounds than simply increasing column length.

A useful approximate relationship for two neighboring reasonably symmetric peaks is R_s ≈ (sqrt(N)/4) × ((alpha − 1)/alpha) × (k_2/(1 + k_2)). This equation assumes comparable efficiencies and uses the later peak's retention factor under an isocratic model. It expresses the three elements of resolution: efficiency, selectivity and adequate retention. The selectivity term vanishes when alpha equals one. The retention term approaches one as k_2 becomes very large; beyond a point, increasing retention provides diminishing returns while analysis time continues to increase. The equation is not an exact formula for every gradient chromatogram.

For an illustrative pair with N = 10,000, alpha = 1.10 and k_2 = 3, the square-root term is 100/4 = 25, the selectivity term is 0.10/1.10 ≈ 0.0909 and the retention term is 3/4 = 0.75. Estimated resolution is about 1.70. If alpha drops to 1.05 while N and k_2 remain unchanged, the estimate is 25 × 0.04762 × 0.75 ≈ 0.89. Even though efficiency is unchanged, a small reduction in selectivity greatly reduces resolution. This is why solvent or stationary-phase screening often has a major impact on the outcome.

Peak asymmetry is another source of diagnostic evidence. A tailing peak has a prolonged trailing portion, whereas a fronting peak extends disproportionately toward earlier elution. Tailing may arise from secondary interactions, a distribution of binding strengths or slow desorption. Fronting can occur with concentration overloading when the retention relationship becomes nonlinear. Neither observation uniquely diagnoses a problem. An injected amount series, a blank, and comparison with a stable standard provide ways to test alternatives. An examiner may provide such comparisons specifically to distinguish an overloaded system from one with chemical interaction or instrumental dispersion.

Instrument components outside the column can also broaden a sample zone. The injection path, connecting tubing and detector cell all possess volumes through which molecules travel. Dispersion from these regions adds to dispersion within the column. The effect is particularly noticeable when the column would otherwise produce very narrow peaks. A measured plate number describes the observed system peak unless those additional contributions are corrected or negligible. It is not always a pure property of the stationary packing. This explains why replacing a column with a shorter or more efficient one may not achieve the expected performance if the rest of the system introduces substantial broadening.

Calibration and quantitative interpretation

A chromatogram becomes a quantitative assay only when the detector response is linked to a known amount or concentration. Suppose a detector responds to a solute as it leaves the column. Integrating the signal above an appropriate baseline over the full peak provides a peak area. If the response is proportional to the amount passing the detector and the flow conditions are comparable, area is often a useful measure of the injected analyte quantity. Peak height can also be calibrated, but it is especially sensitive to changes in peak width. A broader peak of equal area can have a smaller height without representing a smaller amount.

The simplest quantitative model is external calibration. Several standard solutions containing known concentrations are analyzed under the same method as the unknown. The measured responses are plotted against the standard concentrations, and a suitable calibration function is fitted. For a linear model y = m x + b, y is the response, x the concentration, m the response per unit concentration and b the intercept. The unknown concentration is calculated as x = (y − b)/m. The intercept should not automatically be forced to zero because blank signal, baseline offset or other factors may contribute a nonzero response. The model should be supported by the data rather than chosen solely for computational convenience.

Consider a hypothetical pigment assay with peak area in arbitrary detector units and concentration in micrograms per mL. Standards at 0, 5, 10 and 20 micrograms per mL give areas 10, 260, 510 and 1010 respectively. The slope is 50 area units per microgram per mL, and the intercept is 10. If an unknown injected solution gives an area of 610, its concentration is (610 − 10)/50 = 12 micrograms per mL. This value lies within the calibration interval and therefore represents interpolation. A sample response far above 1010 should not be blindly inserted into the equation unless the calibration has been validated over the larger range.

The concentration in the vial is not necessarily the concentration in the original biological sample. Suppose the injected solution above was made by diluting an extract fivefold. The original extract contained 12 × 5 = 60 micrograms per mL. If the total extract volume was 10 mL and came from 2 g of leaf tissue, the amount measured in the extract is 600 micrograms and the amount per gram of tissue is 300 micrograms/g. These calculations assume the extract is homogeneous, volumes are correctly known, and the measured compound has not been lost in subsequent operations. If the extraction recovery is unknown, the result is more accurately described as recovered amount per gram under the chosen extraction method than as the total amount originally present.

An internal standard is a separate substance added in a known quantity or concentration to samples and calibration standards. The substance should produce a distinguishable chromatographic signal and behave sufficiently similarly during the relevant analytical operations. A response ratio, such as analyte peak area divided by internal-standard peak area, can reduce the influence of certain variations in injection or sample preparation. The degree of correction depends on when the standard is introduced and how similarly it behaves. An internal standard added after extraction cannot retrospectively correct losses that occurred before it was added.

For example, in a hypothetical assay the analyte area and internal-standard area are 500 and 250 in one injection, and 400 and 200 in a second injection of the same solution. Both ratios are 2.0. If the area difference arises from proportional variation in injected volume, normalization removes that variation. But if a sample matrix suppresses only the analyte response while leaving the internal-standard response unaffected, the ratio will still be biased. An internal standard does not provide universal immunity against analytical error; its value follows from the extent to which the errors are shared.

Standard addition addresses certain matrix-dependent response differences by comparing several portions of the same sample containing different known added amounts of the analyte. When sample matrix and final volumes are controlled, a linear response against added concentration can be used to infer the original concentration. In an illustrative equal-final-volume series, added concentrations of 0, 2 and 4 units give blank-corrected responses of 30, 50 and 70 units. The slope is 10 response units per concentration unit. The response at zero addition implies an endogenous concentration of 3 units in the final analytical mixture, equivalent to a negative concentration-axis intercept of −3. Any sample dilution must be corrected separately.

Standard addition does not automatically separate an interference from the analyte. A co-eluting compound that contributes a constant response to every portion increases the intercept and can lead to an overestimate of the analyte concentration. The method can help compensate for certain matrix effects on response slope, but it does not replace chromatographic specificity. A well-designed assay therefore considers both matrix-dependent sensitivity and the possibility of overlapping peaks. A spike that increases the expected peak is useful evidence of response but not proof of chemical identity.

Peak-area percentage is sometimes used to describe the relative signals observed within a chromatogram. The calculation is the area of a specified peak divided by the sum of selected peak areas, multiplied by one hundred. However, it equals a mass percentage only under suitable response assumptions or after corrections for differing response factors. Consider a mixture in which compound A and compound B each contribute 1 mg. If their detector response factors are 100 and 200 area units per mg, their areas are 100 and 200. Area normalization assigns A 33.3 percent and B 66.7 percent of signal, even though both contribute 50 percent of mass.

A peak-purity assessment, an identification test and a quantitative calibration answer different questions. Resolution and a stable baseline help define which signal should be integrated. A comparison with standards helps establish the likely chemical assignment. A calibration model then converts the selected response to an amount. Failing at any earlier stage can invalidate the final concentration even if the arithmetic is correct. For this reason it is useful to read a chromatogram in stages: first evaluate the method and controls, then evaluate separation and identity, and finally calculate quantity.
