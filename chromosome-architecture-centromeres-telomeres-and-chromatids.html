1. From chromatin to an individual chromosome

The nucleus of a typical human somatic cell contains many long DNA molecules, yet each molecule has a distinct biological identity. In a photograph of a dividing cell, these identities become visible as separate bodies of different sizes and shapes. Some appear as almost symmetrical crosses, some have very short upper arms, and others are shaped more like rods. These structures are not separate chemical species of DNA. Their differences arise because DNA is folded with proteins into individual chromosomes, each with a particular sequence organization and a set of specialized chromosomal landmarks.

A chromosome is a packaged unit of genetic information. In the conventional eukaryotic nuclear chromosome considered here, an unreplicated chromosome contains one continuous double-stranded DNA molecule and its associated proteins. Its DNA includes genes, regulatory regions, repetitive sequences and many other noncoding sequences. Chromatin is the material of which the chromosome is made: DNA in association with histones and other proteins. The nucleosome is a fundamental repeating unit of this material. A chromosome is therefore not a giant nucleosome, and a chromatin fiber is not necessarily a whole chromosome. The same material can occupy different levels of folding while retaining its identity as part of a particular chromosome.

The word chromosome is used both for the relatively extended structures of interphase and for the compact objects visible during division. A chromosome does not come into existence at metaphase. Rather, its DNA and proteins are organized into a form that becomes particularly easy to distinguish from neighboring chromosomes. A chromosome that is drawn as an X in a textbook has already passed through DNA replication and contains two associated copies. An unreplicated chromosome is equally a chromosome, even though it has no X shape. The distinction is central to interpreting both diagrams and examination questions.

Consider the requirements of a long, linear DNA molecule. It must be copied so that genetic information can be transmitted. Its copies must become physically distinguishable and be distributed in a controlled manner. Its natural ends must be preserved rather than treated as damaged DNA. Chromosome architecture meets these requirements through different structures rather than through DNA compaction alone. Replication origins allow copying to begin, centromeres establish the chromosomal site of a connection to the segregation machinery, telomeres protect the ends, and cohesion holds newly replicated copies together until their separation is appropriate. Replication chemistry and the timing of separation have their own detailed explanations; here the emphasis is on the structures that make these processes possible.

A useful starting point is a single linear chromosome with a centromere somewhere between two termini. The distance from the centromere to each terminus defines an arm. This arrangement can be followed from the unreplicated chromosome to the two sister chromatids and back to separate daughter chromosomes. It provides a reference system for locating genes, identifying chromosomes and interpreting abnormalities. In the preceding study of higher-order chromatin, the problem was how a long DNA chain fits into a small cellular space. The present problem is how particular parts of that folded chain acquire different functional identities.

The discussion assumes conventional linear, monocentric nuclear chromosomes unless an exception is stated. Bacterial chromosomes are often circular, mitochondrial DNA has a different organization, and certain eukaryotes distribute centromeric activity over much of the chromosome. These exceptions are biologically informative rather than violations of a universal X-shaped model. Before using any chromosome-count formula, ask what organism and physical state are represented.

2. Chromatids, sister chromatids and chromosome arms

DNA synthesis produces two duplex copies from an original chromosome. Each resulting duplex contains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand in the standard semiconservative replication model. After completion of replication, each entire DNA–protein copy of the chromosome is a sister chromatid. A chromatid is therefore neither one DNA strand nor one arm of a chromosome. Each sister contains its own double-stranded DNA molecule, its own copy of the centromeric region and two physical ends. It is useful to reserve the term sister chromatids for the replicated pair, rather than infer that every single chromosome in G1 must visibly contain a chromatid.

The sister copies are associated through cohesin, a protein complex that helps establish and maintain the connection between newly replicated chromatids. Cohesin is not DNA and does not covalently connect the two DNA molecules. The two molecules have distinct backbones after replication, even when they are physically held near one another. Some DNA entanglements may also need to be resolved before separation. The structural distinction between a chemical copy and a mechanical association explains why a cell can double its DNA content without immediately doubling its chromosome number.

A familiar X-shaped image often encourages the mistake that only its upper and lower halves are chromatids. To interpret it correctly, trace a continuous path from one end of the left copy, through its centromeric region, to its other end. That is one complete sister chromatid. The same path on the right identifies the second. Each has a short arm and a long arm where the centromere lies off center. A replicated chromosome consequently has four arms in the purely geometric sense that each of two chromatids has two arms. When cytogeneticists describe the arms of a chromosome type, they refer to the corresponding p and q regions, not to four different genomic arm identities.

The shorter arm is conventionally called p, from the French petit, and the longer arm q. These labels remain meaningful in a karyotype even when the chromosome is rotated or drawn upside down. If the arms are almost equal, the conventional orientation and banding pattern still permit a consistent designation. A locus on p does not move to q simply because a photograph is rotated. The ends of the p and q arms are referred to as pter and qter respectively. Positional addresses such as 7q31 identify a chromosome, arm and band using a standard cytogenetic convention; they are not a count of chromatids or necessarily an exact nucleotide coordinate.

Sister chromatids are ordinarily near-identical copies of one homolog before recombination or mutation is considered. Their relationship is defined by their common origin through replication. Two DNA molecules that happen to have identical sequences are not automatically sisters if they arose independently. Conversely, sister identity is not abolished by a mutation in one copy or by an exchange of genetic material during meiosis. These qualifications prevent an overly literal application of the phrase identical copies.

When the connection between sisters is released and the copies move independently, each becomes a daughter chromosome. Neither copy loses a chromosome arm during this transition. Neither needs another round of DNA replication to acquire chromosome status. The same physical chromatid receives a new name because its relationship to the other copy has changed. This explains the temporary increase in the number of chromosomes counted across an undivided mitotic cell after sister separation. The detailed molecular regulation of cohesion and separation is part of cell-cycle biology; the present chapter uses only the resulting structural states.

3. Centromeres: a specialized chromosomal region

The long arms of a condensed chromosome are frequently separated by a narrow region known as the primary constriction. This region corresponds to the centromere in typical monocentric chromosomes. The name refers to a functionally specialized region, not necessarily to the geometric center of the chromosome. A chromosome with its centromere close to an end can be entirely normal. The centromere provides the spatial address at which the apparatus that engages spindle microtubules can assemble.

What makes a chromosomal region suitable for this role? In many organisms, centromeric chromatin contains a variant of histone H3 called CENP-A; plant literature often calls the corresponding variant CENH3. Histone variants are proteins related to ordinary core histones but with distinctive structural and interaction properties. In this case, the variant is incorporated into a subset of nucleosomes at the centromere. These nucleosomes provide a molecular environment that can be recognized by other centromere-associated proteins. A region marked in this way can maintain a particular functional identity even though its DNA is packaged into chromatin in the same general manner as other chromosomal DNA.

In humans, centromeres generally occur in regions rich in tandem alpha-satellite repeats. Repetition is a property of sequence organization; centromere activity is a property of a chromatin region capable of organizing the appropriate protein machinery. These are correlated in many ordinary human chromosomes but should not be equated. Not every alpha-satellite sequence is an active centromere, and centromeric activity can arise at noncanonical sequences in a neocentromere. In the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, by contrast, a short, sequence-defined point centromere can specify activity with a much smaller DNA region than the broad regional centromeres of most vertebrates. Such differences show why a human centromeric repeat cannot be treated as a universal sequence required for segregation in every eukaryote.

Centromeric chromatin and nearby pericentromeric chromatin must also be distinguished. The pericentromere is the chromatin surrounding the centromere. In many species it is enriched in repetitive DNA and compact heterochromatin and contributes to the mechanical organization of the sister pair. The centromeric core is the region that establishes the kinetochore attachment site; the adjacent chromatin contributes to the environment in which the site operates. They have overlapping biological functions but are not simply interchangeable names. A chromosome can retain many repetitive DNA sequences without retaining an active kinetochore-assembly site.

Replication raises a further structural question. When centromeric DNA is copied, the daughter copies must retain or re-establish the specialized chromatin identity. In many CENP-A-based systems, pre-existing centromeric chromatin helps recruit additional centromere-specific components to the newly duplicated region. The molecular details and timing differ among organisms. This is one reason that the primary DNA sequence alone does not always specify a centromere's activity. The ability to maintain a chromatin identity across cell generations is distinct from the copying of the nucleotide sequence itself.

An active centromere can be investigated by combining DNA localization with protein localization. A fluorescent DNA probe complementary to a satellite sequence can reveal where that sequence lies. An antibody against a centromere-associated protein can reveal where that protein is concentrated. If these signals coincide in a normal chromosome, the observation supports an association. If the protein signal appears away from the expected satellite locus, it raises the possibility of altered centromere identity. Neither observation by itself proves successful transmission; a functional test also requires evidence that the chromosome is retained and distributed in dividing cells. This distinction between sequence, protein localization and function is useful when evaluating experimental data.

Clarke and Carbon's 1980 yeast study supplied a complementary functional approach. They isolated a 1.6-kilobase DNA segment from the centromere-linked region of chromosome III and included it in a plasmid that already had a yeast replication origin. Its chromosome-like transmission through mitosis and meiosis provided evidence that the segment conferred a segregation property, not merely the ability to synthesize DNA. Comparison with a replicating plasmid lacking the centromere separates these two functions. Later work localized the budding-yeast point centromere to approximately 125 base pairs. The original 1.6-kb isolated segment and the later minimal functional sequence must not be confused. Nor should this result be generalized to the much larger centromeric chromatin domains of humans. A DNA sequence sufficient in one experimental host is not necessarily sufficient in another.

4. Kinetochore and centromere are not the same structure

Microtubules are polarized polymers of tubulin that form part of the cellular cytoskeleton. A spindle is an organized array of microtubules involved in chromosome distribution. However, a microtubule cannot recognize a chromosome merely because the chromosome contains DNA. A specialized assembly of proteins is required to connect the chromosomal material to the microtubule. That assembly is the kinetochore.

In a simplified structural model, the inner portion of the kinetochore is associated with specialized centromeric chromatin. Proteins recruited at this interface help organize an outer portion that interacts with spindle microtubules. The arrangement allows forces generated at the microtubule–kinetochore interface to be transmitted to the chromosome. The distinction is similar to the distinction between a defined attachment site and the physical attachment apparatus, but both are biological structures rather than passive pieces of hardware. The centromere is DNA plus its characteristic chromatin organization; the kinetochore is a multiprotein complex assembled in relation to it.

A replicated conventional monocentric chromosome has two sister centromeric regions. Each sister normally forms its own kinetochore during division. In a typical mitotic metaphase arrangement the sister kinetochores face opposite poles. Cohesion between sisters permits a mechanical relationship between the two attachments, so that the replicated pair is not simply two unconnected objects. A single unreplicated chromosome in G1 does not need to maintain the same visible outer kinetochore architecture as a mitotic chromosome. Inner centromeric components can persist while other components assemble or reorganize as needed. The number of potential sites and the number of fully assembled visible kinetochores must therefore be interpreted in a specified phase.

Three terms with similar names should be kept distinct. The centromere is a region of the chromosome. A kinetochore is the chromosome-associated attachment complex. A centrosome is a microtubule-organizing structure in many animal cells and is not part of the chromosome. Some cells, including most higher-plant somatic cells, organize spindles without the typical centriole-containing centrosomes of animal cells. Their chromosomes still require a connection to the chromosome-segregation machinery. The presence of a centromere does not imply that a chromosome carries a centrosome.

The functional difference becomes especially clear under perturbation. Imagine a cell in which a centromeric DNA probe produces its normal fluorescent signal but a protein needed for outer kinetochore assembly is missing. The DNA landmark may still be present, while a reliable connection to spindle microtubules is impaired. Conversely, a DNA fragment could recruit centromere-associated proteins but fail to survive because its ends are unprotected. Chromosome transmission depends on several distinguishable structures, and loss of any one cannot necessarily be rescued by increasing another.

The exact machinery by which erroneous attachments are corrected, the checkpoint response to unattached chromosomes and the timed dissolution of cohesion are important but different problems. For interpreting chromosome architecture, the main causal chain is sufficient: specialized chromatin organizes an attachment complex; the complex interacts with microtubules; this creates a mechanical linkage between chromosomal DNA and the spindle. A defect in the first two steps may leave a condensed chromosome visible while making its transmission unreliable.

5. Centromere position and chromosome morphology

A chromosome can be identified by several features, including overall length, the position of its primary constriction and the distribution of bands along its arms. Centromere position is especially useful because it gives the chromosome an intrinsic direction and divides its length into measurable regions. Conventional morphology is commonly evaluated in well-spread condensed chromosomes of comparable preparation quality, where the primary constriction is distinguishable.

A metacentric chromosome has a centromere near the middle. Its two arms are therefore approximately equal in length. A submetacentric chromosome has an appreciably displaced centromere and unequal arms, but both arms remain substantial. An acrocentric chromosome has a centromere near one end and a conspicuously short p arm. A telocentric chromosome has its centromere at, or effectively at, the end in the ideal morphological classification; there is no measurable short arm in the strict terminal case. A telocentric chromosome does not therefore lack end protection. It has a telomere at the terminal region and another at the opposite end, even if the small separation between centromere and end cannot be resolved by light microscopy.

These are descriptions of the relative position of a landmark. They are not statements about the number of genes, chromosome size, chromosome activity, or the number of chromatids. A large metacentric chromosome may contain much more DNA than a small acrocentric chromosome, but one could also compare chromosomes of equal DNA content with different positions of the centromere. A change in shape cannot by itself establish that a gene has been lost. In particular, changing condensation or projection can change the apparent lengths of arms in an image without altering the underlying DNA.

Some conventional drawings use V, L, J and I shapes to represent chromosomes during movement, respectively associated with metacentric, submetacentric, acrocentric and telocentric configurations. They are idealized shapes that depend on how the chromosome is oriented and pulled. They should not be used as an absolute classification based on any arbitrary curved object. A better method is to find the functional primary constriction and compare arm lengths. This also works for a single rod drawn without movement.

In the standard human karyotype, chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22 are the five acrocentric autosome types. Their short arms contain ribosomal DNA arrays and characteristic regions that can form a secondary constriction and a small distal satellite. These are useful examples of acrocentric chromosome structure, but not a universal requirement for acrocentric chromosomes in other species. Normal human chromosomes are not strictly telocentric. Other mammals, including the laboratory mouse, have many chromosomes with terminal or near-terminal centromeres. The difference illustrates normal species variation, not a difference in whether DNA needs to be protected or partitioned.

A secondary constriction is a visible narrowed region that is not the primary centromeric constriction. A nucleolar organizer region (NOR) contains arrays of genes for the large ribosomal RNA precursor. When active, these regions contribute to nucleolus formation; the chromatin at the region can have a distinctive appearance in condensed preparations. A satellite in classical chromosome morphology is the small portion distal to a secondary constriction. Satellite DNA, introduced in the study of repetitive DNA, is a sequence category; the two meanings of satellite should not be confused. An observed constriction needs to be interpreted with molecular and positional evidence before it is assigned centromere function.

For a practical interpretation of an acrocentric chromosome, imagine tracing its axis from pter toward qter. The distal satellite lies near pter, a narrow stalk may occur at the NOR, the remaining short-arm material leads toward the primary constriction, and the long arm extends to qter. The sequence order establishes why a secondary constriction must not be mistaken for a second centromere. A normal variant can have a short arm or satellite of different apparent size; some individual human acrocentric short arms even lack detectable rDNA arrays. This does not change their identity as chromosome types. The position of the primary centromere, not the size of the satellite, defines the broad acrocentric classification.

6. Measuring arm ratio and centromeric index

A description such as near the center is useful for a first inspection but becomes subjective when two chromosomes differ only slightly. Cytogenetic measurements express the same relationship using ratios. Let p and q denote the short-arm and long-arm lengths, measured along the chromatid axis from the primary constriction to the respective terminal end. Both quantities must use the same unit, such as micrometers, and must refer to a comparable condensed state. Define total measured length L = p + q.

The arm ratio r is the long-arm length divided by the short-arm length, r = q/p, for p greater than zero. Because q is chosen to be the longer arm, r is at least one. The centromeric index CI is the proportion of total length occupied by the short arm expressed as a percentage: CI = 100p/(p + q). The highest possible value of CI is 50% because the shorter arm cannot occupy more than half the total. For the ideal terminal case p = 0, CI is zero and the arm ratio is undefined as an ordinary finite number; it tends toward infinity as p approaches zero. These conventions are sometimes reversed in other measurement systems, so the formula in a question matters more than the abbreviation.

The two measures contain the same positional information. To derive their relationship, substitute q = rp into the expression for CI. This gives CI = 100p/[p(1 + r)]. Cancelling p (which requires p greater than zero) yields CI = 100/(1 + r). Solving for r gives r = 100/CI − 1 when CI is expressed as a percentage number. This derivation provides a useful check: a very large arm ratio must correspond to a small centromeric index. If both computed measures increase after shortening the short arm while holding q fixed, one of the calculations is wrong.

Worked example 1. A chromosome has p = 2.4 µm and q = 3.6 µm. Its total length is 6.0 µm. The arm ratio is 3.6/2.4 = 1.5, and CI = 100 × 2.4/6.0 = 40%. The same result follows from 100/(1 + 1.5) = 40%. The result suggests a relatively central centromere, not a terminal one. Under the widely used Levan-style scheme with median-region ratios near 1–1.7, it is consistent with a metacentric or median-region chromosome.

Worked example 2. A second chromosome has total length 8 µm and centromeric index 12.5%. Its short arm is 0.125 × 8 = 1 µm. The long arm is 8 − 1 = 7 µm. Its arm ratio is therefore 7. This is a strongly asymmetric chromosome with a short but nonzero p arm. Different nomenclature systems distinguish subterminal and terminal regions differently; without specifying the classification cutoff, the defensible conclusion is near-terminal or strongly acrocentric-like morphology, not strictly telocentric.

Worked example 3. A photograph is enlarged uniformly by a factor of two. A chromosome with p = 1.5 µm and q = 4.5 µm appears as corresponding lengths of 3 and 9 in the same arbitrary image scale. Both yield r = 3 and CI = 25%. Ratios cancel a common scale factor. This invariance is why arm ratio is useful when comparing calibrated images, but it does not eliminate errors caused by unequal distortion or inconsistent compaction of the two arms. If a chromosome is curved, its length should be followed along the axis rather than measured simply as the straight-line distance between the tips.

A karyotype can also report relative chromosome length. For a selected set of chromosomes, relative length equals 100 times the length of the individual chromosome divided by the sum of lengths of that set. The denominator must be stated: a haploid representative set and a complete diploid spread do not produce the same percentages. Relative length is distinct from centromeric index, which uses only the two arms of a single chromosome. Neither measurement alone gives exact base-pair length, because chromatin compaction can vary. Together with the banding pattern, the measurements provide useful structural identifiers.

7. Telomeres: why natural chromosome ends need protection

A linear DNA molecule has physical ends. This simple fact creates a serious biological problem. A break in a DNA molecule also creates ends, and the cell contains systems that recognize and repair broken DNA. If every natural chromosome end were treated as a break, chromosome termini could be joined to one another, degraded, or provoke a persistent DNA damage response. The distinction between an intentional natural end and a damaged end must therefore be encoded in the physical structure of the chromosome itself.

A telomere is a specialized DNA–protein complex at a natural chromosome terminus. In vertebrates the DNA commonly contains tandem repeats of the sequence 5′-TTAGGG-3′ on the guanine-rich strand. Its complementary strand is cytosine-rich. The guanine-rich strand typically extends beyond the end of its complementary strand to produce a single-stranded 3′ overhang. The term 3′ describes the chemical polarity of that terminus, not a fixed direction on a chromosome diagram. At opposite ends of the same linear chromosome, the outward-facing 3′ overhangs belong to opposite DNA strands. That is a consequence of the antiparallel orientation of DNA strands.

Repeated DNA alone is not sufficient to explain end protection. In mammalian cells, a collection of telomere-associated proteins called shelterin binds the telomeric DNA. TRF1 and TRF2 recognize double-stranded telomeric repeats, while POT1 interacts with single-stranded telomeric DNA. Other components connect and regulate these interactions. At the level needed here, the important principle is that the DNA sequence provides binding sites, and the associated proteins help form and maintain a protected end structure while suppressing inappropriate DNA-damage signaling and repair. The proteins do not make the DNA chemically unbreakable; they help the cell distinguish a normal end from one that requires repair.

At many mammalian telomeres, the single-stranded overhang can fold back and invade a complementary region of the double-stranded repeat tract, forming a looped structure called a t-loop. This can conceal the natural terminus and contribute to end protection. The displacement of one strand at the invasion site is called a D-loop. The t-loop is a useful structural model supported by observations, but it is not appropriate to assume that every telomere has an identical fixed loop at all times or that shelterin has exactly the same composition in all organisms. Telomere organization is dynamic.

Natural ends can lose their protected state even when DNA repeats remain. A defect in end-binding proteins may expose an end to the damage-response or repair machinery before its repeat tract becomes critically short. Conversely, a shortening telomere may remain protected for some time until the remaining structure is no longer adequate. Length and capping are related but distinct properties. This distinction predicts that a telomere-length measurement cannot by itself establish whether a chromosome end is functionally protected.

Suppose two chromosome ends become uncapped and are joined by a DNA end-joining reaction. The product may carry two centromeric regions, one from each original chromosome, if both centromeres are retained. If both are active, the joined chromosome may experience incompatible attachments during division. It can break again, producing another abnormal end. Such behavior illustrates why end protection and centromere function are complementary requirements for chromosome stability. The detailed genetics of rearrangements belongs to inheritance biology; the architectural conclusion is that the presence of DNA and even of a functioning centromere cannot compensate for inadequate end protection.

8. End protection versus end replication and evidence

Capping a chromosome end and maintaining its DNA length are different tasks. DNA polymerases synthesize DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction and require a pre-existing 3′ hydroxyl group. In the usual lagging-strand model, a primer initiates each short fragment. When a terminal primer is removed, there may be no appropriately positioned upstream 3′ hydroxyl group from which the missing terminal region can be copied. Processing of chromosome ends also contributes to the final length and overhang structure. Without an appropriate maintenance mechanism, repeated rounds of replication and processing can lead to progressive loss of terminal DNA. This problem is a consequence of a linear DNA template, not of a particular position of its centromere.

Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein enzyme. It contains a catalytic protein with reverse-transcriptase activity and an RNA component that provides a template for the addition of telomeric repeats. The enzyme uses the G-rich 3′ terminus as the substrate, aligns its RNA template with the end and extends the DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. Complementary strand synthesis and processing can then establish the characteristic double-stranded tract and overhang. The enzyme maintains the repeated end sequence; the complete protective structure depends on associated DNA-binding proteins. A cell with active telomerase is therefore not automatically immune to all forms of end damage.

In many human differentiated somatic cells, telomerase activity is low or undetectable, while it is maintained in germline cells, some stem-cell compartments and many cancers. The relationships are context dependent. Critically dysfunctional ends can contribute to a durable proliferation arrest in cells with appropriate damage-response pathways; cancer cells often gain a means of maintaining chromosome ends during repeated proliferation. A subset of cells and tumors uses recombination-based alternative lengthening of telomeres rather than telomerase. Some organisms, notably Drosophila, maintain chromosome termini using specialized retrotransposon-based mechanisms rather than the familiar vertebrate telomerase-repeat system. These exceptions reinforce the principle that chromosome-end function is broader than any one sequence or enzyme.

Classical discoveries clarified the distinction between end sequence and end-maintenance activity. Blackburn and Gall identified a repeated sequence at the ends of the linear extrachromosomal ribosomal DNA of Tetrahymena. Szostak and Blackburn subsequently showed that chromosome-end DNA from Tetrahymena could stabilize linear DNA molecules in yeast. The key comparison was between linear DNA carrying appropriate terminal sequences and linear DNA without such functional terminal protection. A linear molecule that remains as a stable inherited form provides more informative evidence than simple detection of DNA immediately after entry into a cell. The observations supported conservation of the functional principle of telomeric DNA across distant organisms; they did not show that all species have identical repeats or protein components.

Greider and Blackburn then detected an activity in Tetrahymena extracts that added repeated DNA sequences to suitable oligonucleotide ends. The repeating pattern of products supported an enzyme-mediated repeat-addition mechanism. Follow-up work established the role of an RNA template. An instructive modern teaching comparison would include a suitable primer incubated with active extract, a matched inactive-extract control, and a condition in which the RNA-dependent activity is specifically disrupted. Detecting repeat addition in the active condition supports enzymatic extension, while loss of activity when the necessary RNA component is absent supports its role. It does not directly demonstrate that a full cellular telomere is capped. The classic studies and the hypothetical control design should be distinguished: the latter is a reasoning model, not a claimed reproduction of the original experimental protocol.

A simple quantitative exercise makes length maintenance concrete. Suppose, as an illustrative model, a telomere initially has 7,200 bp of repeats and loses a net 60 bp per completed division with no lengthening activity. After 30 divisions the model predicts 7,200 − 30 × 60 = 5,400 bp. If an assumed functional threshold is 3,600 bp, the threshold is reached after (7,200 − 3,600)/60 = 60 divisions. The calculation assumes a constant net loss and a fixed threshold, neither of which is universal in living cells. Different ends of the same chromosome can have different lengths, and the shortest dysfunctional ends can matter more than an average. In an exercise, the arithmetic is meaningful only under the stated conditions; it is not a clinical prediction of cellular lifespan.

9. Homologous chromosomes, sisters and chromosome sets

The term homologous chromosome describes a relationship between chromosome types. In a typical diploid organism, a homologous pair consists of two chromosomes that carry corresponding loci in broadly the same order, one inherited from each parent. They need not have identical DNA sequences. For a given locus, one homolog might carry allele A and the other allele a. Alleles are alternative sequence forms at corresponding genetic loci. The homologs remain homologs even if they have different alleles, minor length differences or certain structural variants.

Sister chromatids have a different origin. A chromosome carrying allele A is copied during DNA replication to produce two sisters ordinarily carrying allele A. The homolog carrying allele a produces its own two sisters ordinarily carrying allele a. A replicated homologous pair thus contains four chromatids, not two. The four chromatids represent two chromosome identities, and the sisters of one homolog are not sisters of the other homolog. This distinction can be tested by tracing the origin of each DNA duplex rather than relying only on colors in a drawing.

During meiotic pairing, replicated homologs can associate in a structure often called a bivalent or tetrad. Bivalent emphasizes the two homologous chromosomes; tetrad emphasizes their four chromatids. Exchanges between nonsister chromatids of homologs can alter the combinations of alleles carried by the chromatids, but each sister remains defined by its replication origin. Homologous chromosome pairing and exchange are not required to understand a standard mitotic karyotype, in which homologs are usually placed side by side by the analyst rather than physically paired along their length in the cell.

Ploidy describes how many complete chromosome sets are present. A haploid cell has one set, a diploid cell two sets, and a tetraploid cell four sets. The symbol n refers to the haploid chromosome number for a specified normal chromosome complement; 2n is the diploid number. If a diploid cell copies all its DNA without distributing its chromatids, it is still diploid while its chromatids are paired. Increased DNA amount alone is not evidence of increased ploidy. Some organisms and cell types show unusual chromosome replication patterns, but the ordinary cell-cycle accounting model should be established before interpreting them.

The conventional human chromosome complement illustrates the distinctions. Typical diploid nucleated somatic cells have 46 chromosomes: 44 autosomes and two sex chromosomes. The 44 autosomes consist of 22 homologous pairs. A typical XX complement contains two X chromosomes, which are homologous; a typical XY complement contains chromosomes with substantially different sizes and gene content, although their pseudoautosomal regions are homologous. Thus a statement that all 23 human chromosome pairs are fully homologous is not accurate for a typical XY cell. The labels autosome and sex chromosome refer to chromosome categories, not to whether the DNA is present in a particular condensed state. Both autosomes and sex chromosomes replicate, have chromatin organization and require chromosome maintenance structures.

Chromosome number varies widely across species and is not a simple measure of complexity or total DNA content. For example, different organisms can distribute related amounts of DNA among different numbers of chromosomes. One cannot infer the number of genes, genome size or chromosome arm type merely from n. A student who sees a statement such as 2n = 14 should first infer seven members of a haploid chromosome set, and in a standard diploid context seven homologous pairs, rather than infer any particular number of metacentric chromosomes.

10. Chromosome, chromatid, DNA molecule and DNA-content accounting

Many examination problems become straightforward once four measurements are separated. Chromosome count records independent chromosome units according to the phase and convention. DNA molecule count records the number of continuous double-stranded nuclear chromosomal DNA molecules in the standard completed-replication model. Chromatid count describes the copies, especially the associated sisters of replicated chromosomes. DNA content measures the amount of DNA, often in units of C, where 1C denotes the DNA amount in one unreplicated haploid chromosome set. The symbols n and C measure different properties and should never be used as synonyms.

Let N be the number of conventional monocentric chromosomes in an ordinary cell before DNA replication. Assume all chromosomes are linear, replication has been completed when the replicated state is considered, and nuclear chromosomal DNA alone is counted. Before replication there are N DNA duplexes and 2N physical chromosome ends. Each chromosome contains one centromeric region. After replication but before sister separation there are still N replicated chromosomes, now containing 2N chromatids, 2N duplexes and 4N telomeric ends. There are also 2N physical copies of centromeric regions, which normally organize 2N sister kinetochores in mitosis. The convention of counting chromosomes by centromeres is shorthand for independent centromere-bearing chromosome units, not a literal count of every replicated centromeric DNA copy or kinetochore.

During S phase, DNA content increases continuously as replication proceeds at different sites. It is not generally meaningful to multiply chromosome count by a single integer to obtain the number of fully completed duplexes if a question refers to an arbitrary point during replication. A replicating DNA molecule contains branches and intermediate structures. The simple counting relationships apply to the well-defined unreplicated state and to the fully replicated state. This is why an examination question specifying metaphase is easier to interpret than one saying simply that the cell is in interphase, which includes several states.

At mitotic sister separation, the two formerly associated copies become independent chromosomes. In the still-undivided cell there are 2N daughter chromosomes and 2N DNA duplexes. DNA amount has not doubled at that instant: the increase occurred during S phase. The chromosome count has changed because the classification of the copies has changed. If division proceeds normally, each daughter receives N chromosomes. Therefore a temporary chromosome count of 4n in the entire anaphase cell is not evidence that each daughter will become tetraploid. Each pole receives a diploid complement in the ordinary example.

Worked example 4. A diploid species has 2n = 12. Its G1 cell contains 12 chromosomes, 12 duplexes and 24 telomeric ends. After S phase it contains 12 replicated chromosomes, 24 chromatids, 24 duplexes and 48 telomeric ends. At metaphase, there are ordinarily 24 sister kinetochores, not 12, while the chromosome count is still 12. After sister separation in the same cell, the chromosome count becomes 24 but DNA amount remains 4C. After cytokinesis each daughter contains 12 chromosomes and 2C. The example assumes every chromosome has one localized centromere and two ends; it does not apply unchanged to a circular chromosome.

Worked example 5. The unreplicated haploid nuclear genome of a model organism contains 250 Mb of DNA and n = 5. An ordinary diploid G1 nucleus contains 2 × 250 = 500 Mb in 10 DNA duplexes. At G2, it contains 1,000 Mb in 20 duplexes but still only 10 replicated chromosomes. The total number of chromosome ends at G2 is 40. Neither the average 50 Mb per unreplicated chromosome nor the total 1,000 Mb implies that all chromosomes have equal sizes. The arithmetic describes genome equivalents and physical units, not a measurement of the condensation of each chromosome.

The same bookkeeping can be extended to an ordinary meiotic cell if the stage is explicitly given. After replication before the first division, a diploid cell has 2n replicated chromosomes and 4C DNA. After separation of homologs in the first division, each normal daughter nucleus has n replicated chromosomes and 2C DNA. After sister separation in the second division, each resulting nucleus has n unreplicated chromosomes and 1C DNA. This is a structural accounting summary rather than an explanation of meiotic regulation. In any problem, count the number of cells or nuclei included in the observation before assigning totals.

11. Karyotypes and evidence from chromosome imaging

The chromosome architecture visible in a single drawing is most useful when individual chromosomes can be identified within an entire complement. A karyotype describes the chromosome complement of an organism or cell, including chromosome number and characteristic morphology. A karyogram is an arranged image of the chromosomes, often organized into homologous pairs. The words are frequently used interchangeably in practice. An ideogram is a standardized schematic representation of the size, centromere position and banding pattern of chromosome types. The difference is analogous to distinguishing an actual photograph from a diagram summarizing typical landmarks.

Why are metaphase preparations commonly used? The chromosomes are relatively compact and separate enough to be spread and individually observed. In an ordinary preparation, cells are collected in a stage rich in condensed chromosomes, spread onto a surface and stained. The microscope image allows the analyst to count objects, locate primary constrictions and compare overall lengths. A uniform stain can distinguish chromosomes of very different sizes, but several types may have similar sizes and arm ratios. Reproducible banding patterns provide additional positional landmarks.

A common cytogenetic approach is G-banding, which produces alternating darker and lighter regions along many mammalian chromosomes. These bands represent chromosome regions with different chromatin and sequence characteristics under the staining treatment. Dark G-bands often correlate with relatively AT-rich, gene-poor and later-replicating regions, whereas lighter bands often have other characteristics; these are broad associations rather than absolute rules for every gene. Two normal homologs usually share a recognizable large-scale banding pattern, allowing them to be matched even if individual nucleotide sequences differ. The next chapter examines chromatin states more directly. Band color is not by itself a measurement of transcriptional activity.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization, or FISH, uses a labeled nucleic acid probe to locate a complementary target sequence in a chromosome or nucleus. A probe for a telomeric repeat will ordinarily localize near chromosome tips in organisms with that repeat. A probe specific to a chromosome-associated satellite DNA may identify a centromeric or pericentromeric repeat region. A locus-specific probe can mark a particular gene region. Each experiment answers a different positional question. Protein immunostaining is complementary: it localizes a protein rather than a chosen nucleotide sequence. An antibody against CENP-A and a probe against centromeric DNA need not always mark exactly the same regions.

There are limits to optical inference. Two sister-associated probe signals may merge into one spot if they are closer than the resolution of the image. Conversely, one extended repeat array may appear split into several signals due to its organization and the imaging conditions. Counting dots is therefore not automatically equivalent to counting chromosomes. A telomeric probe may also reveal interstitial telomeric repeats away from the ends. Such a signal demonstrates sequence similarity, not necessarily a functioning internal telomere. An active end is defined by its location and protective organization, not by a fluorescent signal alone.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which a replicated chromosome has an internal telomere-probe signal in addition to the four expected terminal locations. A tempting interpretation is that it has a fifth telomere. But an internal repeat tract is not a physical DNA terminus and therefore is not an extra telomere in the architectural sense. Alternatively, suppose a chromosome end has no detectable signal. This may reflect a very short repeat tract, a different repeat sequence, inefficient hybridization or inadequate imaging, as well as genuine loss of repeats. Appropriate positive-control chromosomes and an independent end-protection assay would help discriminate these possibilities. Evidence must be interpreted in light of what the method actually measures.

Basic karyotype interpretation thus uses several independent criteria: the number of separate chromosomes, their relative size, centromere position, band pattern and, where needed, specific sequence or protein markers. Matching by size alone is insufficient. Chromosome rearrangements and numerical abnormalities are investigated more thoroughly in inheritance biology; the present purpose is to understand why the structural landmarks make that investigation possible.

Compare two measurement strategies. Flow cytometry using a DNA-binding dye can distinguish populations with approximately 2C and 4C nuclear DNA content under suitable calibration, but cannot by itself identify which chromosome carries a centromere defect. Microscopy of a stained chromosome spread can reveal individual chromosomal units but is sensitive to overlap, incomplete spreading and chromosome loss during preparation. A 4C fluorescence peak is compatible with a normal G2 diploid cell as well as an unreplicated cell with a doubled genome complement; phase information and a chromosome count are needed to distinguish them. These complementary observations explain why quantitative cell biology rarely treats a single staining result as a complete karyotype.

12. Structural exceptions and experimental tests of function

The usual chromosome model assumes one localized centromere and two protected termini. Biologists learn particularly much when one of these features is changed, because different mechanisms can be distinguished by their consequences. An acentric fragment is a piece of a chromosome without an active centromere. It may contain DNA, histones and genes, but ordinarily lacks the conventional kinetochore attachment site. Even if a fragment can replicate, it can be lost during repeated cell divisions because it has no reliable independent segregation mechanism. DNA replication and chromosome transmission are not the same biological function.

A dicentric chromosome carries two centromeric regions. If both form active attachment sites, they may attach in ways that generate incompatible mechanical forces. A bridge or break can result under some orientations, but it is not inevitable at every division. One centromere may become inactive, allowing a chromosome with two centromeric DNA regions to behave as though it has one functional centromere. This is a particularly instructive example because it demonstrates why the number of centromeric sequences is not always the number of active segregation sites.

A neocentromere is a new functional centromere formed at a chromosomal position that was not previously the usual centromere site. In certain cases it allows a chromosome fragment lacking its original centromere to persist. The identification of centromeric proteins at noncanonical DNA positions in such chromosomes supports an important role for chromatin identity in centromere specification. However, this does not imply that DNA sequence has no influence on centromere establishment or stability. The relationship is organism- and context-dependent. A sequence can promote assembly in a particular setting without being universally necessary in every eukaryote.

Holocentric chromosomes provide another exception. In organisms such as the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, kinetochore activity is distributed along much of the chromosome rather than restricted to one primary constriction. Consequently, conventional metacentric or acrocentric morphology based on one localized centromere is not an appropriate description. Fragment behavior can also differ from that in a monocentric system if fragments retain enough of the distributed segregation machinery. End protection remains necessary for ordinary linear fragments, so distributed centromeric activity alone does not guarantee stable transmission. Nor should a holocentric chromosome be called dicentric simply because attachment activity appears at multiple locations.

Ring chromosomes offer a separate conceptual exception. A linear chromosome that has become circular can lack two conventional physical termini. A circular DNA molecule does not face the same terminal replication geometry as a linear one. Nevertheless, a ring chromosome still needs a means of replication and reliable segregation, and certain ring chromosomes exhibit mechanical difficulties or instability. The statement that every chromosome has exactly two telomeres applies to the conventional linear case, not to all DNA molecules called chromosomes.

A classic experimental strategy for understanding chromosome elements is the construction of an independently maintained DNA molecule in yeast. A replication-capable linear molecule, provided with functional ends and an appropriate centromere, can behave much more like a chromosome than a DNA fragment with only an origin. In a conceptual controlled experiment, start with otherwise comparable constructs containing a replication origin. One is circular without a centromere, one is circular with a centromere, one is linear with protected ends and a centromere, and one is linear without end protection. Measure retention during repeated growth without selection, rather than merely the presence of DNA immediately after introduction. Differences in retention can indicate the contributions of partitioning and end stability; differences in copy number, replication efficiency and initial establishment should also be controlled. The comparison does not require that every stable replicating molecule have a centromere: ordinary plasmids can use alternative partitioning mechanisms.

The same reasoning connects the architecture of the chromosome to its material composition. A chromosome can have the correct sequence length but the wrong chromatin identity at a crucial region. It can have an active centromere but an unprotected terminus. It can possess the correct ends but remain entangled or fail to maintain proper sister association. The functional requirements are separable but cooperate during transmission. The chromosome is not merely a folded container for genes; its specialized regions participate directly in the preservation and distribution of the genetic material.

13. Putting chromosome architecture together

A coherent interpretation begins with a physical DNA molecule and its state of replication. Determine whether the figure represents an unreplicated chromosome, a replicated pair of sisters, two homologs or a collection of chromosomes. Next locate the ends and the specialized attachment region. In a conventional replicated chromosome, trace the two sisters separately, so that four chromosome ends and two copies of the centromeric region are recognized even while only one replicated chromosome is counted. This method prevents confusion between the visible X and the DNA molecules within it.

The same distinction applies to experimental data. A DNA-specific stain can show how much DNA is present; a sequence-specific probe can identify positions; a protein label can reveal the localization of an architectural component; and retention or segregation measurements can demonstrate a functional outcome. Each observation restricts the possible explanations, but no single assay necessarily proves every property. A strong Part C answer therefore identifies both what the evidence supports and what remains untested. If a mutant retains normal telomeric DNA length but shows terminal fusions, an end-protection defect is a reasonable possibility. If a centromeric repeat is present but the kinetochore fails to assemble, the DNA landmark is not sufficient for attachment. If DNA amount doubles but sisters remain together, chromosome number does not necessarily double.

The chromosome must also be understood in its wider chromatin context. Its arms contain many kinds of sequences, and its specialized regions have different molecular compositions and physical organization. A chromosome can preserve the same sequence order while its regions become more or less compact and accessible. The contrast between relatively open euchromatin and more condensed heterochromatin, including constitutive and facultative forms, develops this idea in the following study of chromosome organization. The structural landmarks established here provide a map on which those regional states can be placed.

14. Part B — Conceptual and assertion–reason practice

Original examination-style questions. Select the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. Which structure directly provides the protein interface with spindle microtubules?

  1. Centromeric repeat DNA alone
  2. Kinetochore
  3. Telomerase
  4. Centrosome within the chromosome
Answer and explanation

B. The kinetochore is the attachment assembly. A specifies a possible chromatin site but is not the protein interface; C extends telomeric DNA; D is an organizing structure outside chromosomes.

2. A replicated monocentric chromosome has how many duplex DNA molecules?

  1. One
  2. Two
  3. Four
  4. Eight
Answer and explanation

B. Each sister contains one duplex, giving two. A counts the chromosome unit; C counts the DNA strands or terminal ends, not duplexes; D has no basis in the stated architecture.

3. Which statement about sister chromatids is correct?

  1. They are the two DNA strands
  2. They are short and long arms
  3. They originate from replication of one chromosome
  4. They always arise from different parents
Answer and explanation

C. Sisters are replicated copies of a common chromosome. A and B describe components of one chromatid; D describes typical homolog origin.

4. Which human autosome is normally acrocentric?

  1. 1
  2. 7
  3. 12
  4. 21
Answer and explanation

D. The acrocentric autosome types are 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22. The other listed chromosomes are not members of that normal group.

5. For an ideal terminal centromere, p = 0. Which result is correct?

  1. CI = 50%
  2. CI = 0%; q/p is not finite
  3. CI = 100%
  4. r = 1
Answer and explanation

B. The numerator of CI vanishes, while division by zero is undefined. A and D correspond to equal arms; C would require the short arm to constitute all measured length.

6. Which feature distinguishes a natural telomere from a simple broken DNA end?

  1. All ends are circular
  2. A specialized repeat/protein architecture protects the natural end
  3. A kinetochore is attached to each tip
  4. Every end has the same sequence in all organisms
Answer and explanation

B. End protection depends on specialized DNA–protein organization. A contradicts linear ends; C confuses attachment sites and termini; D ignores evolutionary diversity.

7. A secondary constriction associated with rDNA is most directly related to what?

  1. Nucleolar organizer region
  2. Spindle pole
  3. Replication sister
  4. Telomerase RNA template
Answer and explanation

A. NORs contain rRNA gene arrays. B is unrelated to a chromosome band; C is an entire copy; D is an enzyme component, not the constriction.

8. A holocentric chromosome differs from a standard monocentric chromosome because:

  1. It has no DNA
  2. It lacks all attachment activity
  3. It distributes kinetochore activity along a large part of its length
  4. It necessarily has two active localized centromeres
Answer and explanation

C. Distributed activity is the defining distinction. A and B deny basic structure or function; D describes a different, dicentric organization.

9. Which is the G-rich vertebrate telomeric repeat in the 5′ to 3′ direction?

  1. TTAGGG
  2. CCCTAA
  3. AUGCCC
  4. TTAGGC
Answer and explanation

A. TTAGGG is the conventional vertebrate G-rich repeat. CCCTAA is a complementary orientation motif when written 5′ to 3′, not the specified G-rich motif; C contains RNA U; D changes the repeat sequence.

10. Assertion: a G2 diploid cell normally remains diploid. Reason: replication doubles DNA amount without separating sister chromatids into independent chromosome units.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both are true under normal cell-cycle assumptions. Replication copies each chromosome within its existing set. B misses this causal link; C and D contradict the stated facts.

11. Which statement about CENP-A is best?

  1. It is a telomeric DNA repeat
  2. It is an H3 variant associated with centromere identity in many species
  3. It is the sole universal centromere sequence
  4. It is a microtubule polymer
Answer and explanation

B. CENP-A is a histone variant in many centromeres. A confuses DNA and protein; C incorrectly universalizes a protein as a DNA sequence; D describes tubulin polymers.

12. In a typical XY complement, X and Y:

  1. Are sister chromatids
  2. Are identical throughout
  3. Share homologous pseudoautosomal regions but differ across much of their length
  4. Are autosomes
Answer and explanation

C. They are distinct sex chromosomes with limited corresponding pseudoautosomal regions. A confuses replication origin; B ignores differences; D is the wrong category.

15. Part C — Quantitative and experimental reasoning

All datasets and numerical scenarios below are illustrative teaching problems, not past examination questions or reported experimental measurements.

1. A diploid species has 2n = 18. At conventional mitotic metaphase how many chromosomes, DNA duplexes and telomeric ends are there?

  1. 18, 18, 36
  2. 18, 36, 72
  3. 36, 36, 72
  4. 18, 72, 36
Answer and explanation

B. Replication produces 36 sister DNA duplexes in 18 associated chromosome units. Each duplex has two ends: 36 × 2 = 72. A uses G1; C prematurely counts separated sisters; D confuses strands and duplexes.

2. The same species is at mitotic anaphase before cytokinesis. Which describes the whole cell?

  1. 18 chromosomes and 2C
  2. 36 chromosomes and 4C
  3. 36 chromosomes and 8C
  4. 18 chromosomes and 4C
Answer and explanation

B. Sisters become 36 independent chromosomes without another round of replication. A describes a G1 daughter, C invents new DNA synthesis, D retains the metaphase chromosome count.

3. A chromosome has p = 1.2 µm and q = 4.8 µm. Which pair of indices is correct?

  1. r = 0.25, CI = 80%
  2. r = 4, CI = 20%
  3. r = 5, CI = 25%
  4. r = 4, CI = 80%
Answer and explanation

B. r = 4.8/1.2 = 4; total length = 6.0; CI = 100 × 1.2/6.0 = 20%. A reverses arms and uses the long-arm fraction; C uses the total/short ratio and an inconsistent index; D uses the long-arm fraction.

4. A chromosome is photographed at two uniform magnifications. The apparent arm lengths change from 2 and 6 to 5 and 15 arbitrary units. What happens to CI?

  1. It rises from 25% to 40%
  2. It stays at 25%
  3. It falls to 12.5%
  4. It cannot be calculated without absolute units
Answer and explanation

B. Both images give 100 × 2/8 or 100 × 5/20 = 25%. A and C violate scale invariance; D overlooks that the unit cancels when measuring the ratio.

5. A centromeric DNA probe still labels a chromosome, but an outer kinetochore protein fails to localize. Which interpretation is best?

  1. The centromeric DNA must be deleted
  2. Microtubule attachment may fail even though the DNA landmark persists
  3. Telomerase must be overactive
  4. The chromosome is necessarily acrocentric
Answer and explanation

B. A DNA landmark and functional protein assembly are separable. A contradicts the probe result; C is not measured; D concerns centromere position rather than protein assembly.

6. An alpha-satellite array remains on a chromosome but centromere proteins concentrate at a different non-satellite region, which supports stable segregation. What is most consistent?

  1. A functional neocentromere
  2. A new telomere at the array
  3. A centrosome embedded in the DNA
  4. A necessary conversion to a ring chromosome
Answer and explanation

A. The noncanonical site has evidence of centromeric function. B is end protection and no end evidence is supplied; C confuses organelle and chromosome; D is not implied by relocation.

7. A linear chromosome with two centromeric DNA regions segregates stably, and only one recruits kinetochore proteins. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Both centromeres are necessarily active
  2. One centromeric region is functionally inactive in this assay
  3. It has no centromere
  4. Its DNA cannot replicate
Answer and explanation

B. The assay supports one functional attachment site under the observed conditions. A contradicts the protein localization, C ignores the active site, D does not follow from centromere status.

8. A mutant has normal mean telomere repeat length but frequent chromosome-end fusions. Which first hypothesis best fits?

  1. End-capping function is impaired
  2. Every chromosome lacks a centromere
  3. Chromosome length proves no damage exists
  4. All homologs became sisters
Answer and explanation

A. Repeat length alone does not prove capping. B concerns segregation, not the direct end phenotype; C overinterprets length; D is unrelated. Individual critically short telomeres are another possibility, so the hypothesis requires testing.

9. A hypothetical telomere has 6,300 bp, net loss 45 bp per division, and no lengthening. After 40 divisions how much remains?

  1. 4,500 bp
  2. 6,255 bp
  3. 8,100 bp
  4. 2,700 bp
Answer and explanation

A. Net loss = 45 × 40 = 1,800 bp; remaining = 6,300 − 1,800 = 4,500 bp. B subtracts only one division; C adds instead of subtracts; D subtracts twice the required loss. Real losses need not be constant.

10. In a conventional diploid cell with 2n = 8 and an unreplicated haploid DNA amount of 120 Mb, which statement describes G2?

  1. 8 chromosomes, 16 duplexes, 480 Mb
  2. 16 chromosomes, 16 duplexes, 240 Mb
  3. 8 chromosomes, 8 duplexes, 480 Mb
  4. 4 chromosomes, 16 duplexes, 120 Mb
Answer and explanation

A. G2 contains 4C = 480 Mb and 16 duplexes in eight replicated units. B counts sisters separately and undercounts DNA; C omits replicated duplexes; D assigns haploid chromosome count and DNA mass.

11. A tetraploid species has basic chromosome set x = 6 and a G1 somatic complement of 4x = 24. How many telomeric ends are present after full S phase if all chromosomes are linear?

  1. 24
  2. 48
  3. 96
  4. 192
Answer and explanation

C. Twenty-four replicated chromosomes contain 48 duplexes and 96 ends. A is chromosome count; B is duplex count; D doubles the ends again without justification.

12. A metaphase chromosome has two distinct terminal telomere signals per sister plus one internal signal. What is the strongest inference from the extra internal signal alone?

  1. The chromosome has five functioning physical ends
  2. The internal DNA contains a sequence recognized by the probe
  3. It has gained a new centromere
  4. The entire chromosome has become circular
Answer and explanation

B. Hybridization detects a complementary sequence. A confuses an internal repeat with a physical terminus; C requires centromere evidence; D conflicts with the stated terminal signals and is not shown.

13. Four chromatids of a bivalent carry alleles A, A, a, a at a locus. Which relationship is valid before exchange?

  1. All four are sisters
  2. The two A copies are ordinarily sisters, the A and a chromatids are nonsisters of homologs
  3. Each chromatid is a homologous pair
  4. The bivalent has four independent chromosomes
Answer and explanation

B. Replication copies each homolog, yielding two sister pairs in two replicated chromosomes. A merges the two origins; C misidentifies a single copy; D counts chromatids as independent chromosomes.

14. A yeast linear DNA construct has an origin and a functional centromere but unprotected ends. Another otherwise matched construct also has functional end sequences. What is the best prediction?

  1. The latter is more likely to persist as a stable linear molecule
  2. The origin alone ensures both are equally stable
  3. The centromere directly caps both termini
  4. Neither can replicate because both are linear
Answer and explanation

A. End protection adds a separate structural requirement. B overlooks end degradation or fusion; C confuses attachment with protection; D contradicts the possibility of replication of linear DNA. Actual retention depends on replication and segregation controls.

15. Two normal chromosome types have equal total physical lengths but CI values of 45% and 10%. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. The 10% chromosome has a more displaced centromere
  2. The 45% chromosome has fewer genes
  3. The 10% chromosome has no p arm
  4. The chromosomes must be homologous
Answer and explanation

A. CI measures the short-arm fraction: 10% is more asymmetric than 45%. B cannot be inferred from morphology; C requires a zero index; D requires additional identity evidence.

16. A researcher observes an apparently missing telomeric FISH signal on one end. Which additional observation most directly strengthens the hypothesis of functional uncapping?

  1. Terminal end-to-end chromosome fusion in the same mutant
  2. Unchanged chromosome arm ratio alone
  3. A different microscope magnification
  4. Presence of an unrelated cytoplasmic protein
Answer and explanation

A. End-to-end fusion is a functional consequence consistent with uncapping. B and C cannot establish end protection; D is unrelated. Probe failure and short repeats remain alternatives unless independently controlled.
