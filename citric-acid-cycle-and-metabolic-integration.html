1. Why a cycle rather than another straight pathway?

A cell that has converted glucose to pyruvate has gained only a small fraction of the energy potentially available from the original sugar. Most of its carbon remains in reduced organic molecules. In an oxygen-supported cell, pyruvate can become acetyl-CoA, and the acetyl group can be oxidized much further. The difficulty is not simply removing carbon. The cell must conserve useful energy while removing it, transfer electrons to suitable acceptors, and keep the molecules needed for the next reaction available. The citric acid cycle accomplishes these tasks together. It is also called the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle or Krebs cycle.

Recall three ideas before following the pathway. A carbon atom in a carboxyl group can leave as carbon dioxide in a decarboxylation reaction. Oxidation removes electrons from a molecule, often by transferring hydrogen or a hydride to a coenzyme. NAD⁺ accepts two electrons and one proton as a hydride to form NADH; the remaining proton may be released to solution. Finally, coenzyme A carries acyl groups through a sulfur-containing thioester linkage. Hydrolysis of that linkage is energetically favorable and can drive an otherwise unfavorable chemical change.

Acetyl-CoA carries a two-carbon acetyl group, CH₃–CO–. It cannot be turned into two molecules of CO₂ in one ordinary enzyme-catalyzed step while conveniently capturing the energy in small increments. Instead, it joins a four-carbon acceptor, oxaloacetate, forming a six-carbon intermediate. Two carbons are subsequently removed in distinct oxidation reactions, and the remaining four-carbon skeleton is rearranged and oxidized until it becomes oxaloacetate again. Regeneration is the defining feature of the cycle: the acceptor participates in the reaction sequence but cancels from the net equation.

This design creates two different ways of understanding the same pathway. From a stoichiometric perspective, one acetyl group enters and two CO₂ molecules leave per complete turn. From a molecular perspective, the carbon atoms released in the first turn need not be the atoms that just entered. A regenerated chemical species is not necessarily a regenerated collection of the same atoms. Keeping these perspectives separate will become essential in isotope-labeling problems.

The cycle does not merely serve glucose. Acetyl-CoA is also supplied by fatty acid oxidation and by the degradation of certain amino acids. Other amino acid carbon skeletons enter as cycle intermediates rather than acetyl-CoA. This convergence allows the same oxidation machinery to serve several nutrients while providing starting materials for synthesis. A pathway that is simultaneously involved in degradation and biosynthesis is called amphibolic. By the end of this chapter, the cycle should be recognizable as both an energy-conserving mechanism and a continuously maintained pool of metabolic intermediates.

2. How experiments revealed the cyclic organization

Early investigators could measure oxygen consumption by actively respiring tissues before the individual respiratory enzymes were known. If a tissue preparation were supplied with a small quantity of an oxidizable acid, a simple expectation would be that its additional oxygen uptake could not exceed the oxygen required to oxidize that acid completely, unless the acid affected other reactions. Studies on minced muscle challenged that expectation. Small additions of certain four-carbon organic acids stimulated substantially more respiration than their own complete oxidation would explain.

The critical observation was catalytic in a metabolic rather than strictly enzymatic sense. An intermediate could help oxidize additional substrate if it were continually regenerated. This suggested that the acid was not consumed once and discarded. In 1937, Hans Krebs and William Johnson investigated interconversions of organic acids in pigeon breast muscle and developed the cyclic explanation. Krebs's Nobel lecture recounts the experimental reasoning, including the catalytic stimulation of respiration by fumarate and citrate. The final biochemical details, including acetyl-CoA as the entering two-carbon unit, were established by subsequent work.

An inhibitor offered a way to test the proposed order. Malonate resembles succinate sufficiently to compete for succinate dehydrogenase, but it is not oxidized to fumarate by that enzyme in the normal reaction. A preparation in which succinate oxidation was blocked accumulated succinate. Under those conditions the normal regeneration of the four-carbon acceptor was interrupted. If oxaloacetate can be used repeatedly in an unblocked preparation but must be supplied repeatedly when regeneration fails, that behavior supports a cycle rather than a one-way series.

Consider an illustrative respiration experiment. Suppose 1 micromole of an added intermediate could consume at most 3 micromoles of oxygen if completely oxidized, yet oxygen consumption rises by 18 micromoles relative to a matched preparation without the addition. The extra 15 micromoles cannot be explained by oxidation of the added compound alone. Additional oxidation of other substrates has occurred. This observation is consistent with regeneration of a catalytic intermediate, but stimulation alone does not prove the exact sequence of reactions. Accumulation behind specific blocks, enzyme studies and carbon-labeling experiments are needed to establish the sequence.

Later isotope studies addressed a question oxygen uptake could not answer: which carbon atoms leave as CO₂ and which remain in the organic intermediates? Such evidence distinguishes a chemically correct net equation from the actual fate of atoms. Subcellular fractionation and enzyme assays also located the cycle in mitochondria in eukaryotic cells. These independent lines of evidence explain why the modern pathway is a mechanistic model, not merely a mnemonic drawn as a circle.

3. Where the cycle operates and how substrates reach it

In typical aerobic eukaryotic cells the soluble enzymes of the cycle are found in the mitochondrial matrix. Succinate dehydrogenase is the important exception: it is associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane, with its catalytic site facing the matrix. This location makes sense because that enzyme transfers electrons directly into the membrane respiratory chain. The matrix provides a shared aqueous compartment for organic acid interconversion, coenzyme availability and regulation by local metabolite concentrations.

Pyruvate made in the cytosol enters the mitochondrial matrix through a transport system. Pyruvate dehydrogenase then catalyzes its oxidative decarboxylation: pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺. This is the link reaction, not one of the eight cycle reactions. The three-carbon pyruvate loses one carbon before the two-carbon acetyl group begins a turn. Therefore, the NADH and CO₂ made by pyruvate dehydrogenase must be counted separately unless the question explicitly asks for the combined process.

The pyruvate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complexes share a chemical strategy: thiamine pyrophosphate supports decarboxylation, lipoamide transfers acyl groups and reducing equivalents, CoA accepts an acyl group, FAD participates in reoxidizing lipoamide, and NAD⁺ receives electrons ultimately. This resemblance is useful for predicting the consequences of a missing cofactor. It does not make the enzymes interchangeable: they recognize different substrates and produce different acyl-CoA molecules.

Fatty acid β-oxidation in mitochondria supplies acetyl-CoA independently of pyruvate dehydrogenase. Acetyl-CoA cannot freely cross the inner mitochondrial membrane; when its carbon is required in the cytosol, a transportable derivative such as citrate can carry that carbon. Thus the compartment in which a metabolite is measured matters: the total cellular acetyl-CoA concentration need not reveal the concentration accessible to citrate synthase.

Prokaryotes do not possess mitochondria. In aerobic bacteria that use the conventional oxidative cycle, most soluble reactions occur in the cytoplasm and membrane-associated electron transfer occurs at the cytoplasmic membrane. Some microorganisms lack parts of the oxidative cycle or use different versions for biosynthesis. Mammalian mature erythrocytes lack mitochondria and therefore do not run the mitochondrial cycle despite requiring ATP. They rely mainly on glycolysis for ATP production. These examples show that the pathway requires suitable enzymes, compartments and electron acceptors, not simply the presence of glucose.

4. The eight reactions as a single chemical strategy

It is easier to understand the pathway by first dividing it into two phases. The first phase joins carbon and then removes two carboxyl groups while capturing electrons. The second phase regenerates the four-carbon acceptor, harvesting additional energy in the process. The boundary is succinyl-CoA: at that point the net carbon loss has occurred, but the four-carbon molecule still has a high-energy thioester and must be converted back to the carbonyl-containing acceptor.

Beginning with oxaloacetate (4C), citrate synthase adds acetyl-CoA (2C), producing citrate (6C). Aconitase rearranges citrate into isocitrate without changing the number of carbons. Isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase each remove one carbon as CO₂, producing α-ketoglutarate (5C) and then succinyl-CoA (4C), respectively. The remaining reactions keep the carbon number at four.

Succinyl-CoA synthetase converts the thioester into succinate and makes a nucleotide triphosphate. Succinate dehydrogenase introduces a double bond, producing fumarate. Fumarase adds water across that bond, yielding L-malate. Finally, malate dehydrogenase oxidizes the hydroxyl group of malate to the carbonyl of oxaloacetate. Notice the logic of the last three steps: introduce a double bond, hydrate it to make an alcohol, and oxidize the alcohol to a ketone. This sequence prepares the acceptor for another carbon–carbon bond formation.

The cycle contains four oxidation steps. Three transfer reducing equivalents to NAD⁺ and one transfers them through enzyme-bound FAD to ubiquinone. There are two oxidative decarboxylations, one substrate-level phosphorylation, one isomerization and two other carbon-skeleton transformations. These categories overlap: an oxidative decarboxylation is both oxidation and carbon loss, so adding category counts is not an appropriate way to count reactions.

5. Step 1: citrate synthase and the energetic commitment

The first reaction combines acetyl-CoA, oxaloacetate and water to produce citrate and free CoA. Written in simplified biochemical notation: oxaloacetate + acetyl-CoA + H₂O → citrate + CoA-SH. Although joining two carbon skeletons could be energetically difficult, the enzyme couples carbon–carbon bond formation to hydrolysis of a thioester. This provides the thermodynamic driving force for entry into the cycle.

Oxaloacetate binds first in the classical ordered mechanism. Its binding changes enzyme conformation and helps create the acetyl-CoA binding site. An active-site base removes a proton from the methyl group of the bound acetyl unit, creating an enol or enolate-like nucleophile. That carbon attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of oxaloacetate. The six-carbon product initially retains its CoA linkage as citryl-CoA. Hydrolysis of that thioester yields citrate, and CoA is released to participate in another acyl-transfer reaction.

The order of binding is meaningful. A thioester is susceptible to hydrolysis, but hydrolyzing acetyl-CoA without making citrate would dissipate useful free energy without transferring carbon into the pathway. Substrate-induced changes align the reactants and favor hydrolysis after carbon–carbon bond formation. The enzyme therefore connects chemical specificity with energy coupling, illustrating principles from enzyme catalysis and induced fit.

Citrate contains three carboxyl groups and a central hydroxyl-bearing carbon. Its two chemically equivalent carboxymethyl arms make free citrate achiral, yet an asymmetric enzyme active site can distinguish those arms. This property is called prochirality. The distinction is important because citrate is not an indiscriminate mixture of all possible carbon arrangements. Once the original carbons have been placed in specific positions, the next enzyme can act stereospecifically, preserving information about which atoms came from oxaloacetate.

Citrate synthase is strongly favorable under normal mitochondrial conditions. Its reaction helps maintain a low oxaloacetate concentration by consuming oxaloacetate as it becomes available. This pulls the final reaction of the cycle, which is otherwise unfavorable under standard conditions. Nevertheless, a large negative free-energy change does not imply that the enzyme alone sets the rate of the entire pathway. Substrate access, product levels, the redox state and downstream reactions all influence steady-state flux. If oxaloacetate is depleted by biosynthetic withdrawal, acetyl-CoA cannot simply replace it as the initial four-carbon acceptor.

6. Step 2: aconitase prepares citrate for oxidation

Citrate's central hydroxyl group is attached to a carbon bearing three carbon substituents. An ordinary alcohol dehydrogenase cannot oxidize this tertiary alcohol to a ketone by removing hydrogen from the hydroxyl-bearing carbon, because that carbon lacks the required hydrogen. A rearrangement is therefore needed before the first useful NAD⁺-dependent oxidation can occur.

Aconitase catalyzes a reversible isomerization through cis-aconitate. It first removes water from citrate, forming a double bond, and then adds water back in a different orientation. Isocitrate is the resulting six-carbon molecule with a secondary alcohol group. The reaction has not gained or lost any carbon and has not yet captured electrons. Its achievement is to create the chemical arrangement needed for the next step.

Aconitase contains a [4Fe–4S] cluster. In this enzyme the cluster helps bind and activate substrate for dehydration and rehydration rather than functioning primarily as an electron-transfer carrier. The iron center coordinates the organic acid, helping orient water and the substrate. The asymmetric active site ensures that a particular isocitrate stereoisomer is generated. This specificity resolves the apparent paradox of a symmetric citrate molecule yielding asymmetric carbon fates.

Under standard biochemical conditions the citrate-to-isocitrate equilibrium favors citrate, but the isocitrate produced is consumed by isocitrate dehydrogenase. Removal of product shifts the actual reaction quotient and permits forward flux. A reversible enzyme can therefore participate in a strongly directional pathway without itself having a large negative standard free-energy change.

A useful inhibitor example is fluoroacetate, which can be metabolically converted to fluoroacetyl-CoA and then fluorocitrate. An inhibitory fluorocitrate stereoisomer interferes with aconitase, causing citrate metabolism to be impaired. The logic is a substrate-derived inhibitory product rather than simple competition of fluoroacetate with citrate at the outset. In an examination experiment, citrate accumulation together with reduced downstream formation of isocitrate-derived products suggests an early pathway block, although concentrations alone are insufficient to identify the molecular inhibitor uniquely.

7. Step 3: first oxidative decarboxylation

Isocitrate dehydrogenase converts isocitrate into α-ketoglutarate, a five-carbon dicarboxylic acid also called 2-oxoglutarate. In the canonical NAD⁺-linked cycle reaction, isocitrate + NAD⁺ → α-ketoglutarate + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺. This is the first point at which carbon dioxide is released and reducing equivalents are captured.

The reaction becomes intelligible when divided into oxidation and decarboxylation. The secondary alcohol of isocitrate is oxidized to a carbonyl group, transferring a hydride to NAD⁺. The immediate intermediate, oxalosuccinate, contains a carboxyl group positioned appropriately relative to the new keto group. That chemical arrangement favors carbon dioxide release because the developing negative charge can be stabilized in an enolate-like intermediate. Protonation and rearrangement then yield α-ketoglutarate.

Divalent metal ions, commonly Mg²⁺ or Mn²⁺, help bind the negatively charged substrate and stabilize reaction intermediates. This is not an oxidation of magnesium or manganese in the pathway. The metal is a catalytic cofactor, while NAD⁺ is the electron acceptor. Distinguishing these functions avoids the common assumption that every metal-containing active site participates in redox transfer.

In mammalian mitochondria, the NAD⁺-dependent IDH3 enzyme is central to the conventional oxidative TCA cycle. NADP⁺-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenases also occur in mitochondria and cytosol (IDH2 and IDH1 respectively) and can supply NADPH; their biochemical roles and reversibility differ. Do not count the NADPH produced by those enzymes as an additional obligatory product of the standard NAD⁺-linked cycle. In a problem that specifies the canonical cycle, count one NADH here.

The NAD⁺-dependent reaction is a major regulatory site. High NADH signals a reduced matrix redox state and inhibits oxidative flux; high ATP can signal sufficient energy supply, whereas ADP promotes activity in mammalian IDH3. Calcium can stimulate the enzyme in appropriate tissues. These regulatory properties connect the rate of producing reduced carriers to the demand for their oxidation. They will be considered together with the other regulated steps once the cycle has been completed.

8. Step 4: second oxidative decarboxylation and thioester formation

The next enzyme must remove another carbon from α-ketoglutarate and preserve the energy associated with oxidation. The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex catalyzes α-ketoglutarate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → succinyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺. As in the preceding step, the carbon skeleton becomes shorter and NADH is formed, but the product is now an activated thioester rather than a free organic acid.

The complex operates through three cooperating catalytic components. E1, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, binds thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) and initiates decarboxylation. The TPP-associated intermediate is stabilized by the coenzyme's ability to form a reactive carbon center and delocalize charge. E2, dihydrolipoyl succinyltransferase, receives the succinyl group and transfers it to CoA. A covalently attached lipoyl group can move between catalytic sites, conveying reaction intermediates without requiring every intermediate to diffuse into bulk solution.

The lipoyl group becomes reduced while the acyl group is transferred. E3, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase, restores the oxidized lipoyl disulfide. Its enzyme-associated FAD accepts electrons, and NAD⁺ subsequently receives them, regenerating the flavin and producing NADH. The ultimate reduced carrier counted in the net reaction is therefore NADH, not an additional free FADH₂. FAD functions here as a recyclable intermediate within a complex.

The five coenzymes are TPP (derived from vitamin B₁), lipoamide, CoA (vitamin B₅ derivative), FAD (vitamin B₂ derivative) and NAD⁺ (vitamin B₃ derivative). A shortage of thiamine can affect both this complex and pyruvate dehydrogenase because both require TPP. Arsenite can interfere with reduced lipoyl groups, impairing these complexes. In an exam case, decreased oxidation of both pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate with other enzyme activities preserved points toward a shared cofactor or component rather than two entirely unrelated catalytic failures.

This strongly favorable reaction removes the second carboxyl carbon and captures energy twice: once in NADH and once in the succinyl-CoA thioester. The latter is especially useful because it can drive the only direct nucleotide phosphorylation of the cycle. High succinyl-CoA and NADH inhibit the complex; calcium can activate it in mammalian tissues such as contracting muscle. Unlike mammalian pyruvate dehydrogenase, the canonical α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase control scheme does not rely on the same dedicated phosphorylation switch. Similar catalytic chemistry does not necessarily imply identical regulation.

9. Step 5: substrate-level phosphorylation from succinyl-CoA

The cycle has now released both carbon dioxide molecules, yet its energy-conserving work is not finished. Succinyl-CoA synthetase, also known as succinate-CoA ligase or succinate thiokinase, catalyzes succinyl-CoA + GDP + Pᵢ ⇌ succinate + CoA-SH + GTP in a GTP-producing isoform. Some isoforms use ADP and make ATP instead. The ATP- and GTP-specific forms differ in nucleotide specificity but conserve the same type of chemical energy.

Why can phosphate be transferred here without an electron transport chain? The succinyl-CoA thioester has a high group-transfer potential. In the enzyme, inorganic phosphate replaces CoA, producing an activated succinyl phosphate intermediate. The phosphate is then transferred transiently to a histidine residue in the enzyme, and subsequently to GDP or ADP. The resulting nucleotide triphosphate leaves, along with succinate. The active-site histidine is a catalytic carrier of phosphate, not a permanently phosphorylated regulator.

This is substrate-level phosphorylation because phosphoryl transfer is driven by the energy of a chemical substrate in the pathway, rather than by a transmembrane proton gradient. The analogous principle in glycolysis is used by phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase, although their substrates and mechanisms differ. The cycle's direct yield is one nucleotide triphosphate per acetyl-CoA oxidized. It is incorrect to describe all the ATP equivalents of the cycle as ATP molecules produced by this enzyme.

GTP is energetically useful in its own right and can also be exchanged with ATP through nucleoside diphosphate kinase: GTP + ADP ⇌ GDP + ATP. Under suitable conditions this makes one GTP equivalent to one ATP for conventional energy accounting. The exact physiological use of the GTP depends on cellular location and tissue needs.

The succinyl-CoA synthetase reaction is reversible and can run toward succinyl-CoA under appropriate substrate and product conditions. Some biosynthetic demands use succinyl-CoA; for example, the first step of heme synthesis in animals uses succinyl-CoA together with glycine. Reversibility in an isolated assay is compatible with forward net flux in the complete oxidative pathway. If succinyl-CoA is diverted before substrate-level phosphorylation, the GTP yield of the diverted carbon cannot simply be credited as if it had completed the entire cycle.

10. Step 6: succinate dehydrogenase connects the cycle to respiration

Succinate contains a saturated two-carbon segment between its terminal carboxyl groups. Succinate dehydrogenase removes two hydrogen atoms and creates a trans double bond, yielding fumarate. A conventional shorthand is succinate + FAD → fumarate + FADH₂, but the enzyme's FAD is a tightly associated prosthetic group, not a freely diffusing product. Its electrons pass through iron–sulfur centers and ultimately reduce ubiquinone (Q) to ubiquinol (QH₂). The physiologically relevant net reaction is succinate + Q → fumarate + QH₂.

The distinction between FAD and NAD⁺ here has a chemical basis. Oxidation of a relatively unreactive saturated carbon–carbon bond is not sufficiently favorable with free NAD⁺ as the acceptor under standard conditions. The protein environment tunes the redox properties of its flavin, enabling succinate oxidation and transfer to Q. Enzyme-bound cofactors are not all characterized by the same potential as their free forms. Thus the choice of FAD is a property of the coupled catalytic and electron-transfer system.

Succinate dehydrogenase is respiratory Complex II. Its catalytic domains face the matrix, while membrane-associated domains interact with ubiquinone. Because this route supplies electrons directly to Q rather than through Complex I, succinate-derived electrons generally support less proton translocation and less ATP formation than matrix NADH. Complex II itself is not a proton pump. The difference in ATP yield is due to the route through the respiratory machinery, not to a different number of electrons: both NADH and QH₂ carry two reducing equivalents.

Malonate is the classic competitive inhibitor of succinate oxidation. It is a dicarboxylate resembling succinate but has one fewer carbon. In a purified enzyme preparation at fixed electron acceptor availability, increasing succinate can reduce the fractional inhibition caused by malonate. For ideal competitive inhibition, apparent Kₘ increases while Vₘₐₓ remains unchanged. In an intact mitochondrion, however, blocking this step changes intermediate concentrations and electron flow, so the total respiratory response is not identical to the simple isolated-enzyme rate equation.

This step is also a direct illustration of how a defect can simultaneously affect metabolic carbon flow and oxidative phosphorylation. If succinate oxidation is interrupted, fumarate production from succinate and Q reduction through Complex II decline. Other entry routes into the Q pool, including NADH oxidation through Complex I, may remain functional initially. Consequently, inhibition of Complex II does not automatically imply zero oxygen consumption in a preparation supplied with other oxidizable substrates.

11. Steps 7 and 8: hydration, oxidation and regeneration

Fumarase, also called fumarate hydratase, adds water across fumarate's trans double bond to generate L-malate: fumarate + H₂O ⇌ L-malate. No electron acceptor is used, and the carbon number remains four. Water supplies a hydroxyl group and a hydrogen atom, preparing the molecule for oxidation at the next step. The enzyme catalyzes stereospecific hydration and produces L-malate rather than an equal mixture of malate enantiomers.

The geometry of fumarate matters. Fumarate is trans-butenedioate, whereas maleate is its cis geometric isomer. The normal substrate fits the enzyme's binding arrangement and permits the required placement of water and protons. The reaction illustrates why molecules with identical elemental formulas need not be biologically interchangeable. A stereospecific enzyme recognizes spatial relationships, not just the presence of functional groups.

Malate dehydrogenase then catalyzes L-malate + NAD⁺ ⇌ oxaloacetate + NADH + H⁺. The secondary hydroxyl group is oxidized to a carbonyl, creating the electrophilic site needed by citrate synthase. The resulting oxaloacetate may immediately accept another acetyl group. A chemical transformation that appears unfavorable under standard conditions is made possible by the concentrations maintained by the surrounding pathway.

For the forward malate dehydrogenase reaction, a commonly tabulated standard transformed free-energy change is about +29.7 kJ mol⁻¹. This is not a statement that the reaction always runs backward. The actual transformed free-energy change depends on the reaction quotient, ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q. Here Q = a(oxaloacetate)a(NADH)/[a(malate)a(NAD⁺)], with proton activity incorporated into the biochemical standard convention at fixed pH. Activities are dimensionless; concentration ratios provide an approximate calculation in dilute solutions.

At 298 K, RT = 2.478 kJ mol⁻¹. For the forward reaction to be favorable, Q must be below exp(−29.7/2.478), approximately 6.3 × 10⁻⁶. Suppose the oxaloacetate-to-malate concentration ratio is 10⁻⁴ and the NADH-to-NAD⁺ ratio is 0.01. Then Q = 10⁻⁶ and ΔG ≈ 29.7 + 2.478 ln(10⁻⁶) = −4.53 kJ mol⁻¹. These are illustrative concentrations, not a claim that every mitochondrion has these ratios. Raising NADH relative to NAD⁺ by tenfold would raise Q to 10⁻⁵ and make ΔG approximately +1.17 kJ mol⁻¹ at unchanged organic acid ratios.

Citrate synthase continually consumes oxaloacetate, and the respiratory chain helps maintain available NAD⁺. These processes make forward malate oxidation possible. The reaction therefore couples the final step to the beginning of the cycle through substrate/product concentrations rather than by a physical requirement for both enzymes to act as one catalytic complex. A student who sees only the positive ΔG°′ might predict no forward reaction; a student who accounts for Q can predict the conditions under which the pathway works.

12. Deriving carbon, electron and nucleotide balances

A net reaction should be obtained by adding the individual equations, not by attempting to remember all the products as an isolated list. Every intermediate produced in one step is consumed in a later step. Citrate, isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, succinate, fumarate, malate and oxaloacetate all cancel when one complete turn is summed. CoA is consumed by α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and released by citrate synthase and succinyl-CoA synthetase. The net release is one CoA for each entering acetyl-CoA.

Water is consumed during citrate formation and fumarate hydration; aconitase removes and adds water without net consumption. NAD⁺ is reduced in steps 3, 4 and 8. Ubiquinone is reduced in step 6, while GDP and inorganic phosphate are incorporated into GTP in step 5. In simplified biochemical proton notation the sum for a GTP-producing turn is:

Net equation

Acetyl-CoA + 3 NAD⁺ + Q + GDP + Pᵢ + 2 H₂O → 2 CO₂ + CoA-SH + 3 NADH + QH₂ + GTP + 3 H⁺

Proton terms depend on the chosen protonation and biochemical convention; carbon, electron and nucleotide balances are invariant.

The three NADH each carry two electrons, and one QH₂ carries two more. Hence the four oxidations capture eight electrons per net acetyl group oxidized. This is consistent with formal carbon oxidation-state accounting. In the acetyl thioester, the methyl carbon has oxidation state −3 and the carbonyl carbon +3; their sum is zero. In two CO₂ molecules each carbon is at +4, giving a total of +8. The change corresponds to loss of eight electrons, captured in the four reduced carriers. This is net accounting and does not contradict the first-turn isotope behavior.

For two acetyl-CoA molecules derived from one glucose, the cycle itself generates four CO₂, six NADH, two QH₂ equivalents and two GTP. The link reaction adds two CO₂ and two NADH. Glycolysis adds its own two NADH and two net ATP and contributes no CO₂ in the conventional pathway. Summing all three stages before respiratory reoxidation gives six CO₂, ten NADH (two initially cytosolic), two QH₂ equivalents and four direct ATP equivalents. The fate of the cytosolic NADH depends on the shuttle system, a topic developed in the respiratory chapter.

In traditional textbook bookkeeping, NADH was assigned three ATP and FADH₂ two ATP, giving twelve ATP equivalents per turn. Contemporary approximate mitochondrial P/O assumptions are 2.5 per NADH and 1.5 per QH₂ supplied through succinate dehydrogenase. The modern illustrative yield is (3 × 2.5) + (1 × 1.5) + 1 = 10 ATP equivalents per acetyl-CoA. Of those ten, only one is generated directly by the cycle. The other nine require respiratory electron transfer and a functioning phosphorylation apparatus. State which convention is used when solving an examination problem.

The values are estimates, not a strict integer stoichiometry of ATP synthase. Proton leak, transport costs, respiratory conditions and other uses of the proton gradient can change the realized yield. A complete glucose yield often quoted as about 30–32 ATP equivalents follows from the assumptions above and two different cytosolic NADH shuttle routes. It should not be interpreted as a universal fixed number for every species or tissue.

13. Tracing individual carbon atoms through successive turns

When a labeled acetyl group is introduced into an unlabeled oxaloacetate pool, the first carbon dioxide molecules arise from the oxaloacetate-derived part of citrate, not from the newly entering acetyl group. This is one of the most important distinctions in the chapter. The net equation is correct because the total number of carbon atoms in the intermediate pool is unchanged after a complete turn. It does not require the identities of those atoms to remain unchanged.

Label the carbonyl carbon of acetyl-CoA A1 and its methyl carbon A2. Label the four oxaloacetate carbons O1–O4, with O1 and O4 representing the terminal carboxyl carbons. Citrate synthase joins A2 to the oxaloacetate carbonyl carbon O2. Stereospecific aconitase action prepares the oxaloacetate-derived arm for oxidation, and the two oxidative decarboxylations remove O-derived carbon atoms. In the resulting succinate, the acetyl-derived carbonyl carbon A1 occupies a terminal carboxyl position and A2 an internal methylene position. Neither is lost in that first turn of the canonical pathway.

Succinate has two chemically equivalent terminal carbons and two equivalent internal carbons. On subsequent enzyme-catalyzed conversion to fumarate, malate and oxaloacetate, its orientation relative to the enzyme can differ. In the ideal freely equilibrating model this produces positional randomization: a label initially at one terminal succinate position can appear in either terminal oxaloacetate position, and an internal label can appear in either internal position. The label does not jump randomly from a terminal carbon to an internal carbon merely because succinate is symmetric. The two symmetry classes must be kept distinct.

In the second turn, a carbon label at a terminal oxaloacetate position will be released as CO₂ in one of the two decarboxylations. Consequently, with a single pulse of [1-¹³C]acetyl-CoA (carbonyl labeled), unlabeled starting oxaloacetate, no diversion and complete canonical turns, the label survives the first turn and is released in the second. With [2-¹³C]acetyl-CoA (methyl labeled), the label is internal after the first turn and survives the second; it can enter a carboxyl position during subsequent regeneration and be lost in later turns. The exact observed labeling distribution in a real cell depends on dilution, intermediate exchange and the timing of sampling.

A separate question arises if both acetyl carbons are labeled. Citrate initially carries two labels among six carbons, and succinate still carries both among four. At the end of the first turn, oxaloacetate therefore contains two labels in the ideal closed system. If the next acetyl group is unlabeled, one of the two labels is released in the second turn and the other remains. A pool measurement may report a smaller labeled fraction if unlabeled anaplerotic substrates enter or if labeled carbon is withdrawn for biosynthesis.

Carbon-labeling experiments therefore distinguish pathway topology from the gross stoichiometry. An appearance of label in glutamate or aspartate can result from transfer between the cycle and amino acid pools, not necessarily from complete carbon oxidation. When interpreting an exam problem, write the labeling position, the initial pool assumptions, the number of turns and whether exchange or dilution is allowed. Without those assumptions, a unique quantitative label distribution may not be inferable.

14. Regulation: coupling cycle flux to energy demand

A living mitochondrion does not try to run the TCA cycle at its maximum possible rate at all times. Instead, it coordinates acetyl-CoA supply, the availability of oxaloacetate, the oxidation of reduced coenzymes and the demand for biosynthetic intermediates. A high flux is useful only if the downstream electron-transfer system can accept its electrons and the cell can use or dissipate the resulting energy.

Three strongly favorable reactions are commonly emphasized as control points: citrate synthase, NAD⁺-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. Their large negative cellular free-energy changes distinguish them from reactions that readily adjust to near-equilibrium metabolite ratios. The fact that these reactions are far from equilibrium makes them useful candidates for regulation; it does not prove that one enzyme is always the unique rate-limiting step.

Citrate synthase responds to substrate availability, particularly the low matrix concentration of oxaloacetate, as well as acetyl-CoA. Product citrate and succinyl-CoA can inhibit citrate synthase under suitable conditions. High NADH and ATP are associated with reduced oxidative demand, but the exact direct sensitivities of citrate synthase vary by species and enzyme form. Do not assume a universal allosteric mechanism merely because a metabolite changes flux in intact cells.

Mammalian NAD⁺-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase responds to the adenine nucleotide state. ADP favors activity, particularly at limiting isocitrate, whereas ATP and NADH tend to inhibit. In an energy-demanding muscle cell, ADP rises as ATP is consumed, and respiratory NADH oxidation helps make NAD⁺ available. The enzyme can then accelerate production of reduced equivalents. This is a feed-forward-like adjustment to demand rather than a response to carbon shortage alone.

α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase is inhibited by its products succinyl-CoA and NADH. Product inhibition links this step to both the following conversion and the availability of an oxidized coenzyme. In mammalian muscle, calcium released during contraction stimulates oxidative metabolism, including IDH3 and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and can stimulate pyruvate dehydrogenase indirectly through activation of a phosphatase. The calcium signal connects ATP-consuming work to ATP-producing pathways before a large fall in ATP concentration is necessary.

The NADH/NAD⁺ ratio affects more than enzyme binding. NAD⁺ is a substrate in three cycle reactions, and increasing NADH increases their reaction quotients. If electron transport slows, the redox state becomes more reduced. The immediate result can be a fall in dehydrogenase flux even when acetyl-CoA remains abundant. Conversely, greater NADH oxidation can favor forward reactions without an increase in enzyme abundance. Regulation is therefore both kinetic and thermodynamic.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase regulates one major input to the cycle. Its kinase phosphorylates and inhibits the complex, and its phosphatase reverses the modification. High acetyl-CoA and NADH favor inhibition through product effects and regulation of the kinase, while pyruvate can inhibit the kinase. If fatty acid oxidation is supplying ample acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents, glucose-derived pyruvate oxidation may decrease. This illustrates how substrate pathways compete and cooperate through shared metabolic signals.

15. Why a cycle that does not consume oxygen depends on respiration

None of the eight conventional cycle reactions uses molecular oxygen as a direct substrate. The cycle nevertheless depends on the availability of oxidized electron acceptors. In most aerobic animal tissues, NADH passes electrons to the respiratory chain and ultimately to oxygen. QH₂ is also reoxidized through downstream respiratory complexes. Without these processes, NAD⁺ and Q become limiting relative to their reduced forms.

When oxygen supply suddenly falls in a normally aerobic tissue, electron transport through the oxygen-reducing terminal complex slows. NADH tends to accumulate and the Q pool becomes more reduced. The oxidative reactions of the cycle cannot maintain their former rates, and carbon flux usually falls. This is an indirect dependency on oxygen. The correct explanation is not that oxygen is an unlisted reactant of citrate synthase or of a decarboxylase.

An isolated mitochondrion provided with oxidizable substrate but little ADP also exhibits limited phosphorylating respiration. A high proton motive force makes further proton translocation more difficult, slowing electron transport and hence NADH oxidation. Adding ADP can increase respiratory flux because ATP synthesis consumes the gradient. The cycle responds by supplying reducing equivalents as their oxidation becomes favorable. The detailed relation between proton motive force and respiratory control belongs to the following chapter, but the cycle's redox dependence is essential here.

Not all oxygen-limited cells use the same response. Mammalian glycolysis can continue by reducing pyruvate to lactate and regenerating cytosolic NAD⁺. This does not directly regenerate mitochondrial NAD⁺ at the rates needed by the oxidative cycle. Some microorganisms use alternative terminal electron acceptors and may sustain respiratory metabolism without molecular oxygen; others operate incomplete or differently directed pathways primarily for synthesis. A statement that every TCA reaction is universally impossible without oxygen is therefore too broad.

In an experimental comparison, oxygen consumption is a useful indicator of respiratory electron flow but not an exact direct measurement of every cycle reaction. Electrons can also come from fatty acid oxidation, pyruvate dehydrogenase and other enzymes. Measuring oxygen uptake together with substrate disappearance, carbon dioxide formation and specific intermediate levels gives a stronger inference about which process has changed.

16. Amphibolic metabolism: the cycle is also a biosynthetic junction

If every molecule entering the cycle were necessarily oxidized completely, a cell would lose an important opportunity to build new material. Growing cells require carbon skeletons for amino acids, nucleotides, lipids and porphyrins. Many of these skeletons are already present among TCA intermediates. The cycle's amphibolic character means that oxidative energy production and biosynthetic precursor production share a common set of molecules.

Citrate can leave the mitochondrion when carbon and energy are available for lipid synthesis. Cytosolic ATP-citrate lyase splits citrate into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate at the expense of ATP. Acetyl-CoA can then contribute carbon to fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis. Exporting citrate does not mean that the mitochondrial acetyl-CoA itself has crossed the membrane. It means that its acetyl carbon was transported in another chemical form. The fate of the accompanying oxaloacetate and its recycling determine the net effect on the matrix intermediate pool.

α-Ketoglutarate accepts an amino group in transamination reactions to form glutamate. Glutamate in turn provides nitrogen and carbon for several amino acids, and its derivatives support nitrogen metabolism. The reaction between α-ketoglutarate and glutamate can connect the cycle to both amino acid synthesis and degradation. When the carbon skeleton moves out of the pool to make biomass, it is a cataplerotic withdrawal. When amino acid degradation supplies α-ketoglutarate to the pool, it is an anaplerotic input if it adds to the available cycle intermediates.

Succinyl-CoA supplies carbon to porphyrin biosynthesis. In animals, glycine and succinyl-CoA are used to form δ-aminolevulinate, an early precursor of heme. A portion of succinyl-CoA diverted into this pathway is no longer available for immediate substrate-level phosphorylation. The cell must balance such withdrawals with sufficient replenishment if energy production is to continue.

Oxaloacetate can be transaminated to aspartate, which contributes to amino acid and nucleotide synthesis. In liver and other gluconeogenic tissues, oxaloacetate-derived carbon can be converted to phosphoenolpyruvate and ultimately glucose. Malate can carry carbon and reducing equivalents between compartments and can also serve as a substrate for malic enzyme, producing pyruvate and, for NADP-dependent forms, NADPH. These branches explain why the concentration of a cycle intermediate is not simply determined by the preceding and succeeding cycle enzymes.

A decrease in ATP production during rapid biosynthesis need not imply that the cycle has stopped. The carbon flow may have been redistributed toward precursor export while replenishing reactions preserve part of the oxidative flux. Equally, an accumulation of citrate may reflect impaired use rather than accelerated synthesis. One should distinguish the size of a metabolite pool from the rate at which molecules enter and leave it.

17. Anaplerosis: keeping the cycle supplied with acceptors

The net reaction cancels oxaloacetate and all other intermediates. This suggests that a small initial pool could support many turns, but the statement assumes that nothing leaves that pool. In a real cell, intermediates are continually diverted for biosynthesis. If oxaloacetate or its precursors are not replaced, the available acceptor pool shrinks and citrate formation can become limited even when acetyl-CoA is plentiful.

Anaplerotic reactions replenish the cycle with intermediates. The most important classical mammalian example is mitochondrial pyruvate carboxylase: pyruvate + HCO₃⁻ + ATP → oxaloacetate + ADP + Pᵢ, written with the usual simplified biochemical proton convention. This adds a carbon from bicarbonate to a three-carbon substrate, using ATP. Unlike pyruvate dehydrogenase, which removes a carbon and produces a two-carbon acetyl group, pyruvate carboxylase increases the size of the carbon skeleton to four.

Pyruvate carboxylase requires biotin. ATP-dependent activation of bicarbonate enables carboxylation of enzyme-bound biotin; the carboxyl group is transferred to pyruvate to produce oxaloacetate. Biotin acts as a mobile carbon dioxide carrier, not as the ultimate carbon source. Acetyl-CoA is an important allosteric activator of mammalian pyruvate carboxylase. When acetyl-CoA accumulates, its presence signals a need for oxaloacetate either to support oxidation or, in gluconeogenic contexts, to supply carbon for glucose production. This is a striking example of regulation based on the relationship between two substrates that participate in different reactions.

Additional replenishing routes are context-dependent. Transamination of aspartate can supply oxaloacetate, and glutamate can supply α-ketoglutarate through transamination or oxidative deamination. In the latter case glutamate dehydrogenase can produce α-ketoglutarate, ammonium and reduced coenzyme. These reactions are reversible or depend on surrounding nitrogen metabolism; they are not automatically net anaplerotic merely because they can produce an intermediate in one direction. Net addition must exceed withdrawal through the reverse or other pathways.

Propionyl-CoA formed from odd-chain fatty acids and certain amino acids can become succinyl-CoA. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase uses biotin and ATP to form D-methylmalonyl-CoA, an epimerase generates the L form, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase uses adenosylcobalamin (vitamin B₁₂) to form succinyl-CoA. The resulting four-carbon intermediate can replenish the pool. This distinguishes odd-chain fatty acid carbon from the acetyl-CoA produced by even-chain fatty acids. The full degradation pathways are covered in the next subunit.

In plants and many microorganisms, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase provides another important anaplerotic route: PEP + HCO₃⁻ → oxaloacetate + Pᵢ. It uses the energy associated with PEP rather than directly consuming ATP. It should not be confused with PEP carboxykinase, which participates in gluconeogenesis by converting oxaloacetate toward PEP with nucleotide triphosphate expenditure and carbon dioxide release in the usual mammalian direction.

Consider a simplified steady-state pool containing 10 micromoles of cycle intermediates. If 3 micromoles of intermediates per minute leave for synthesis and 1 micromole per minute enters through net anaplerosis, the pool decreases at 2 micromoles per minute. Suppose that all rates remain constant for two minutes: the pool falls to 6 micromoles. A high citrate synthase rate does not offset this loss because a complete turn regenerates an intermediate rather than producing an additional net intermediate. To maintain the original pool under these assumptions, total replenishment must rise to 3 micromoles per minute. In real cells fluxes adjust as concentrations change, so linear extrapolation to complete depletion would not be justified.

18. The glyoxylate cycle and the problem of net carbohydrate synthesis

The ordinary oxidative TCA cycle cannot provide a net gain of four-carbon intermediates from acetyl-CoA alone. Adding two carbons to oxaloacetate and losing two as CO₂ returns the same number of intermediate molecules to the pool. Although some of the entering carbon becomes part of the regenerated oxaloacetate, no additional oxaloacetate has been created in net terms. This is why isotope incorporation into glucose is not proof that acetyl-CoA can support net glucose synthesis.

Mammals lack a functional conventional glyoxylate cycle, and pyruvate dehydrogenase is effectively irreversible under physiological conditions. Acetyl-CoA from even-chain fatty acids therefore cannot be converted into glucose in net amounts through the conventional mammalian pathways. The glycerol portion of triacylglycerol can contribute to glucose synthesis, and propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids can contribute through succinyl-CoA. These exceptions refer to different carbon inputs, not to a reversal of pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Many plants, fungi and bacteria possess an alternative pathway that conserves acetyl carbon. The glyoxylate cycle shares citrate synthase and aconitase with the TCA cycle, but isocitrate is cleaved by isocitrate lyase into succinate (4C) and glyoxylate (2C). This avoids the first oxidative decarboxylation. Malate synthase then combines glyoxylate with a second acetyl-CoA and water to produce malate and CoA. Malate dehydrogenase converts malate to oxaloacetate, regenerating the initial acceptor and producing NADH.

The two unique enzymes bypass both carbon-losing reactions of the ordinary cycle. The net carbon result is two acetyl groups (four carbons total) converted into one four-carbon succinate equivalent, while oxaloacetate is regenerated. In simplified biochemical notation the net reaction is 2 acetyl-CoA + 2 H₂O + NAD⁺ → succinate + 2 CoA-SH + NADH + H⁺; proton notation may differ with ionic forms. Unlike a complete oxidative TCA turn, this route does not directly generate GTP or QH₂ in its core sequence. It sacrifices some immediate reducing-equivalent yield in order to conserve carbon for biosynthesis.

During germination of oil-rich seeds, stored triacylglycerols can supply acetyl-CoA through fatty acid oxidation. Before photosynthesis is established, the seed requires carbohydrate for growing tissues. The glyoxylate pathway, associated with specialized peroxisomes called glyoxysomes in germinating oilseeds, provides net four-carbon products that can feed gluconeogenesis after appropriate intercompartmental transport. It is not a direct conversion of fat into glucose in one enzyme reaction. The pathway creates net gluconeogenic precursors; the rest of gluconeogenesis performs the carbohydrate synthesis.

The two cycles can coexist. Partition of isocitrate between dehydrogenase and isocitrate lyase helps determine whether carbon is oxidized or conserved. An organism growing on acetate as its major carbon source needs a way of conserving carbon for cell material as well as a way of producing energy. Blocking isocitrate lyase in a bacterium that relies on the glyoxylate pathway can therefore impair growth on acetate while leaving growth on some other carbon sources possible. The conclusion is conditional on the organism's metabolic alternatives, since not all acetate-assimilating microorganisms use this exact pathway.

19. Reading metabolic experiments and pathway blocks

A typical examination question gives a change in an intermediate, enzyme activity or oxygen consumption and asks which pathway is affected. Start with the reaction immediately upstream of the measured compound and the reaction immediately downstream. A buildup often indicates that consumption has fallen relative to production, but it can also occur if production rises. One measurement cannot distinguish the two explanations. The time scale, substrate supply and other measurements determine the strength of the inference.

Suppose an aerobic mitochondrial preparation receives succinate as its main respiratory substrate. Its oxygen consumption is 80 arbitrary units per minute. Adding malonate reduces it to 25, and adding a much higher succinate concentration increases it to 65. The partial rescue is consistent with competitive interference at succinate dehydrogenase, because a higher substrate concentration competes with the inhibitor. If the same inhibitor had blocked the terminal oxygen-reducing complex completely, increasing succinate would not restore electron transfer to oxygen. These values are an original illustrative dataset, not published experimental observations.

Now suppose a preparation supplied with pyruvate and malate has a falling NAD⁺/NADH ratio, lower CO₂ output and higher NADH fluorescence after an intervention. These observations are consistent with impaired NADH reoxidation, which feeds back on TCA dehydrogenases and pyruvate dehydrogenase. They do not by themselves identify the exact respiratory complex inhibited. If direct succinate-supported respiration remains high, an NADH-specific entry defect becomes more plausible; if it also falls, a downstream respiratory limitation may be involved. Such comparisons are more informative than a single fluorescence measurement.

Another experiment withdraws glutamate for biosynthesis from cells grown on glucose. If the rate of new α-ketoglutarate formation from glucose-derived carbon is sufficient but the total intermediate pool declines, replenishment may be inadequate. Supplying a precursor that generates oxaloacetate or α-ketoglutarate can restore the pool, provided the necessary transporters and enzymes operate. The increase in respiration after supplementation can support the replenishment hypothesis, but it does not prove that the added substrate entered exclusively through one pathway.

A useful way to reason about pool size is to define P as the amount of cycle intermediates, measured in micromoles of molecules, and Jin and Jout as the rates of net intermediate addition and withdrawal. For an ideal pooled balance, dP/dt = Jin − Jout. Complete cycles do not appear in this equation because the number of pool molecules regenerated is the same as the number consumed. A steady pool requires Jin = Jout; it does not require either flux to be zero. This equation is a bookkeeping approximation that ignores transient sequestration and treats all intermediates as belonging to a single accessible pool. It explains why a stable concentration can coexist with vigorous turnover.

Always check the basis of a yield calculation. For example, if a preparation fully oxidizes 5 micromoles of acetyl-CoA through the cycle in one minute, the ideal rates are 15 micromoles NADH, 5 micromoles QH₂ equivalents, 5 micromoles GTP and 10 micromoles CO₂ per minute. If all the carriers are subsequently reoxidized by oxygen, eight electrons per turn imply two O₂ per turn because O₂ accepts four electrons in its complete reduction to water. Thus the theoretical oxygen consumption attributable to these cycle-derived electrons is 10 micromoles per minute. Additional oxygen consumption from upstream reactions must not be included unless the problem supplies those fluxes. With 2.5 and 1.5 P/O assumptions, the theoretical total ATP-equivalent production is 50 micromoles per minute, of which only 5 are direct.

20. Part B — conceptual and assertion–reason practice

These original questions test the reaction sequence, chemical logic and regulation. Attempt each question before opening its explanation.

1. Which reaction is not one of the eight cycle reactions?

A. Aconitase; B. Pyruvate dehydrogenase; C. Fumarase; D. Citrate synthase.

Answer and reasoning

B. Pyruvate dehydrogenase supplies acetyl-CoA from pyruvate before the turn. The other three are cycle enzymes.

2. Which enzyme makes a nucleotide triphosphate directly?

A. Citrate synthase; B. Succinyl-CoA synthetase; C. Succinate dehydrogenase; D. Malate dehydrogenase.

Answer and reasoning

B. Thioester energy drives phosphate transfer to GDP or ADP. A hydrolyzes a thioester to drive condensation, while C and D transfer electrons.

3. Why is citrate rearranged before the first oxidation?

A. It has only five carbons; B. It is already a ketone; C. It contains a tertiary hydroxyl group; D. Its oxidation requires oxygen directly.

Answer and reasoning

C. Isomerization produces a secondary alcohol suitable for NAD⁺-linked oxidation. A and B misidentify its structure; D confuses the cycle with respiration.

4. Which coenzyme is the ultimate electron acceptor of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase?

A. TPP; B. CoA; C. FAD; D. NAD⁺.

Answer and reasoning

D. FAD is recycled internally; TPP helps decarboxylation and CoA receives the succinyl group.

5. Which statement about Complex II is correct?

A. It releases soluble FADH₂; B. It pumps protons directly; C. It reduces Q using succinate-derived electrons; D. It uses NADH as the immediate substrate.

Answer and reasoning

C. Its bound FAD and iron–sulfur centers transfer electrons to Q. A, B and D incorrectly describe the carrier or mechanism.

6. Which reaction supplies net oxaloacetate?

A. Citrate synthase; B. Pyruvate carboxylase; C. A complete TCA turn alone; D. Pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Answer and reasoning

B. Carboxylation adds bicarbonate carbon to pyruvate. A consumes oxaloacetate, C regenerates it without net gain and D produces acetyl-CoA.

7. Assertion: The TCA cycle can slow in hypoxia. Reason: Reoxidation of NADH can become limited when oxygen-dependent respiration slows.

Choose A: both true and reason explains; B: both true but reason does not explain; C: assertion true only; D: reason true only.

Answer and reasoning

A. Reduced electron acceptor regeneration and product accumulation suppress oxidative reactions, although oxygen is not directly used by the eight reactions.

8. Assertion: Succinate symmetry permits positional label randomization. Reason: Every succinate carbon is chemically equivalent.

Choose A: both true and reason explains; B: both true but reason does not explain; C: assertion true only; D: reason true only.

Answer and reasoning

C. The terminal pair and internal pair are separately equivalent, but terminal and internal carbons are not equivalent.

9. Which metabolite directly supplies a carbon skeleton for heme synthesis in animals?

A. Citrate; B. Fumarate; C. Succinyl-CoA; D. Isocitrate.

Answer and reasoning

C. Succinyl-CoA condenses with glycine to form aminolevulinate. Citrate supports lipid synthesis; fumarate and isocitrate are not the direct substrates of that step.

10. Which pair distinguishes the glyoxylate cycle?

A. Citrate synthase and aconitase; B. Isocitrate lyase and malate synthase; C. Succinate dehydrogenase and fumarase; D. IDH3 and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.

Answer and reasoning

B. A is shared; C participates in the oxidative cycle; D removes the carbons the bypass conserves.

21. Part C — analytical and quantitative practice

State the assumptions, distinguish direct nucleotide synthesis from respiratory ATP equivalents, and trace the carbon or electron balance before checking the answer.

1. A preparation oxidizes 3 mmol acetyl-CoA in complete turns. Calculate direct GTP, CO₂, NADH, QH₂ and ATP equivalents (P/O 2.5 and 1.5).

Worked solution

Multiply each per-turn yield by three: 3 mmol GTP, 6 mmol CO₂, 9 mmol NADH, 3 mmol QH₂. ATP equivalents = 3 + 9 × 2.5 + 3 × 1.5 = 30 mmol, provided the carriers are fully reoxidized with these assumed coupling efficiencies.

2. A student reports 12 ATP per turn. Does this necessarily imply a different number of chemical reactions?

Worked solution

No. Older accounting uses 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per flavin-linked electron pair. This gives 9 + 2 + 1 = 12. Modern approximate accounting gives 7.5 + 1.5 + 1 = 10. Direct GTP and electron/carbon yields are unchanged.

3. At 298 K, ΔG°′ for malate oxidation is +29.7 kJ/mol. Let OAA/malate = 2 × 10⁻⁵ and NADH/NAD⁺ = 0.10. Determine ΔG.

Worked solution

Q = 2 × 10⁻⁶; RT = 2.478 kJ/mol. ln Q = −13.1224. ΔG = 29.7 − 32.52 ≈ −2.82 kJ/mol. Forward flux is thermodynamically favored despite the positive standard value. A negative ΔG does not specify the enzyme rate.

4. The above NADH/NAD⁺ ratio increases fivefold while the organic acid ratio stays fixed. Predict the direction.

Worked solution

Q becomes 10⁻⁵. ΔG ≈ 29.7 − 28.53 = +1.17 kJ/mol, so the forward reaction is unfavorable at those fixed ratios. The cell could shift the reaction by consuming oxaloacetate or oxidizing NADH.

5. A pulse of carbonyl-labeled acetyl-CoA enters a cycle containing unlabeled oxaloacetate. When does the label first appear in CO₂ under ideal closed-turn assumptions?

Worked solution

The second turn. The acetyl carbonyl carbon survives turn one as a terminal succinate carbon, hence a terminal oxaloacetate carbon. The two terminal OAA carbons are removed in turn two. First-turn CO₂ comes from the original OAA.

6. Repeat the experiment with methyl-labeled acetyl-CoA. Would all the label leave in turn two?

Worked solution

No. The methyl label remains an internal carbon after the first turn, and the two second-turn decarboxylations remove terminal OAA carbons. Later turns can distribute and release that label; do not apply a universal fraction if exchange is permitted.

7. A pulse contains both labeled acetyl carbons. Assuming no dilution and unlabeled acetyl input in turn two, how many labels remain in OAA after turns one and two?

Worked solution

Two after turn one; one after turn two. The carbonyl-derived label is terminal and lost during turn two. The methyl-derived label survives. Actual cellular pool averages depend on unlabeled inputs and withdrawals.

8. Malonate-treated mitochondria supplied only with succinate respire at 20% of the control rate. Increasing succinate partially restores respiration. Which mechanism is best supported, and what control would strengthen it?

Worked solution

Competitive inhibition at succinate dehydrogenase is supported. Measure isolated enzyme rates over a succinate concentration series at fixed Q availability; ideal competitive inhibition increases apparent Km without lowering Vmax. A respiratory assay alone cannot establish precise kinetic constants.

9. Cells supplied with glucose display citrate accumulation and decreased isocitrate formation, with preserved citrate synthase activity. Which step is implicated?

Worked solution

Aconitase is a plausible block because citrate production is preserved while its conversion is impaired. Test aconitase directly and measure transport and downstream flux; accumulation alone does not identify a specific molecular toxin.

10. A cell withdraws 4 μmol/min of intermediates for synthesis. Pyruvate carboxylase supplies 2.5 and glutamate metabolism adds 0.5 μmol/min. How does the pool change after 3 min if rates stay constant?

Worked solution

Net change = 2.5 + 0.5 − 4 = −1 μmol/min, giving −3 μmol over three minutes. Increasing complete cycle turnover does not replace net withdrawn intermediate molecules. At least 1 μmol/min extra net replenishment is needed.

11. Two otherwise identical cell lines receive an even-chain fatty acid as their sole carbon source. Line X expresses functional isocitrate lyase and malate synthase; line Y lacks them. Which has the additional canonical route to net four-carbon products from acetyl-CoA?

Worked solution

X. The glyoxylate bypass combines two acetyl groups into a net four-carbon succinate product. Y can oxidize acetyl-CoA but cannot obtain net OAA from it through the ordinary TCA cycle alone. Other metabolic alternatives are excluded by the question.

12. A cofactor deficiency depresses pyruvate oxidation and α-ketoglutarate oxidation but not purified isocitrate dehydrogenase. TPP addition rescues both affected activities in an extract. Explain.

Worked solution

Both multienzyme dehydrogenase complexes require TPP for α-ketoacid decarboxylation; IDH does not. Rescue by TPP supports a shared cofactor limitation rather than inhibition of every NAD⁺-dependent enzyme. Additional intact-cell evidence would be needed to identify the cause of deficiency.

13. A preparation reoxidizes electrons from 7 complete turns using oxygen as the only terminal acceptor. Calculate O₂ consumption attributable to cycle-derived electrons.

Worked solution

Each turn captures 8 electrons; total 56 electrons. Each O₂ accepts 4 electrons, giving 14 molecules (or 14 mol if the input is 7 mol) of O₂. This omits pyruvate dehydrogenase and any other respiratory substrates.

14. A metabolic treatment increases NADH fluorescence while decreasing CO₂ production. Does that prove increased NADH synthesis?

Worked solution

No. A pool can accumulate because its consumption decreases. Impaired respiratory NADH oxidation can raise NADH despite lower production by TCA dehydrogenases. Measure NADH turnover or electron transport and substrate flux to distinguish synthesis from consumption.

15. During exercise, matrix calcium and ADP increase. Explain why their effect on respiration can be greater than a change in substrate alone.

Worked solution

ADP provides demand for ATP synthesis and activates IDH3; calcium stimulates several mitochondrial dehydrogenases in suitable mammalian tissues. Electron transport reoxidizes NADH, so availability of NAD⁺ and enzyme activity rise together. The result is coordinated supply of electrons and ATP demand, not necessarily an increase in enzyme abundance.

22. Consolidation and the next step in energy conversion

A complete oxidative turn begins with a two-carbon acetyl group joining oxaloacetate and ends with regeneration of oxaloacetate. The chemical strategy can be reconstructed from the functional groups: form citrate, rearrange its hydroxyl group, perform two oxidative decarboxylations, conserve thioester energy as a nucleotide triphosphate, introduce a double bond, hydrate it and oxidize the alcohol to the original carbonyl acceptor.

The net turn captures three NADH and one QH₂ equivalent, forms one GTP or ATP and releases two CO₂. Only one nucleotide triphosphate is made directly. The carbon atoms released in the first turn belong to the oxaloacetate-derived portion of citrate; acetyl-derived carbons remain in the regenerated pool. Succinate symmetry redistributes positional information within terminal and internal carbon classes, but does not make every carbon equivalent.

The cycle relies on oxidized carriers, suitable substrates and an intact pool of intermediates. Its regulatory behavior reflects the energetic and redox state of the cell; its biosynthetic role requires anaplerosis to balance withdrawal. The glyoxylate cycle demonstrates that avoiding two decarboxylations changes the fundamental carbon economy, allowing two acetyl groups to yield a net four-carbon product.

The remaining question is how the energy stored in NADH and QH₂ becomes ATP. Electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation explain how respiratory complexes use electron transfer to establish a proton motive force, and how ATP synthase converts that force into nucleotide synthesis. Understanding this next mechanism is necessary to justify the approximate ATP-equivalent values used in the present chapter.
