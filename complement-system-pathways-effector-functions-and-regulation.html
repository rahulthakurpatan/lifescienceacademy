1. A soluble defense that turns recognition into action

A bacterium that enters a wound encounters far more than the cells visible in a microscope. It also enters a fluid environment containing proteins that can recognize its surface, alter that surface and attract cells capable of destroying it. The complement system is one of the most important of these extracellular defense mechanisms. It consists of soluble and membrane-associated proteins, including recognition molecules, proteases, substrates, receptors and regulators. These proteins cooperate in a controlled biochemical network. Several components circulate in a relatively inactive form, allowing the body to respond rapidly without first synthesizing an entire new set of defensive molecules at the site of infection.

Consider a small population of bacteria newly introduced into connective tissue. Resident macrophages may detect bacterial molecules through pattern-recognition receptors, but not every bacterium will be in immediate contact with a macrophage. Complement proteins in extracellular fluid can reach microbial surfaces without the direct contact required by a cellular receptor. Activation changes the chemistry of the target surface. A previously difficult-to-ingest bacterium can acquire many molecules of a protein fragment that bind receptors on phagocytes. This transformation does not require the bacterium to die first. Indeed, an important property of complement is that it can make viable microorganisms easier for other immune mechanisms to destroy.

A second problem is one of coordination. Local macrophages and the small number of neutrophils already present in a tissue cannot necessarily eliminate a growing infection. Additional cells must leave the blood and move toward the source of danger. Complement activation produces small soluble peptides that communicate with cells in the surrounding tissue. Some alter local inflammatory responses; others are especially effective in promoting directed migration and activation of leukocytes. A third problem is physical destruction. Certain susceptible microbial membranes can be damaged by a protein assembly that penetrates the membrane and compromises its barrier properties. These three solutions are related but not interchangeable. Labeling for ingestion, attracting an effector cell and producing a membrane lesion are distinct biological outcomes of the same network.

The adjective humoral refers here to an activity mediated through substances in body fluids rather than exclusively through direct cell-to-cell contact. Both antibodies and complement are humoral immune factors, but that shared location does not make them identical. Antibodies are products of B-cell lineages whose specificity depends on rearranged immunoglobulin genes and subsequent selection. Complement proteins are generally encoded by inherited genes without the antigen-receptor rearrangement that generates individual B-cell specificities. Complement can therefore function during a first encounter with a microbe, even before newly generated antibodies appear. It can also work with antibodies produced by the adaptive immune system. The latter relationship explains why complement belongs primarily to innate immunity while making major contributions to adaptive effector responses.

Most circulating complement proteins are produced principally by the liver. Other cells, including immune cells and tissue-resident cells, can produce selected components locally. This distinction helps explain why the concentration measured in blood is not necessarily a complete description of complement activity in a particular tissue. The system is distributed across different environments: plasma, extravascular fluid, cell surfaces and sites where inflammation increases permeability. Nevertheless, for the purposes of understanding the canonical pathways, circulating proteins and reactions on extracellular surfaces provide the clearest starting point. Intracellular complement biology is not required to understand the standard NET and SET pathway models.

The system solves a fundamental problem of defense: how can a response be both rapid and local? A circulating molecule may be present throughout the body, while its active products must remain preferentially associated with the target or the site of activation. Many complement reactions achieve localization by generating short-lived reactive groups that form bonds with neighboring surfaces, or by assembling transient enzyme complexes on deposited fragments. Soluble products can act over a wider region, but they too are subject to degradation and regulation. The combination allows defense to spread enough to recruit help without automatically converting the whole blood compartment into an inflammatory reaction.

A useful comparison with cellular signal transduction is the idea of a cascade. In a kinase cascade an activated enzyme modifies multiple downstream substrates. In complement, proteolytic enzymes cut peptide bonds in downstream components, often producing fragments with new activities. Both networks amplify a small initiating event, and both need mechanisms that limit duration and prevent inappropriate activation. Their chemistry is different: the canonical complement cascade is extracellular and is not powered by the transfer of phosphate groups from ATP at every activation step. Its key switching events are controlled proteolysis, conformational change, noncovalent assembly and, for selected fragments, covalent attachment to nearby targets. This is why an extracellular defense can operate without the direct energy metabolism of the target cell.

It is also important to distinguish complement activation from the mere presence of complement. An immunoassay might report a normal concentration of a precursor while a functional assay shows impaired activity. Conversely, a low concentration can reflect rapid use rather than an inherited inability to produce the protein. A component can be present yet structurally defective, or it can be normal but unable to participate because an interacting component is missing. The relevant biological variable is the ability of the network to produce an appropriate local response. Concentration, cleavage, deposition, receptor binding and lysis are separate experimental readouts of that ability.

The main teaching question of this chapter is therefore not simply which component follows which. It is how three different initiating mechanisms generate a shared biochemical response, how the products of that response perform distinct functions, and how normal host tissues prevent the same chemistry from injuring them. Following the cascade with those questions in mind turns its unfamiliar names into a logical sequence. The student should be able to predict the effects of removing a component, restoring a purified protein, changing a target surface or inhibiting one stage without assuming that every output rises or falls together.

2. Experimental origins: separating recognition from destruction

The complement system was discovered because the ability of serum to destroy microorganisms could not be explained by a single stable substance. In the late nineteenth century investigators found that the bactericidal property of serum was sensitive to heating. Hans Buchner used the name alexin for a heat-sensitive protective activity. Jules Bordet subsequently demonstrated that immune bacteriolysis involved a heat-sensitive serum factor and a relatively heat-resistant sensitizing factor. The sensitizing factor is now understood in terms of antibody. The older term alexin refers to the activity later understood as complement. Paul Ehrlich helped establish the terminology of complement, emphasizing how this activity completes the effect of specific immune recognition.

The distinction was conceptually powerful because a mixture can have a biological property that neither fraction displays under the same conditions when tested alone. If a serum sample contains antibodies that recognize a microorganism and functional complement that damages the organism, heating under suitable conditions may inactivate much of its complement activity while retaining sufficient antibody-binding activity. Adding an appropriate fresh source of complement can restore the ability to lyse a susceptible target. The interpretation is not that heat creates a new antibody or that heating reversibly removes its antigen-binding site. Rather, the experiment separates relatively heat-resistant recognition from a heat-labile effector function.

A simplified reconstruction illustrates the causal logic. Imagine four tubes containing identical susceptible antibody-recognized targets. Tube one receives untreated immune serum; tube two receives the same immune serum after complement inactivation; tube three receives a fresh complement source that has been checked for lack of specific sensitizing antibody; tube four receives the combination of heat-treated immune serum and fresh complement. Under conditions where antibody-dependent classical activation is necessary for the chosen endpoint, strong lysis in tubes one and four with weak lysis in the other tubes supports a requirement for both activities. This reconstruction is an illustrative experimental design, not a report of the original nineteenth-century numerical results. It also shows why the target and complement source must be selected carefully: some targets activate complement even in the absence of the particular antibody.

The experiment separates requirements but does not identify the molecular pathway. To do that, researchers must distinguish a loss of function caused by one missing protein from loss of multiple heat-sensitive proteins. Fractionation of serum, functional reconstitution and identification of individual components progressively revealed the network. If a particular fraction cannot support lysis on its own but restores activity to an otherwise complete preparation lacking that fraction, it supplies a necessary activity under the conditions used. Such reasoning motivated the identification of multiple complement components. Modern immunological methods permit the same logic to be applied to purified proteins, genetically deficient serum and specific blocking antibodies.

Historical complement terminology reflects the order of discovery and the changing understanding of the cascade, not a universal numerical order of activation. In the classical pathway, activation of C1 leads to cleavage of C4 and C2 before the principal cleavage of C3. A person who memorizes C1, C2, C3 and C4 as successive integers will therefore draw the wrong pathway. This is a common problem in biological nomenclature: a name may be historical, whereas the function is defined by experimental evidence. The pathway must be learned from the substrate-enzyme relationships rather than inferred from the number on each protein.

Recognition that complement could operate without an experimentally added specific antibody was another major change. The alternative pathway was established as a mechanism independent of the classical C1-dependent initiation route. Properdin, investigated in mid-twentieth-century complement research, became an important molecule in understanding this system. Modern teaching emphasizes its stabilizing effect on the alternative pathway convertase and recognizes that, in some contexts, it can also participate in recruitment of pathway components. Discovery of a lectin-dependent route later made it clear that a soluble recognition molecule can recognize repeated microbial carbohydrates and recruit associated proteases, converging on the same early convertase used by the classical pathway.

The classical, lectin and alternative names consequently identify routes of activation, not three unrelated sets of antimicrobial proteins. The classical pathway provided the historical demonstration of antibody-dependent killing. The lectin pathway illustrates recognition of certain molecular patterns without requiring antigen-specific antibody. The alternative pathway illustrates the capacity for basal C3 activation and amplification on permissive surfaces. A mature understanding recognizes that complement response in biological serum can involve more than one route. Classical initiation, for instance, can deposit the C3b that drives additional alternative pathway amplification. The identification of one initiating route does not show that the other routes make no contribution.

Early hemolysis experiments remain useful because erythrocytes provide a measurable membrane-damage endpoint. Intact red cells retain hemoglobin; when susceptible cells lyse, hemoglobin appears in the surrounding medium. The resulting absorbance can quantify lysis. One can change the way targets are sensitized or the ionic conditions to emphasize particular complement routes. However, lysis is an endpoint of the terminal pathway, not a universal measure of every effect of complement. A preparation may lack the ability to lyse erythrocytes but still deposit C3 fragments and recruit phagocytes. This distinction between the mechanism and the assay endpoint is crucial when interpreting modern experiments built on the same historical tradition.

3. Components, fragments and the chemistry of amplification

The name C3 identifies a large precursor protein, whereas C3a and C3b identify products of its controlled cleavage. They are not simply two arbitrary portions that happen to be smaller than the precursor. The cleavage exposes structural features and separates activities. C3a functions as a soluble signaling peptide. C3b undergoes a structural rearrangement that makes a previously shielded internal thioester available for reaction with neighboring chemical groups. A fraction of the generated C3b can consequently become covalently associated with a surface near the site of cleavage. C3b that fails to react with a suitable surface can be inactivated through reaction with water and regulatory processes. This chemical behavior gives the system both reach and localization.

The thioester is an example of a reactive functional group protected inside a folded protein until the correct molecular event occurs. In the precursor, the arrangement of protein domains helps limit uncontrolled reaction. Cleavage changes the structure and allows the internal bond to be attacked by nucleophiles. Hydroxyl and amino groups on nearby molecules can participate in covalent attachment, producing ester or amide bonds depending on the reaction. The resulting association is different from the reversible recognition of an antigen by an antibody. Surface retention of C3b permits several subsequent interactions at the same location even when the surrounding liquid is moving. C4b uses a related thioester-dependent attachment strategy, although its functional role in the initiating convertase differs from that of C3b.

The small fragments released during activation do not remain tethered in the same way. A small diffusible signal can influence receptor-bearing cells away from the precise cleavage site. This difference in physical fate is a repeated design principle. The larger fragment may become a scaffold or tag at the surface, while a smaller fragment may deliver information about activation into the surrounding fluid. One must not infer identical activities for all small fragments simply because they are named with the letter a. The best-established anaphylatoxins for standard immunology are C3a and C5a, with C5a especially potent in many inflammatory settings. C4a is often listed historically as an anaphylatoxin but its physiological activity and receptor assignments are far less established.

Several complement components are zymogens, inactive precursors of proteases. A zymogen acquires an active catalytic conformation through cleavage or related activation events. In the classical pathway C1r and C1s are examples of serine proteases recruited into the C1 complex. Factor B in the alternative pathway becomes the catalytically active Bb fragment following cleavage when appropriately associated with C3b or hydrolyzed C3. Other proteins are not proteases at all. C1q is a recognition molecule; C3b is a major binding and scaffolding fragment; properdin stabilizes particular complexes; and many terminal components cooperate in nonenzymatic membrane assembly. Calling every C-numbered protein an enzyme is therefore incorrect.

A convertase is an enzyme complex that converts a named complement precursor into its active products. The C3 convertase cleaves C3, and the C5 convertase efficiently cleaves C5. The names describe substrate specificity, not the identity of every protein within the complex. The classical and lectin pathways use C4b2a as their canonical C3 convertase in widely used textbook notation. The alternative pathway uses C3bBb on a surface. Both contain a surface-associated component and a catalytic fragment. C4b and C3b position the complex; C2a and Bb provide protease activity. C3bBb does not mean that the large fragment C3b is itself the enzyme responsible for C3 cleavage.

There is a historical complication in C2 terminology. Unlike most familiar complement proteins, older and still widely used textbook conventions call its larger catalytic fragment C2a and its smaller released fragment C2b. Some revised nomenclatures reverse these labels for consistency. An examination problem using C4b2a generally follows the conventional textbook scheme; a source using C4b2b may be following the alternative naming convention rather than describing a different biochemical mechanism. In this chapter C4b2a denotes the classical and lectin C3 convertase. Functional interpretation depends on identifying the catalytic fragment, not on imposing a suffix rule on every component.

The biochemical amplification is possible because an active protease complex can cleave multiple molecules of substrate before it dissociates or is regulated. Each successful C3 cleavage yields a C3b molecule that may itself become a platform for factor B recruitment. This is a positive feedback loop: the product helps assemble an enzyme capable of making more of the same product. The loop does not mean that every C3b necessarily produces another convertase. Attachment efficiency, local availability of factor B and factor D, convertase lifetime, regulatory proteins, and competing reactions all determine whether the loop grows or dies out. A productive reaction on a microbial surface is thus a probability-dependent process rather than a mechanical guarantee of unlimited amplification.

Proteolysis differs from a reversible ligand-receptor binding event. Once the peptide chain of an individual precursor has been cut, the original chain is not restored by simple dissociation of the enzyme. The system must maintain stores of precursors and remove or process their fragments. Restoration of the circulating precursor supply depends on production and turnover at the organismal level. Termination at a particular surface relies instead on transient enzyme assembly, inhibition of activating proteases, regulatory cleavage of deposited fragments, and limitation of membrane pore assembly. This distinction explains why a burst of excessive activation can consume complement proteins even though the initiating stimulus eventually disappears.

Calcium and magnesium ions contribute to specific molecular interactions in complement complexes. Their roles are not to serve as general energy sources, but to help maintain the binding geometry and assembly of relevant protein complexes. Calcium supports aspects of classical and lectin initiation, whereas magnesium supports alternative pathway convertase assembly. The dependence provides a useful experimental lever. Chelators that sequester divalent ions can suppress activation; conditions that bind calcium while supplying magnesium can favor the measurement of alternative activity over calcium-dependent initiation. The ionic environment must be treated as part of the assay design, rather than assumed irrelevant because the main actors are proteins.

The general design can now be read as a sequence of transformations: an initiating recognition mechanism enables a protease to create a surface-associated scaffold; another catalytic fragment assembles with that scaffold; C3 is cleaved repeatedly; deposited C3b supports tagging and further amplification; sufficiently favorable conditions promote C5 cleavage; and the terminal components form membrane lesions. Soluble fragments communicate with cells at several stages. Every arrow in such a diagram represents a particular type of event, such as cleavage, binding, conformational exposure or diffusion. Keeping these event types distinct is the foundation for understanding the three routes rather than memorizing them as disconnected lists.

4. Classical pathway: converting bound recognition into protease activity

The classical pathway begins with the recognition complex C1. It contains one C1q recognition molecule associated with two molecules each of C1r and C1s. C1q is organized with multiple globular recognition heads at the ends of collagen-like arms. This multivalent geometry is suited to detecting clustered recognition ligands. A single free immunoglobulin molecule floating in plasma is generally not equivalent to a group of appropriately positioned Fc regions on a target. The structural arrangement of C1q allows a spatial pattern of binding sites to influence whether the attached protease system becomes activated.

When suitable antibodies bind their antigen on a microbial surface, their constant regions can become arranged in a way that permits effective C1q engagement. The antigen-binding region of antibody supplies recognition of the target, but it is principally the Fc region that interacts with C1q in this mechanism. The distinction allows adaptive specificity to direct a relatively general effector cascade. Different antibody isotypes and subclasses vary substantially in their capacity to activate complement. IgM and certain IgG subclasses are the standard examples in human immunology. A later chapter discusses antibody structure and isotypes in detail; here the important point is that not every antigen-binding antibody automatically initiates complement.

IgM illustrates how geometry and multivalency influence pathway activation. Secreted IgM is commonly pentameric and can present multiple suitable C1q-binding regions when it interacts with an antigen-bearing surface. A single suitably engaged IgM molecule can often initiate the classical pathway effectively. IgG molecules are monomeric, and efficient activation generally requires several neighboring Fc regions with suitable orientation. The comparison is not a fixed counting rule independent of the antigen or antibody subclass. The density and mobility of antigen, the accessibility of Fc, and the arrangement of neighboring molecules all influence C1 binding. Free IgM circulating without antigen should not be regarded as constitutively causing a classical complement response simply because it contains multiple heavy chains.

Antibody-independent classical activation can also occur. C1q can interact with certain recognition molecules or altered structures, including C-reactive protein associated with appropriate ligands and some dying-cell surfaces. Thus the pathway is not defined strictly as an adaptive response. It is defined by its molecular initiation through C1 and the subsequent use of C4 and C2. Antibody-mediated initiation is a major biological example that helps connect the innate complement machinery to the specificity of acquired immunity. When a question asks whether the classical pathway always requires specific antibody, the correct reasoning is to distinguish its most familiar trigger from its full set of recognized triggers.

After productive C1 engagement, C1r activation leads to activation of C1s. Active C1s cleaves C4 into C4a and C4b. The activated C4b exposes its thioester and may attach near the initiating complex. This localized placement matters because the next substrate, C2, must bind to C4b. If the C4b is generated too far from a useful attachment site, or if it is inactivated before recruiting C2, the pathway fails to form sufficient active convertase. The recognition step and the subsequent deposition step are therefore separate points at which the target surface can influence the response.

C2 binds deposited C4b and is cleaved by C1s, generating the catalytic fragment conventionally called C2a. The C4b2a complex is now a functional C3 convertase. Its activity depends on the association of the catalytic fragment with its surface scaffold; it does not persist indefinitely after assembly. The C3 precursor reaches the complex from the surrounding fluid and is cleaved into C3a and C3b. The released C3a contributes to local communication, while the C3b molecules provide opportunities for surface deposition, receptor engagement and additional convertase formation. This is the stage at which many initiating events become a broad coating of complement fragments rather than remaining confined to the original antibody molecules.

Consider the effect of a selective deficiency of C4. The C1 complex may still recognize appropriate immune complexes and its proteases may be activated, but the canonical C4b scaffold required for efficient classical C3 convertase assembly will not be generated. C2 cannot simply substitute for this surface scaffold. Classical initiation therefore fails to produce its normal downstream C3 cleavage. The alternative pathway, which uses C3b and factor B rather than C4 and C2, may still function if its own components remain intact. This kind of reasoning is more informative than describing C4 as merely an early protein, because it specifies both what is lost and what remains possible.

Adding C3b to the classical C3 convertase-containing surface changes the functional capacity of the complex and surrounding assembly. The canonical classical and lectin C5 convertase is commonly represented as C4b2a3b. This shorthand communicates that C3b is required for efficient C5 conversion in addition to the C3 convertase machinery. It does not imply that every C5 convertase in a biological membrane has exactly one isolated C3b molecule arranged in a single rigid architecture. Surface density and the position of additional C3b molecules matter. For standard examination reasoning, however, the shorthand correctly distinguishes a C3-cleaving assembly from the downstream C5-cleaving activity.

The conversion of C5 releases C5a and generates C5b, which initiates terminal complex assembly. There is no need for an antibody molecule to recognize C5 directly. Once the upstream classical recognition event has assembled the relevant enzymes, substrate processing is determined by complement interactions. One antigen-bound antibody assembly can thereby lead indirectly to the generation of many complement-derived effector molecules. The biological consequence is a coupling of selective recognition to powerful, relatively general downstream chemistry. It also means that a downstream defect such as absence of C5 can abolish the terminal route even when antibody recognition, C4 cleavage and C3 deposition remain functional.

5. Lectin pathway: recognition of carbohydrate patterns

A soluble recognition molecule need not recognize a protein antigen with an antibody's specificity in order to identify a useful target. Carbohydrate residues are widespread in the biological world, but their organization on surfaces is not uniform. A microbial surface may display repeated terminal sugar groups at spacings that allow a multivalent host recognition protein to bind several at once. Mannose-binding lectin, often abbreviated MBL, is an example. It belongs to the collectin family and has collagen-like regions associated with carbohydrate-recognition domains. Its recognition depends on interactions with suitable carbohydrate arrangements and calcium ions. A single weak interaction can become a much more stable collective interaction when several recognition domains engage the same surface.

The relevant distinction is not that mannose exists exclusively in microorganisms. Mammalian glycoconjugates can also contain mannose and related sugars. On many host cell surfaces, however, those residues are not exposed in the same repeated accessible arrangements as on some microbial targets, and normal cells also carry powerful complement regulators. The ability to distinguish surfaces depends on density, accessibility, spacing and the surrounding chemical environment as well as the identity of a single sugar. This explains why a purified monosaccharide does not necessarily behave as an effective complement-activating target. It may bind weakly or compete with recognition, without supplying the multivalent surface geometry needed for productive activation.

MBL is not the only recognition molecule that can initiate the lectin route. Ficolins recognize selected acetylated structures, and additional collectins participate in lectin-pathway recognition. The useful general principle is that distinct recognition proteins are associated with mannose-binding lectin-associated serine proteases, abbreviated MASPs. The student should distinguish a lectin, which is a recognition protein, from a protease, which catalyzes cleavage of a substrate. MBL does not itself cut C4 or C2. Instead it provides a platform through which ligand binding permits the associated proteases to become functionally active.

MASP-2 is the principal protease responsible for C4 cleavage and can cleave C2; MASP-1 contributes to activation of MASP-2 and can help process C2. At examination level, the important order is recognition by a lectin, MASP activation, C4 cleavage and attachment of C4b, C2 recruitment and processing, and formation of C4b2a. The detailed contribution of individual MASPs can vary with the assay and preparation. It is not necessary to reproduce all regulatory complexes to explain the core mechanism, but it is important not to confuse MASP-2 with C1s. Both can perform similar downstream processing in their respective pathways while being associated with different upstream recognition complexes.

The presence of C4b2a in both the classical and lectin routes illustrates a general principle of biological networks: independent inputs can converge on a shared processing module. Once the lectin pathway has generated C4b2a, it does not produce a unique lectin-specific form of C3b. The C3 precursor is the same substrate, and its cleavage products can bind the same complement receptors or assemble the same alternative pathway amplification machinery. This convergence is efficient because the organism does not need a separate molecular killing mechanism for every type of microbial recognition. It is also a vulnerability: a defect in C4 or C2 can impair both initiating routes even when the recognition molecules are normal.

A simple comparison between a C1q-deficient preparation and an MBL/MASP-dependent preparation illustrates the logic. If the target is sensitized with a complement-fixing antibody and has little lectin-pathway activation under the chosen conditions, removal of C1q strongly reduces the classical component of C4 deposition. If the target instead displays a suitable carbohydrate pattern and a functional lectin complex is provided, C4 deposition can occur despite absence of C1q. Removing C4 itself prevents either route from forming the normal shared C3 convertase. These predictions depend on distinguishing isolated pathway conditions from mixed serum; alternative activation and antibody-independent recognition may otherwise obscure the expected pattern.

One experimental approach is to immobilize a suitable carbohydrate-containing ligand on a solid surface and expose it to a complement source. Measuring MBL binding establishes recognition but does not prove that the cascade proceeded. Measuring C4 deposition provides evidence of downstream proteolytic activation. Measuring C3 fragment deposition tests whether further conversion occurred. A terminal complex readout can indicate later activation, but it may be influenced by the physical nature of the assay surface. The strength of the inference grows if the investigator compares conditions lacking a particular recognition protein, a relevant MASP or C4, and restores the missing component. A control surface lacking the recognition pattern helps establish that the observed deposition depends on the intended ligand rather than nonspecific adhesion.

The lectin pathway also demonstrates how inherited variation can alter the effective threshold of innate defense. A reduction in functional MBL can lower lectin-pathway activation on some targets without eliminating every complement-mediated defense mechanism. The other pathways and cellular effectors can compensate to varying degrees. It would therefore be incorrect to predict that every person with a low MBL level has the same severe infection phenotype. A mechanistic deficiency alters the contribution of a particular input; clinical consequences depend on target properties, other immune functions and physiological context. For NET and SET, understanding compensation and shared downstream components is more important than remembering the prevalence of individual genetic variants.

Because MBL recognition is not dependent on antibody generated after infection, it can contribute early in an encounter. Nevertheless the same surface may later become antibody-coated and simultaneously support classical activation. The output of each input need not simply add in a linear fashion: the C3b deposited by either route can enhance alternative amplification, and regulatory processes can become limiting. It is therefore reasonable to speak of three activation pathways, but misleading to picture three completely isolated cascades running from independent recognition events to unrelated endpoints. The lectin route prepares the conceptual transition to the alternative pathway, which acts not only as an initiator but also as a powerful amplifier of the other routes.

6. Alternative pathway: basal C3 hydrolysis and surface-dependent amplification

The alternative pathway challenges the intuitive assumption that immunity must begin with a receptor finding a specific foreign ligand. The central precursor C3 undergoes a low level of spontaneous hydrolysis of its internal thioester in aqueous environments. This phenomenon is often called tick-over. The hydrolyzed form is conventionally written C3(H2O), where the parentheses indicate the hydrolytic modification rather than a separate complement fragment. It is important to distinguish this form from proteolytically generated C3b. Hydrolysis changes the chemical state and structure of C3 without releasing C3a. Although the resulting molecule has certain functional similarities to C3b, it has not been produced by the same cleavage event.

The changed conformation of C3(H2O) permits factor B association under appropriate ionic conditions. Factor D, a protease with a preference for factor B when bound to the relevant complement scaffold, cleaves associated factor B into Ba and Bb. The Bb fragment remains associated with the hydrolyzed C3 scaffold, producing a soluble C3 convertase conventionally denoted C3(H2O)Bb. This convertase can cleave additional intact C3 into C3a and C3b. Because the reactive thioester of the newly generated C3b has not already been consumed by the earlier hydrolysis event, some of that C3b can become attached to nearby surfaces.

The spontaneous hydrolysis route should not be imagined as indiscriminate attack on every membrane. The initial reactions produce opportunities for deposition. Whether those opportunities become an extensive complement response depends strongly on the environment around deposited C3b. If a suitable surface lacks effective protection against alternative pathway activity, deposited C3b can recruit factor B. Factor D then cleaves the recruited factor B, releasing Ba and leaving Bb associated with C3b. The new surface complex C3bBb is the canonical alternative pathway C3 convertase. It is magnesium-dependent and relatively short-lived unless stabilized or continuously replaced.

There are two important distinctions in this sequence. First, factor D does not simply cleave large amounts of freely circulating factor B at random; association with C3b or hydrolyzed C3 helps create the appropriate substrate state. Second, the protease that cleaves C3 in the assembled alternative pathway convertase is Bb, not factor D itself. Factor D performs an upstream processing step that makes Bb available within an active complex. Confusing these two enzymatic roles leads to incorrect predictions about which activity can be restored by supplying an already assembled convertase versus adding purified unprocessed factor B.

Once C3bBb forms, its C3-cleaving activity produces further C3b, and some fraction of that C3b is retained on the same or neighboring surfaces. Each retained molecule can potentially provide another platform for factor B and factor D. This creates a product-dependent amplification loop. The same logic applies if the original C3b was made by C4b2a after classical or lectin pathway initiation. Alternative amplification does not care whether the C3b scaffold originated from a soluble tick-over convertase or an antibody-directed initiating convertase. It requires appropriate deposited C3b and its own associated components. This is why it is useful to distinguish initiation from amplification whenever an experimental result is attributed to a particular pathway.

Properdin, also called factor P, is a positive regulator that stabilizes alternative pathway convertases. By increasing their effective lifetime, it can increase the number of C3 molecules cleaved by an assembled complex. This mechanism is different from increasing the catalytic rate of a particular active site, although either change could increase cumulative product formation. Properdin can also support initiation or recruitment in particular contexts; the general exam-level picture is stabilization of C3bBb. It is not itself the protease responsible for C3 cleavage, and it does not replace factor B or factor D. Removal of properdin may reduce amplification substantially while leaving some ability to form unstable convertases.

Why do many microbial surfaces support the loop more efficiently than intact host cells? A deposited C3b molecule cannot make this distinction by recognizing a genomic marker of species identity. Rather, the local chemistry affects the competition between amplification proteins and regulatory proteins. Factor H can interact with C3b and with host-associated molecular features, including particular sialylated or glycosaminoglycan-containing surfaces. On protected host surfaces this favors inhibition, convertase decay and factor I-mediated processing of C3b. On permissive surfaces the balance can favor factor B binding and new convertase formation. This is a form of discrimination by differential regulation as much as by selective recognition.

The model also predicts exceptions. A pathogen can acquire host regulatory proteins or display surface features that help recruit them. Such a target may be unusually resistant to alternative amplification even though it is foreign. Conversely an injured host cell may lose normal protection or expose patterns that favor complement recognition and deposition. Self versus nonself is therefore not a binary chemical property possessed by C3b itself. It emerges from the combination of target recognition, local surface characteristics, regulatory availability and amplification. This is why complement can participate in both microbial clearance and removal of damaged or dying host material.

At sufficiently high C3b deposition, the alternative pathway produces efficient C5-converting activity, canonically written C3bBbC3b or C3b2Bb. As with the classical C5 convertase shorthand, the notation represents an additional C3b requirement for efficient C5 cleavage. It is the increased local C3b density and the organization of the complex that help switch the system from predominantly C3 processing to effective C5 processing. The actual molecular organization of surface complexes can be more complicated than the simple formula. The exam-level distinction is that C3bBb cleaves C3, whereas C3b-associated additional assembly supports downstream cleavage of C5.

One can now predict the consequences of factor B deficiency. Formation of the normal alternative pathway C3 convertase is impaired. Classical and lectin C3 convertases can still operate if their own components are intact, so some C3b deposition and downstream C5 cleavage may remain. The reduction in total complement response may nevertheless be larger than expected from merely removing one of three starting pathways because factor B-dependent amplification is lost for all sources of deposited C3b. An experiment that measures classical initiation using C4 deposition could therefore show preserved activity, while an experiment measuring final C3 deposition or microbial lysis shows a marked reduction.

The alternative pathway is sometimes summarized as an antibody-independent route. That description is correct regarding its ability to initiate without antigen-specific antibody, but it becomes misleading if interpreted to mean the alternative convertase never participates in a response started by antibodies. Classical activation and alternative amplification can operate on the same antibody-coated target at the same time. The conceptual lesson is to trace a molecule's origin and function rather than assign every event to a single isolated named pathway. A C3b fragment is still C3b after either route, and the same regulatory machinery can influence its fate.

7. From C3 conversion to C5 conversion: understanding the common hub

The central location of C3 is best understood by comparing the outputs of the three activation routes rather than merely their inputs. C1-dependent and lectin-dependent activation both create the C4b2a complex, whereas factor B-dependent activation creates C3bBb. Although these enzyme complexes contain different proteins, both can process intact C3. The resulting C3a and C3b do not retain a label identifying which convertase made them. A receptor that binds C3b on a bacterium cannot generally determine whether it originated from the classical, lectin or alternative route simply by recognizing the fragment. An antibody recognizing a cleavage neoepitope may reveal that C3 was activated, but it does not by itself identify the initiating pathway.

It follows that C3 is much more than a junction in a diagram. It supplies the major opsonin, an inflammatory signal and a substrate product that enables further amplification and helps generate C5-converting activity. Loss of C3 therefore interrupts multiple classes of defense at once. By contrast, loss of a particular initiating recognition molecule removes only one route by which C3 activation normally begins. In a network with several inputs and one shared hub, a central substrate deficiency generally has broader direct consequences than loss of one input, provided the other inputs are genuinely functional.

The transition from C3 cleavage to C5 cleavage is biologically important because it links the degree of surface activation with generation of terminal effector components. C5 resembles C3 in the sense that proteolytic cleavage separates a small soluble fragment from a much larger fragment with a different activity. Unlike C3b and C4b, however, C5b does not serve as the canonical covalently attached thioester-dependent opsonin. Its important downstream role is to bind C6 and initiate assembly of the terminal complex. Cleavage of C5 thus does not simply repeat the entire C3 amplification loop. It creates a different functional branch, one concerned with powerful inflammation and membrane damage.

C5 is processed efficiently when appropriate C5 convertase activity has formed. The classical and lectin C5 convertase is conventionally represented by C4b2a3b; the alternative pathway C5 convertase is represented by C3bBbC3b. The additional C3b is important in substrate recognition and organization of the C5 cleavage environment. A student should not reason that the extra C3b is an extra protease, since C3b has no such catalytic role. Instead, it contributes to the architecture and substrate handling of the conversion machinery. In either case, the protease-containing convertase acts on C5 to generate C5a and C5b.

One useful thought experiment is to compare two surfaces with similar numbers of initial recognition events but different efficiencies of C3b retention. The first has many neighboring sites that retain C3b and lacks strong regulatory activity. The second rapidly inactivates deposited C3b or provides few usable attachment sites. Both can show initial C3 cleavage and release of C3a. However, the first is more likely to support efficient amplification and progression to C5 cleavage. This means that a signal indicating initiation is not necessarily evidence of equivalent terminal pathway activation. The distance between early and late readouts can reveal a regulatory or assembly defect.

The C3 and C5 outputs can also differ temporally. A convertase may form, cleave some C3 and decay before enough C3b accumulates to promote substantial C5 conversion. If the investigator samples the reaction at an early time, they may detect C3 activation products but little evidence of C5b-dependent terminal assembly. A later measurement under sustained activation could show a stronger terminal response. Conversely, terminal complexes deposited on a surface can remain detectable after upstream convertase activity declines. An endpoint assay cannot reconstruct the entire time course. Designing a time series with separate early and late readouts provides a clearer test of the predicted order.

In biological serum, feedback, substrate depletion and regulation complicate the idea of an orderly single-file cascade. A C3 convertase can continue to process C3 while neighboring assemblies process C5. Many copies of each substrate and complex are distributed over a surface. Some fragments are degraded, some bind receptors, and some are incorporated into new assemblies. Diagrams showing a vertical sequence communicate causal dependence rather than requiring every molecule in the sample to complete the same stage before the next stage begins. For examinations, the key is to recognize the branching logic: inhibiting C5 can leave C3-dependent labeling intact, whereas blocking C3 has upstream consequences for both the amplification and terminal branches.

This organization makes complement inhibition an instructive example of network perturbation. A selective block of C5 cleavage prevents production of new C5a and C5b through the inhibited pathway. It does not directly inhibit the protease activity of C4b2a or C3bBb. C3a release and C3b deposition can therefore continue under suitable conditions. A selective block of C3 cleavage has a broader effect on the canonical cascade, because it reduces the generation of the major C3-derived opsonin and makes efficient downstream C5 convertase formation difficult. In an experiment the precise outcome will depend on inhibition completeness and pre-existing deposited fragments, so the comparison is a prediction of the ordinary canonical pathway, not a guarantee that every measurable signal becomes zero.

The system can now be separated into modules useful for solving questions. The initiation module establishes the appropriate C3 convertase. The C3 hub supplies the major deposition and amplification chemistry. The C5 stage links strong activation to an inflammatory peptide and terminal assembly precursor. The terminal module damages suitable membranes. Receptors and regulatory molecules interact with several modules without necessarily abolishing the rest. Such modular reasoning helps students identify what an assay measures, what a deficiency affects directly, and which consequences are secondary to changes upstream.

8. Opsonization, complement receptors and clearance of immune complexes

The word opsonization refers to coating a target in a way that facilitates recognition and uptake by phagocytic cells. It is not synonymous with lysis. A microorganism can remain structurally intact and metabolically viable after becoming coated with C3b, yet become much more susceptible to uptake by a neutrophil or macrophage. The benefit to the host arises because many immune cells express receptors for complement fragments. These receptors provide an additional attachment mechanism between an effector cell and a target that would otherwise interact less efficiently. The term opsonin describes the molecule contributing to that facilitated interaction.

C3b is particularly well suited to this function because its covalent attachment can generate a relatively stable surface label. Complement receptors recognize structural features of C3-derived fragments rather than the microbial antigen that initially triggered activation. The same receptor-bearing phagocyte can therefore interact with many different kinds of complement-coated targets. Receptor specificity is directed toward a shared host-generated label, while recognition of diverse microbial surfaces is handled by upstream complement initiation and other immune recognition mechanisms. This division of labor allows a limited set of cellular receptors to participate in defense against a much larger diversity of pathogens.

The complement receptor CR1, also known as CD35, can recognize C3b and C4b and has important regulatory and immune-complex-handling functions. CR3, also known as Mac-1 or CD11b/CD18, and CR4 are integrin-family complement receptors that can recognize iC3b. The prefix i in iC3b indicates an inactivated form in relation to convertase participation. It does not mean biologically useless. Processing of C3b by factor I in the presence of suitable cofactors produces iC3b, which can no longer serve as the same alternative convertase scaffold but remains an important ligand for phagocytic complement receptors.

The transition from C3b to iC3b demonstrates that regulation and effector activity need not be opposing absolutes. A host regulator can limit further convertase formation without immediately removing every useful recognition label from a target. In fact, changing the fragment can change the receptor repertoire available for subsequent recognition. Further processing of C3 fragments can leave surface-associated C3-derived material such as C3dg and C3d. CR2, also known as CD21, recognizes relevant C3 degradation fragments and participates in a different biological context, including B-cell co-receptor function. Fragment processing is thus a way of changing the functional meaning of a deposited protein over time.

The receptor interaction must also be interpreted within the physiology of a phagocyte. Adhesion of a target to a cell is not the same as complete internalization, and uptake is not the same as killing. A phagocyte must organize its cytoskeleton, enclose the target and process the resulting phagosome. The activation state of the phagocyte and other signals can affect the efficiency of these steps. Some complement receptor-mediated interactions cooperate with antibody Fc receptors and innate receptor signals to produce efficient ingestion. Consequently, measuring surface binding at a low temperature or under conditions that limit internalization cannot establish that the target has been killed. These distinct experimental endpoints should be considered separately.

Complement provides a route for clearance of soluble immune complexes as well as microorganisms. Antibody bound to soluble antigen can form complexes capable of activating complement, depending on size, composition and geometry. Complement deposition can allow these complexes to bind CR1 on human erythrocytes. The erythrocytes transport complexes through the circulation, where specialized phagocytic cells in organs such as the liver and spleen help remove them. The red cell itself is not acting as a classical phagocyte. It is carrying a bound cargo that can be transferred to cells equipped for clearance. This is an instructive example of a nonimmune cell participating in immune homeostasis through surface receptors.

If the early classical pathway is severely defective, normal handling of immune complexes and dying-cell material can be impaired. This helps explain the association between deficiencies in early classical components and certain autoimmune conditions. The association should not be interpreted as a claim that all autoimmune disease results from complement deficiency. Complement may also contribute to tissue injury when activated at sites of inappropriate antibody binding. It can therefore have protective effects on clearance and harmful effects during persistent or misdirected activation. Which aspect dominates depends on the component, location, substrate and regulatory state.

Finally, C3 fragments can help link innate recognition with subsequent B-cell activation. When a B-cell receptor binds an antigen that also carries an appropriate complement fragment, co-engagement of CR2 in the B-cell co-receptor complex can enhance the response to that antigen. The complement fragment does not provide the antigen specificity of the B-cell receptor. It provides an additional signal indicating that the antigen has encountered the complement system. The result is another example of a shared innate chemical label influencing an antigen-specific adaptive response. The detailed development and signaling of B cells belongs to a later node; the complement contribution is understood here as a mechanistic bridge.
