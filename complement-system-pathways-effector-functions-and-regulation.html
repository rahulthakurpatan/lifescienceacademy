1. A soluble defense that turns recognition into action

A bacterium that enters a wound encounters far more than the cells visible in a microscope. It also enters a fluid environment containing proteins that can recognize its surface, alter that surface and attract cells capable of destroying it. The complement system is one of the most important of these extracellular defense mechanisms. It consists of soluble and membrane-associated proteins, including recognition molecules, proteases, substrates, receptors and regulators. These proteins cooperate in a controlled biochemical network. Several components circulate in a relatively inactive form, allowing the body to respond rapidly without first synthesizing an entire new set of defensive molecules at the site of infection.

Consider a small population of bacteria newly introduced into connective tissue. Resident macrophages may detect bacterial molecules through pattern-recognition receptors, but not every bacterium will be in immediate contact with a macrophage. Complement proteins in extracellular fluid can reach microbial surfaces without the direct contact required by a cellular receptor. Activation changes the chemistry of the target surface. A previously difficult-to-ingest bacterium can acquire many molecules of a protein fragment that bind receptors on phagocytes. This transformation does not require the bacterium to die first. Indeed, an important property of complement is that it can make viable microorganisms easier for other immune mechanisms to destroy.

A second problem is one of coordination. Local macrophages and the small number of neutrophils already present in a tissue cannot necessarily eliminate a growing infection. Additional cells must leave the blood and move toward the source of danger. Complement activation produces small soluble peptides that communicate with cells in the surrounding tissue. Some alter local inflammatory responses; others are especially effective in promoting directed migration and activation of leukocytes. A third problem is physical destruction. Certain susceptible microbial membranes can be damaged by a protein assembly that penetrates the membrane and compromises its barrier properties. These three solutions are related but not interchangeable. Labeling for ingestion, attracting an effector cell and producing a membrane lesion are distinct biological outcomes of the same network.

The adjective humoral refers here to an activity mediated through substances in body fluids rather than exclusively through direct cell-to-cell contact. Both antibodies and complement are humoral immune factors, but that shared location does not make them identical. Antibodies are products of B-cell lineages whose specificity depends on rearranged immunoglobulin genes and subsequent selection. Complement proteins are generally encoded by inherited genes without the antigen-receptor rearrangement that generates individual B-cell specificities. Complement can therefore function during a first encounter with a microbe, even before newly generated antibodies appear. It can also work with antibodies produced by the adaptive immune system. The latter relationship explains why complement belongs primarily to innate immunity while making major contributions to adaptive effector responses.

Most circulating complement proteins are produced principally by the liver. Other cells, including immune cells and tissue-resident cells, can produce selected components locally. This distinction helps explain why the concentration measured in blood is not necessarily a complete description of complement activity in a particular tissue. The system is distributed across different environments: plasma, extravascular fluid, cell surfaces and sites where inflammation increases permeability. Nevertheless, for the purposes of understanding the canonical pathways, circulating proteins and reactions on extracellular surfaces provide the clearest starting point. Intracellular complement biology is not required to understand the standard NET and SET pathway models.

The system solves a fundamental problem of defense: how can a response be both rapid and local? A circulating molecule may be present throughout the body, while its active products must remain preferentially associated with the target or the site of activation. Many complement reactions achieve localization by generating short-lived reactive groups that form bonds with neighboring surfaces, or by assembling transient enzyme complexes on deposited fragments. Soluble products can act over a wider region, but they too are subject to degradation and regulation. The combination allows defense to spread enough to recruit help without automatically converting the whole blood compartment into an inflammatory reaction.

A useful comparison with cellular signal transduction is the idea of a cascade. In a kinase cascade an activated enzyme modifies multiple downstream substrates. In complement, proteolytic enzymes cut peptide bonds in downstream components, often producing fragments with new activities. Both networks amplify a small initiating event, and both need mechanisms that limit duration and prevent inappropriate activation. Their chemistry is different: the canonical complement cascade is extracellular and is not powered by the transfer of phosphate groups from ATP at every activation step. Its key switching events are controlled proteolysis, conformational change, noncovalent assembly and, for selected fragments, covalent attachment to nearby targets. This is why an extracellular defense can operate without the direct energy metabolism of the target cell.

It is also important to distinguish complement activation from the mere presence of complement. An immunoassay might report a normal concentration of a precursor while a functional assay shows impaired activity. Conversely, a low concentration can reflect rapid use rather than an inherited inability to produce the protein. A component can be present yet structurally defective, or it can be normal but unable to participate because an interacting component is missing. The relevant biological variable is the ability of the network to produce an appropriate local response. Concentration, cleavage, deposition, receptor binding and lysis are separate experimental readouts of that ability.

2. Experimental origins: separating recognition from destruction

The complement system was discovered because the ability of serum to destroy microorganisms could not be explained by a single stable substance. In the late nineteenth century investigators found that the bactericidal property of serum was sensitive to heating. Hans Buchner used the name alexin for a heat-sensitive protective activity. Jules Bordet subsequently demonstrated that immune bacteriolysis involved a heat-sensitive serum factor and a relatively heat-resistant sensitizing factor. The sensitizing factor is now understood in terms of antibody. The older term alexin refers to the activity later understood as complement. Paul Ehrlich helped establish the terminology of complement, emphasizing how this activity completes the effect of specific immune recognition.

The distinction was conceptually powerful because a mixture can have a biological property that neither fraction displays under the same conditions when tested alone. If a serum sample contains antibodies that recognize a microorganism and functional complement that damages the organism, heating under suitable conditions may inactivate much of its complement activity while retaining sufficient antibody-binding activity. Adding an appropriate fresh source of complement can restore the ability to lyse a susceptible target. The interpretation is not that heat creates a new antibody or that heating reversibly removes its antigen-binding site. Rather, the experiment separates relatively heat-resistant recognition from a heat-labile effector function.

A simplified reconstruction illustrates the causal logic. Imagine four tubes containing identical susceptible antibody-recognized targets. Tube one receives untreated immune serum; tube two receives the same immune serum after complement inactivation; tube three receives a fresh complement source that has been checked for lack of specific sensitizing antibody; tube four receives the combination of heat-treated immune serum and fresh complement. Under conditions where antibody-dependent classical activation is necessary for the chosen endpoint, strong lysis in tubes one and four with weak lysis in the other tubes supports a requirement for both activities. This reconstruction is an illustrative experimental design, not a report of the original nineteenth-century numerical results. It also shows why the target and complement source must be selected carefully: some targets activate complement even in the absence of the particular antibody.

The experiment separates requirements but does not identify the molecular pathway. To do that, researchers must distinguish a loss of function caused by one missing protein from loss of multiple heat-sensitive proteins. Fractionation of serum, functional reconstitution and identification of individual components progressively revealed the network. If a particular fraction cannot support lysis on its own but restores activity to an otherwise complete preparation lacking that fraction, it supplies a necessary activity under the conditions used. Such reasoning motivated the identification of multiple complement components. Modern immunological methods permit the same logic to be applied to purified proteins, genetically deficient serum and specific blocking antibodies.

Historical complement terminology reflects the order of discovery and the changing understanding of the cascade, not a universal numerical order of activation. In the classical pathway, activation of C1 leads to cleavage of C4 and C2 before the principal cleavage of C3. A person who memorizes C1, C2, C3 and C4 as successive integers will therefore draw the wrong pathway. This is a common problem in biological nomenclature: a name may be historical, whereas the function is defined by experimental evidence. The pathway must be learned from the substrate-enzyme relationships rather than inferred from the number on each protein.

Recognition that complement could operate without an experimentally added specific antibody was another major change. The alternative pathway was established as a mechanism independent of the classical C1-dependent initiation route. Properdin, investigated in mid-twentieth-century complement research, became an important molecule in understanding this system. Modern teaching emphasizes its stabilizing effect on the alternative pathway convertase and recognizes that, in some contexts, it can also participate in recruitment of pathway components. Discovery of a lectin-dependent route later made it clear that a soluble recognition molecule can recognize repeated microbial carbohydrates and recruit associated proteases, converging on the same early convertase used by the classical pathway.

The classical, lectin and alternative names consequently identify routes of activation, not three unrelated sets of antimicrobial proteins. The classical pathway provided the historical demonstration of antibody-dependent killing. The lectin pathway illustrates recognition of certain molecular patterns without requiring antigen-specific antibody. The alternative pathway illustrates the capacity for basal C3 activation and amplification on permissive surfaces. A mature understanding recognizes that complement response in biological serum can involve more than one route. Classical initiation, for instance, can deposit the C3b that drives additional alternative pathway amplification. The identification of one initiating route does not show that the other routes make no contribution.

Early hemolysis experiments remain useful because erythrocytes provide a measurable membrane-damage endpoint. Intact red cells retain hemoglobin; when susceptible cells lyse, hemoglobin appears in the surrounding medium. The resulting absorbance can quantify lysis. One can change the way targets are sensitized or the ionic conditions to emphasize particular complement routes. However, lysis is an endpoint of the terminal pathway, not a universal measure of every effect of complement. A preparation may lack the ability to lyse erythrocytes but still deposit C3 fragments and recruit phagocytes. This distinction between the mechanism and the assay endpoint is crucial when interpreting modern experiments built on the same historical tradition.

3. Components, fragments and the chemistry of amplification

The name C3 identifies a large precursor protein, whereas C3a and C3b identify products of its controlled cleavage. They are not simply two arbitrary portions that happen to be smaller than the precursor. The cleavage exposes structural features and separates activities. C3a functions as a soluble signaling peptide. C3b undergoes a structural rearrangement that makes a previously shielded internal thioester available for reaction with neighboring chemical groups. A fraction of the generated C3b can consequently become covalently associated with a surface near the site of cleavage. C3b that fails to react with a suitable surface can be inactivated through reaction with water and regulatory processes. This chemical behavior gives the system both reach and localization.

The thioester is an example of a reactive functional group protected inside a folded protein until the correct molecular event occurs. In the precursor, the arrangement of protein domains helps limit uncontrolled reaction. Cleavage changes the structure and allows the internal bond to be attacked by nucleophiles. Hydroxyl and amino groups on nearby molecules can participate in covalent attachment, producing ester or amide bonds depending on the reaction. The resulting association is different from the reversible recognition of an antigen by an antibody. Surface retention of C3b permits several subsequent interactions at the same location even when the surrounding liquid is moving. C4b uses a related thioester-dependent attachment strategy, although its functional role in the initiating convertase differs from that of C3b.

The small fragments released during activation do not remain tethered in the same way. A small diffusible signal can influence receptor-bearing cells away from the precise cleavage site. This difference in physical fate is a repeated design principle. The larger fragment may become a scaffold or tag at the surface, while a smaller fragment may deliver information about activation into the surrounding fluid. One must not infer identical activities for all small fragments simply because they are named with the letter a. The best-established anaphylatoxins for standard immunology are C3a and C5a, with C5a especially potent in many inflammatory settings. C4a is often listed historically as an anaphylatoxin but its physiological activity and receptor assignments are far less established.

Several complement components are zymogens, inactive precursors of proteases. A zymogen acquires an active catalytic conformation through cleavage or related activation events. In the classical pathway C1r and C1s are examples of serine proteases recruited into the C1 complex. Factor B in the alternative pathway becomes the catalytically active Bb fragment following cleavage when appropriately associated with C3b or hydrolyzed C3. Other proteins are not proteases at all. C1q is a recognition molecule; C3b is a major binding and scaffolding fragment; properdin stabilizes particular complexes; and many terminal components cooperate in nonenzymatic membrane assembly. Calling every C-numbered protein an enzyme is therefore incorrect.

A convertase is an enzyme complex that converts a named complement precursor into its active products. The C3 convertase cleaves C3, and the C5 convertase efficiently cleaves C5. The names describe substrate specificity, not the identity of every protein within the complex. The classical and lectin pathways use C4b2a as their canonical C3 convertase in widely used textbook notation. The alternative pathway uses C3bBb on a surface. Both contain a surface-associated component and a catalytic fragment. C4b and C3b position the complex; C2a and Bb provide protease activity. C3bBb does not mean that the large fragment C3b is itself the enzyme responsible for C3 cleavage.

There is a historical complication in C2 terminology. Unlike most familiar complement proteins, older and still widely used textbook conventions call its larger catalytic fragment C2a and its smaller released fragment C2b. Some revised nomenclatures reverse these labels for consistency. An examination problem using C4b2a generally follows the conventional textbook scheme; a source using C4b2b may be following the alternative naming convention rather than describing a different biochemical mechanism. In this chapter C4b2a denotes the classical and lectin C3 convertase. Functional interpretation depends on identifying the catalytic fragment, not on imposing a suffix rule on every component.

The biochemical amplification is possible because an active protease complex can cleave multiple molecules of substrate before it dissociates or is regulated. Each successful C3 cleavage yields a C3b molecule that may itself become a platform for factor B recruitment. This is a positive feedback loop: the product helps assemble an enzyme capable of making more of the same product. The loop does not mean that every C3b necessarily produces another convertase. Attachment efficiency, local availability of factor B and factor D, convertase lifetime, regulatory proteins, and competing reactions all determine whether the loop grows or dies out. A productive reaction on a microbial surface is thus a probability-dependent process rather than a mechanical guarantee of unlimited amplification.

Proteolysis differs from a reversible ligand-receptor binding event. Once the peptide chain of an individual precursor has been cut, the original chain is not restored by simple dissociation of the enzyme. The system must maintain stores of precursors and remove or process their fragments. Restoration of the circulating precursor supply depends on production and turnover at the organismal level. Termination at a particular surface relies instead on transient enzyme assembly, inhibition of activating proteases, regulatory cleavage of deposited fragments, and limitation of membrane pore assembly. This distinction explains why a burst of excessive activation can consume complement proteins even though the initiating stimulus eventually disappears.

Calcium and magnesium ions contribute to specific molecular interactions in complement complexes. Their roles are not to serve as general energy sources, but to help maintain the binding geometry and assembly of relevant protein complexes. Calcium supports aspects of classical and lectin initiation, whereas magnesium supports alternative pathway convertase assembly. The dependence provides a useful experimental lever. Chelators that sequester divalent ions can suppress activation; conditions that bind calcium while supplying magnesium can favor the measurement of alternative activity over calcium-dependent initiation. The ionic environment must be treated as part of the assay design, rather than assumed irrelevant because the main actors are proteins.

4. Classical pathway: converting bound recognition into protease activity

The classical pathway begins with the recognition complex C1. It contains one C1q recognition molecule associated with two molecules each of C1r and C1s. C1q is organized with multiple globular recognition heads at the ends of collagen-like arms. This multivalent geometry is suited to detecting clustered recognition ligands. A single free immunoglobulin molecule floating in plasma is generally not equivalent to a group of appropriately positioned Fc regions on a target. The structural arrangement of C1q allows a spatial pattern of binding sites to influence whether the attached protease system becomes activated.

When suitable antibodies bind their antigen on a microbial surface, their constant regions can become arranged in a way that permits effective C1q engagement. The antigen-binding region of antibody supplies recognition of the target, but it is principally the Fc region that interacts with C1q in this mechanism. The distinction allows adaptive specificity to direct a relatively general effector cascade. Different antibody isotypes and subclasses vary substantially in their capacity to activate complement. IgM and certain IgG subclasses are the standard examples in human immunology. A later chapter discusses antibody structure and isotypes in detail; here the important point is that not every antigen-binding antibody automatically initiates complement.

IgM illustrates how geometry and multivalency influence pathway activation. Secreted IgM is commonly pentameric and can present multiple suitable C1q-binding regions when it interacts with an antigen-bearing surface. A single suitably engaged IgM molecule can often initiate the classical pathway effectively. IgG molecules are monomeric, and efficient activation generally requires several neighboring Fc regions with suitable orientation. The comparison is not a fixed counting rule independent of the antigen or antibody subclass. The density and mobility of antigen, the accessibility of Fc, and the arrangement of neighboring molecules all influence C1 binding. Free IgM circulating without antigen should not be regarded as constitutively causing a classical complement response simply because it contains multiple heavy chains.

Antibody-independent classical activation can also occur. C1q can interact with certain recognition molecules or altered structures, including C-reactive protein associated with appropriate ligands and some dying-cell surfaces. Thus the pathway is not defined strictly as an adaptive response. It is defined by its molecular initiation through C1 and the subsequent use of C4 and C2. Antibody-mediated initiation is a major biological example that helps connect the innate complement machinery to the specificity of acquired immunity. When a question asks whether the classical pathway always requires specific antibody, the correct reasoning is to distinguish its most familiar trigger from its full set of recognized triggers.

After productive C1 engagement, C1r activation leads to activation of C1s. Active C1s cleaves C4 into C4a and C4b. The activated C4b exposes its thioester and may attach near the initiating complex. This localized placement matters because the next substrate, C2, must bind to C4b. If the C4b is generated too far from a useful attachment site, or if it is inactivated before recruiting C2, the pathway fails to form sufficient active convertase. The recognition step and the subsequent deposition step are therefore separate points at which the target surface can influence the response.

C2 binds deposited C4b and is cleaved by C1s, generating the catalytic fragment conventionally called C2a. The C4b2a complex is now a functional C3 convertase. Its activity depends on the association of the catalytic fragment with its surface scaffold; it does not persist indefinitely after assembly. The C3 precursor reaches the complex from the surrounding fluid and is cleaved into C3a and C3b. The released C3a contributes to local communication, while the C3b molecules provide opportunities for surface deposition, receptor engagement and additional convertase formation. This is the stage at which many initiating events become a broad coating of complement fragments rather than remaining confined to the original antibody molecules.

Consider the effect of a selective deficiency of C4. The C1 complex may still recognize appropriate immune complexes and its proteases may be activated, but the canonical C4b scaffold required for efficient classical C3 convertase assembly will not be generated. C2 cannot simply substitute for this surface scaffold. Classical initiation therefore fails to produce its normal downstream C3 cleavage. The alternative pathway, which uses C3b and factor B rather than C4 and C2, may still function if its own components remain intact. This kind of reasoning is more informative than describing C4 as merely an early protein, because it specifies both what is lost and what remains possible.

Adding C3b to the classical C3 convertase-containing surface changes the functional capacity of the complex and surrounding assembly. The canonical classical and lectin C5 convertase is commonly represented as C4b2a3b. This shorthand communicates that C3b is required for efficient C5 conversion in addition to the C3 convertase machinery. It does not imply that every C5 convertase in a biological membrane has exactly one isolated C3b molecule arranged in a single rigid architecture. Surface density and the position of additional C3b molecules matter. For standard examination reasoning, however, the shorthand correctly distinguishes a C3-cleaving assembly from the downstream C5-cleaving activity.

The conversion of C5 releases C5a and generates C5b, which initiates terminal complex assembly. There is no need for an antibody molecule to recognize C5 directly. Once the upstream classical recognition event has assembled the relevant enzymes, substrate processing is determined by complement interactions. One antigen-bound antibody assembly can thereby lead indirectly to the generation of many complement-derived effector molecules. The biological consequence is a coupling of selective recognition to powerful, relatively general downstream chemistry. It also means that a downstream defect such as absence of C5 can abolish the terminal route even when antibody recognition, C4 cleavage and C3 deposition remain functional.

A modern test of the spatial-recognition model was reported by Diebolder and colleagues in 2014. Their study examined how IgG molecules organize after binding antigens on cells and found that interactions between neighboring Fc regions favor ordered hexameric arrangements capable of recruiting C1. The finding helps explain why an antibody's affinity for an antigen is not sufficient to predict its complement-activating efficiency. In an experimental comparison, changing Fc interactions while maintaining antigen recognition can alter complement activation. Such a result supports the distinction between antigen binding and effector assembly. It does not imply that every active antibody arrangement must always adopt exactly the same geometry on every natural target. See the linked Science study in the references.

5. Lectin pathway: recognition of carbohydrate patterns

A soluble recognition molecule need not recognize a protein antigen with an antibody's specificity in order to identify a useful target. Carbohydrate residues are widespread in the biological world, but their organization on surfaces is not uniform. A microbial surface may display repeated terminal sugar groups at spacings that allow a multivalent host recognition protein to bind several at once. Mannose-binding lectin, often abbreviated MBL, is an example. It belongs to the collectin family and has collagen-like regions associated with carbohydrate-recognition domains. Its recognition depends on interactions with suitable carbohydrate arrangements and calcium ions. A single weak interaction can become a much more stable collective interaction when several recognition domains engage the same surface.

The relevant distinction is not that mannose exists exclusively in microorganisms. Mammalian glycoconjugates can also contain mannose and related sugars. On many host cell surfaces, however, those residues are not exposed in the same repeated accessible arrangements as on some microbial targets, and normal cells also carry powerful complement regulators. The ability to distinguish surfaces depends on density, accessibility, spacing and the surrounding chemical environment as well as the identity of a single sugar. This explains why a purified monosaccharide does not necessarily behave as an effective complement-activating target. It may bind weakly or compete with recognition, without supplying the multivalent surface geometry needed for productive activation.

MBL is not the only recognition molecule that can initiate the lectin route. Ficolins recognize selected acetylated structures, and additional collectins participate in lectin-pathway recognition. The useful general principle is that distinct recognition proteins are associated with mannose-binding lectin-associated serine proteases, abbreviated MASPs. The student should distinguish a lectin, which is a recognition protein, from a protease, which catalyzes cleavage of a substrate. MBL does not itself cut C4 or C2. Instead it provides a platform through which ligand binding permits the associated proteases to become functionally active.

MASP-2 is the principal protease responsible for C4 cleavage and can cleave C2; MASP-1 contributes to activation of MASP-2 and can help process C2. At examination level, the important order is recognition by a lectin, MASP activation, C4 cleavage and attachment of C4b, C2 recruitment and processing, and formation of C4b2a. The detailed contribution of individual MASPs can vary with the assay and preparation. It is not necessary to reproduce all regulatory complexes to explain the core mechanism, but it is important not to confuse MASP-2 with C1s. Both can perform similar downstream processing in their respective pathways while being associated with different upstream recognition complexes.

The presence of C4b2a in both the classical and lectin routes illustrates a general principle of biological networks: independent inputs can converge on a shared processing module. Once the lectin pathway has generated C4b2a, it does not produce a unique lectin-specific form of C3b. The C3 precursor is the same substrate, and its cleavage products can bind the same complement receptors or assemble the same alternative pathway amplification machinery. This convergence is efficient because the organism does not need a separate molecular killing mechanism for every type of microbial recognition. It is also a vulnerability: a defect in C4 or C2 can impair both initiating routes even when the recognition molecules are normal.

A simple comparison between a C1q-deficient preparation and an MBL/MASP-dependent preparation illustrates the logic. If the target is sensitized with a complement-fixing antibody and has little lectin-pathway activation under the chosen conditions, removal of C1q strongly reduces the classical component of C4 deposition. If the target instead displays a suitable carbohydrate pattern and a functional lectin complex is provided, C4 deposition can occur despite absence of C1q. Removing C4 itself prevents either route from forming the normal shared C3 convertase. These predictions depend on distinguishing isolated pathway conditions from mixed serum; alternative activation and antibody-independent recognition may otherwise obscure the expected pattern.

One experimental approach is to immobilize a suitable carbohydrate-containing ligand on a solid surface and expose it to a complement source. Measuring MBL binding establishes recognition but does not prove that the cascade proceeded. Measuring C4 deposition provides evidence of downstream proteolytic activation. Measuring C3 fragment deposition tests whether further conversion occurred. A terminal complex readout can indicate later activation, but it may be influenced by the physical nature of the assay surface. The strength of the inference grows if the investigator compares conditions lacking a particular recognition protein, a relevant MASP or C4, and restores the missing component. A control surface lacking the recognition pattern helps establish that the observed deposition depends on the intended ligand rather than nonspecific adhesion.

The lectin pathway also demonstrates how inherited variation can alter the effective threshold of innate defense. A reduction in functional MBL can lower lectin-pathway activation on some targets without eliminating every complement-mediated defense mechanism. The other pathways and cellular effectors can compensate to varying degrees. It would therefore be incorrect to predict that every person with a low MBL level has the same severe infection phenotype. A mechanistic deficiency alters the contribution of a particular input; clinical consequences depend on target properties, other immune functions and physiological context. For NET and SET, understanding compensation and shared downstream components is more important than remembering the prevalence of individual genetic variants.

Because MBL recognition is not dependent on antibody generated after infection, it can contribute early in an encounter. Nevertheless the same surface may later become antibody-coated and simultaneously support classical activation. The output of each input need not simply add in a linear fashion: the C3b deposited by either route can enhance alternative amplification, and regulatory processes can become limiting. It is therefore reasonable to speak of three activation pathways, but misleading to picture three completely isolated cascades running from independent recognition events to unrelated endpoints. The lectin route prepares the conceptual transition to the alternative pathway, which acts not only as an initiator but also as a powerful amplifier of the other routes.

Experimental separation of the two lectin proteases strengthened this mechanistic interpretation. Héja and colleagues in 2012 used selective inhibitors to investigate the respective contributions of MASP-1 and MASP-2. The results supported a central role for MASP-1 in activation of MASP-2 and showed that MASP-1 contributes to C2 processing. The logic is instructive: a selective inhibitor can interrupt a defined step without removing the recognition molecule, so a loss of downstream conversion need not reflect loss of lectin binding. An inhibitor must itself be characterized for specificity before the result can be attributed to the intended protease. The evidence refines rather than replaces the canonical C4b2a pathway model.

6. Alternative pathway: basal C3 hydrolysis and surface-dependent amplification

The alternative pathway challenges the intuitive assumption that immunity must begin with a receptor finding a specific foreign ligand. The central precursor C3 undergoes a low level of spontaneous hydrolysis of its internal thioester in aqueous environments. This phenomenon is often called tick-over. The hydrolyzed form is conventionally written C3(H2O), where the parentheses indicate the hydrolytic modification rather than a separate complement fragment. It is important to distinguish this form from proteolytically generated C3b. Hydrolysis changes the chemical state and structure of C3 without releasing C3a. Although the resulting molecule has certain functional similarities to C3b, it has not been produced by the same cleavage event.

The changed conformation of C3(H2O) permits factor B association under appropriate ionic conditions. Factor D, a protease with a preference for factor B when bound to the relevant complement scaffold, cleaves associated factor B into Ba and Bb. The Bb fragment remains associated with the hydrolyzed C3 scaffold, producing a soluble C3 convertase conventionally denoted C3(H2O)Bb. This convertase can cleave additional intact C3 into C3a and C3b. Because the reactive thioester of the newly generated C3b has not already been consumed by the earlier hydrolysis event, some of that C3b can become attached to nearby surfaces.

The spontaneous hydrolysis route should not be imagined as indiscriminate attack on every membrane. The initial reactions produce opportunities for deposition. Whether those opportunities become an extensive complement response depends strongly on the environment around deposited C3b. If a suitable surface lacks effective protection against alternative pathway activity, deposited C3b can recruit factor B. Factor D then cleaves the recruited factor B, releasing Ba and leaving Bb associated with C3b. The new surface complex C3bBb is the canonical alternative pathway C3 convertase. It is magnesium-dependent and relatively short-lived unless stabilized or continuously replaced.

There are two important distinctions in this sequence. First, factor D does not simply cleave large amounts of freely circulating factor B at random; association with C3b or hydrolyzed C3 helps create the appropriate substrate state. Second, the protease that cleaves C3 in the assembled alternative pathway convertase is Bb, not factor D itself. Factor D performs an upstream processing step that makes Bb available within an active complex. Confusing these two enzymatic roles leads to incorrect predictions about which activity can be restored by supplying an already assembled convertase versus adding purified unprocessed factor B.

Once C3bBb forms, its C3-cleaving activity produces further C3b, and some fraction of that C3b is retained on the same or neighboring surfaces. Each retained molecule can potentially provide another platform for factor B and factor D. This creates a product-dependent amplification loop. The same logic applies if the original C3b was made by C4b2a after classical or lectin pathway initiation. Alternative amplification does not care whether the C3b scaffold originated from a soluble tick-over convertase or an antibody-directed initiating convertase. It requires appropriate deposited C3b and its own associated components. This is why it is useful to distinguish initiation from amplification whenever an experimental result is attributed to a particular pathway.

Properdin, also called factor P, is a positive regulator that stabilizes alternative pathway convertases. By increasing their effective lifetime, it can increase the number of C3 molecules cleaved by an assembled complex. This mechanism is different from increasing the catalytic rate of a particular active site, although either change could increase cumulative product formation. Properdin can also support initiation or recruitment in particular contexts; the general exam-level picture is stabilization of C3bBb. It is not itself the protease responsible for C3 cleavage, and it does not replace factor B or factor D. Removal of properdin may reduce amplification substantially while leaving some ability to form unstable convertases.

Why do many microbial surfaces support the loop more efficiently than intact host cells? A deposited C3b molecule cannot make this distinction by recognizing a genomic marker of species identity. Rather, the local chemistry affects the competition between amplification proteins and regulatory proteins. Factor H can interact with C3b and with host-associated molecular features, including particular sialylated or glycosaminoglycan-containing surfaces. On protected host surfaces this favors inhibition, convertase decay and factor I-mediated processing of C3b. On permissive surfaces the balance can favor factor B binding and new convertase formation. This is a form of discrimination by differential regulation as much as by selective recognition.

The model also predicts exceptions. A pathogen can acquire host regulatory proteins or display surface features that help recruit them. Such a target may be unusually resistant to alternative amplification even though it is foreign. Conversely an injured host cell may lose normal protection or expose patterns that favor complement recognition and deposition. Self versus nonself is therefore not a binary chemical property possessed by C3b itself. It emerges from the combination of target recognition, local surface characteristics, regulatory availability and amplification. This is why complement can participate in both microbial clearance and removal of damaged or dying host material.

At sufficiently high C3b deposition, the alternative pathway produces efficient C5-converting activity, canonically written C3bBbC3b or C3b2Bb. As with the classical C5 convertase shorthand, the notation represents an additional C3b requirement for efficient C5 cleavage. It is the increased local C3b density and the organization of the complex that help switch the system from predominantly C3 processing to effective C5 processing. The actual molecular organization of surface complexes can be more complicated than the simple formula. The exam-level distinction is that C3bBb cleaves C3, whereas C3b-associated additional assembly supports downstream cleavage of C5.

One can now predict the consequences of factor B deficiency. Formation of the normal alternative pathway C3 convertase is impaired. Classical and lectin C3 convertases can still operate if their own components are intact, so some C3b deposition and downstream C5 cleavage may remain. The reduction in total complement response may nevertheless be larger than expected from merely removing one of three starting pathways because factor B-dependent amplification is lost for all sources of deposited C3b. An experiment that measures classical initiation using C4 deposition could therefore show preserved activity, while an experiment measuring final C3 deposition or microbial lysis shows a marked reduction.

The alternative pathway is sometimes summarized as an antibody-independent route. That description is correct regarding its ability to initiate without antigen-specific antibody, but it becomes misleading if interpreted to mean the alternative convertase never participates in a response started by antibodies. Classical activation and alternative amplification can operate on the same antibody-coated target at the same time. The conceptual lesson is to trace a molecule's origin and function rather than assign every event to a single isolated named pathway. A C3b fragment is still C3b after either route, and the same regulatory machinery can influence its fate.

7. From C3 conversion to C5 conversion: understanding the common hub

The central location of C3 is best understood by comparing the outputs of the three activation routes rather than merely their inputs. C1-dependent and lectin-dependent activation both create the C4b2a complex, whereas factor B-dependent activation creates C3bBb. Although these enzyme complexes contain different proteins, both can process intact C3. The resulting C3a and C3b do not retain a label identifying which convertase made them. A receptor that binds C3b on a bacterium cannot generally determine whether it originated from the classical, lectin or alternative route simply by recognizing the fragment. An antibody recognizing a cleavage neoepitope may reveal that C3 was activated, but it does not by itself identify the initiating pathway.

It follows that C3 is much more than a junction in a diagram. It supplies the major opsonin, an inflammatory signal and a substrate product that enables further amplification and helps generate C5-converting activity. Loss of C3 therefore interrupts multiple classes of defense at once. By contrast, loss of a particular initiating recognition molecule removes only one route by which C3 activation normally begins. In a network with several inputs and one shared hub, a central substrate deficiency generally has broader direct consequences than loss of one input, provided the other inputs are genuinely functional.

The transition from C3 cleavage to C5 cleavage is biologically important because it links the degree of surface activation with generation of terminal effector components. C5 resembles C3 in the sense that proteolytic cleavage separates a small soluble fragment from a much larger fragment with a different activity. Unlike C3b and C4b, however, C5b does not serve as the canonical covalently attached thioester-dependent opsonin. Its important downstream role is to bind C6 and initiate assembly of the terminal complex. Cleavage of C5 thus does not simply repeat the entire C3 amplification loop. It creates a different functional branch, one concerned with powerful inflammation and membrane damage.

C5 is processed efficiently when appropriate C5 convertase activity has formed. The classical and lectin C5 convertase is conventionally represented by C4b2a3b; the alternative pathway C5 convertase is represented by C3bBbC3b. The additional C3b is important in substrate recognition and organization of the C5 cleavage environment. A student should not reason that the extra C3b is an extra protease, since C3b has no such catalytic role. Instead, it contributes to the architecture and substrate handling of the conversion machinery. In either case, the protease-containing convertase acts on C5 to generate C5a and C5b.

One useful thought experiment is to compare two surfaces with similar numbers of initial recognition events but different efficiencies of C3b retention. The first has many neighboring sites that retain C3b and lacks strong regulatory activity. The second rapidly inactivates deposited C3b or provides few usable attachment sites. Both can show initial C3 cleavage and release of C3a. However, the first is more likely to support efficient amplification and progression to C5 cleavage. This means that a signal indicating initiation is not necessarily evidence of equivalent terminal pathway activation. The distance between early and late readouts can reveal a regulatory or assembly defect.

The C3 and C5 outputs can also differ temporally. A convertase may form, cleave some C3 and decay before enough C3b accumulates to promote substantial C5 conversion. If the investigator samples the reaction at an early time, they may detect C3 activation products but little evidence of C5b-dependent terminal assembly. A later measurement under sustained activation could show a stronger terminal response. Conversely, terminal complexes deposited on a surface can remain detectable after upstream convertase activity declines. An endpoint assay cannot reconstruct the entire time course. Designing a time series with separate early and late readouts provides a clearer test of the predicted order.

In biological serum, feedback, substrate depletion and regulation complicate the idea of an orderly single-file cascade. A C3 convertase can continue to process C3 while neighboring assemblies process C5. Many copies of each substrate and complex are distributed over a surface. Some fragments are degraded, some bind receptors, and some are incorporated into new assemblies. Diagrams showing a vertical sequence communicate causal dependence rather than requiring every molecule in the sample to complete the same stage before the next stage begins. For examinations, the key is to recognize the branching logic: inhibiting C5 can leave C3-dependent labeling intact, whereas blocking C3 has upstream consequences for both the amplification and terminal branches.

This organization makes complement inhibition an instructive example of network perturbation. A selective block of C5 cleavage prevents production of new C5a and C5b through the inhibited pathway. It does not directly inhibit the protease activity of C4b2a or C3bBb. C3a release and C3b deposition can therefore continue under suitable conditions. A selective block of C3 cleavage has a broader effect on the canonical cascade, because it reduces the generation of the major C3-derived opsonin and makes efficient downstream C5 convertase formation difficult. In an experiment the precise outcome will depend on inhibition completeness and pre-existing deposited fragments, so the comparison is a prediction of the ordinary canonical pathway, not a guarantee that every measurable signal becomes zero.

8. Opsonization, complement receptors and clearance of immune complexes

The word opsonization refers to coating a target in a way that facilitates recognition and uptake by phagocytic cells. It is not synonymous with lysis. A microorganism can remain structurally intact and metabolically viable after becoming coated with C3b, yet become much more susceptible to uptake by a neutrophil or macrophage. The benefit to the host arises because many immune cells express receptors for complement fragments. These receptors provide an additional attachment mechanism between an effector cell and a target that would otherwise interact less efficiently. The term opsonin describes the molecule contributing to that facilitated interaction.

C3b is particularly well suited to this function because its covalent attachment can generate a relatively stable surface label. Complement receptors recognize structural features of C3-derived fragments rather than the microbial antigen that initially triggered activation. The same receptor-bearing phagocyte can therefore interact with many different kinds of complement-coated targets. Receptor specificity is directed toward a shared host-generated label, while recognition of diverse microbial surfaces is handled by upstream complement initiation and other immune recognition mechanisms. This division of labor allows a limited set of cellular receptors to participate in defense against a much larger diversity of pathogens.

The complement receptor CR1, also known as CD35, can recognize C3b and C4b and has important regulatory and immune-complex-handling functions. CR3, also known as Mac-1 or CD11b/CD18, and CR4 are integrin-family complement receptors that can recognize iC3b. The prefix i in iC3b indicates an inactivated form in relation to convertase participation. It does not mean biologically useless. Processing of C3b by factor I in the presence of suitable cofactors produces iC3b, which can no longer serve as the same alternative convertase scaffold but remains an important ligand for phagocytic complement receptors.

The transition from C3b to iC3b demonstrates that regulation and effector activity need not be opposing absolutes. A host regulator can limit further convertase formation without immediately removing every useful recognition label from a target. In fact, changing the fragment can change the receptor repertoire available for subsequent recognition. Further processing of C3 fragments can leave surface-associated C3-derived material such as C3dg and C3d. CR2, also known as CD21, recognizes relevant C3 degradation fragments and participates in a different biological context, including B-cell co-receptor function. Fragment processing is thus a way of changing the functional meaning of a deposited protein over time.

The receptor interaction must also be interpreted within the physiology of a phagocyte. Adhesion of a target to a cell is not the same as complete internalization, and uptake is not the same as killing. A phagocyte must organize its cytoskeleton, enclose the target and process the resulting phagosome. The activation state of the phagocyte and other signals can affect the efficiency of these steps. Some complement receptor-mediated interactions cooperate with antibody Fc receptors and innate receptor signals to produce efficient ingestion. Consequently, measuring surface binding at a low temperature or under conditions that limit internalization cannot establish that the target has been killed. These distinct experimental endpoints should be considered separately.

Complement provides a route for clearance of soluble immune complexes as well as microorganisms. Antibody bound to soluble antigen can form complexes capable of activating complement, depending on size, composition and geometry. Complement deposition can allow these complexes to bind CR1 on human erythrocytes. The erythrocytes transport complexes through the circulation, where specialized phagocytic cells in organs such as the liver and spleen help remove them. The red cell itself is not acting as a classical phagocyte. It is carrying a bound cargo that can be transferred to cells equipped for clearance. This is an instructive example of a nonimmune cell participating in immune homeostasis through surface receptors.

If the early classical pathway is severely defective, normal handling of immune complexes and dying-cell material can be impaired. This helps explain the association between deficiencies in early classical components and certain autoimmune conditions. The association should not be interpreted as a claim that all autoimmune disease results from complement deficiency. Complement may also contribute to tissue injury when activated at sites of inappropriate antibody binding. It can therefore have protective effects on clearance and harmful effects during persistent or misdirected activation. Which aspect dominates depends on the component, location, substrate and regulatory state.

Finally, C3 fragments can help link innate recognition with subsequent B-cell activation. When a B-cell receptor binds an antigen that also carries an appropriate complement fragment, co-engagement of CR2 in the B-cell co-receptor complex can enhance the response to that antigen. The complement fragment does not provide the antigen specificity of the B-cell receptor. It provides an additional signal indicating that the antigen has encountered the complement system. The result is another example of a shared innate chemical label influencing an antigen-specific adaptive response. The detailed development and signaling of B cells belongs to a later node; the complement contribution is understood here as a mechanistic bridge.

9. Anaphylatoxins and the recruitment of cellular defense

The generation of a surface label is useful only if cellular effectors can reach and respond to that surface. Complement provides a mechanism for communicating activation beyond the immediate region of a target. Cleavage of C3 liberates C3a, and cleavage of C5 liberates C5a. Both peptides can participate in inflammatory signaling through receptors on responsive cells. They are conventionally called anaphylatoxins because of their ability to produce selected inflammatory reactions that resemble aspects of anaphylaxis. The terminology does not mean that every release of these fragments causes systemic anaphylaxis, nor that the fragments are antibodies or allergens.

C3a and C5a act in part through specific G-protein-coupled receptors. C3aR recognizes C3a, whereas the canonical signaling receptor for C5a is C5aR1, also known as CD88. Another receptor, C5aR2, participates in modulation in context-dependent ways. It is unnecessary to characterize its full signaling repertoire for the present syllabus. The central concept is that a proteolytic event outside a cell creates a ligand that can bind a cell-surface receptor and initiate intracellular responses. This provides a direct bridge between the complement cascade and the general principles of receptor-mediated signaling studied earlier in Unit 4.

In appropriate cell types, these signals contribute to changes such as activation, secretion of inflammatory mediators and directed movement. C5a is particularly important as a chemotactic and activating signal for neutrophils and several other leukocyte populations. Chemotaxis requires a spatial difference in the effective signal, not simply a large uniform concentration. A cell moving toward a source must integrate receptor occupancy at different regions of its surface over time and organize its movement accordingly. If a signal becomes nearly uniform or saturates the relevant receptors, increasing total ligand concentration does not necessarily improve directional information. Receptor signaling and migration are thus related but experimentally distinguishable phenomena.

Inflammation also alters the vascular environment. Complement-derived signals can promote mediator release from responsive cells, including mast cells in appropriate settings. The resulting local responses may contribute to vascular permeability, leukocyte recruitment and changes in the surrounding tissue. Increased permeability allows soluble plasma proteins to reach tissue in larger amounts, while recruitment supplies additional cells capable of acting on complement-coated targets. This creates a coordinated relationship between molecular and cellular immunity. C5a is not the sole agent controlling this process; chemokines, cytokines, lipid mediators and endothelial adhesion mechanisms contribute alongside complement.

A conceptual example makes the distinction clear. In a wound model with active complement but impaired neutrophil adhesion to endothelium, C5a may still be generated and detected in the tissue. Nevertheless, large numbers of circulating neutrophils may fail to reach the wound because a separate step in the recruitment process is defective. In a different model with functional adhesion molecules but impaired C5a signaling, recruitment can be reduced or altered, yet other chemotactic signals may still operate. Neither result would justify the statement that complement-derived chemotactic peptides are identical to adhesion molecules. A successful inflammatory response depends on cooperation among distinct mechanisms.

The activity of anaphylatoxins is also regulated after their release. Plasma carboxypeptidases can remove the terminal arginine of C3a and C5a to generate des-Arg derivatives with altered receptor activity. C3a-desArg has markedly reduced activity at the canonical C3a receptor, whereas C5a-desArg retains some biological activity depending on the receptor and context. This processing helps restrict inflammatory activity but is not equivalent to instantaneous disappearance of every biological effect. A measurement of peptide-related immunoreactivity must therefore distinguish, where relevant, the active parent peptide from its processed forms. The distinction is particularly useful when comparing an immunochemical measurement with a functional migration assay.

The inflammatory branch explains why more complement activation is not always better. At a localized infection, generation of inflammatory fragments can help concentrate effector cells and promote clearance. When activation is excessive, persistent or poorly restricted, these same signals can amplify tissue inflammation and contribute to collateral injury. The effect of C5a can also depend on dose and timing: prolonged intense receptor exposure may alter responsiveness rather than continuously increasing a cell's useful directed activity. The appropriate biological goal is not maximal production of every fragment but a response proportionate to the challenge and compatible with maintenance of tissue function.

A receptor-blocking experiment provides an instructive way to separate the generation of a ligand from its action. Suppose a selective antagonist blocks C5aR1 in a leukocyte migration assay but leaves serum complement reactions intact. C5a can still be produced by the complement convertase, and C5b can still participate in terminal assembly. The leukocyte response attributable to the blocked receptor is reduced, but this does not demonstrate a loss of C5 cleavage. In contrast, a selective intervention that prevents C5 cleavage reduces production of both C5a and C5b. The two interventions may produce similar changes in one inflammation readout while producing different changes in membrane lysis. This pattern is a powerful way to reason from a perturbation to the position of its target within a branching pathway.

10. The terminal pathway: assembling a membrane attack complex

The terminal complement pathway takes the extracellular cascade in a new direction. It no longer depends primarily on production of a succession of proteolytic enzymes. Instead it depends on the ordered association of proteins that create a membrane-active assembly. The precursor C5b generated by cleavage of C5 has a transient capacity to bind C6. Association with C6 stabilizes a state from which later terminal components can assemble. If C5b fails to engage the appropriate downstream component, its productive membrane attack potential can be lost. Temporal coordination is therefore important even after the upstream convertase has successfully processed C5.

The C5b6 complex can bind C7, producing C5b-7. Association with C7 exposes or creates properties that promote membrane association. Recruitment of C8 follows, with membrane engagement and insertion contributing to the developing lesion. C9 molecules can then assemble into the growing membrane attack complex, commonly abbreviated MAC and written C5b-9. This sequence is a protein assembly process rather than a series in which C6 cleaves C7, C7 cleaves C8 and C8 cleaves C9. The terminal proteins share structural principles with other membrane attack and pore-forming proteins. In the completed assembly, conformational changes allow regions that were compatible with the aqueous environment to participate in a membrane-inserted structure.

A lipid bilayer is an effective barrier because its hydrophobic interior restricts the passage of many charged or polar solutes. Cells spend considerable energy establishing ionic gradients across such barriers. A protein-lined membrane lesion undermines that organization by allowing inappropriate movement of solutes and water. The precise physical consequences depend on the target. A susceptible erythrocyte can lose its normal osmotic control and release hemoglobin. A susceptible bacterium may suffer membrane damage and disruption of essential physiological processes. It is misleading to state that the MAC always destroys its target by one universal pattern of water entry, since different cells possess different envelope structures, repair capacities and responses to membrane injury.

Not all microorganisms are equally vulnerable to direct complement-mediated lysis. Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane that can be susceptible to terminal complement attack, and members of the genus Neisseria provide important examples of the protective significance of terminal components. Nevertheless, Gram-negative species and strains differ greatly in susceptibility. Surface polysaccharides, the structure and length of lipopolysaccharide, recruitment of regulatory proteins and the organization of the cell envelope can influence whether terminal complexes reach and injure relevant membranes. Even the presence of detectable C5b-9 on a target surface does not always prove successful bactericidal membrane insertion.

Gram-positive bacteria possess a relatively thick peptidoglycan layer external to their cytoplasmic membrane. This architecture generally makes direct killing by the MAC less effective than against susceptible Gram-negative bacteria, because the terminal assembly must interact productively with an accessible vulnerable membrane. Complement can still be extremely useful against Gram-positive organisms through C3-derived opsonization and inflammation. Thus a comparison of organisms based only on direct lysis would underestimate the significance of complement for targets that depend mainly on phagocytic clearance. The biological success of the system must be assessed through the combined effector outputs.

Viruses and parasites present still other surface arrangements. Some enveloped viruses can be affected by complement-mediated neutralization or membrane damage, but the outcome depends on the viral envelope, antibodies and complement regulators, including host-derived proteins that may be incorporated into the envelope. Nonenveloped viruses do not possess the same lipid membrane target for MAC insertion. Complement can still influence their recognition and clearance through mechanisms other than direct membrane attack. The purpose of this comparison is not to develop the full immune response to every pathogen class, which is assigned to later nodes, but to illustrate why the chemical identity of a target membrane constrains a particular effector mechanism.

The MAC also needs to be distinguished from the pore-forming mechanism used by cytotoxic lymphocytes. NK cells and cytotoxic T cells can deploy perforin to promote delivery of granzymes into target cells. Complement C9 and perforin share broad structural relationships within membrane attack complex/perforin-domain proteins, but their physiological contexts and assembly mechanisms differ. The complement terminal complex is assembled extracellularly following cleavage of C5 and recruitment of C6 through C9. Perforin is released from cytotoxic cell granules as part of a cellular killing mechanism. C9 is not a granzyme delivery protein in the standard complement pathway, and NK-cell killing does not require that C5 convertase be generated first.

Terminal assembly on nucleated host cells does not inevitably produce immediate cell death. A limited number of lesions may be removed, shed or repaired, and sublytic terminal complement exposure can alter cellular signaling and function without causing outright lysis. Erythrocytes are useful experimental targets because lysis is easy to quantify, but their behavior cannot be transferred without qualification to every nucleated cell. A terminal component deposition assay, a membrane permeability assay and a cell survival assay therefore provide complementary rather than identical information. The interpretation must specify which endpoint was measured and whether the number and location of complexes were sufficient to produce the biological effect of interest.

A terminal deficiency can be understood by following what happens upstream of the missing assembly partner. If C6 is absent, C5 may still be cleaved normally, releasing C5a and producing C5b. C3-derived opsonization can also remain intact. The inability to stabilize the C5b6 complex, however, strongly impairs canonical terminal assembly. A missing C9 affects a later stage; complexes containing C5b-8 can themselves have some membrane-damaging activity, so lack of C9 is not identical in every experimental context to absence of all terminal membrane effects. The standard examination conclusion is that terminal deficiencies impair effective MAC-mediated killing, with especially important susceptibility to meningococcal and other Neisseria infections. It is not correct to infer a total loss of every complement function.

There is also a soluble fate for terminal components. Regulatory molecules such as vitronectin and clusterin can interact with terminal intermediates and help prevent inappropriate membrane insertion, producing soluble terminal complex forms. A blood measurement of soluble C5b-9 can consequently indicate that terminal pathway activation has occurred, without proving that pores formed on a particular cell population. If the scientific question concerns damage to a specific membrane, the location of deposition and a functional membrane endpoint must be examined. This is another example of the difference between a marker of pathway activity and proof of a particular biological consequence.

Structural evidence provides an independent perspective on terminal assembly. Serna and colleagues reported an electron cryomicroscopy structure of human MAC in 2016. The structural organization revealed how the nonidentical terminal components contribute to an asymmetric assembly containing repeated C9 subunits. A structural observation of a completed complex establishes physical relationships within that complex, while biochemical reconstitution and functional membrane assays establish the order and consequences of its formation. Together these approaches support the distinction between proteolytic initiation at C5 and subsequent assembly of a membrane-active protein complex.

11. Protecting the host: control of initiation, amplification and membrane attack

A system that operates within blood and tissue fluids must protect the very surfaces that supply its proteins. Host protection cannot be explained by assuming that activation never occurs near a normal host cell. Basal C3 hydrolysis and occasional deposition can occur without infection, and immune recognition events may take place close to host structures. Protection instead depends on regulation at several stages. By restricting formation, duration and consequences of active complexes, host tissues lower the chance that an isolated deposition event develops into destructive amplification. The organization resembles several independent safety barriers rather than one universal switch that makes all complement proteins inactive.

C1 inhibitor, abbreviated C1-INH, regulates early activation by inhibiting active C1r and C1s and relevant lectin-pathway proteases. It belongs to the serpin family of protease inhibitors. Its function is particularly important because unrestricted early protease activity could allow inappropriate C4 and C2 consumption. A serpin can trap an active protease through a mechanism involving cleavage of an exposed reactive region and formation of a stable inhibitory complex. At the level of the complement pathway, the consequence is interruption of upstream proteolytic activity before additional convertases can accumulate. This differs mechanistically from an inhibitor that removes deposited C3b after it has already been generated.

C1-INH also regulates proteases in other plasma systems, including the contact system involved in production of bradykinin. This overlap matters when interpreting hereditary angioedema associated with C1-INH deficiency or dysfunction. The swelling characteristic of that disorder is primarily bradykinin-mediated rather than simply the direct consequence of excessive C3a or C5a release or mast-cell histamine. Low C4 can accompany complement consumption, but that marker does not establish that the clinical swelling is caused by C4 cleavage products. The example is valuable because one regulatory protein participates in multiple networks; a biochemical abnormality in one network is not automatically the direct cause of every physiological manifestation.

The central regulatory challenge is control of C3b-dependent amplification. Factor H is a soluble regulator that can bind C3b and interact with particular host-associated surface features. Its positioning helps compete with factor B for productive association, promotes decay of alternative convertase complexes and provides cofactor activity for factor I. These effects are complementary rather than redundant. Preventing factor B recruitment reduces formation of new convertases. Accelerating dissociation shortens the activity of those already present. Promoting cleavage of C3b changes the chemical and functional state of the scaffold, reducing the capacity of the same deposited material to rebuild an active alternative convertase.

Factor I is a soluble protease that acts on appropriate complement substrates in the presence of regulatory cofactors. It can process C3b and C4b with the help of suitable partners. It is important to separate a cofactor from the enzyme performing cleavage. Factor H can supply cofactor activity for C3b processing, but factor I is the protease carrying out the relevant cleavage. A deficiency of factor I can therefore impair regulation even when factor H is present. The resulting excessive activation and consumption may lower circulating C3. This is an instructive departure from the assumption that deletion of a complement-related protein always reduces activation directly. Deletion of a negative regulator can increase pathway use and secondarily deplete a central precursor.

There are other regulators relevant to the classical and lectin convertase. C4-binding protein can bind C4b and contribute to regulation of the C4b-containing complex, including cofactor activity for factor I. Membrane cofactor protein, CD46, is expressed on many nucleated host cells and provides cofactor activity for regulatory processing of C3b and C4b. Decay-accelerating factor, CD55, promotes dissociation of convertase components rather than performing the cleavage carried out by factor I. CR1 can participate in both decay acceleration and cofactor functions. The regulatory proteins are not interchangeable simply because they reduce complement activity; each has particular substrates, molecular roles and distributions.

The presence of membrane-associated regulators localizes protection to a cell surface. For example, a regulator anchored at the plasma membrane can act on convertases assembled nearby. An experimentally prepared cell deficient in CD55 may sustain convertase activity for longer than an otherwise comparable cell, whereas a cell deficient in CD46 may show impaired cofactor-dependent processing of deposited fragments. If an experiment measures only the final amount of membrane lysis, both defects might appear to increase susceptibility. Separate measurements of convertase decay and fragment composition would help distinguish the underlying mechanisms. In real cells, overlapping regulators mean that a single loss may have a smaller effect than expected from a diagram in which every function is represented by exactly one protein.

CD59 acts at a later stage. It limits productive MAC formation by interfering with incorporation and polymerization of C9 at the membrane attack complex. The location of this control is especially important: it can protect a host membrane from terminal injury even if complement fragments have already been deposited upstream. Unlike CD55, CD59 does not primarily accelerate decay of the C3 convertase. Unlike CD46, it does not serve as the canonical factor I cofactor that generates iC3b. A selective loss of CD59 is therefore predicted to increase susceptibility to terminal membrane injury more directly than it increases initial C4 or C3 deposition. The distinction is one of the most useful ways to classify complement regulatory proteins in an examination.

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria provides a biological illustration of the significance of membrane protection. Acquired defects affecting synthesis of the glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor in a hematopoietic cell clone can lead to loss of GPI-anchored surface proteins, including CD55 and CD59, on affected blood cells. Such erythrocytes become unusually susceptible to complement-mediated injury. The cause is not the appearance of a new microbial antigen on every affected cell. The key mechanistic point is a defect in host protection against complement that would ordinarily be restrained. This disease example is included to clarify regulatory functions, not as a guide to clinical diagnosis or management.

Factor H-associated discrimination likewise illustrates why a normal complement concentration cannot by itself prove effective host protection. A regulator may be present in the fluid at a normal concentration yet have altered interaction with certain surfaces. If its ability to bind host-associated features is impaired while some fluid-phase functions are retained, control at specific tissues can be disproportionately affected. Conversely, a regulator that circulates at a very low concentration may allow inappropriate fluid-phase consumption before proteins reach a target. The site of regulation therefore matters as much as the amount of regulator measured in a sample. Different mutations in the same gene can produce different functional patterns.

Complement protection can be exploited by pathogens. A microorganism that recruits factor H to its surface can potentially reduce the formation or persistence of alternative pathway convertases in its immediate environment. A pathogen that masks relevant recognition patterns may reduce initiation instead. A third may produce molecules that interfere with the terminal pathway or prevent deposition from translating into effective killing. These strategies have different predictions for experimental readouts. Reduced C4 deposition suggests a possible change in classical or lectin initiation, whereas preserved C4 deposition with reduced C3 amplification suggests a defect further downstream. Preserved C3 deposition with impaired lysis may indicate resistance at the terminal stage or a physical barrier to effective membrane insertion.

Regulation thus permits multiple outcomes from an apparently similar activation event. One surface can remain a strong opsonized target after conversion of C3b to iC3b while no longer supporting extensive alternative amplification. Another surface can accumulate early fragments without forming many MAC lesions because CD59 provides terminal protection. A third can prevent much of the activation before the C3 convertase forms. These possibilities explain why the term complement inhibition is incomplete unless the target and stage of inhibition are specified. A student who can place each regulator on the appropriate part of the pathway can predict not only which signal will decrease but also which beneficial or harmful outputs may remain.

12. Predicting component deficiencies and biological outcomes

A loss of complement function is most informative when it is located precisely within the cascade. The question is not simply whether the affected person or experimental preparation has reduced immunity. It is whether the missing activity belongs to a recognition complex, a shared substrate, an amplification module, a soluble signaling branch, a terminal assembly step or a negative regulator. Each of these positions yields a different pattern of preserved and impaired outputs. The same logic applies when interpreting genetically altered animals, serum deficient in a particular protein or selective inhibitors in a cell-free experiment.

Defects involving early classical components such as C1q, C2 and C4 can reduce antibody-dependent classical activity. A deficiency of C4 or C2 also compromises the canonical lectin route, since the two pathways use the same C3 convertase. Alternative activation can still supply C3b in an appropriate setting. However, preserved alternative activity does not necessarily compensate for all loss of classical-pathway functions. The geometry and composition of some immune complexes make the early classical route particularly useful for their processing. Impaired clearance of immune complexes and apoptotic material is associated with autoimmunity, especially with severe deficiencies of certain early classical components. This association has a mechanistic explanation without implying that the presence of an early deficiency is sufficient to explain every instance of autoimmune disease.

Loss of C3 is especially consequential because C3-derived products contribute to surface labeling, inflammatory signaling and formation of efficient C5 convertases. A preparation without functional C3 may retain the ability to recognize ligands through C1q or lectins and may still cleave C4 and C2, but it lacks the major substrate necessary for ordinary downstream amplification. It therefore has severe impairment of complement-mediated opsonization and canonical progression to terminal attack. In people with profound C3 deficiency, susceptibility to severe or recurrent bacterial infection is a major concern. This is not because C3 is the only immune protein, but because it lies at the intersection of several complementary defenses.

Terminal component deficiency yields a different pattern. A C7-deficient preparation may contain functional classical, lectin and alternative C3 convertases and may generate abundant C3b, C3a and C5a. Its inability to assemble a normal membrane attack complex can nonetheless markedly impair killing of terminal-pathway-sensitive targets. Neisseria susceptibility is particularly associated with inherited defects in the terminal complement pathway. Thus a bacterium can be recognized, coated and exposed to recruited immune cells without the full benefit of direct complement-mediated membrane attack. The preservation of one effector branch does not guarantee the preservation of the others.

An alternative pathway defect can also be detected despite apparently normal early classical activity. Factor D or factor B deficiency impairs formation of the C3bBb convertase. Properdin deficiency reduces its stabilization and affects amplification. The extent of impairment depends on the particular deficiency and target, and properdin deficiency can also be associated with susceptibility to meningococcal infection. These examples demonstrate that the association between Neisseria infection and complement failure is not exclusive to a defect in the final C5b-9 components. Alternative pathway amplification can be important for generating enough terminal activity against a susceptible target.

In contrast, a deficiency of a negative regulator can initially increase rather than decrease complement activity. Loss of factor I-mediated processing may permit continued use of C3b as an amplification scaffold, leading to consumption of C3. If a blood sample is examined after prolonged activation, the measured precursor concentration may be low. A superficial interpretation might conclude that the primary mutation disables C3 synthesis. More complete reasoning considers a mass balance: the amount of circulating intact C3 depends on production, ordinary turnover and consumption through activation. A high rate of consumption can produce the same low steady-state concentration as a low production rate. Measurement of activation fragments, regulatory proteins and functional reconstitution can help distinguish the mechanisms.

The distinction between inherited deficiency and acquired consumption is also important for experimental interpretation. A sample that is initially normal can lose functional activity if complement becomes activated during collection or inappropriate storage. A low functional readout may therefore have a preanalytical explanation rather than reflect the patient's physiological state. A functional assay should include a known active control and suitable sample handling. If testing a biological hypothesis, the investigator should distinguish a reduction caused by an experimental mutation from one caused by exposure to conditions that destabilize complement proteins.

One must also distinguish a receptor deficiency from a deficiency in the generation of a receptor ligand. A phagocyte deficient in a major iC3b-binding receptor may ingest a complement-coated particle less efficiently, even though complement activation in the surrounding fluid is normal. A defect in C5a signaling may alter migration without directly preventing MAC assembly. These examples are valuable because they show that the complement system extends beyond soluble precursors and convertases: its effects also depend on the responsiveness of the target and effector cells. An assay of serum function and an assay of cellular responsiveness answer different questions.

13. Quantitative reasoning: amplification, decay, occupancy and consumption

The complement cascade can be understood without elaborate mathematical models, but simple quantitative relationships sharpen several important distinctions. A model should represent an explicitly stated biological question. If the question concerns convertase lifetime, the relevant quantity is the amount of catalytically competent complex remaining over time. If it concerns C3b deposition, the quantity is surface-associated material after accounting for production and loss. If it concerns an inflammatory receptor, ligand concentration and receptor occupancy may matter. It is not appropriate to use the same equation for all three processes simply because they occur in the same pathway.

Begin with a convertase preparation that has been assembled on a surface and then washed to prevent formation of new complexes. Suppose the loss of active convertase follows a first-order approximation. Let E(t) denote the concentration of active convertase at time t, E0 the initial concentration and k the effective decay constant, measured in inverse minutes. In a short interval dt, the expected change in active concentration is proportional to the amount still present: dE/dt = −kE. Separation of variables gives dE/E = −k dt. Integrating from the starting time to t yields ln[E(t)/E0] = −kt, so E(t) = E0 exp(−kt). This expression assumes a single effective decay rate, constant experimental conditions and no replenishment of active complexes.

The half-life is the interval required for E(t) to reach half of E0. Setting E(t)/E0 to one half yields t1/2 = ln(2)/k. Suppose an unstabilized convertase preparation has a modeled half-life of two minutes and a properdin-stabilized preparation has a modeled half-life of six minutes. At six minutes the first retains one eighth of its initial activity, while the second retains one half. The fourfold difference in remaining activity does not imply that the stabilized complex possesses a fourfold higher instantaneous catalytic rate. It arises solely from a difference in decay. These illustrative lifetimes are hypothetical teaching inputs, not reported measurements of any particular purified complement system.

Suppose that substrate is abundant and each unit of active convertase cleaves C3 at a constant proportional rate. The rate of product generation is v(t) = rE(t), where r has units of substrate converted per unit active enzyme per minute. The cumulative product after time T is the integral of v over the interval: P(T) = rE0[1 − exp(−kT)]/k. In the idealized limit of very long time, P approaches rE0/k. If the two preparations have the same initial active enzyme concentration and the same catalytic rate per active complex, a threefold increase in half-life implies a threefold increase in this limiting product yield. This is one way to understand how stabilization by properdin can enhance the amount of C3 processed without changing the chemistry of each individual catalytic event.

The approximation fails if substrate concentration falls enough to limit reaction velocity or if additional convertases are continuously generated. In a physiological alternative pathway response, new C3b can supply additional platforms for convertase assembly, so the number of active complexes is not simply declining from a fixed starting value. Conversely, the concentration of available factor B may become limiting, or factor H and factor I may rapidly eliminate productive scaffolds. A model derived from a washed, fixed-complex experiment should not be used without qualification to predict the complete plasma response. The difference is not a defect of mathematics; it is a difference between the assumptions of the model and the biological system.

A second instructive model tracks surface amplification through discrete generations. Suppose one active convertase cleaves an average of m substrate molecules during its effective lifetime. Let f be the fraction of newly generated C3b that attaches to the relevant surface and g the fraction of attached molecules that form a productive next-generation convertase. If these fractions remain approximately constant, the expected number of productive successor complexes per starting complex is R = mfg. This dimensionless quantity describes the amplification potential in a simplified branching model. It is not a standard clinical complement index, and it should not be mistaken for an empirically universal constant.

Consider m = 40, f = 0.20 and g = 0.25. The model yields R = 2. Starting from 10 active convertases, the expected next generation contains 20, followed by 40 in the subsequent generation if conditions remain constant. Now consider a protected host surface where regulation lowers g to 0.05 while the other inputs are unchanged. R becomes 0.4, so 10 starting complexes produce an expected four successors and then 1.6 successors. The fractional number is an expected value over repeated realizations, not a physically fractional enzyme molecule. The contrast illustrates why differences in the fate of deposited C3b can decide whether an initial activation event grows or subsides.

The branching model also helps distinguish an effect of substrate processing from an effect of surface attachment. A mutation that changes the reactivity of the C3 thioester could reduce f without directly altering the catalytic activity of an upstream convertase. A regulator that competes for surface-bound C3b could reduce g without changing the probability that newly generated C3b initially becomes attached. Both changes reduce R, but they predict different amounts of C3 cleavage products and different surface fragment patterns. A measurement of final amplification alone is insufficient to distinguish them. Measuring fluid-phase activation products and initial surface deposition provides additional information.

A third model considers binding of a soluble complement peptide to its receptor. Under a simple equilibrium model with one class of independent binding sites, fractional occupancy θ is given by L/(Kd + L), where L is the free ligand concentration and Kd is the equilibrium dissociation constant in the same concentration units. This expression follows from Kd = [R][L]/[RL] and total receptor concentration [R] + [RL]. At L = Kd, half the available receptors are occupied. At L = 9Kd, occupancy is 0.90. A ninefold rise in ligand from the half-occupancy concentration therefore raises occupancy from 0.50 to 0.90, not to 4.5 times the available receptors. Receptor number imposes a finite limit.

The occupancy relationship is useful for considering chemotaxis, although equilibrium occupancy is not itself a model of cell movement. If ligand concentration is close to Kd, differences in concentration across a cell can produce substantial differences in occupancy. At very high ligand concentrations, occupancy approaches saturation on both sides of the cell, and the same absolute concentration difference may produce a smaller occupancy contrast. Actual migrating cells use more sophisticated dynamic signaling and adaptation, but the simple model explains why an unlimited increase in concentration cannot be assumed to improve directional guidance indefinitely. Receptor internalization and changes in cell state may further alter the response.

Finally, consider the concentration of intact C3 in plasma. Let S be its production rate per unit volume, C the concentration and k the combined effective first-order removal constant for baseline turnover and activation-associated consumption. Under constant volume and steady conditions, dC/dt = S − kC. At steady state dC/dt equals zero, giving Css = S/k. If production is 8 arbitrary concentration units per day and removal has a rate constant of 0.2 per day, the steady concentration is 40 units. If removal doubles to 0.4 per day without a change in production, the concentration falls to 20 units. Identical low concentrations could result from reduced production at the original removal rate. The result makes explicit why a concentration measurement cannot identify a mechanism without additional evidence.

All four models describe different aspects of the same network. Exponential decay describes loss of preassembled activity. Integrated catalytic yield describes total product generated under fixed substrate conditions. Discrete branching describes the chance of producing successor complexes. Receptor occupancy describes a binding equilibrium, while mass balance describes precursor abundance. These models are intentionally simplified. Their biological value is in making assumptions visible and generating testable predictions. When a question presents an unfamiliar graph, identifying the quantity on each axis and the experimental conditions should precede any calculation.

14. Functional experiments and interpretation of complement assays

A functional complement assay is designed around an endpoint. One historically important endpoint is hemolysis of erythrocytes. In a classical-pathway hemolytic assay, antibody-sensitized erythrocytes are exposed to a serum preparation under conditions supporting classical activation. Complement-dependent membrane injury releases hemoglobin into the surrounding solution. By comparing the signal with controls for spontaneous release and complete lysis, the investigator can estimate the fraction lysed. The classical total hemolytic complement measurement, commonly called CH50, reflects the functional capacity of the classical-to-terminal pathway under standardized assay conditions. It is not a direct concentration measurement of C3 or any other individual component.

The precise laboratory reporting units of CH50 depend on assay conventions. In the traditional dilution-based interpretation, activity is related to the amount of serum required to achieve 50 percent hemolysis in a specified system. A serum capable of achieving that endpoint at a greater dilution has more hemolytic activity under the same conditions. Modern assays may use other substrates and calibrated reporting systems. It is therefore inappropriate to compare numerical units from different methods without checking how the assay defines them. The scientific principle remains that an endpoint requiring the integrated action of multiple components is sensitive to defects at several possible stages.

An alternative pathway functional assay, often designated AH50 or AP50, can use an appropriate susceptible erythrocyte target under conditions that restrict calcium-dependent classical and lectin activation while supporting magnesium-dependent alternative activity. Magnesium with the calcium chelator EGTA is a familiar conceptual example. An assay using EDTA to bind both calcium and magnesium would instead broadly suppress the ion-dependent canonical pathways. The choice of ions, target species, incubation conditions and controls determines which interpretation is justified. Simply describing a reaction as antibody-free is insufficient to prove that the alternative pathway was the only pathway active.

The combination of classical and alternative functional assays provides a useful localization strategy. If classical activity is severely impaired but alternative activity is preserved, an early component uniquely required for classical activity or shared by classical and lectin activation becomes a plausible candidate. If alternative activity is severely impaired with relatively preserved classical activity, a defect in an alternative-specific component becomes plausible. If both are impaired, a common component such as C3 or a terminal component may be involved, but other explanations include multiple component deficiencies, consumption and sample degradation. These patterns guide investigation; they do not uniquely identify a molecular defect in every biological sample.

Consider the following teaching example. A serum sample supports substantial antibody-dependent C4 deposition on an experimental surface. It also generates near-normal levels of deposited C3 fragments, but hemolysis is very low. Addition of purified C6 restores hemolysis under the same assay conditions, whereas addition of C4 does not. The combination of normal upstream readouts and selective rescue supports a functional defect at the C6-dependent stage of terminal assembly. The rescue does not prove that the person's C6 gene contains a particular mutation. A protein may be absent, dysfunctional or otherwise unavailable. Identifying the underlying cause would require additional biochemical or genetic evidence.

A different hypothetical sample has little classical hemolysis and substantial alternative hemolysis. Addition of purified C2 restores classical hemolysis. Measuring C4 deposition in the original sample could reveal whether the reaction reached the stage immediately preceding C2 recruitment and cleavage. If C4 deposition was preserved, the result would support a defect after C4 cleavage rather than a failure of C1-mediated recognition. This combination of a functional endpoint, an intermediate marker and selective rescue offers stronger localization than any one observation alone. Appropriate controls should establish that the added component is active, that its vehicle is not responsible for the change and that the assay's other components remain functional.

Complement deposition assays address a different question from hemolytic assays. A target may be incubated with serum and then examined with reagents that recognize deposited C3-derived fragments. Detection by a fluorescent label or an enzyme-linked signal can reveal differences in target coating. The specificity of the detecting reagent matters: a reagent recognizing a shared epitope on several C3-derived fragments does not automatically distinguish active C3b from iC3b or C3d. To investigate a proposed defect in regulatory cleavage, reagents or biochemical methods that distinguish fragment states are needed. In contrast, a total C3 deposition measurement may be adequate for a narrower question about whether the target acquired complement-derived material at all.

A phagocytosis experiment should separately test binding and internalization. For example, fluorescently labeled particles may be exposed to complement-active serum or heat-treated serum and then presented to phagocytes under comparable conditions. An increase in cell-associated fluorescence in the active-serum group suggests that serum activity promotes association, but it does not alone prove uptake. An extracellular fluorescence-quenching approach or an imaging method that resolves intracellular localization can strengthen the inference. If blocking an iC3b-binding receptor reduces internalization without reducing prior particle opsonization, the result supports a cellular receptor-dependent uptake step. Measuring viability of ingested microorganisms is a further separate endpoint.

Migration experiments likewise require a defined endpoint and controls. An investigator studying C5a-dependent recruitment can compare migration toward a concentration gradient with migration in an arrangement where the same ligand concentration is present uniformly. Greater directional migration in the gradient condition supports chemotaxis rather than a mere rise in random motility. Receptor blockade can test whether the response depends on the intended receptor. A viability control is needed because a decline in recovered cells could otherwise be caused by cell death. A positive migration control using an independent chemoattractant can help distinguish a selective defect in complement responsiveness from a general defect in cell movement.

Finally, terminal complex detection should be interpreted alongside membrane function. Antibodies that recognize terminal complex-related epitopes can reveal assembly or deposition, but a damaged membrane may be repaired or the complex may have formed in a soluble state. Measuring permeability, hemoglobin release or survival addresses downstream consequences. An experiment comparing a normal cell and a CD59-deficient cell should ideally control upstream deposition, because a larger difference in terminal injury could otherwise reflect a difference in initiation rather than terminal regulation. The more precisely the readouts correspond to different positions in the pathway, the more confidently an investigator can infer where the perturbation acts.

These experimental strategies return to the original historical distinction between recognition and lysis. They show that neither a single serum concentration nor a single terminal assay is a complete description of complement biology. The strongest examination answers identify the hypothesis, select an endpoint appropriate to it, include controls that exclude plausible alternatives, and state the conclusion no more broadly than the evidence permits.

15. Evolution, physiological integration and the limits of the textbook pathway

Complement illustrates an evolutionary strategy in which a small repertoire of recognition and effector proteins is used to generate extensive defense against diverse targets. Complement-like thioester-containing proteins and other related components are found across animal lineages, including lineages that lack the full vertebrate antibody system. It is important to distinguish a conserved molecular strategy from a claim that every animal possesses the complete mammalian set of classical, lectin, alternative and terminal components. The mammalian textbook scheme is a particular elaboration of older innate defense principles. Different animals use related or alternative molecules to recognize surfaces and promote clearance, and their pathways should not be assumed identical merely because similar names are used.

Plants also use innate recognition systems and have mechanisms for rapid defense amplification, but they do not operate the vertebrate antibody-complement cascade described here. Bacteria are targets of complement in animal hosts and have their own systems for environmental sensing and defense, not the mammalian complement system. This comparison matters because shared principles such as recognition, amplification and regulation can evolve or be implemented through very different molecular machinery. Functional analogy is not evidence of identical protein ancestry or pathway architecture. The comparison remains within the syllabus by illuminating the specificity and limits of the animal immune mechanism.

In the intact mammal, complement participates in an integrated defense rather than acting as an autonomous killing fluid. A tissue macrophage can detect microbial patterns and produce inflammatory mediators. Local complement activation can increase surface coating and generate signals that recruit additional leukocytes. Those leukocytes must adhere to endothelium, migrate into tissue and use phagocytic or antimicrobial mechanisms. Antigen-presenting cells can carry information to lymphoid tissues, while antibodies generated later can direct classical activation toward particular antigens. At each stage the complement system supplies a distinct contribution but depends on cooperation with other processes for the overall outcome.

The relationship between complement and antibody is especially important for the next chapter. A bound antibody has two logically separable roles: its binding region selects an antigen, and its constant regions can recruit selected effector mechanisms. Complement is one such mechanism. Not every antibody that binds an antigen activates complement efficiently, and complement can act without antibodies. Thus antigen binding should not be equated with complement fixation, and complement activation should not be equated with antigen-specific adaptive recognition. These distinctions will make the study of antibody isotypes, antigen–antibody binding and biological function more intelligible.

Complement also contributes to the physiological handling of cell debris and immune complexes. Normal turnover continually creates cellular material that must be recognized and removed without initiating uncontrolled inflammation. When recognition and regulated clearance work efficiently, complement fragments can facilitate disposal. If activation persists at vulnerable tissue surfaces, the same system may contribute to inflammation and injury. A useful way to reconcile these outcomes is to ask whether complement activity is spatially restricted, whether regulators are functional, whether the target is cleared and whether the generated effectors are proportionate to the biological challenge.

Selective complement inhibition provides an application of mechanistic knowledge. In biomedical research, blocking a particular component or its receptor allows upstream and downstream functions to be separated experimentally. Therapeutic examples include inhibitors directed against C5 in disorders where terminal complement injury is an important mechanism. Their logic is to reduce terminal activation while preserving more upstream C3-dependent functions than a direct C3 block would preserve. This is a mechanistic comparison, not a clinical recommendation. Complement inhibition has trade-offs because the blocked components normally contribute to antimicrobial defense. The biological lesson is that selecting a molecular target determines both the intended effect and the functions that may be compromised.

The canonical pathway diagrams are indispensable but have limitations. They depict the major sequence of cleavage and assembly, not the complete spatial distribution of molecular complexes in blood and tissues. They often show one copy of a component where many copies and multiple binding arrangements exist. They do not capture all noncanonical proteases or cross-talk with other plasma systems, and they may omit local production or cell-specific differences in receptor expression. These omissions are appropriate when the purpose is to teach the core pathway, provided the simplified diagram is not mistaken for an exhaustive description of every biological setting. For NET and SET, the canonical dependencies are the default unless the problem explicitly introduces an exception.

Worked numerical examples

All numerical values below are hypothetical teaching data unless stated otherwise.

1. Convertase remaining

A washed preparation has 96 arbitrary activity units and half-life 3 min. After 9 min, what remains?

Solution. Three half-lives have elapsed: 96 × (1/2)³ = 12 units. This assumes no new convertase assembly and constant decay rate. A low remaining activity does not imply lower catalytic efficiency per surviving complex.

2. Integrated product

Two preassembled convertases start at 10 nM active complex with catalytic rate 4 substrate molecules per active complex per minute. Their half-lives are 2 and 6 min. Calculate the theoretical total substrate consumed as time tends to infinity.

Solution. k = ln 2 / half-life = 0.3466 or 0.1155 min⁻¹. P∞ = rE₀/k = 40/0.3466 ≈ 115.4 nM and 40/0.1155 ≈ 346.2 nM. The threefold ratio assumes excess substrate and no replenishment.

3. Branching amplification

One convertase produces 50 C3b molecules; 12% attach and 30% of attached molecules yield active successor complexes. Find the effective branching ratio and expected next generation from 20 complexes.

Solution. R = 50 × 0.12 × 0.30 = 1.8. Expected successors = 20 × 1.8 = 36. In the simple fixed-condition model the loop grows because R exceeds 1; resource depletion or regulators can later change R.

4. Receptor occupancy

For a hypothetical ligand with Kd = 5 nM, compare equilibrium occupancy at 5 nM and 45 nM ligand.

Solution. θ = L/(Kd + L). At 5 nM, θ = 5/10 = 0.50. At 45 nM, θ = 45/50 = 0.90. Occupancy approaches saturation; the calculation does not independently predict chemotactic speed.

5. C3 precursor mass balance

Production is 12 concentration units/day; effective removal increases from 0.3 to 0.6 day⁻¹. What are the steady-state concentrations?

Solution. Css = S/k, yielding 12/0.3 = 40 units and 12/0.6 = 20 units. A low precursor concentration can result from increased removal rather than decreased synthesis.

6. Background-corrected hemolysis

Hypothetical absorbance: spontaneous release 0.10, complete release 0.90, sample 0.50. Calculate percentage lysis.

Solution. Fraction = (0.50 − 0.10)/(0.90 − 0.10) = 0.40/0.80 = 0.50; lysis = 50%. This assumes absorbance is proportional to released hemoglobin and controls are matched. It is not a CH50 unit conversion.

  3. A functional defect at the C7-dependent terminal stage
  4. An isolated defect in the phagocyte CR3 receptor
Answer and explanation

C. A functional defect at the C7-dependent terminal stage

The upstream products and selective rescue place the functional defect downstream of C5 cleavage. Lack of C3 or C1-mediated recognition is inconsistent with the preserved outputs; a cell-free lysis assay need not involve CR3.

Question 25

In a washed convertase assay, active enzyme starts at 80 units with a half-life of 4 min. What is remaining after 12 min?

  1. 10 units
  2. 20 units
  3. 40 units
  4. 0 units
Answer and explanation

A. 10 units

Three half-lives elapse, giving 80 × (1/2)³ = 10 units. Twenty corresponds to two half-lives; 40 to one. Exponential decay does not reach zero at a finite time in this model.

Question 26

One convertase generates 60 C3b molecules, 10% attach and 20% of attached fragments form new convertases. In the simplified branching model what is R?

  1. 0.12
  2. 6
  3. 12
  4. 1.2
Answer and explanation

D. 1.2

R = 60 × 0.10 × 0.20 = 1.2. Six fragments are attached, but only one fifth yield successors. The value exceeds unity in this hypothetical fixed-condition model.

Question 27

Two washed enzyme preparations have identical initial activity and catalytic rate, but decay constants 0.2 and 0.4 min⁻¹. Under excess substrate, what is the ratio of limiting product yields (first:second)?

  1. 1:2
  2. 1:1
  3. 2:1
  4. 4:1
Answer and explanation

C. 2:1

Integrated product at infinite time is rE₀/k. Inverse dependence on k gives (1/0.2)/(1/0.4) = 2. Equal initial activity does not imply equal cumulative conversion.

Question 28

An inhibitor prevents C5 cleavage but does not inhibit C3 convertases. Which canonical result is expected?

  1. C3a formation must cease immediately
  2. C3b deposition may continue but new C5a and C5b formation fall
  3. C1q recognition becomes impossible
  4. C9 directly cleaves C3 in compensation
Answer and explanation

B. C3b deposition may continue but new C5a and C5b formation fall

C5 is downstream of C3 conversion. Inhibiting C5 cleavage directly affects both C5-derived outputs, not the upstream proteases. C9 cannot replace a C3 convertase.

Question 29

A selective C5aR1 antagonist is added only to leukocytes after complement has been activated. Which observation best fits its action?

  1. Absence of C5b6 in the serum because C5 is no longer cut
  2. Loss of all bound C3b from the microbial surface
  3. Reduced C5a-dependent migration with preserved prior complement cleavage
  4. Direct inhibition of factor D by the receptor antagonist
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced C5a-dependent migration with preserved prior complement cleavage

The antagonist changes cellular responsiveness to a generated ligand rather than ligand production itself. Prior serum cleavage and deposition need not change. Factor D is not the targeted receptor.

Question 30

A serum sample has low CH50 and normal AH50 in validated assays. Which isolated defect is a plausible candidate?

  1. C3 deficiency
  2. C2 deficiency
  3. C7 deficiency
  4. Complete factor B deficiency
Answer and explanation

B. C2 deficiency

C2 is required for classical C3 convertase but not alternative conversion. C3 and C7 are shared downstream requirements. Factor B loss is expected to impair the alternative assay most directly. The assay pattern is suggestive, not diagnostic.

Question 31

Both CH50 and AH50 are very low; C4 deposition is normal, but C3b deposition is nearly absent. Which hypothesis is most directly supported?

  1. A functional C3 defect
  2. Isolated C1q deficiency
  3. Isolated C9 deficiency
  4. Loss of leukocyte C5aR1
Answer and explanation

A. A functional C3 defect

Preserved C4 deposition localizes the problem after initial classical C4 processing. Failure of the shared C3 step explains both pathway endpoints. C9 deficiency leaves upstream C3 deposition intact; the receptor defect affects a separate cellular readout.

Question 32

A factor I-deficient preparation initially shows increased deposition but subsequently depleted intact C3. What mechanism best explains these observations?

  1. Factor I is the only C3-synthesizing enzyme
  2. The missing factor I prevents all C3 cleavage
  3. CD59 becomes the C3 convertase
  4. Loss of negative regulation permits excessive activation and consumption
Answer and explanation

D. Loss of negative regulation permits excessive activation and consumption

Factor I normally processes C3b with cofactors. Loss can preserve amplification scaffolds and enhance consumption. It is not responsible for biosynthesis of C3, and CD59 does not supply convertase catalysis.

Question 33

Two surfaces bind equal amounts of initial C3b. Surface X recruits factor H strongly; Y does not. Assuming other conditions are comparable, which outcome is most likely?

  1. More Bb formation on X because factor H is factor B
  2. No difference because C3b itself determines all outcomes
  3. Complete elimination of classical C1q from the fluid around X
  4. Less alternative amplification on X
Answer and explanation

D. Less alternative amplification on X

Factor H favors restraint of alternative convertases and factor I-mediated processing. Initial deposition alone does not specify subsequent amplification. Factor H is not the protease precursor B and does not remove C1q.

Question 34

A researcher measures equal total particle-associated fluorescence in two phagocyte groups but claims that both groups internalized equal numbers of particles. Which control most directly tests the claim?

  1. Measure serum IgG concentration alone
  2. Measure C4 before adding particles without assessing cells
  3. Quench extracellular fluorescence or image intracellular localization
  4. Count total fluorescence again with identical settings
Answer and explanation

C. Quench extracellular fluorescence or image intracellular localization

Total association includes external binding. Quenching or imaging distinguishes attached from internalized material. The other measures do not independently test the location of particles.

Question 35

A purified lectin pathway system contains functional MBL and MASPs but no C4. What is predicted on a suitable carbohydrate-coated surface?

  1. MBL cannot bind any carbohydrate without C4
  2. Recognition can occur, but canonical C4b2a formation fails
  3. C4b2a forms normally because C4 is not a subunit
  4. C9 becomes the recognition protein
Answer and explanation

B. Recognition can occur, but canonical C4b2a formation fails

MBL recognition is upstream of C4. Without C4b there is no normal shared convertase scaffold; the terminal component cannot substitute for the missing early protein.

Question 36

In a hemolysis experiment, background absorbance is 0.15, complete lysis is 0.95 and the sample is 0.35. What percentage lysis is indicated?

  1. 25%
  2. 37%
  3. 50%
  4. 80%
Answer and explanation

A. 25%

Background-corrected fraction is (0.35−0.15)/(0.95−0.15) = 0.20/0.80 = 0.25. Using uncorrected absorbance or treating the denominator as the background alone produces misleading values.

Question 37

A peptide receptor has Kd = 8 nM. Occupancy is measured at 24 nM free ligand under the one-site equilibrium model. What is the expected value?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.75
  3. 0.50
  4. 3.00
Answer and explanation

B. 0.75

Occupancy = 24/(8+24) = 0.75. The ligand is threefold Kd, but occupancy is a bounded fraction, not the simple L/Kd ratio. This equilibrium value does not specify migration.

Question 38

An experiment uses Mg-EGTA conditions to inhibit calcium-dependent initiation while maintaining alternative activity. Which substitution would broadly suppress canonical activation rather than isolate the alternative pathway?

  1. Extra magnesium while calcium remains chelated
  2. A susceptible alternative-pathway target
  3. Removal of specific antibody under otherwise permissive conditions
  4. EDTA sufficient to chelate both Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺
Answer and explanation

D. EDTA sufficient to chelate both Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺

EDTA binds both necessary divalent ions and can interfere with assembly across pathways. Supplying magnesium with calcium restricted is the basis of the selective assay conditions. Target selection and antibody removal do not substitute for both-ion chelation.

Question 39

A bacterial strain has abundant C3-derived deposition but very poor complement-mediated killing. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. Deposition may be intact while terminal insertion or vulnerability is limited
  2. The C3 deposition result proves MAC killed all bacteria
  3. Complement never acts on this bacterium
  4. No complement receptors can recognize the deposited fragments
Answer and explanation

A. Deposition may be intact while terminal insertion or vulnerability is limited

Coating does not guarantee successful membrane injury. Surface architecture or downstream resistance can uncouple deposition from lysis. Opsonization can still matter even when killing in a serum-only assay is weak.

Question 40

A CH50 assay is normal, AH50 is normal, but a lectin-specific functional assay is low. What is the most coherent interpretation?

  1. All three complement pathways are necessarily normal
  2. A complete C3 deficiency is the most likely isolated defect
  3. An isolated lectin recognition or initiation defect remains possible
  4. C9 must be absent
Answer and explanation

C. An isolated lectin recognition or initiation defect remains possible

CH50 and AH50 do not directly measure the unique lectin initiation machinery. A shared C3 or terminal deficiency would generally impair the other functional endpoints as well.

Question 41

Production of intact C3 is fixed at 15 units/day, and the effective removal constant rises from 0.5 to 0.75 day⁻¹. What steady-state change follows from the model?

  1. 20 to 30 units
  2. 15 to 22.5 units
  3. 30 to 20 units
  4. 7.5 to 11.25 units
Answer and explanation

C. 30 to 20 units

Using Css=S/k, the initial concentration is 15/0.5=30 and the final concentration 15/0.75=20. Increased removal lowers precursor abundance even when synthesis is unchanged.

Question 42

An inhibitor selectively prevents factor I processing of C3b but leaves CD59 active. Which pair of observations is most plausible?

  1. Persistent convertase-capable C3b with retained terminal protection from CD59
  2. Universal loss of C3b and absence of all complement activation
  3. Increased iC3b generation through the inhibited reaction
  4. Failure of lectin binding because CD59 is the lectin
Answer and explanation

A. Persistent convertase-capable C3b with retained terminal protection from CD59

Blocking regulatory processing leaves more C3b available for amplification, provided other requirements are met. CD59 can still limit MAC at the membrane. The other predictions reverse the action of the inhibitor or misassign CD59.

Question 43

A researcher finds more leukocytes moving in a uniform high C5a concentration than in buffer but cannot detect movement toward a particular direction. What is the strongest inference?

  1. The result proves a directional gradient was present
  2. C5a must be a terminal pore component
  3. Increased motility proves all cells were killed
  4. C5a may increase motility; directional chemotaxis has not been demonstrated
Answer and explanation

D. C5a may increase motility; directional chemotaxis has not been demonstrated

Uniform ligand can alter motility without supplying a direction. A gradient experiment is needed to establish directed chemotaxis. Increased movement is a different endpoint from membrane attack or death.

Question 44

Figure 12 compares convertases with half-lives of 2 and 6 min, identical starting amounts. At 6 min, by what factor is retained activity higher in the stabilized preparation?

  1. 3-fold
  2. 4-fold
  3. 2-fold
  4. 8-fold
Answer and explanation

B. 4-fold

The fractions are 1/8 and 1/2, so the ratio is (1/2)/(1/8)=4. The half-life ratio is three but is not the activity ratio at this time. The result concerns remaining activity, not an intrinsic catalytic rate difference.

Assertion: C3b is a major opsonin. Reason: C3b can become attached to targets and be recognized by complement receptors.

  2. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion

  4. Assertion false, reason true

A. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion

A retained C3b-derived label enables receptor-dependent recognition. This explains its opsonic role, although downstream uptake also depends on cell state.

Question 46

Assertion: CD59 deficiency directly abolishes C3 convertase formation. Reason: CD59 normally restrains terminal C9 incorporation.

  1. Both statements true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both statements true, but the reason does not explain the assertion
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

C. Assertion false, reason true

CD59 functions late, not in assembling the C3 convertase. Its loss can enhance membrane injury without directly eliminating upstream C3 conversion.

Question 47

Select the correctly matched set: (i) C1q, (ii) Bb, (iii) CD55, (iv) C9.

  1. i terminal pore; ii recognition; iii C9 cleavage; iv C3 convertase
  2. i convertase decay; ii membrane pore; iii lectin recognition; iv factor I cofactor
  3. i C3 substrate; ii opsonin; iii antibody synthesis; iv C4 cleavage
  4. i recognition; ii catalytic fragment; iii convertase decay; iv terminal assembly
Answer and explanation

D. i recognition; ii catalytic fragment; iii convertase decay; iv terminal assembly

C1q recognizes suitable ligands, Bb supplies alternative protease activity, CD55 destabilizes convertases, and C9 contributes to terminal membrane attack. The alternative sets assign these functions to unrelated proteins.

Question 48

Which statements are correct? I: C4b2a is shared by classical and lectin activation. II: Alternative amplification can follow classical initiation. III: C5b is the main neutrophil chemoattractant.

  1. I and III only
  2. I and II only
  3. II and III only
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

B. I and II only

I and II reflect shared conversion and alternative feedback. III is false: C5a is the relevant potent soluble chemotactic fragment, whereas C5b starts terminal assembly.

Question 49

In Figure 7, an additional surface C3b appears in both C5 convertase representations. What does it indicate?

  1. Replacement of both C2a and Bb by a C3b protease
  2. Direct polymerization of C3b into a lipid pore
  3. The obligatory release of an antibody at the C5 stage
  4. A change in assembly that supports efficient C5 cleavage
Answer and explanation

D. A change in assembly that supports efficient C5 cleavage

Additional C3b helps organize C5-converting activity. C2a or Bb remains the catalytic fragment; C3b is not the terminal pore protein or an antibody.

Question 50

Which ordering correctly traces an antibody-directed response from early processing to terminal assembly?

  1. C5b-9 → C3b → C1s → C4b2a
  2. C1s activation → C4b deposition → C4b2a → C3b generation → C5 cleavage → C5b-9
  3. C4b2a → C1q binding → C9 → C3 cleavage
  4. C3a → C1q → factor I → antibody rearrangement → C5b6
Answer and explanation

B. C1s activation → C4b deposition → C4b2a → C3b generation → C5 cleavage → C5b-9

C1 activation precedes C4/C2-dependent convertase formation. C3b supports efficient C5 conversion and C5b initiates terminal assembly. The alternatives invert causal dependencies or introduce unrelated antibody gene rearrangement.

Question 51

A purified serum preparation has normal C4b2a activity. Its added phagocytes lack an iC3b-binding receptor. Which readout is most likely selectively altered?

  1. C1s-mediated cleavage of C4 in the serum
  2. Formation of C5b from C5 in the absence of cells
  3. Uptake of appropriately iC3b-coated targets
  4. The chemical reactivity of the C3 thioester
Answer and explanation

C. Uptake of appropriately iC3b-coated targets

Receptor deficiency affects the cell's ability to use an existing opsonic label. It does not inherently change a purified serum convertase or the chemical reactivity of C3.

Question 52

Assertion: A low C3 concentration can arise from excessive complement activation. Reason: The steady precursor concentration depends on both synthesis and removal.

  1. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both statements true, but the reason does not explain the assertion
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

A. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion

In a mass balance increased activation-associated consumption can lower intact precursor abundance even with unchanged synthesis. Concentration therefore cannot uniquely identify the primary cause.

Examination traps and misconceptions

1. All complement activation needs antibodies.

Lectin and alternative routes can initiate without antigen-specific antibody. Classical activation also has selected antibody-independent triggers.

Why it is tempting: Antibody-mediated lysis is the familiar historical example, so it is often mistaken for the entire system.

Examination distinction: Ask whether MBL or basal C3 hydrolysis can initiate activity before antigen-specific antibody production.

2. C1, C2, C3 and C4 act in numerical order.

Classical C1s processes C4 before C2-dependent C3 conversion. Numbers reflect historical nomenclature rather than sequence.

Why it is tempting: The component names look like a numbered sequence.

Examination distinction: Order C1 activation, C4 cleavage and C2 processing by molecular dependence, not the numeral.

3. C3(H₂O) is C3b.

Hydrolyzed intact C3 has not released C3a; proteolytic C3b is a distinct product.

Why it is tempting: Both forms bind factor B, which makes them appear identical.

Examination distinction: Ask which event releases C3a and which simply hydrolyzes the thioester.

4. C3b is the alternative convertase protease.

Bb is catalytic; C3b supplies a platform.

Why it is tempting: The scaffold name appears first in the convertase formula.

Examination distinction: Ask which fragment actually contains the catalytic site.

5. Factor D directly cleaves C3.

Factor D processes scaffold-bound factor B; Bb cleaves C3 in the assembled convertase.

Why it is tempting: Factor D is a protease in the alternative pathway, so its substrate is easily confused with C3.

Examination distinction: Identify the bound factor B substrate and distinguish its processing from Bb catalysis.

6. Properdin is an inhibitor.

Properdin generally stabilizes alternative convertases and promotes amplification.

Why it is tempting: Many named complement regulators inhibit activation, so properdin is incorrectly grouped with them.

Examination distinction: Predict the effect of properdin loss on convertase lifetime rather than on initial C3 synthesis.

7. Lectin recognition always depends on MBL alone.

Ficolins and other collectins can also initiate lectin-pathway recognition.

Why it is tempting: The pathway is frequently introduced with the name mannose-binding lectin.

Examination distinction: Compare pattern recognition by MBL and ficolins without assuming a unique recognition molecule.

8. Classical and alternative responses are mutually exclusive.

C3b from classical initiation can feed alternative amplification on the same target.

Why it is tempting: Separate pathway diagrams conceal the connection between routes.

Examination distinction: Trace C3b generated by classical initiation into factor B-dependent amplification.

9. All small complement fragments have equivalent inflammatory activity.

C3a and C5a are established anaphylatoxins; C4a does not have equivalent well-established physiological activity.

Why it is tempting: A shared fragment suffix is mistaken for equal receptor activity.

Examination distinction: Compare C3a and C5a with the less established C4a activity.

10. C5b attracts neutrophils through C5aR1.

C5a supplies that soluble ligand; C5b initiates terminal assembly.

Why it is tempting: The two products of C5 are adjacent in diagrams and easy to reverse.

Examination distinction: Assign C5a to soluble receptor signaling and C5b to C6 recruitment.

11. iC3b is biologically inactive in every sense.

It cannot act as the original alternative convertase scaffold but remains an important opsonin.

Why it is tempting: The prefix i suggests total loss of function.

Examination distinction: Predict whether iC3b supports CR3 recognition despite loss of convertase scaffold function.

12. MAC lyses all microbes equally.

Target envelope architecture and regulatory protection determine vulnerability; opsonization may be more important for resistant targets.

Why it is tempting: The terminal pathway is commonly illustrated as a single effective pore.

Examination distinction: Compare susceptibility of a Gram-positive bacterium and a MAC-sensitive Gram-negative target.

13. C6 through C9 are sequential proteases.

Terminal components assemble and insert into membranes through protein interactions.

Why it is tempting: The terminal components are arranged sequentially, like upstream proteases.

Examination distinction: Distinguish ordered binding and insertion from cleavage of a downstream precursor.

14. CD55 and CD59 have the same target.

CD55 accelerates convertase decay; CD59 limits terminal assembly.

Why it is tempting: Both regulators protect host surfaces and may be lost together in GPI-anchor defects.

Examination distinction: Predict the most direct defect after selectively removing each protein.

15. Factor H is the protease that cuts C3b.

Factor H can supply cofactor activity; factor I performs the relevant proteolysis.

Why it is tempting: Factor H and factor I are frequently taught as a functional pair.

Examination distinction: Identify the cofactor and the enzyme in a reconstitution experiment.

16. Low circulating C3 always implies deficient synthesis.

Excess activation and consumption can also lower the concentration.

Why it is tempting: A concentration result is treated as a direct measure of production.

Examination distinction: Use precursor mass balance to explain low concentration after excessive activation.

17. A low CH50 alone identifies a specific component deficiency.

Multiple defects can affect an integrated endpoint; use alternative assays, intermediate markers and selective rescue.

Why it is tempting: A single functional assay is often presented as a screening test for complement.

Examination distinction: Use AH50, intermediate products and rescue to narrow the location rather than asserting a unique diagnosis.

18. Normal CH50 and AH50 rule out all lectin pathway defects.

These tests do not directly test the unique lectin initiation route.

Why it is tempting: Two pathway tests can seem to cover the whole network because they share downstream components.

Examination distinction: Identify the untested MBL/MASP initiation machinery.

19. A positive deposition assay establishes killing.

Coating, terminal insertion, permeability and viability are distinct endpoints.

Why it is tempting: Deposition frequently correlates with damage in susceptible targets.

Examination distinction: Separate coating, pore formation and cell survival in a resistant-target experiment.

20. C1-INH-associated swelling is caused solely by C3a and C5a.

Hereditary angioedema associated with C1-INH dysfunction is primarily bradykinin-mediated; the regulator acts in multiple plasma networks.

Why it is tempting: C1-INH is taught within complement, making other plasma substrates easy to overlook.

Examination distinction: Identify bradykinin-mediated permeability rather than assuming a histamine or anaphylatoxin mechanism.

Revision and mastery

Integrated summary

Classical C1-dependent and lectin MASP-dependent activation generate C4b2a. Alternative C3-derived scaffolds recruit factor B, which factor D processes to create C3bBb. Both convertases produce C3a and C3b; additional C3b supports efficient C5 conversion. C3b-derived fragments label targets, C3a and C5a signal, and C5b recruits C6–C9 for terminal attack. C1-INH, factor H, factor I, membrane cofactors, decay accelerators and CD59 regulate different stages.

Essential quantitative relationships

  • E(t) = E₀ exp(−kt); t½ = ln 2/k.
  • P(T) = rE₀[1−exp(−kT)]/k under fixed enzyme decay and excess substrate.
  • R = mfg is an illustrative branching model, not a clinical index.
  • θ = L/(Kd + L) for independent equilibrium sites.
  • Css = S/k for constant-volume steady-state production and first-order removal.
  • Fraction lysed = (sample − background)/(complete lysis − background).
