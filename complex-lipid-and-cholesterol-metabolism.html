1. Why cells assemble complex lipids

A cell receiving fatty acids faces several quite different demands. It may need to store chemical energy for tomorrow, expand a membrane during growth, make a steroid precursor, or send a rapid chemical message to a neighbouring cell. A fatty acid alone cannot do all these jobs efficiently. Its long hydrocarbon chain is valuable as fuel and as a hydrophobic membrane component, but its exposed carboxyl group and amphipathic character make large unregulated pools undesirable. The solution is to activate fatty acids and attach them to selected molecular frameworks. A change in framework or head group transforms the physical behaviour, cellular destination and metabolic purpose of the resulting molecule.

Recall three prerequisites. An ester connects a carboxyl group to an alcohol; hydrolysis can separate them. An amide connects a carboxyl group to an amino group and requires different enzymes for cleavage. A thioester such as fatty acyl-CoA transfers an acyl group to an appropriate acceptor. Glycerol has three hydroxyl groups and can therefore carry three fatty acyl groups or two fatty acyl groups plus a phosphorylated head group. Sphingolipids instead use a long-chain amino alcohol, whereas cholesterol is a four-ring sterol. These are not interchangeable structures. Enzymes distinguish them through the chemistry and geometry of their substrates.

This chapter follows the major choices in mammalian lipid metabolism. First we follow glycerol and activated fatty acids into storage lipid; then we follow shared intermediates into membrane phospholipids. We examine a second membrane lipid family based on ceramide, before tracing acetyl-CoA into cholesterol. Finally we solve the problem of moving hydrophobic molecules through blood, removing cholesterol as bile acids and releasing specialised lipid signals. The underlying question throughout is how a cell directs material into the right product, in the right compartment, at the right time.

A useful distinction is between pool size and flux. A cell may have a large store of triacylglycerol (TAG) even if its rate of synthesis is low today. A small pool of phosphatidic acid can support high flux if it is made and consumed rapidly. Consequently, a metabolite concentration, enzyme abundance or single endpoint cannot by itself establish the rate of a pathway. We will repeatedly use carbon balance, enzyme perturbation and tracer reasoning to distinguish these possibilities.

2. Building triacylglycerol: glycerol, acyl donors and the branch point

TAG is an efficient form of stored energy because its three fatty acids are highly reduced and its ester groups leave no strongly polar head group. It forms a separate oily phase rather than a hydrated polymer. Compared with hydrated glycogen, TAG stores considerably more energy per unit stored mass. This is why adipose tissue can hold an extensive fuel reserve without an equally extensive water load. The absence of a polar head also means that TAG cannot serve as the principal bilayer-forming lipid.

Where does its glycerol come from? During glycolysis, the three-carbon intermediate dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) can receive reducing equivalents from NADH. Cytosolic glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase produces sn-glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and NAD+. This reaction preserves the three-carbon skeleton and installs the stereochemically appropriate phosphate-bearing backbone. In the liver, glycerol kinase can also phosphorylate free glycerol using ATP. White adipose tissue generally has low glycerol kinase activity and depends substantially on glucose-derived G3P and glyceroneogenesis. Low is not equivalent to zero: tissue and metabolic state influence the contribution of each route.

Glyceroneogenesis forms G3P from precursors such as pyruvate or lactate through a shortened gluconeogenic sequence involving phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. It does not need to proceed all the way to glucose. The pathway becomes especially useful when glucose uptake falls but fatty acid re-esterification continues. Thus adipose tissue can recycle some of the fatty acids released from its own stores rather than release every molecule immediately into the circulation. In a tracer experiment, label entering G3P from lactate during fasting would not demonstrate that the adipocyte is releasing glucose; the relevant carbon can stop at the glycerol backbone.

Fatty acids are activated by acyl-CoA synthetases: fatty acid + CoA + ATP → fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi. Hydrolysis of PPi by inorganic pyrophosphatase favours forward flux. Since ATP is converted to AMP, activation costs two high-energy phosphate equivalents per fatty acid, not one. This cost matters whenever free fatty acids are supplied as the starting materials of a calculation. If the question already supplies acyl-CoA, activation has already occurred and should not be counted again.

In the glycerol-3-phosphate pathway, glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT) transfers a first fatty acyl group, generally to sn-1, forming lysophosphatidic acid (LPA). An acylglycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (AGPAT) adds the second chain to sn-2, giving phosphatidic acid (PA). Each transfer releases CoA; it does not directly consume another ATP. The two esterified chains and phosphate make PA a membrane-associated intermediate rather than a neutral oil.

PA now sits at a major metabolic fork. Phosphatidate phosphatase, including lipin proteins, removes its phosphate to form 1,2-diacylglycerol (DAG). DAG has a free hydroxyl at sn-3, so diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT) adds a third acyl group and completes TAG. Alternatively, DAG may receive an activated phosphorylated head group, or PA may itself be activated to form CDP-DAG. These alternatives create membrane phospholipids. Thus a defect upstream of PA affects several branches, while a specific DGAT defect primarily restricts the final TAG-forming step. It need not abolish phosphatidylcholine formation.

The enzymes of storage glycerolipid synthesis are largely associated with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), with relevant isoforms also present at other membranes. This organisation solves a physical problem: long-chain substrates do not freely dissolve in cytosol, and newly made neutral lipid must be segregated. TAG collects between the leaflets of the ER and grows into lipid droplets. A droplet has a hydrophobic core coated by a phospholipid monolayer and associated proteins, unlike a bilayer vesicle with an aqueous interior. This arrangement exposes polar head groups to cytosol and keeps neutral lipid away from water. Droplet proteins control enzyme access and are therefore active participants in metabolism, not merely structural decorations.

Intestinal enterocytes use another important route after a fat-containing meal. Pancreatic lipase preferentially hydrolyses the outer ester bonds of dietary TAG, producing two fatty acids and a 2-monoacylglycerol. After absorption, monoacylglycerol acyltransferase (MGAT) makes DAG and DGAT adds the final fatty acid. This conserves the incoming glycerol backbone and provides an efficient way to reassemble dietary lipid. The enterocyte packages newly synthesised TAG into chylomicrons; it does not export large quantities of naked TAG through blood.

Consider the energy accounting for synthesising one TAG from three unactivated fatty acids and glycerol, assuming glycerol kinase supplies G3P. Activation consumes 3 × 2 = 6 ATP equivalents. Glycerol kinase consumes one additional ATP, so the total is seven ATP equivalents. This is the direct preparation cost, not the energy needed to synthesise the fatty acids from acetyl-CoA or to maintain the cell. If DHAP supplies the glycerol backbone, the glycerol kinase cost is absent, but one cytosolic NADH is required. The upstream cost of obtaining DHAP must be stated separately if included.

In an experiment, a hepatocyte is supplied labelled glycerol and unlabelled fatty acids. Label in TAG glycerol does not require de novo fatty acid synthesis. Conversely, supplying labelled acetate can label acyl chains through fatty acid synthesis without immediately labelling the glycerol backbone to the same extent. To separate these results, hydrolyse the lipid and analyse the backbone and fatty acid fractions separately. A single measurement of total TAG radioactivity cannot tell which part of the molecule is labelled.

3. Mobilising stored TAG: hydrolysis, hormonal control and substrate fate

When fuel demand rises, adipocytes must expose ester bonds to water and lipases. TAG hydrolysis proceeds through three distinct intermediates. Adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) releases the first fatty acid, forming DAG. Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) acts particularly effectively on DAG, forming monoacylglycerol (MAG). Monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL) releases the last fatty acid and glycerol. HSL can also act on other substrates, but it is inaccurate to assign all physiological adipose TAG hydrolysis to HSL. Distinguishing the three enzymes is essential for interpreting perturbation experiments.

Complete net hydrolysis is TAG + 3 H₂O → glycerol + 3 fatty acids. This is hydrolysis, not beta oxidation. Lipolysis releases carbon-rich molecules without oxidising their hydrocarbon chains to acetyl-CoA or producing respiratory NADH directly. Oxidation occurs after fatty acids are transported to suitable cells, activated and delivered to the appropriate oxidative compartment. The fatty acids circulate mainly bound to albumin as non-esterified fatty acids. Glycerol, being much more water-soluble, travels without needing the same hydrophobic carrier and can be phosphorylated in the liver for gluconeogenesis or glycolysis.

How does the organism decide when to hydrolyse its store? In human adipose tissue, sympathetic catecholamines acting on beta-adrenergic receptors are major acute stimulators. Through Gs they activate adenylyl cyclase, increase cAMP and activate protein kinase A (PKA). PKA phosphorylates HSL and lipid droplet-associated perilipin 1. The latter change frees the coactivator ABHD5 (CGI-58), which promotes ATGL activity. HSL also gains access to the droplet and its DAG substrate. A hormone signal therefore changes both catalytic regulation and physical access to the stored lipid.

In the fed state insulin favours glucose uptake in adipocytes via GLUT4, provides substrate for G3P formation and promotes TAG storage. Insulin also suppresses lipolysis, in part by stimulating phosphodiesterase activity that lowers cAMP and counteracts PKA-dependent activation. Adipose lipoprotein lipase activity supports entry of acyl groups from circulating TAG-rich particles. Direct glucagon effects on human adipocytes are not the principal physiological fasting signal; glucagon acts strongly on the liver, while catecholamines and low insulin explain much of adipose mobilisation. Species differences should not be mistaken for universal rules.

Fatty acids released inside the adipocyte face competing fates. Some leave the cell, some are oxidised locally, and some are reactivated and esterified into glycerolipids. The TAG–fatty acid cycle consumes energy, since hydrolysed acyl groups must be activated again. Yet it allows precise control of net fatty acid release. A high rate of TAG hydrolysis is not necessarily a high rate of net fatty acid export. This explains why measurements of glycerol release and fatty acid release can diverge.

Suppose adipocytes completely hydrolyse 12 nmol of TAG per minute. If none of the products is retained, they generate 12 nmol/min of glycerol and 36 nmol/min of fatty acids. If net fatty acid export is only 21 nmol/min while glycerol export remains 12 nmol/min, 15 nmol/min of generated fatty acids are not exported. Under the simplifying assumption of no local oxidation or other storage, the re-esterified fraction is 15/36 = 41.7%. If oxidation is not excluded, the same data establish only total non-exported fatty acid flux. One must not label every missing fatty acid as re-esterified without additional evidence.

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) is different from the intracellular droplet lipases. It hydrolyses TAG in circulating chylomicrons and VLDL at the capillary surface, delivering fatty acids to nearby tissues. Pancreatic lipase acts in the intestinal lumen on dietary lipid. These lipases share ester hydrolysis chemistry but differ in location, substrate presentation and physiological purpose. Their names must be interpreted in the context of the compartment.

4. Membrane glycerophospholipids: two activation strategies

A growing cell cannot make a membrane from TAG alone. Membrane glycerophospholipids contain hydrophobic chains and a phosphate-bearing polar head. These molecules can pack into bilayers, interact with membrane proteins and provide reservoirs for signalling precursors. Their synthesis must be coordinated with the production of storage lipid because both draw from common fatty acyl-CoA and PA pools. The cell therefore needs mechanisms for selecting a particular head group and attaching it in an energetically favourable manner.

Two broad strategies solve this chemical problem. One activates the head group with cytidine triphosphate (CTP); the other activates the phosphatidyl group. In the first, choline is phosphorylated by choline kinase at the expense of ATP. CTP:phosphocholine cytidylyltransferase transfers the cytidylyl portion of CTP to phosphocholine, producing CDP-choline and PPi. The activated head group is then transferred to DAG by a phosphotransferase, releasing CMP and forming phosphatidylcholine (PC). Phosphate in the original PA was removed to produce DAG; the phosphate in this final PC comes from phosphocholine. This distinction matters in isotope questions.

The same general Kennedy pathway operates with ethanolamine: ethanolamine kinase makes phosphoethanolamine, a cytidylyltransferase forms CDP-ethanolamine, and a phosphotransferase attaches the head to DAG to yield phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Although the reaction designs are analogous, the enzymes and their regulation can differ. In the PC pathway, CTP:phosphocholine cytidylyltransferase is a major regulatory step. Its association with membranes responds to membrane composition, helping couple PC synthesis to membrane demand.

PC is abundant in mammalian membranes and in lipoprotein surfaces. Its approximately cylindrical geometry supports relatively flat bilayer packing. PE has a smaller head group and favours negative curvature in appropriate membrane environments, so a change in the PC:PE ratio can influence both membrane architecture and protein function. The cell must produce a suitable mixture rather than maximise one lipid. In the liver, PE can also be methylated to PC through three successive methyl-group transfers from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), producing three S-adenosylhomocysteine molecules. This route links membrane metabolism to methionine and one-carbon metabolism, which will be explored in later nodes.

Phosphatidylserine (PS) in mammals is mainly made by replacing the head group of PC or PE with serine in ER-associated reactions. This is base exchange, not a universal requirement to activate serine with CTP. In mitochondria, PS decarboxylase removes a carboxyl group from the serine head to produce PE and CO₂. The glycerol backbone and the acyl chains remain. This provides a particularly important source of mitochondrial PE and demonstrates why local phospholipid composition can depend on transfer of precursors between organelles. Many bacteria instead synthesise PS from CDP-DAG and serine, so the mammalian base-exchange route should not be applied blindly across organisms.

Now consider the other activation strategy. CDP-DAG synthase converts PA and CTP into CDP-DAG plus PPi. The activated phosphatidyl moiety can be transferred to inositol, producing phosphatidylinositol (PI) and CMP. PI is an important precursor of phosphoinositides. Kinases can phosphorylate the inositol ring at selected positions, establishing membrane identity and providing regulated substrate for signalling. The glycerol backbone remains part of the membrane even when the polar head is modified.

CDP-DAG also contributes to phosphatidylglycerol (PG) synthesis. In mitochondria CDP-DAG reacts with glycerol-3-phosphate to make phosphatidylglycerophosphate, which loses a phosphate to produce PG. In mammals, cardiolipin synthase transfers a phosphatidyl group from another CDP-DAG molecule to PG. The resulting cardiolipin has four acyl chains and two phosphate groups. It is enriched in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it supports organisation of respiratory complexes and cristae. Most bacteria use a different cardiolipin synthesis route involving two PG molecules; the exam context determines which reaction is appropriate.

A simple cost comparison highlights the difference between the activation strategies. Starting from preformed DAG and unphosphorylated choline, the Kennedy pathway uses one ATP and one CTP. Starting from preformed PA and inositol, PI synthesis uses one CTP without first phosphorylating inositol. CTP regeneration and PPi hydrolysis imply additional energy accounting if all nucleotide regeneration is included. Do not treat ATP and CTP as interchangeable substrates of the particular enzymes. The important mechanistic conclusion is that both routes couple formation of a new bond to nucleotidyl activation and release of CMP in the final transfer.

5. Phospholipid turnover, remodelling and signalling

Membrane synthesis does not end with attaching the head group. Acyl chain length and unsaturation influence membrane packing, so the cell remodels existing phospholipids rather than discarding every complete molecule. In the Lands cycle a phospholipase removes a fatty acyl chain and a lysophospholipid acyltransferase adds another activated chain. A common route is phospholipase A₂-mediated removal of the sn-2 acyl group followed by reacylation. This permits a membrane to enrich particular phospholipids in arachidonic acid even when the first de novo molecules had different acyl groups.

The names of phospholipases specify the bond they hydrolyse. Phospholipase A₁ acts on the sn-1 ester bond; A₂ acts on sn-2. Both yield a lysophospholipid and a fatty acid, but the retained chain occupies a different position. Phospholipase C cleaves between glycerol and phosphate, leaving DAG and a phosphorylated head group. Phospholipase D cleaves between phosphate and the terminal head group, producing PA and the free head. These products are biologically different, so identifying which product accumulates is a direct way to infer the class of hydrolytic activity.

For example, the phosphoinositide phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂) can be cleaved by signalling phospholipase C. Its hydrophobic DAG product remains in the membrane and participates in protein kinase C regulation, whereas inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP₃) is water soluble and can stimulate Ca²⁺ release from the ER. The two messengers arise from one substrate but occupy different physical compartments. Phospholipase D on PC would instead generate PA and choline, not IP₃. The full receptor pathways belong to cell signalling; here the lesson is how lipid bond cleavage creates products with distinct fates.

Remodelling also explains why measuring total PC need not reveal a dramatic change in the composition of PC molecular species. Replacing a saturated chain with a polyunsaturated chain may leave the total number of PC molecules unchanged while shifting membrane properties and future signalling capacity. In a pulse–chase study, labelled acyl chains can be lost and replaced while a separately labelled phosphate or head group remains. Tracking both markers therefore helps distinguish reacylation from degradation and resynthesis of the entire molecule.

A special family, the ether phospholipids, replaces the conventional sn-1 ester linkage with an ether bond. Plasmalogens contain a vinyl ether at sn-1 and are prominent in certain tissues, including nervous tissue and heart. Early steps of their synthesis occur in peroxisomes using DHAP-derived intermediates, and later steps occur in the ER. A peroxisomal synthesis defect can thus reduce plasmalogen abundance without eliminating ordinary ER glycerophospholipid synthesis. This is a valuable example of a pathway that cannot be understood by considering all phospholipids identical.

Cardiolipin is also remodelled after synthesis. Tafazzin catalyses acyl transfer between phospholipids and lysophospholipids; its dysfunction alters cardiolipin composition and can increase monolysocardiolipin. The resulting mitochondrial dysfunction illustrates how chain composition, rather than simply lipid class abundance, matters for membrane protein organisation. The molecular interpretation is sufficient here; the detailed clinical manifestations are not needed for the metabolic reasoning.

6. Ceramide: a second lipid backbone and its derivatives

Unlike glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids do not start from glycerol. Their backbone is a long-chain amino alcohol. The nitrogen provides an amide linkage to an additional fatty acid, giving a molecule with two hydrophobic chains and a free hydroxyl group that can receive a head group. This central molecule, ceramide, connects synthesis, degradation and lipid signalling. In sphingomyelin the head is phosphocholine; in glycosphingolipids it consists of one or more sugars. The chemistry of the backbone explains why the degradative enzymes differ from those acting on glycerolipid esters.

De novo synthesis begins at the cytosolic face of the ER. Serine palmitoyltransferase, a pyridoxal phosphate-dependent enzyme, condenses serine and palmitoyl-CoA. The condensation releases CO₂ and CoA, forming 3-ketosphinganine, an 18-carbon intermediate when C16 palmitoyl-CoA is used. NADPH-dependent reduction converts the keto group into an alcohol, giving sphinganine (dihydrosphingosine). Ceramide synthase transfers a fatty acyl group from acyl-CoA to the amino group, producing dihydroceramide. A desaturase introduces the characteristic double bond of the sphingosine backbone to form ceramide. Different ceramide synthases prefer different acyl chain lengths, which helps generate diverse molecular species.

Carbon accounting reveals why this pathway is not simply a condensation of two intact fatty acids. Serine contributes two carbon atoms after loss of its carboxyl carbon, while palmitoyl-CoA contributes sixteen. The long-chain base therefore contains eighteen carbons; attaching a C24 fatty acid by an amide bond gives a C42 ceramide. The acyl donor is the second chain and is not a second molecule of serine. Oxygen and cofactors involved in reduction and desaturation change oxidation state, but not the carbon number of the backbone.

Ceramide is made primarily in the ER and subsequently delivered to Golgi compartments by lipid transfer mechanisms and membrane trafficking. Sphingomyelin synthase transfers phosphocholine from PC to ceramide, producing sphingomyelin and DAG. Thus the reaction connects the glycerophospholipid and sphingolipid networks. Ceramide can also receive glucose from UDP-glucose, forming glucosylceramide, or galactose from UDP-galactose, forming galactosylceramide. Further glycosylation generates neutral and acidic glycosphingolipids; gangliosides contain one or more sialic acid residues. Sulfatides arise through sulfation of galactosylceramide. These modifications alter surface recognition and membrane organisation while retaining the underlying ceramide.

Galactosylceramide and sulfatides are important components of myelin. Gangliosides are abundant in nervous tissue and participate in cell surface recognition. Their sugar head groups are oriented towards the extracellular side of the plasma membrane after secretory trafficking. The reason is topological: many glycosylation reactions occur on the luminal side of the secretory pathway, and membrane fusion preserves the equivalence of lumen and extracellular surface. Glucosylceramide synthesis starts on a cytosolic membrane face and requires subsequent transfer for elaboration of complex glycans. This exception prevents an oversimplified rule that every sphingolipid head-group attachment is luminal.

Sphingolipid production must be balanced by turnover. A lysosomal glycosphingolipid can be degraded stepwise by removing sugar residues until ceramide remains. Ceramidases then hydrolyse the amide bond to produce sphingosine and a fatty acid. Sphingosine can be reacylated to ceramide in a salvage route or phosphorylated by sphingosine kinase to sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P). S1P phosphatases can return it to sphingosine, whereas S1P lyase cleaves it irreversibly into a long-chain aldehyde and phosphoethanolamine. The latter step provides a true exit from the sphingolipid backbone pool. Sphingomyelinases similarly generate ceramide by hydrolysing sphingomyelin's head-group linkage.

Ceramide and S1P can themselves participate in signalling. Ceramide often accumulates under cellular stress and can favour stress-associated responses; extracellular S1P acts through specific receptors and can support survival and migration in relevant contexts. These tendencies do not define an invariant switch between death and survival. Molecular species, localisation, enzymes, receptor expression and cell type determine the response. At examination level it is more useful to trace the conversions and distinguish salvage from irreversible degradation than to memorise a simplistic universal outcome.

Sequential degradation and enzyme defects

Lysosomal storage disorders offer a natural experiment in substrate specificity. Deficiency of glucocerebrosidase in Gaucher disease compromises glucosylceramide degradation; deficiency of hexosaminidase A in Tay–Sachs disease compromises GM2 ganglioside degradation; deficiency of acid sphingomyelinase in Niemann–Pick disease types A and B causes sphingomyelin accumulation. Fabry disease involves alpha-galactosidase A and accumulation of globotriaosylceramide. In each case, the important mechanistic principle is that the substrate of a blocked step tends to accumulate when input continues. The exact pattern can extend to secondary metabolites, so a storage phenotype is not necessarily one chemically pure compound.

Niemann–Pick type C is a particularly instructive contrast. It involves defects in intracellular lipid trafficking, commonly NPC1 or NPC2, rather than a primary loss of acid sphingomyelinase. Cholesterol accumulates in late endosomal and lysosomal compartments because its export is impaired. The same broad category of storage phenotype can thus result from either a catalytic block or failure to deliver substrate or product to the correct compartment.

If a lysosomal fraction from a mutant accumulates labelled GM2 ganglioside, adding functional hexosaminidase A plus the necessary GM2 activator protein in a suitable assay may restore degradation if that enzyme was the limiting defect. Failure of added enzyme alone to restore turnover could indicate an accessory protein or substrate-access problem. Such reasoning is stronger than simply associating a disorder's name with a lipid. It links the observed pool to the catalytic and physical conditions needed for its conversion.

7. Cholesterol synthesis: from two-carbon units to a C27 sterol

Cholesterol serves several distinct purposes. Its rigid ring system inserts among membrane phospholipids and modulates packing and permeability; it is also the starting material for steroid hormones and bile acids. Vitamin D₃ formation is connected to the sterol pathway through 7-dehydrocholesterol. Unlike a TAG, cholesterol cannot be efficiently converted into three fatty acids by ester hydrolysis. Esterification of its single hydroxyl group produces a cholesteryl ester, but hydrolysis of that ester merely restores cholesterol and one fatty acid. The ring skeleton remains.

Most nucleated mammalian cells can synthesise cholesterol. Liver is particularly important in whole-body production and disposal. Cytosolic acetyl-CoA supplied by citrate export and ATP-citrate lyase, among other routes, feeds a pathway associated with the cytosol and ER. The reducing power is largely NADPH, linking sterol synthesis to the pentose phosphate pathway and other NADPH-generating reactions. Unlike beta oxidation, this pathway consumes energy and reducing power to assemble a complex reduced carbon skeleton.

First, thiolase condenses two acetyl-CoA molecules to acetoacetyl-CoA. Cytosolic HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS1) adds a third acetyl-CoA to make 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA), a six-carbon thioester. HMG-CoA reductase, an ER membrane protein whose catalytic domain faces the cytosol, uses two NADPH to reduce the thioester to mevalonate and releases CoA. The net reaction, omitting ionisation details, is HMG-CoA + 2 NADPH + 2 H⁺ → mevalonate + CoA-SH + 2 NADP⁺. Reduction removes the activated thioester and commits carbon toward the mevalonate pathway, not exclusively cholesterol because other isoprenoid products also branch from it.

The name HMG-CoA also appears in ketone body formation. The apparent similarity can be misleading: mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS2) and HMG-CoA lyase support ketogenesis, whereas HMGCS1 and reductase support cytosolic sterol synthesis. Compartmentation and enzyme identity keep these pools and metabolic decisions distinct. The reductase does not generate acetoacetate, and mitochondrial ketogenesis is not a required intermediate of cholesterol synthesis.

The next challenge is to transform a relatively stable six-carbon alcohol into a reactive five-carbon unit. Mevalonate kinase uses ATP to form mevalonate-5-phosphate; phosphomevalonate kinase uses another ATP to form mevalonate-5-diphosphate. ATP-dependent mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase then generates isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP), with loss of CO₂ and inorganic phosphate. Three ATP are consumed per IPP generated from mevalonate. Isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase interconverts IPP and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP). These activated C5 compounds carry diphosphate leaving groups and can participate in carbon–carbon bond formation.

DMAPP combines with IPP to form geranyl diphosphate (GPP, C10), which combines with another IPP to form farnesyl diphosphate (FPP, C15). Each condensation releases diphosphate. Two FPP molecules undergo a reductive head-to-head condensation catalysed by squalene synthase, producing the C30 linear hydrocarbon squalene using NADPH. This differs from the earlier head-to-tail condensations. FPP is an important fork in the pathway: it also supports protein prenylation and longer isoprenoid products such as ubiquinone and dolichol. Therefore inhibiting an enzyme before FPP formation affects more branches than blocking squalene synthesis alone.

How does a flexible thirty-carbon chain become four fused rings? Squalene monooxygenase uses molecular oxygen and reducing equivalents to create 2,3-oxidosqualene. Lanosterol synthase initiates a coordinated cyclisation cascade through the activated epoxide, producing lanosterol. The folded precursor and enzyme active site guide formation of the sterol ring system with controlled stereochemistry. Lanosterol still contains thirty carbons and differs from cholesterol in methyl substitution, double-bond position and other structural features. Subsequent demethylation, reduction and double-bond rearrangements yield C27 cholesterol. Mammalian sterol synthesis may proceed through alternative sequences of late intermediates; desmosterol and 7-dehydrocholesterol are useful examples of final precursors in different branches. Memorising every late intermediate is unnecessary, but the carbon loss and chemical transformations are essential.

Sterol formation is oxygen dependent at multiple steps, especially squalene epoxidation and oxidative demethylation. Consequently a cell with plenty of acetyl-CoA and NADPH may still be unable to complete cholesterol production under severe oxygen limitation. This provides an exam-relevant distinction from pathways whose individual synthetic reactions do not directly consume O₂. In budding yeast, the related sterol product is ergosterol rather than cholesterol, and higher plants synthesise several phytosterols. The broad isoprenoid logic is shared, but terminal products and pathways differ among organisms.

Carbon and energy ledger

One squalene contains six C5 units, which require six mevalonate molecules. Since each mevalonate is assembled from three acetyl-CoA, eighteen acetyl-CoA supply 36 carbon atoms. Six carbon atoms are lost as six CO₂ molecules in the conversion from mevalonate to IPP. Three additional carbons are removed during conversion of the C30 sterol skeleton to C27 cholesterol. The carbon retention in cholesterol, relative to the eighteen acetyl-CoA inputs, is 27/36 = 75%. This is carbon recovery in the final sterol, not energy efficiency. The remaining carbon leaves in products of decarboxylation and demethylation.

At the mevalonate activation stage, six mevalonate × three ATP = eighteen ATP consumed for the six C5 units. Reductase uses six × two = twelve NADPH before IPP formation, and squalene synthase consumes a further NADPH. Further reducing equivalents and oxygen are needed for squalene epoxidation and subsequent sterol maturation. State the accounting boundary instead of quoting eighteen ATP or twelve NADPH as the total cellular energetic cost. Additional ATP may be spent on acetyl-CoA provision and regeneration of cofactors. This distinction often determines the correct answer in a numerical problem.

Classical acetate tracer studies established the assembly of sterol carbon from two-carbon precursors. In interpreting such experiments, acetate labelling alone cannot show that an intermediate belongs uniquely to cholesterol synthesis, since acetate also labels other lipids and acetyl-CoA-dependent pathways. A more informative design follows labelled acetate into mevalonate, squalene and sterols over time and uses selective enzyme inhibition. If a reductase block prevents label incorporation into sterol but exogenous mevalonate restores it, the rescuing precursor places the inhibited reaction upstream of mevalonate, assuming downstream enzymes and transport remain functional.

8. Regulating sterol supply: HMG-CoA reductase and feedback

A cell needs a relatively stable accessible cholesterol pool despite changing uptake and biosynthesis. Because excessive unesterified cholesterol can perturb membrane properties, making cholesterol at a constant maximum rate would be wasteful and potentially harmful. Control occurs on several time scales: phosphorylation alters catalytic activity, regulated transcription changes enzyme and receptor abundance, and sterol-dependent proteolysis changes enzyme lifetime.

The sterol regulatory element-binding protein SREBP-2 is initially synthesised as an ER membrane-associated precursor. When accessible ER sterol is low, its escort protein SCAP permits trafficking of the SREBP complex from ER to Golgi. Two proteolytic cleavages in the Golgi release the amino-terminal transcription factor. It enters the nucleus and increases expression of genes required for cholesterol biosynthesis and LDL uptake, including HMG-CoA reductase and LDL receptor. This is a coordinated response: both endogenous production and acquisition from extracellular lipoproteins rise when the cell senses a shortage.

When ER sterol rises, SCAP associates with Insig proteins and the SREBP complex is retained in the ER, reducing its processing and nuclear activity. Sterol and nonsterol products of the mevalonate pathway also contribute to accelerated degradation of HMG-CoA reductase, limiting further synthesis. Feedback operates through the regulatory machinery; it is misleading to describe cholesterol as simply a competitive inhibitor of the reductase catalytic site. A cell with high bulk cholesterol trapped in a lysosomal compartment may nevertheless have inadequate cholesterol delivery to the ER sensor. Total cell cholesterol is therefore not always a reliable prediction of SREBP activity.

Short-term regulation links biosynthesis to the energy state. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylates HMG-CoA reductase and decreases its activity, helping restrain an ATP-intensive biosynthetic pathway during energetic stress. Dephosphorylation can restore activity. The fed state generally favours synthesis, whereas energetic stress and fasting oppose many biosynthetic processes; actual enzyme activity depends on sterol supply, hormonal context and tissue. Increased transcription does not guarantee increased flux if the protein is catalytically inhibited or degraded.

Statins are a useful biochemical illustration of competitive inhibition: they bind HMG-CoA reductase and reduce mevalonate production. In liver, reduced accessible cholesterol can stimulate SREBP processing and LDL receptor expression, increasing removal of circulating LDL. Thus an inhibitor of intracellular synthesis can lower extracellular LDL cholesterol through a compensatory increase in uptake. It can also induce expression of the very enzyme it inhibits. This is not a contradiction; enzyme abundance and effective activity are different variables. The mechanism is discussed as pathway reasoning, not as guidance for medication.

Intracellular excess cholesterol can be stored as cholesteryl ester. Sterol O-acyltransferase (SOAT, historically ACAT) transfers an acyl group from acyl-CoA to cholesterol. This masks the hydroxyl group and promotes storage in neutral lipid droplets. Cholesteryl ester hydrolases release cholesterol when required. SOAT must be distinguished from thiolase or acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase, which may also be abbreviated ACAT in other contexts. Plasma lecithin:cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) is another distinct enzyme, using phosphatidylcholine rather than acyl-CoA as its acyl donor. We will return to it while following HDL.

Imagine a cultured cell exposed to a reductase inhibitor. After a short time its sterol synthesis declines, but after a longer period LDL receptor mRNA and reductase protein increase. This pattern is consistent with negative feedback. Providing cholesterol through a functional LDL receptor can suppress the transcriptional response, while providing mevalonate can bypass the inhibited enzymatic step. Neither rescue necessarily occurs if uptake or downstream transport is defective. A carefully designed experiment therefore measures the product pool, isotope flux and transcriptional response separately.

9. The lipid transport problem: lipoprotein structure and apolipoproteins

Blood is predominantly aqueous. Free long-chain fatty acids can circulate bound to albumin, but large amounts of TAG and cholesteryl ester need a different packaging system. Lipoproteins are particles with a neutral lipid-rich core and an amphipathic surface. Phospholipids, unesterified cholesterol and apolipoproteins cover the surface; TAG and cholesteryl ester occupy the hydrophobic interior. This is not a membrane-bound organelle and it is not a bilayer vesicle. The surface permits dispersion in plasma and provides binding sites for enzymes and receptors.

Density depends on particle composition. Protein has a greater density than neutral lipid, so particles containing a high proportion of TAG are relatively light and particles enriched in protein are relatively dense. Chylomicrons are very large and least dense, followed in increasing density by VLDL, IDL, LDL and HDL. This sequence describes typical classes, not a requirement that every individual particle move through all five classes. In particular, chylomicrons do not become LDL; they form remnants that are generally cleared by the liver. LDL normally derives from hepatic VLDL metabolism.

Apolipoproteins provide structural support or convey information about the particle to a receptor or enzyme. ApoB-48 is the nonexchangeable structural protein of intestinal chylomicrons; apoB-100 is the structural protein of hepatic VLDL and its IDL and LDL descendants. In humans the intestine edits apoB mRNA to introduce an earlier stop codon, forming the shorter apoB-48 protein. ApoB-48 lacks the carboxy-terminal region required for the normal apoB-100 interaction with LDL receptor. Each of these apoB-containing particles typically carries one apoB molecule, allowing apoB concentration to serve as an approximate marker of the number of atherogenic particles, rather than the exact cholesterol mass carried.

Exchangeable apolipoproteins have regulatory roles. ApoC-II activates LPL; apoC-III can inhibit TAG-rich particle metabolism and remnant clearance. ApoE provides a major recognition ligand for hepatic remnant uptake. ApoA-I is a major HDL structural protein and an activator of LCAT. These distinctions explain why one defective apolipoprotein can alter the distribution of plasma lipids despite normal catalytic enzymes. A loss of apoC-II prevents effective stimulation of LPL, while a loss of apoE-dependent remnant recognition affects a later step.

The term LDL cholesterol means the mass of cholesterol carried in the LDL fraction, not a chemically distinct type of cholesterol. HDL cholesterol likewise describes the carrier fraction. A higher cholesterol content per particle can raise measured LDL-C without a proportional increase in particle number. Conversely, numerous relatively cholesterol-poor particles can generate a modest LDL-C value. The distinction between cargo mass, particle number and flux will be useful when analysing experimental transport data.

10. Exogenous lipid pathway: from a meal to the liver and tissues

The dietary pathway begins before a lipoprotein appears. Bile salts emulsify lipid droplets and facilitate mixed micelle formation. Pancreatic lipase, aided by colipase, hydrolyses dietary TAG primarily at sn-1 and sn-3. Cholesteryl ester hydrolase and phospholipase activities help digest other lipid classes. The products enter mixed micelles containing bile salts, which improve delivery of poorly soluble lipids to the absorptive surface. The micelle is not the same as a chylomicron: it exists in the intestinal lumen and does not carry apoB.

Fatty acids and monoacylglycerols enter the enterocyte and are re-esterified. Cholesterol uptake involves the NPC1L1 transporter, and a portion is esterified by SOAT2. Microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP) helps load lipid onto nascent apoB-48 in the ER. Without effective lipidation, apoB is poorly assembled into a secretion-competent particle. Chylomicrons pass through the secretory pathway and enter intestinal lymph before reaching systemic blood. Many shorter-chain fatty acids are relatively water soluble and can reach the portal circulation without the same requirement for chylomicron assembly.

In the circulation, chylomicrons acquire apoC-II and apoE, in part through interactions with HDL. LPL produced by nearby tissues is presented at the luminal surface of capillary endothelial cells by GPIHBP1. Activated by apoC-II, LPL hydrolyses the particle's TAG and releases fatty acids that enter muscle for oxidation or adipose tissue for re-esterification. Particle TAG decreases and the remaining particle becomes smaller and relatively cholesterol enriched. Some surface phospholipids and exchangeable apolipoproteins return to HDL during remodelling.

The resulting remnant retains apoB-48 and apoE. ApoE-dependent recognition by hepatic receptors, including LDL receptor and related receptor systems, promotes its clearance. Cholesterol derived from diet thus reaches the liver even though apoB-48 lacks the normal LDL receptor-binding region of apoB-100. Hepatic uptake depends on remnant ligands, not on the assumption that every apoB species behaves identically.

An interruption at the LPL step leads to accumulation of TAG-rich particles and impaired fatty acid delivery, while a remnant uptake defect produces a different pattern: substantial lipolysis may occur, but remnants remain in circulation. Suppose two plasma samples have the same total TAG concentration. One is dominated by large nascent particles with little hydrolysis; the other contains an increased number of partially processed particles. Total TAG alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms. Particle fractionation, apolipoprotein identification and a time course of substrate disappearance supply more information.

11. Endogenous pathway and the LDL receptor discovery

Hepatocytes receive fatty acids from circulation and synthesise new ones from excess carbohydrate. They may oxidise acyl-CoA, store TAG, or export TAG in VLDL particles. MTP supports apoB-100 lipidation and VLDL assembly, conceptually paralleling the enterocyte pathway but using a different apoB form. In blood, LPL removes much of the VLDL TAG, yielding IDL. Some IDL is taken up by liver through apoE-dependent recognition; the remaining particles may undergo further TAG removal, in part by hepatic lipase, to form cholesterol-rich LDL. The same apoB-100 molecule persists as the particle changes composition.

LDL delivers cholesterol to cells through LDL receptor-mediated uptake. ApoB-100 binds the receptor, and receptor–particle complexes enter clathrin-coated vesicles. Acidification in endosomes promotes separation of receptor and ligand, allowing receptor recycling. LDL is directed towards lysosomes, where proteins and cholesteryl esters are hydrolysed. Free cholesterol is transferred out of lysosomes and distributed to cellular membranes and ER. The cell responds to the newly available sterol by reducing SREBP-2 processing and synthesis, lowering LDL receptor expression and, when appropriate, esterifying excess cholesterol.

Brown and Goldstein's studies of cultured fibroblasts from individuals with familial hypercholesterolaemia established the importance of a saturable receptor-dependent route and its connection to feedback regulation. Normal cells exposed to LDL show suppression of endogenous cholesterol synthesis. Cells with severe LDL receptor defects show impaired uptake and fail to respond normally to extracellular LDL, even though they may have functional intracellular cholesterol-sensing machinery. The finding established a causal distinction between failure to deliver cholesterol to the sensor and failure of the biosynthetic enzyme itself. The work was recognised by the 1985 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

An instructive experiment compares a labelled LDL binding assay performed at low temperature, where endocytosis is strongly reduced, with an uptake and degradation assay at physiological temperature. A receptor-binding defect lowers specific surface binding and downstream degradation. A receptor internalisation defect can preserve surface binding but reduce internalisation and degradation. A lysosomal processing defect may permit internalisation but impair release of usable cholesterol. A single uptake measurement cannot separate all these steps.

An idealised binding model for one class of independent binding sites is B = Bmax × L/(Kd + L), where B is specifically bound LDL, Bmax is the maximal binding capacity, L is free LDL concentration and Kd is the concentration yielding half-maximal binding. If Bmax = 100 fmol/mg protein and Kd = 10 nM, then at L = 10 nM the predicted specific binding is 50 fmol/mg. A receptor-number defect that halves Bmax with unchanged Kd gives 25 fmol/mg at the same ligand concentration and a plateau of 50 rather than 100. A change in affinity instead shifts the concentration at which half-maximal binding occurs. This equilibrium model assumes independent sites and free ligand known; an uptake flux also depends on recycling and internalisation, so binding alone cannot predict it.

PCSK9 binds LDL receptor and favours its lysosomal degradation rather than recycling. Increasing PCSK9 activity can reduce surface receptor number and slow LDL clearance; reducing its activity can increase receptor recycling. This is another illustration of the distinction between receptor production and receptor lifetime. Similarly, a patient or experimental system with normal receptor mRNA can have low functional receptor abundance at the cell surface.

A simple steady-state mass balance makes the transport consequence quantitative. For a well-mixed LDL pool M, constant input J and first-order fractional clearance k, dM/dt = J − kM. At steady state dM/dt = 0, so M = J/k. If J = 120 arbitrary mass units/day and k = 0.4/day, M = 300 units. Halving k to 0.2/day at unchanged input doubles the steady pool to 600 units. This is a model of whole-pool turnover, not a claim that biological LDL clearance is always strictly first order. It explains why impaired clearance can elevate LDL even when production is unchanged.

12. HDL, cholesterol esterification and reverse cholesterol transport

Many peripheral cells need cholesterol, but they also need a way to remove excess cholesterol that is not consumed in steroid synthesis. HDL participates in reverse cholesterol transport, the net movement of cholesterol from peripheral tissues toward the liver for redistribution or elimination. The early step is particularly important because unesterified cholesterol is amphipathic and can leave membranes only when an acceptor and appropriate transport machinery are available.

Nascent lipid-poor apoA-I secreted by liver and intestine accepts phospholipid and cholesterol through the ATP-dependent transporter ABCA1. The resulting discoidal particles can acquire additional cholesterol from other routes, including ABCG1-mediated transfer to mature HDL and passive exchange. ABCA1 deficiency markedly compromises nascent HDL formation and can lead to accumulation of cholesterol in tissues. This observation supports the idea that cholesterol efflux is an organised cellular process, not merely spontaneous dissolution of a lipid in water.

LCAT acts on the lipoprotein surface, using the sn-2 fatty acyl group of PC to esterify the hydroxyl group of cholesterol. The products are cholesteryl ester and lysophosphatidylcholine. ApoA-I stimulates the reaction. The newly formed cholesteryl ester is more hydrophobic than free cholesterol and moves into the HDL core. This promotes maturation from discoidal to more spherical particles and reduces free cholesterol at the particle surface, helping support further acceptance. Unlike intracellular SOAT, LCAT uses a phospholipid rather than fatty acyl-CoA and functions in plasma.

HDL cholesteryl esters can reach the liver through selective uptake mediated by scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI). In this process the cell takes up lipid cargo without obligatorily degrading the entire HDL particle. Another route, especially important in humans, uses cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) to exchange HDL cholesteryl esters for TAG in apoB-containing particles; those particles can later be cleared through hepatic receptors. Rodents such as laboratory mice do not naturally express appreciable plasma CETP, so transport mechanisms inferred from mice cannot always be mapped directly onto humans.

Reverse cholesterol transport requires a final destination. If hepatic cholesterol is simply resecreted in another lipoprotein, peripheral efflux need not produce net loss of cholesterol from the organism. Conversion to bile acids and excretion of neutral sterols in bile and faeces provide major elimination pathways. Thus HDL-C, a static measurement of cholesterol mass in HDL, is not identical to HDL-mediated cholesterol flux or whole-body sterol disposal. An increase in HDL-C can occur because cholesterol entry increases or because removal from HDL slows. A tracer tracking movement from peripheral cells into hepatic and faecal pools is a better direct test of reverse transport than HDL-C concentration alone.

Consider a model with 40 nmol of labelled cholesterol initially in macrophages. After one hour, 12 nmol appears in HDL, 3 nmol in other extracellular fractions and 25 nmol remains in cells. Total measured recovery is 40 nmol. Cell efflux over that interval is (12 + 3)/40 = 37.5%; HDL-associated fraction of released label is 12/15 = 80%. Neither number tells whether the cholesterol eventually leaves the body. If a liver acceptor subsequently receives 8 nmol of HDL label, that is 8/12 = 66.7% of the HDL-associated pool in the simplified experiment, not 66.7% of the starting macrophage pool.

13. Bile acids: converting a sterol into an amphipathic detergent

Mammals cannot completely degrade the cholesterol ring system to CO₂ and water as they do many fatty acids. They instead eliminate cholesterol as free sterol or transform it into bile acids. This provides both an exit for sterol carbon and a solution to another problem: digestion and absorption of dietary hydrophobic molecules. Bile acids have a steroid nucleus bearing hydroxyl groups and a side chain ending in an acidic group. Their asymmetric hydrophilic and hydrophobic surfaces permit interaction with both water and lipid droplets.

In the classical hepatic pathway cholesterol 7-alpha-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) catalyses the initial major regulated step, introducing a hydroxyl group at carbon 7. Subsequent reactions modify the steroid nucleus and shorten the C27 side chain to a C24 bile acid. Cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid are major human primary bile acids. Sterol 12-alpha-hydroxylase (CYP8B1) contributes to formation of cholic acid; relative activity of this enzyme helps determine the ratio of the two primary products. An alternative pathway begins with sterol side-chain hydroxylation by CYP27A1 and involves CYP7B1. It contributes to the bile acid pool but the classical pathway is the main teaching route in normal adult human liver.

Before secretion, primary bile acids are commonly conjugated to glycine or taurine through an amide linkage at the side-chain carboxyl group. Conjugation lowers the effective pKa and promotes ionisation at intestinal pH, yielding bile salts that are effective amphipathic molecules. In the gut these compounds help disperse fat and form mixed micelles with digestion products, cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins. They are not digestive enzymes: they do not catalyse ester hydrolysis. Instead they improve substrate presentation to lipases and transport of products to the brush border.

Gut microorganisms can deconjugate bile salts and chemically modify primary bile acids. In particular, 7-alpha-dehydroxylation of cholic acid yields deoxycholic acid, and that of chenodeoxycholic acid yields lithocholic acid. These secondary bile acids may be reabsorbed to different extents. The hepatic pathway therefore produces primary bile acids; microbial transformations account for important secondary bile acids. The organism's lipid metabolism depends partly on reactions outside its own cells.

Most bile salts are recovered in the intestine, especially by active uptake in the terminal ileum, and return through portal blood to the liver. Hepatocytes extract and resecrete them. This enterohepatic circulation reuses a relatively small bile acid pool several times. The small fraction lost in faeces is replenished by new synthesis from cholesterol. Pool cycling and net loss must be distinguished. A molecule can participate in multiple rounds of absorption without being newly synthesised each time.

The bile acid pool also regulates its own production. Bile acids activate the nuclear receptor FXR, which induces hepatic regulatory responses that suppress CYP7A1 expression. Intestinal FXR activation promotes production of FGF19 in humans (FGF15 in mice); the signal reaches liver and suppresses bile acid synthesis. This gut–liver feedback prevents excessive conversion when adequate bile acids are returning. Reduced ileal return can lower this feedback and stimulate hepatic synthesis, provided the liver retains adequate function and substrates.

For a simplified numerical illustration, assume a bile acid pool of 3 g, six complete circulations per day and 95% reabsorption in each circulation. Gross intestinal delivery is 18 g/day if the pool is maintained. The loss is 0.05 × 18 = 0.9 g/day, and at steady state synthesis must replace 0.9 g/day. If recovery falls to 90% at the same delivery, loss doubles to 1.8 g/day. These calculations assume a constant maintained pool and circulation rate; without replacement the pool shrinks and simple multiplication no longer applies. The model separates gross recycling from net synthesis and explains why interruption of recycling increases the need for cholesterol conversion.

Another sterol fate is formation of steroid hormones. Cholesterol side-chain cleavage in steroidogenic mitochondria produces pregnenolone, a C21 precursor. Different tissues then use distinct enzyme sets to make glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids and sex steroids. In skin, UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D₃, which rearranges to vitamin D₃; later hydroxylation forms active vitamin D derivatives. The detailed endocrine pathways and vitamin metabolism belong to their respective nodes. The present lesson is that cholesterol and its biosynthetic intermediates feed multiple products, whereas bile acid and neutral sterol excretion supply major routes for net elimination.

14. Eicosanoids: releasing a membrane precursor to make a local signal

Storage lipids answer the question of fuel availability; phospholipids answer the question of membrane organisation. Some membrane acyl chains also form an inactive reservoir of potential signals. Arachidonic acid (20:4, n-6) is often esterified at the sn-2 position of membrane phospholipids. It can be obtained from diet or produced from essential linoleic acid through elongation and desaturation. In mammals, the absence of the enzymes needed to introduce the n-6 or n-3 double-bond families from scratch makes dietary essential fatty acids the ultimate precursors of many polyunsaturated signalling lipids.

When a cell is stimulated, cytosolic phospholipase A₂ can release arachidonic acid. Calcium and phosphorylation help regulate this step in suitable cells. The free fatty acid may be re-esterified, or enter oxygenase pathways to generate short-lived signalling molecules collectively referred to as eicosanoids. They generally act close to their site of production, often through cell-surface receptors, and are rapidly metabolised. Rather than storing large pools of active prostaglandins, cells regulate their synthesis on demand. This limits the spatial and temporal range of the response.

Cyclooxygenase enzymes COX-1 and COX-2 have two coupled catalytic activities. Their cyclooxygenase reaction oxygenates and cyclises arachidonic acid to PGG₂, which contains a hydroperoxide group. Their peroxidase reaction reduces PGG₂ to PGH₂. PGH₂ is a common precursor, not a single final signal. Tissue-specific terminal synthases convert it into prostaglandins such as PGE₂, PGD₂ and PGF₂α, prostacyclin (PGI₂) and thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂). These products share a twenty-carbon origin but differ structurally and act on distinct receptors.

A particularly useful physiological example is the balance between platelet TXA₂ and endothelial PGI₂. Platelet thromboxane favours aggregation and vasoconstriction; prostacyclin generally opposes platelet aggregation and promotes vasodilation. Opposing products can be formed from the same precursor because different tissues express different terminal synthases. PGE₂ contributes to pain sensitisation, fever and several homeostatic responses; its action depends on the receptor subtype and tissue. COX-1 is frequently constitutive and supports homeostatic processes, while COX-2 is often induced during inflammation, but both can have constitutive or regulated expression depending on the tissue.

The lipoxygenase pathway does not pass through PGH₂. In leukocytes, 5-lipoxygenase with its activating protein generates 5-HPETE and then the epoxide LTA₄. LTA₄ hydrolase forms LTB₄, which promotes leukocyte recruitment. Alternatively, addition of glutathione forms LTC₄; subsequent processing yields LTD₄ and LTE₄. These cysteinyl leukotrienes can contribute to bronchoconstriction and vascular permeability. Other lipoxygenases participate in the formation of lipoxins, some of which support resolution of inflammation. Cytochrome P450 enzymes also generate oxygenated arachidonic acid derivatives including epoxyeicosatrienoic acids. Their detailed receptor pharmacology is unnecessary for identifying the metabolic branches.

Alternative polyunsaturated precursors produce different mediator series. EPA (20:5, n-3) can yield series-3 prostanoids and series-5 leukotrienes, rather than the common arachidonic acid-derived series-2 prostanoids and series-4 leukotrienes. Their functional effects are not uniformly identical or uniformly opposite to those of arachidonic acid products. Substrate competition and enzyme preference affect product profiles. DHA-derived docosanoids have twenty-two carbons and should not all be classified strictly as eicosanoids, whose name refers to twenty-carbon fatty acid derivatives.

Aspirin illustrates the logic of inhibiting an upstream shared enzyme. It acetylates cyclooxygenase and inhibits conventional prostanoid production. Platelets have limited ability to replace the inhibited enzyme because they lack nuclei, whereas nucleated endothelial cells can synthesise new enzyme. Most other conventional NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase reversibly. In contrast, a 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor directly reduces leukotriene production rather than PGH₂ production. Blocking PLA₂-dependent precursor liberation can restrict multiple branches, although alternative precursor release and metabolic compensation complicate the actual response.

Consider cells given equal arachidonic acid input. In an untreated sample, production of prostanoids and leukotrienes is 60 and 30 pmol/min respectively. After a selective COX inhibitor, prostanoids fall to 6 and leukotrienes remain 30 pmol/min. The 90% decrease in prostanoid production is consistent with a COX block. It does not establish that all remaining arachidonic acid has been diverted to lipoxygenase: reacylation, other oxygenases and changes in free substrate pools may account for the difference. If leukotrienes instead rise, substrate redistribution is a plausible hypothesis but must be tested by measuring precursor pools and relevant enzyme activities. This is the same mass-balance discipline used in TAG mobilisation.

15. Integration: following carbon through tissues and compartments

Now return to the original problem of allocating carbon. After a carbohydrate-rich meal, insulin promotes glucose utilisation, production of G3P and lipid storage in adipose tissue. Liver can channel citrate-derived acetyl-CoA into fatty acid and sterol synthesis, while exporting some newly assembled TAG through VLDL. Dietary fat arrives separately through chylomicrons. Membrane biosynthesis continues as needed, supported by shared pools of activated fatty acids, glycerolipids and nucleotide cofactors. The sources of energy and membrane lipid are connected, but they are not the same as an unregulated single pool.

During fasting, lower insulin and sympathetic signals increase adipose lipolysis. Albumin-bound fatty acids supply oxidation in muscle and liver, and glycerol supplies hepatic gluconeogenesis. The liver can generate ketone bodies from mitochondrial acetyl-CoA when conditions favour ketogenesis. Cytosolic cholesterol synthesis is separately regulated by energy and sterol signals; it does not automatically rise just because mitochondrial acetyl-CoA rises. Meanwhile bile salt recycling continues, membranes turn over and local signalling lipids are produced when specific stimuli occur.

Two competing effects can occur in the same tissue. A liver receiving excessive fatty acids may esterify them to TAG as a way to buffer acyl-CoA and free fatty acid pools. If TAG synthesis exceeds oxidation of its acyl chains and VLDL export over time, hepatic neutral lipid accumulates. Inhibiting TAG assembly might reduce the TAG pool while increasing other lipid intermediates such as DAG or ceramide, depending on pathway fluxes. Therefore an increased storage lipid pool does not always mean the storage pathway itself is the primary source of metabolic harm; storage can also buffer more reactive intermediates.

To interpret pathway data, establish which molecules and compartments were measured, choose the relevant mass balance and identify whether the intervention changes input, output or both. A labelled acyl-CoA can enter TAG, PC or ceramide; a labelled serine can enter PS as a head group or supply the sphingoid base after decarboxylation. A labelled choline can enter PC and later sphingomyelin through phosphocholine transfer. The same radioactive label appearing in two lipid classes need not mean one intact lipid was converted into the other. Chemical separation of head groups, chains and backbones resolves such ambiguities.

Imagine an experiment that reduces GPAT activity, DGAT activity or serine palmitoyltransferase activity in separate otherwise comparable cell samples. A GPAT defect restricts the first committed acylation of G3P and can influence PA-derived TAG and phospholipids. A DGAT defect specifically limits DAG-to-TAG conversion; DAG may be diverted to membrane lipids, subject to regulation. A serine palmitoyltransferase defect restricts de novo sphingoid base production but does not directly block glycerolipid synthesis; salvage may partly maintain ceramide. Predicting the first direct consequence is safer than assuming a downstream lipid must always rise or fall regardless of compensation.

The same reasoning applies to cholesterol metabolism. Reductase inhibition reduces mevalonate pathway flux, LDL receptor impairment reduces cholesterol uptake, and CYP7A1 inhibition reduces classical bile acid synthesis. All three affect the sterol balance, but their direct substrates and products are different. A measured cholesterol accumulation is compatible with increased synthesis, increased uptake, reduced esterification or reduced disposal, depending on which cholesterol fraction and compartment is measured. The mechanistic evidence must select among these explanations.

16. Experimental logic: distinguishing metabolic alternatives

An enzyme inhibitor is useful when its specificity is known, but an observed fall in product concentration does not by itself identify the blocked reaction. For example, lower sphingomyelin can result from reduced ceramide synthesis, impaired phosphocholine transfer or faster sphingomyelin hydrolysis. Measure labelled incorporation into ceramide and sphingomyelin as well as the pre-existing pools. If newly labelled ceramide accumulates while labelled sphingomyelin formation falls, a downstream conversion is plausible. If both labelling rates fall, an upstream substrate-supply defect is plausible. Changes in pool size and isotope dilution still need to be considered.

The same logic applies to rescue experiments. Mevalonate can bypass HMG-CoA reductase but cannot bypass a block in squalene synthase. Supplying exogenous cholesterol can restore membrane sterol without restoring all nonsterol isoprenoid products. Supplying a preformed lipid may also fail if it cannot reach the proper membrane compartment. A valid rescue experiment should therefore include controls for uptake, cell viability and a relevant downstream marker. A rescue demonstrates that a particular input is sufficient under the tested conditions, not necessarily that the original drug binds only one protein.

A particularly useful lipid experiment compares a backbone tracer with a head-group or acyl-chain tracer. Suppose cells receive labelled glycerol for a short pulse, followed by unlabelled glycerol during a chase. The initial PA label is followed by DAG, TAG and phospholipid label. If TAG label rises while PA label falls, this timing supports precursor–product transfer, but shared entry into several pools prevents a simple proof based only on timing. A selective DGAT block, combined with fractionation of lipids and knowledge of tracer dilution, provides stronger evidence that a fraction of the labelled DAG becomes TAG.

For transport studies, a labelled lipid cargo and a separately labelled apolipoprotein can distinguish selective uptake from whole-particle degradation. If a liver cell receives cholesteryl ester label from HDL while most protein label remains extracellular, the result is consistent with selective lipid uptake. If both labels are internalised together and the protein is degraded, whole-particle uptake is more likely. Appropriate controls exclude label exchange into other particles and unequal stability of the labels. This distinction gives a direct experimental interpretation of the difference between SR-BI-mediated lipid transfer and classical LDL receptor uptake.

For exam purposes, the strongest approach is to draw the smallest relevant reaction network, mark the directly inhibited arrow, and then reason through competing branches. Specify whether the prediction refers to the initial response or a later steady state. A substrate may accumulate initially but be depleted later if feedback suppresses its production. A product may decline immediately and partially recover through an alternative pathway. In this way the same chemical knowledge becomes a tool for solving unfamiliar Part C questions instead of a list of pathways to memorise.

  1. PLA₁
  2. PLA₂
  3. PLC
  4. PLD
Answer and reasoning

B. PLA₂ hydrolyses the sn-2 ester. A₁ acts on sn-1; C and D cleave phosphodiester linkages.

Question 6

Which pair correctly matches acyl donor and esterifying enzyme?

  1. LCAT—acyl-CoA
  2. SOAT—PC
  3. LCAT—PC
  4. SOAT—ATP
Answer and reasoning

C. LCAT transfers an acyl chain from PC. SOAT instead uses acyl-CoA; ATP is not an acyl donor.

Question 7

Which enzyme generates mevalonate?

  1. HMG-CoA lyase
  2. HMG-CoA reductase
  3. Squalene synthase
  4. CYP7A1
Answer and reasoning

B. Reductase uses NADPH to reduce HMG-CoA. Lyase belongs to ketogenesis, squalene synthase acts on FPP, CYP7A1 initiates classic bile acid synthesis.

Question 8

Which particle normally contains apoB-48?

  1. Chylomicron
  2. LDL
  3. HDL
  4. VLDL
Answer and reasoning

A. Intestinal apoB editing gives B-48. Hepatic VLDL and LDL carry B-100; HDL primarily carries apoA-I.

Question 9

Which apolipoprotein activates LPL?

  1. ApoA-I
  2. ApoB-48
  3. ApoC-II
  4. ApoE
Answer and reasoning

C. ApoC-II stimulates LPL. ApoA-I activates LCAT, apoB-48 is structural and apoE supports remnant uptake.

Question 10

Assertion: Cholesterol and ketone synthesis both involve HMG-CoA. Reason: HMG-CoA reductase catalyses mitochondrial ketone body formation.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

C. Different HMG-CoA pools participate in both routes, but reductase produces cytosolic mevalonate; mitochondrial lyase produces acetoacetate.

Question 11

Assertion: Bile salts support fat digestion without hydrolysing TAG. Reason: Their amphipathic structure helps emulsify lipid and form mixed micelles.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Their physical action aids lipases and absorption; the ester cleavage itself requires an enzyme.

Question 12

Assertion: LDL-C and HDL-C contain chemically distinct forms of cholesterol solely because they are carried by different particles. Reason: LDL and HDL differ in composition and metabolic pathways.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

D. Carrier classes differ, but cholesterol molecules are not different chemical species merely because of the carrier.

18. Part C practice: quantitative and experimental reasoning

Use the assumptions stated in each question. Distinguish initial effects from long-term compensation and count only the energy inputs specified.

Question 13

Four TAG molecules are synthesised from glycerol and twelve free fatty acids. Count direct ATP-equivalent expenditure if glycerol kinase and acyl-CoA synthetase are the only nucleotide-consuming preparation steps.

  1. 12
  2. 16
  3. 24
  4. 28
Answer and reasoning

D. Twelve activations cost 24 equivalents (ATP to AMP); four glycerol phosphorylation steps cost four ATP. Total 28. Twelve or sixteen ignore the second equivalent per activation; 24 omits glycerol kinase.

Question 14

Cells hydrolyse 20 nmol TAG/min completely. Glycerol exits quantitatively and 42 nmol fatty acid/min exits. Assuming no oxidation, what fraction of released fatty acids is re-esterified?

  1. 18%
  2. 30%
  3. 42%
  4. 70%
Answer and reasoning

B. Complete hydrolysis produces 60 nmol FA/min. Missing FA = 18 nmol/min; 18/60 = 30%. 70% is the exported fraction; 18 is the absolute amount, not percent.

Question 15

A mutant has normal labelled G3P entry into PA, increased labelled DAG and strongly reduced labelled TAG. Which direct enzyme defect best explains these data?

  1. GPAT
  2. DGAT
  3. AGPAT
  4. Serine palmitoyltransferase
Answer and reasoning

B. DAG is made but not converted efficiently to TAG. GPAT and AGPAT defects would impair PA labelling; SPT belongs to sphingolipid synthesis.

Question 16

Labelled phosphocholine is transferred from PC to sphingolipid while labelled glycerol remains in a DAG fraction. Which reaction fits?

  1. Sphingomyelin synthase
  2. Ceramidase
  3. Glucosylceramide synthase
  4. PLA₂
Answer and reasoning

A. Phosphocholine moves from PC to ceramide, leaving DAG. Ceramidase removes an amide-linked acyl chain; glucosylceramide synthase transfers glucose; PLA₂ removes an acyl chain.

Question 17

A C18 sphingoid base is derived from C16 palmitoyl-CoA and C3 serine. How many carbons from serine remain and what is the total carbon number after N-acylation by C22 acyl-CoA?

  1. 3 and 41
  2. 2 and 40
  3. 1 and 39
  4. 2 and 22
Answer and reasoning

B. One serine carbon leaves as CO₂; 16 + 2 = 18. Adding C22 gives C40. Amide formation does not remove a carbon from the acyl donor.

Question 18

How many acetyl-CoA and ATP molecules are required respectively to supply six IPP units by the mevalonate route, counting ATP only in conversion of mevalonate to IPP?

  1. 6 and 18
  2. 18 and 6
  3. 18 and 18
  4. 27 and 36
Answer and reasoning

C. Each mevalonate requires three acetyl-CoA; each IPP requires three ATP after mevalonate. Six times each gives 18 and 18; later reactions and acetyl-CoA provision are outside the stated boundary.

Question 19

A selective reductase inhibitor lowers acetate labelling of cholesterol. Mevalonate restores labelling of downstream sterols when itself labelled, but addition of acetyl-CoA does not. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The block is downstream of lanosterol
  2. Mevalonate bypasses the affected step
  3. CYP7A1 is directly inhibited
  4. The cell cannot synthesise IPP
Answer and reasoning

B. Supplying the product of the blocked reductase bypasses that reaction if later enzymes work. The other explanations contradict the rescue or identify an unrelated step. Substrate entry must be controlled.

Question 20

Cells with high total cholesterol have low accessible ER cholesterol because lysosomal export is blocked. What is a plausible regulatory response?

  1. SREBP-2 remains activated despite high total sterol
  2. HMG-CoA reductase expression must fall to zero
  3. LDL receptor necessarily disappears
  4. LCAT becomes an ER enzyme
Answer and reasoning

A. The ER sensor responds to accessible local sterol. Neither total abundance nor trapped sterol guarantees feedback suppression. LCAT remains extracellular.

Question 21

At free LDL concentration 20 nM, Bmax = 90 fmol/mg and Kd = 10 nM. Under a one-site equilibrium model, calculate B and B after a 50% reduction in receptor number with unchanged Kd.

  1. 30 and 15
  2. 60 and 30
  3. 90 and 45
  4. 45 and 22.5
Answer and reasoning

B. B = 90 × 20/(10 + 20) = 60. The new Bmax is 45 and B = 30. The plateau values are not binding at finite L; 45 is not half occupancy at L = 20.

Question 22

LDL input is 100 units/day and fractional clearance is 0.5/day. If clearance falls to 0.25/day at unchanged input, how does the steady pool change?

  1. 100 to 200
  2. 200 to 400
  3. 50 to 25
  4. 400 to 200
Answer and reasoning

B. M = J/k. Initially 100/0.5 = 200; subsequently 100/0.25 = 400. Lower clearance raises pool size, not lowers it.

Question 23

A cultured liver cell takes up HDL cholesteryl ester label with little uptake of separately labelled apoA-I. Which mechanism is favoured?

  1. Selective uptake through SR-BI
  2. Complete degradation of every HDL particle
  3. LDL receptor recognition of B-100 on HDL
  4. Pancreatic lipase activity
Answer and reasoning

A. Cargo transfer with little protein uptake supports selective uptake. Whole-particle degradation predicts protein entry; HDL does not normally have apoB-100 as its structural protein; pancreatic lipase is luminal.

Question 24

A maintained bile acid pool cycles 5 times/day, delivering 15 g/day total. If 92% is recovered, what new synthesis is necessary at steady state?

  1. 0.24 g/day
  2. 0.8 g/day
  3. 1.2 g/day
  4. 13.8 g/day
Answer and reasoning

C. Loss is 8% of 15 = 1.2 g/day. 13.8 is recovered material; 0.24 is loss per 3-g pool cycle.

Question 25

A block of CYP8B1 most directly changes which relationship?

  1. Cholic acid versus chenodeoxycholic acid synthesis
  2. TAG versus DAG hydrolysis
  3. IPP versus DMAPP isomerisation
  4. LCAT versus SOAT location
Answer and reasoning

A. CYP8B1 supplies 12-alpha hydroxylation important for cholic acid formation. The other relationships concern separate enzyme families.

Question 26

Cells receive equal arachidonic acid. A selective 5-LOX inhibitor reduces LTB₄ and LTC₄ but leaves PGI₂ unchanged. What follows?

  1. COX must also be inhibited
  2. LTA₄-derived products require the 5-LOX branch
  3. All released fatty acid becomes PGI₂
  4. PLA₂ cleaves the head group
Answer and reasoning

B. LTA₄ is generated through 5-LOX; PGI₂ arises through COX and terminal synthase. There is no evidence for all carbon being diverted or head-group cleavage.

Question 27

A mutant binds normal amounts of LDL at low temperature but internalises little at physiological temperature. Which defect is most consistent?

  1. Failure of receptor internalisation
  2. Complete absence of surface receptor
  3. Complete lack of apoB in the added ligand
  4. Increased LDL synthesis
Answer and reasoning

A. Binding is retained but transport is impaired. Lack of receptor or ligand recognition would lower binding; synthesis does not explain handling of externally added labelled LDL.

Question 28

A cell loses lysosomal GM2 degradation despite having normal measurable hexosaminidase A activity on a soluble artificial substrate. Which additional defect is plausible?

  1. GM2 activator protein
  2. Choline kinase
  3. CYP7A1
  4. DGAT
Answer and reasoning

A. Presentation of membrane GM2 to hexosaminidase A requires the activator. Soluble artificial substrate testing may bypass that need. The other enzymes do not perform this lysosomal reaction.

Question 29

During a chase, label in the sn-2 acyl chain of PC declines while a separately labelled PC head group remains. What mechanism is most directly consistent?

  1. Lands cycle deacylation/reacylation
  2. Complete cleavage of every PC into glycerol
  3. Conversion of all PC to cholesterol
  4. De novo purine synthesis
Answer and reasoning

A. Chain replacement can preserve the head and backbone. Total degradation would also remove the labelled head from PC; the other pathways do not explain selective chain turnover.

Question 30

A mutation decreases LPL activity but leaves hepatic apoE-mediated uptake intact. Which immediate change is expected after a meal?

  1. Impaired clearance of TAG from chylomicrons
  2. Failure of bile acid conjugation
  3. Increased conversion of all chylomicrons directly to LDL
  4. Direct block of intracellular HSL
Answer and reasoning

A. LPL is required for capillary TAG hydrolysis; intact remnant recognition cannot substitute for that step. The other choices conflate independent routes.

19. Consolidation and the next chapter

The major organising principle is molecular allocation. Activated acyl groups can be stored as TAG, used to form membrane glycerolipids or attached to a sphingoid base. Phosphatidic acid and DAG are key glycerolipid branch points; ceramide is a separate branch point for sphingolipids. Cholesterol is constructed from acetyl-CoA through the mevalonate pathway, in which reduction, nucleotide activation, condensation and cyclisation successively change the properties of the intermediate. Its synthesis and uptake are coordinated by sterol sensing, while conversion into bile acids and excretion help control the whole-body pool.

For revision, try to reconstruct the pathways without a diagram. Explain why G3P is necessary for TAG assembly, why ATGL and LPL are not interchangeable, why phosphatidylcholine can donate phosphocholine to sphingomyelin, and why a reductase inhibitor can increase LDL receptor expression. Then trace the two main apoB particle pathways and the role of HDL in cholesterol efflux. Finally, derive the C36-to-C27 carbon balance and distinguish direct ATP costs from upstream energetic expenditure. These exercises connect the reaction chemistry to its biological meaning.

The next chapter follows amino acid carbon skeletons and nitrogen. Several connections are already visible: serine supplies the sphingoid backbone and PS head group, methionine-derived SAM supplies methyl groups for PE methylation, and glycine or taurine conjugates bile acids. Unlike glycerolipids, amino acids carry nitrogen that must be transferred, reused or safely eliminated. The next stage of metabolic study will therefore extend carbon accounting into nitrogen homeostasis.
