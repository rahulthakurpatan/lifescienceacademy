1. Light as developmental information

A newly germinated seedling encounters a problem that an adult green leaf rarely faces. Its stored reserves are finite, its photosynthetic apparatus is immature, and it may be buried below a layer of soil. While underground, rapid extension of the hypocotyl can help bring its shoot toward the surface. Once the shoot is exposed, continued investment in length at the expense of leaf expansion becomes less advantageous. The emerging plant must redirect development toward opening its cotyledons, assembling functional chloroplasts and capturing light. The transition requires a mechanism capable of detecting illumination before the seedling has become a fully photosynthetic organism. Photoreceptors supply that information.

The hypocotyl is the embryonic stem region between the root and cotyledons. In a typical dark-grown Arabidopsis seedling, it becomes long, the cotyledons remain relatively closed and an apical hook protects the shoot tip. This developmental pattern is known as skotomorphogenesis or etiolation. Illumination initiates de-etiolation, a coordinated transformation that reduces hypocotyl elongation, opens the hook and expands the cotyledons. Chloroplast development and pigment accumulation occur in parallel. Photomorphogenesis refers more broadly to development regulated by light signals; it is not simply another name for photosynthesis.

The distinction between energy and information is experimentally important. A photon absorbed by a photosynthetic reaction center can initiate charge separation and contribute to the production of reducing power or an ion gradient. A photon absorbed by a cryptochrome can alter the activity of a protein that controls development. Both processes begin with light absorption, but their outputs differ fundamentally. Cryptochrome does not transfer electrons into photosystem II, does not pump protons across the thylakoid membrane and does not replace ferredoxin–NADP reductase. It influences gene expression and cellular behaviour through signaling. Even a relatively small light input can modify the regulatory state of many downstream molecules, so the number of photons absorbed by a sensor need not match the total metabolic energy required for the resulting growth.

Light carries several kinds of information. Wavelength distinguishes spectral components; photon flux density describes how many photons arrive per unit area per unit time; duration indicates how long the stimulus persists; direction describes its spatial distribution. These variables are not interchangeable. A brief intense exposure and a long weak exposure can supply the same total number of photons but produce different regulatory states if the receptor is continually turning on and off. Cryptochrome is particularly relevant to blue and near-ultraviolet wavelengths, although its physiological response cannot be predicted from wavelength alone. Blue light also activates phototropins, while red and far-red wavelengths engage phytochromes. Several receptors may therefore be active simultaneously in daylight.

This chapter uses Arabidopsis thaliana as the principal experimental model. It is a small flowering plant with well-characterized mutants and short generation time. The underlying logic is useful across flowering plants, but gene family sizes and relative receptor contributions vary among species. The behavior of a particular Arabidopsis allele is evidence about that system under the tested conditions, not a rule that every plant species must follow.

The apparent paradox of a light-dependent response before extensive photosynthesis can be resolved by considering the seed as an initial source of chemical energy. Germination mobilizes stored compounds, and respiration can supply ATP to cells in darkness. A photoreceptor need not provide the ATP required for new protein synthesis; it tells an already metabolically active cell which proteins and growth programs to favor. The signaling pathway may actually consume ATP through phosphorylation, protein turnover and biosynthesis. When photosynthesis becomes established, it supplies an additional source of chemical energy, but the logical distinction between sensing and metabolism remains. A light sensor can work in cells that are not themselves carrying out substantial photosynthesis, and a photosynthetic cell can use several types of light information simultaneously.

The developmental response depends on the duration for which growth is observed. Hypocotyl length is a cumulative variable. At any given time its rate of change reflects the expansion of cells along the organ. If one seedling elongates at 0.3 mm per hour and another at 0.1 mm per hour over the same eight-hour interval, the difference in length added during that interval is 1.6 mm. The final lengths may also reflect differences that existed before the treatment. To measure an acute light response, record the change from each seedling's own starting length rather than relying exclusively on final length. To measure a sustained developmental phenotype, use a common germination time and compare organs at the same age.

An instructive distinction is between competence and stimulus. An individual plant cell may contain CRY protein but lack the signaling partners or growth machinery required for the response measured in another tissue. Mature cells with limited expansion capacity cannot behave like elongating hypocotyl cells merely because they receive the same photon flux. Similarly, a photoreceptor that is expressed in a leaf may influence gene transcription involved in floral induction, whereas the same receptor family in a seedling affects growth. The stimulus is shared, but the molecular context determines the outcome. This principle explains why one receptor can regulate multiple seemingly unrelated traits without requiring a separate chromophore for every trait.

When using the word blue in a biological experiment, specify the approximate wavelength range or central wavelength and bandwidth of the light source. Visible blue and near-ultraviolet radiation are neighboring spectral regions, and broad-spectrum illumination can activate more than one receptor class. A blue LED usually emits a band of wavelengths rather than an exact mathematical wavelength. A red filter can transmit a small amount of unwanted light, and a white lamp provides multiple spectral signals. A light treatment is therefore a measured experimental condition, not merely a color name. Such precision becomes particularly important when comparing mutants in photoreceptors with overlapping sensitivity.

2. Discovery: from a growth phenotype to a photoreceptor

Before the molecular identity of cryptochromes became known, physiologists could already show that certain developmental changes were especially responsive to blue light. Such observations established a wavelength-dependent physiological response, but they did not reveal the molecule absorbing the light. Chlorophyll also absorbs blue wavelengths. A plausible scientific explanation therefore required evidence for a dedicated sensory molecule rather than an inference based solely on the color of the stimulus.

A powerful approach was to screen Arabidopsis seedlings for defective light-regulated development. Wild-type seedlings grown under blue light usually restrict hypocotyl elongation relative to seedlings kept in darkness. A mutant that remains relatively long under blue light provides a way to identify genes necessary for the response. The historical hy4 mutant drew attention because of its failure to show normal blue-light inhibition of elongation. In 1993 Ahmad and Cashmore reported the molecular identification of HY4, revealing a sequence related to DNA photolyases. Subsequent biochemical and genetic work established the protein as the blue-light receptor now called CRY1.

A mutant phenotype by itself cannot distinguish a photoreceptor from a downstream signaling component. For example, a mutation in a transcription factor required to restrain growth might also produce a long hypocotyl in blue light, although that transcription factor absorbs no light. A defect in seedling health could alter growth regardless of light. A wavelength-specific phenotype increases confidence that the gene participates in a light-response pathway, yet does not prove that the gene product itself captures photons. The identification of HY4 was strengthened by its relationship to flavin-binding photolyases and by evidence that the protein binds a light-absorbing cofactor and mediates blue-light responses.

Complementation is an important genetic test in this context. If a mutant plant carries a defective CRY1 gene, introducing a functional CRY1 copy in an appropriate expression context can restore a response that was impaired. The comparison should include wild type, the mutant, a complemented line and preferably a control carrying the empty transformation vector. A rescue supports a causal role for CRY1 in the phenotype. Rescue does not on its own demonstrate that every interacting protein lies downstream of CRY1; biochemical and epistasis experiments address those relationships separately.

The discovery of CRY2 showed that blue-light signaling is not a one-gene system. In Arabidopsis, CRY1 and CRY2 have partially overlapping functions, but their effects differ with irradiance, developmental response and light conditions. CRY2 is particularly important in many experiments investigating floral induction under long days. A single mutant can therefore have a modest phenotype in one assay and a large phenotype in another. The existence of overlapping receptors also explains why removing one gene may fail to eliminate the response.

Consider the logic of identifying a new mutant with a blue-light-specific elongated hypocotyl. The first useful control is to measure its length in darkness; a mutant already much larger than wild type in darkness may have a general growth defect. Next, compare red light and blue light under matched photon flux density. Retention of red-light sensitivity with impaired blue-light response suggests a defect more closely related to blue signaling. A combined blue and red treatment can test whether independent signaling pathways compensate for one another. Genetic mapping, rescue and direct biochemical measurements are subsequently needed to establish molecular identity. This progression from phenotype to pathway to molecule is a general pattern in plant molecular genetics.

A classical forward-genetic experiment begins with a phenotype rather than with a candidate gene. A population is screened for individuals showing an unusual response, after which inheritance and mapping identify the affected locus. The method is valuable because the investigator need not know beforehand whether the causal gene encodes a receptor, enzyme, structural protein or transcription factor. In a screen for blue-light-insensitive seedlings, the initial criterion is a defect in a physiological response. The discovery of a photolyase-related protein at the HY4 locus therefore represented a connection between a genetic phenotype and a biochemical hypothesis about sensing. In contrast, a reverse-genetic experiment begins with a gene, deliberately perturbs its function and tests the resulting phenotype. Both approaches are useful in establishing cryptochrome biology.

The specificity of a mutant's defect needs to be evaluated at more than one level. A seedling that fails to inhibit elongation in blue light may also show altered accumulation of a light-regulated pigment such as anthocyanin. If multiple blue-light-dependent traits are affected by an allele, that observation supports a role in an upstream signaling system shared by those traits. However, a broadly acting transcription factor could also influence several responses. Thus a pleiotropic phenotype increases confidence that a gene has a general regulatory function but does not by itself settle whether the gene encodes the primary sensor. Molecular identification, receptor spectroscopy and rescue fill different parts of this evidential gap.

Ahmad, Lin and Cashmore subsequently studied additional HY4 mutations, examining effects on growth inhibition and anthocyanin-related responses. The importance of examining multiple alleles is that a mutation at one location in a gene may have a peculiar effect on protein abundance or conformation. Distinct damaging mutations in the same gene that impair related blue-light responses support the assignment of the gene's function. Mapping which changes affect the receptor can also motivate structure–function experiments. A mutation in the PHR might impair cofactor binding, structural integrity or signaling interactions, while a change in the extension might affect downstream communication. These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions that follow from mutation position alone. See the original study of HY4 alleles in the references.

Overexpression offers an informative converse to loss of function. If increasing the amount of a functional receptor enhances response under an illumination regime that produces a partial wild-type response, the result is consistent with a limiting contribution of receptor abundance. It does not mean that the extra molecules remain active in darkness or that response will increase indefinitely with gene dosage. Downstream partners may become limiting, and excessive expression can disturb normal stoichiometry or localization. When both loss of function reduces responsiveness and increased receptor expression enhances it under suitable conditions, the evidence is stronger than either manipulation alone. Native-promoter complementation is preferable for demonstrating restoration of normal rather than exaggerated function.

Lin and colleagues' 1998 investigation of CRY2 helped clarify the contribution of a second receptor to seedling sensitivity. Comparisons of CRY2 abundance and response under different light regimes were important because an explanation based exclusively on the amount of blue light arriving at a receptor would overlook the regulation of the receptor protein itself. Their experiments supported a post-transcriptional contribution to changes in CRY2 abundance during blue illumination. This finding suggests a general exam principle: the measured concentration of a receptor can depend on the stimulus that activates it. An assay that measures mRNA alone can miss a substantial level of regulation.

A robust discovery story therefore has a sequence of testable claims. The candidate gene is required for a response; its product has properties appropriate for light detection; restoring it restores function; and the activated protein changes downstream regulation. These claims are not redundant. If a purified protein binds FAD but its disruption causes no measurable phenotype, it may be irrelevant to the particular response or redundant with another receptor. If mutation changes the response but the protein lacks appropriate photochemistry, it may be downstream. Learning to separate these possibilities is more valuable than memorizing a discovery date without knowing why the evidence mattered.

3. Cryptochrome architecture and evolutionary relationship to photolyases

Plant CRY1 and CRY2 are flavoproteins. A flavoprotein contains a flavin cofactor that participates in its biochemical activity. Flavin adenine dinucleotide, abbreviated FAD, is the principal light-absorbing chromophore associated with the photolyase homology region of these receptors. A chromophore is the component responsible for absorption of radiation in the relevant wavelength range. The apoprotein is the protein without its bound cofactor; the functional cofactor-containing assembly is called a holoprotein. Thus FAD is not a separate receptor acting freely throughout the cytoplasm. Its placement in the protein creates a chemical environment that influences absorption and subsequent events.

The N-terminal part of a typical plant cryptochrome contains a large photolyase homology region, or PHR. Homology indicates common evolutionary ancestry, inferred from molecular sequence and structural relationships. The PHR has a fold related to that of DNA photolyases and contains a pocket for FAD. The C-terminal extension, also known as the cryptochrome carboxy-terminal region, differs more substantially between cryptochromes. It participates in regulation, interactions with other proteins and signaling. This two-region description is a useful functional map rather than a claim that each region acts independently. The PHR also contributes to signaling interactions and self-association; the regulatory tail can influence how light-induced structural changes are transmitted.

FAD includes an isoalloxazine ring system capable of absorbing light and undergoing redox changes. Its adenine-containing portion provides additional chemical contacts in the binding pocket. For the purposes of sensory photobiology, the important principle is that the electronic properties of the flavin make photon absorption possible, while the surrounding amino acid residues constrain and couple that chemistry to conformational changes in the receptor. The flavin is not required to be consumed in a one-photon, one-receptor-use reaction. A receptor can return toward an inactive state and respond again.

Classical DNA photolyases use photon energy to repair particular UV-induced DNA lesions. Their biochemical task is different from that of CRY1 and CRY2, which principally regulate light-responsive development in Arabidopsis. A related fold is not proof of an identical substrate or catalytic function. Over evolutionary time, a protein scaffold can be conserved while interaction surfaces and regulatory regions change. In exam questions, the observation that a protein resembles photolyase should suggest testing flavin binding and blue-light chemistry; it should not justify the conclusion that it necessarily repairs double-stranded DNA photoproducts.

Some photolyases contain an additional light-harvesting antenna cofactor that transfers excitation energy toward catalytic FAD. Antenna cofactors have also been considered in cryptochrome photochemistry. However, their identity, occupancy and functional importance cannot be assigned universally to every cryptochrome from analogy alone. FAD is the key established chromophore to identify for plant CRY1 and CRY2. Arabidopsis also has a CRY-DASH family member, CRY3, whose characteristics differ from the canonical developmental receptors. The distinction reinforces the need to specify which member of the larger cryptochrome/photolyase family is being discussed.

A structure–function experiment can probe the PHR and C-terminal extension separately. Imagine two engineered proteins expressed at comparable levels: one retains FAD binding but is deficient in an essential downstream interaction surface, whereas the other disrupts the FAD-binding pocket while leaving its tail largely intact. Both might fail to rescue a blue-light growth phenotype, but the mechanisms of failure differ. The first may capture photons yet fail to communicate the signal. The second may lack normal primary photochemical activation. Verifying protein abundance, localization, cofactor occupancy and interaction capacity is required before attributing loss of function to any single step.

A domain is a region of a protein that forms a relatively coherent structural or functional unit. Domains can be identified through sequence comparison, structural analysis and biochemical experiments. A simple linear diagram of the PHR and extension shows the order of regions along the amino acid sequence, but the folded receptor is three-dimensional. Distant positions in sequence can be close in the folded protein, and two adjacent regions can participate in a shared binding surface. A mutation that changes a single amino acid in the PHR may influence the tail indirectly through conformational coupling. Therefore a domain map should be read as an explanatory model, not a scaled atomic structure.

A cofactor-binding pocket is formed by the folded chain. The side chains in the pocket can make hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts and electrostatic interactions with parts of the FAD molecule. Those interactions matter because the cofactor must be held in a suitable orientation for light-dependent chemistry. A free FAD molecule in solution has its own absorption properties, but embedding the molecule in a protein can shift those properties and influence the fate of excitation energy. If the cofactor pocket is disrupted by mutation, reduced absorption or altered photochemistry may follow even when the overall amount of receptor protein appears normal. Conversely, an unstable mutant may lose both protein and cofactor, making it necessary to distinguish a specific binding defect from gross misfolding.

The FAD abbreviation contains the word adenine because the cofactor includes an adenine nucleotide moiety. Its role in cryptochrome should not be confused with that of ATP in phosphoryl transfer. FAD primarily provides the photochemically relevant flavin group in the receptor, whereas ATP is used by kinases and other energy-dependent cellular processes. FMN lacks the additional adenine nucleotide part of FAD and serves as the chromophore associated with phototropin LOV domains. Both molecules are derived from riboflavin-related chemistry. This chemical relationship explains why both receptors can use blue wavelengths without implying that the cofactors are functionally interchangeable within their protein pockets.

The distinction between a photosensory core and a regulatory output region can be demonstrated by considering a hypothetical deletion construct. If the protein retains the PHR but loses its extension, the isolated core may still bind FAD and absorb light. A reduction in seedling signaling would indicate that absorption is not sufficient for normal developmental output. Yet the result would not show that the entire output function is confined to the tail, because removing a region may disturb interactions or stability elsewhere. If a regulatory fragment is expressed in a way that activates some downstream components even without normal light input, it may reveal a signaling capability that is ordinarily constrained in the intact receptor. Such experiments require cautious interpretation because isolated protein domains can behave differently from the native molecule.

The photolyase family provides an evolutionary lesson about biochemical repurposing. A protein that originally recognizes a damaged nucleic acid can share structural ancestry with a protein whose principal partners are regulatory proteins. Conservation of a flavin-binding fold offers a starting point for detecting light, whereas changes in recognition surfaces can redirect the molecular consequence. To decide whether an unfamiliar homolog functions in DNA repair, a scientist would test the restoration of damaged DNA under defined illumination and substrate conditions. To decide whether it functions as a sensory receptor, the scientist would examine light-dependent signaling and physiology. Similarity of sequence is a reason to formulate these experiments, not a substitute for carrying them out.

Species comparisons require similar caution. Plant developmental CRYs, CRY-DASH proteins and animal cryptochrome families are related but differ in biological roles. Some cryptochrome-related proteins have DNA-repair activity on particular substrates, whereas the principal Arabidopsis CRY1 and CRY2 functions discussed here are regulatory. In animals, certain cryptochromes participate in circadian oscillator mechanisms with relationships to light sensing that differ among groups. A question about plant cryptochrome should not be answered by automatically importing the role of mammalian CRY in repression of CLOCK–BMAL1. Identify the organism and receptor class first, then apply the mechanism appropriate to that context.

4. From photon absorption to receptor activation

At the molecular level, light absorption changes the electronic state of the chromophore. The energy of a photon is E = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light in vacuum and λ is the wavelength. A shorter wavelength has a greater photon energy. At 450 nm, conversion to metres gives 4.50 × 10⁻⁷ m. Taking h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ gives an energy of approximately 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon. At 660 nm the value is approximately 3.01 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The ratio is about 1.47. A more energetic photon is not necessarily more effective at activating a particular receptor: its wavelength must fall within a range absorbed by the receptor, and the subsequent excited state must lead to productive signaling.

An absorption spectrum plots absorption by a substance as a function of wavelength. An action spectrum instead plots a biological response produced by illumination at different wavelengths. If cryptochrome is responsible for inhibition of hypocotyl growth, the action spectrum for that inhibition should reflect the spectral sensitivity of the signaling system. It need not match a purified protein's absorption spectrum exactly. Tissue penetration, other receptors, cofactor environment, receptor abundance and signal amplification can alter the relationship between absorption and response. Furthermore, if length rather than inhibition is plotted on the vertical axis, a stronger response produces a smaller value; the direction of the curve must be interpreted with care.

An excited flavin can participate in electron-transfer reactions, giving rise to different redox states. Photochemical models for plant cryptochromes have considered oxidized FAD, semiquinone intermediates and subsequent recovery. The precise identity and necessity of particular flavin intermediates in living plant cells have been subject to experimental investigation. A simple exam-level description should distinguish a robust conclusion—blue illumination changes cryptochrome signaling competence—from a more specific photochemical hypothesis. Unlike the classic phytochrome Pr–Pfr scheme, cryptochrome activation is not adequately described as a pair of red/far-red photoreversible states.

One important feature of CRY activation is light-dependent self-association. Individual receptor molecules can associate with others to form signaling-competent oligomers. The precise number and arrangement of subunits can depend on the receptor, experimental system and conditions, so the term oligomer is more general than dimer. Activated receptors expose or rearrange interaction surfaces and can engage regulatory partners. The active population does not remain constant indefinitely. Dark recovery, regulatory proteins and post-translational changes can reduce or redirect signaling. This creates a dynamic balance between activation and deactivation.

Phosphorylation of cryptochrome proteins occurs in response to light in relevant contexts. A phosphate group can alter electrostatic interactions, binding affinity, stability or subcellular distribution. It is essential not to infer from the presence of a phosphate group that cryptochrome is itself a conventional receptor protein kinase. Phototropins possess a recognizable C-terminal serine/threonine kinase module and undergo autophosphorylation as a core feature of their signaling. Plant cryptochrome signaling involves other protein kinases and interaction-dependent regulation. The contrast is an important reason to distinguish blue-light receptor families by architecture rather than wavelength alone.

A useful teaching model represents the receptor population as an inactive pool D and an active pool A, with D + A = R, the total receptor population. Suppose the activation rate per inactive molecule is k_on I, proportional to photon flux density I, and the deactivation rate is k_off per active molecule. Then dA/dt = k_on I(R − A) − k_off A. At a steady state, the active fraction is A/R = k_on I/(k_on I + k_off). This expression predicts that doubling intensity has a large relative effect at low intensity but a small effect near saturation. It is not a complete biochemical model of CRY oligomerization or degradation, because it assumes constant total receptor, first-order deactivation and a linear light activation term.

For illustration, let k_on I equal 0.20 min⁻¹ and k_off equal 0.10 min⁻¹. The predicted active fraction is 0.20/0.30 = 0.667. If flux is doubled while all parameters remain constant, k_on I becomes 0.40 min⁻¹, and the fraction becomes 0.40/0.50 = 0.80. The receptor signal rises from about 67% to 80% of the available population, not to 134%. If the light is removed, activation stops in this simplified model and the active pool decays exponentially. Its characteristic half-time is ln 2/k_off = 6.93 min. These numbers are hypothetical teaching parameters, not measured CRY1 or CRY2 rate constants.

The model also explains a limitation of total fluence as a predictor. Fluence equals photon flux density multiplied by exposure time when the flux is constant. A treatment of 10 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ for 60 s gives 600 μmol m⁻², as does 1 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 600 s. In a system with appreciable deactivation, the weaker longer exposure can reach a different active fraction and temporal response than the stronger brief exposure. Equal fluence does not establish equal signaling. Observing how responses depend on both flux and duration can reveal whether a receptor system behaves as a simple integrator or as a dynamic signaling network.

Photochemical activation is an example of energy conversion at a molecular scale rather than an example of macroscopic energy production. Absorption promotes electrons in the chromophore to an excited state. Subsequent processes can include relaxation, energy transfer or electron transfer. Only a fraction of excitations may result in a productive signaling event. This fraction is related to the photochemical quantum yield, which in a simplified context expresses the number of defined photochemical events per absorbed photon. An incident photon that is not absorbed cannot directly excite the chromophore, and an absorbed photon may not generate a long-lived signaling state. Thus incident photon number, absorbed photon number and successful activation events are distinct quantities.

For wavelength comparisons, photon flux is often a more meaningful independent variable than power density. At a given radiant power, lower-energy photons are more numerous than higher-energy photons. A beam delivering 1 W m⁻² at 450 nm supplies about 1/(4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹) photons m⁻² s⁻¹, or approximately 2.27 × 10¹⁸ photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Dividing by Avogadro's constant and multiplying by 10⁶ yields roughly 3.77 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. A beam of the same power at 660 nm supplies roughly 5.52 μmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Consequently, an experiment comparing equal watts per square metre across wavelengths has not matched the number of incident photons. A calibrated quantum sensor or an appropriate spectroradiometric conversion is required.

An action spectrum is most interpretable when the investigator defines how response is quantified. If the vertical axis represents the inhibition of growth relative to darkness, a peak indicates strong inhibition. If it represents final hypocotyl length, a trough may indicate the same strong response. If it represents the inverse photon dose required for a specified effect, larger values indicate higher sensitivity. These conventions must not be mixed. When an action spectrum is obtained from a whole seedling, receptor contributions can overlap, and the tissue's optical properties influence how much light reaches receptor molecules. Purified receptor absorption data therefore help identify plausible spectral mechanisms, but the in vivo action spectrum integrates multiple processes.

The receptor state can also depend on its recent illumination history. If a plant has been kept in blue light before a test pulse, some receptors may already be in an active state, and some may have undergone regulatory modification or turnover. The same pulse given to a dark-adapted plant could produce a larger incremental response. Conversely, a long dark interval can allow active states to recover and receptor pools to be replenished. Experimental protocols should therefore specify pretreatment as well as the stimulus itself. Matching the final illumination while ignoring initial conditions is especially problematic when comparing proteins with different stability.

The simplified two-state activation equation introduced above has a characteristic relaxation time determined by the sum of the activation and deactivation rate constants. After switching on constant illumination, the approach to the steady state is exponential under the model's assumptions. If a system starts with no active receptor, the active fraction at time t is f_ss[1 − exp(−(k_on I + k_off)t)]. For the teaching example with k_on I = 0.20 min⁻¹ and k_off = 0.10 min⁻¹, the relaxation time is 1/0.30 ≈ 3.33 min. At this time the active fraction has reached approximately 63.2% of its eventual value, or about 0.421 of the total receptor pool. The model therefore distinguishes the eventual fraction from the fraction reached after a brief pulse.

The formula can be used to compare two equal-fluence treatments under specified assumptions. Suppose a strong pulse gives k_on I = 0.50 min⁻¹ for two minutes and a weaker pulse gives 0.10 min⁻¹ for ten minutes, while k_off remains 0.10 min⁻¹. The integrated activation opportunities k_on I × duration are equal, both 1.0. Under the strong pulse, the predicted steady fraction is 0.833 and the two-minute active fraction is approximately 0.833(1 − e⁻¹·²) = 0.582. Under the weaker pulse, the steady fraction is 0.50 and the ten-minute active fraction is 0.50(1 − e⁻²) = 0.432. Despite equal integrated input, the model predicts different fractions at the ends of the respective pulses. These are calculations from an illustrative mathematical system, not measured cryptochrome activation fractions.

Real cryptochrome systems need not obey simple first-order kinetics. Oligomer formation involves encounters among multiple protein molecules, which can introduce dependence on receptor concentration. Regulatory partners can compete for binding, and the formation of higher-order assemblies can alter the probability of subsequent interactions. CRY2 turnover means that total receptor concentration need not remain constant over a sustained treatment. Thus the model provides a baseline expectation against which additional biological complexity can be recognized. A curve that differs from the simple model can motivate further experiments, but the difference alone cannot uniquely identify which assumption has failed.

Regulatory proteins can influence the active receptor population directly. Blue-light inhibitors of cryptochromes, commonly called BIC proteins, provide an example of factors that can restrain CRY self-association and signaling in Arabidopsis. Their role illustrates how a receptor pathway can be attenuated near its input stage rather than only at the final transcriptional output. If an inhibitor accumulates in a light-regulated manner, it can contribute to limiting or reshaping the response during sustained illumination. This is distinct from the elimination of all CRY protein: a receptor may still bind its cofactor and absorb photons while its ability to form functional associations is restrained. The details of individual BIC family members are less important here than the principle of regulated receptor activation.

A genetic test of a receptor inhibitor could compare seedlings with normal inhibitor levels, increased inhibitor expression and reduced inhibitor function. If higher inhibitor abundance reduces blue-light-dependent CRY oligomerization and attenuates hypocotyl inhibition without changing the basic absorption properties of FAD, the results support control at the level of activation or assembly. A reciprocal phenotype in the loss-of-function line would strengthen that interpretation. Still, the most direct mechanistic conclusion requires protein interaction evidence, expression controls and demonstration that the altered response depends on CRY. Other effects of the inhibitor would otherwise remain possible.

5. CRY1, CRY2 and physiological response specificity

Arabidopsis CRY1 and CRY2 share the overall architecture of plant cryptochromes, yet their functions are not interchangeable in every situation. CRY1 is prominent in blue-light inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, especially in many moderate and higher irradiance regimes. CRY2 contributes significantly to responses under relatively weak blue light and is particularly important in the regulation of flowering time under inductive photoperiods. These are relative functional tendencies, not exclusive assignments. Both proteins can influence seedling development, and both can participate in responses that involve other photoreceptors.

Differences in physiological sensitivity reflect more than the absorption properties of the bound FAD. The abundance of a receptor, its activation dynamics, the persistence of its active state and the availability of signaling partners all influence the eventual response. CRY2 is often subject to blue-light-dependent turnover, especially under stronger illumination. Thus a higher photon flux can stimulate activation while also promoting degradation. The initial response and the steady-state response need not vary in the same way. CRY1 is relatively more stable under many comparable conditions. These properties help explain why different receptors contribute differently across light regimes.

A protein immunoblot provides one way to investigate receptor stability. Seedlings can be grown under a common starting condition and transferred to blue light for different times. Equal amounts of total protein are loaded, and a receptor-specific antibody is used to detect CRY2. If the signal falls with time, the result is consistent with a decline in receptor abundance. Controls for total loading, extraction efficiency and detection range are necessary. A reduction in band intensity does not automatically prove increased degradation: reduced synthesis could also lower abundance. Comparing turnover after synthesis is inhibited, with appropriate controls, helps distinguish those possibilities.

Subcellular localization is another determinant of function. CRY1 can occur in the nucleus and cytosol, and CRY2 is strongly associated with nuclear signaling in Arabidopsis. Nuclear localization places receptors near transcriptional regulators, but a receptor does not need to bind DNA directly to change gene expression. Fluorescent protein fusions can reveal distribution if the tagged receptor retains function. A bright nuclear spot does not by itself prove an active molecular complex; an interaction assay or a functional test provides independent information. Conversely, the absence of visually resolvable spots does not establish that a receptor is inactive, since functional complexes may be below the resolution of the imaging method.

Partial redundancy is best studied using a combination of single and double mutants. If a cry1 mutant retains substantial inhibition of elongation under weak blue light and a cry2 mutant retains inhibition under stronger blue light, a cry1 cry2 double mutant may reveal a larger dependence on the receptor family. The strength of a double mutant phenotype is informative but should be interpreted in relation to a specific response and exposure. Other photoreceptors may still contribute, so even a double mutant does not guarantee an absence of all light sensing. The genetic background and allele type also matter: a weak allele that retains activity can produce a different phenotype from a null allele.

A comparison of responses should therefore use a matrix rather than a single light condition. At minimum, include darkness, low blue, higher blue and red light, with equal-aged plants of each genotype. Where relevant, include white light or a defined combination of wavelengths. Keeping temperature, medium, seedling density and developmental stage comparable reduces confounding variation. Measure hypocotyl length with a consistent anatomical definition, and report variation among biological replicates. This approach distinguishes a change in response threshold from a change in maximum response and from a general defect in growth.

The concept of a response threshold deserves care. A threshold in a growth assay is the smallest stimulus that produces a detectable change under that assay's conditions. It is not necessarily the smallest stimulus capable of exciting a single receptor molecule. At a very low photon flux, a small fraction of receptors may be active but downstream changes may remain below the experimental noise or below the level needed to change organ growth. A mutant with a higher apparent threshold could have fewer receptors, less effective coupling to transcriptional regulators or a growth system that requires a larger regulatory input. Therefore sensitivity is a property of the entire measured pathway, even when differences between CRY1 and CRY2 are a major determinant.

One practical way of summarizing a dose-response curve is to compare its midpoint and maximum. For two genotypes, one may reach the same maximum inhibition as wild type but require a greater blue flux, suggesting a shift in sensitivity. Another may fail to reach the wild-type maximum even at stronger blue light, suggesting limited capacity or additional defects. In a living seedling, the highest tested flux should not be assumed to represent receptor saturation unless the response truly plateaus and other effects are excluded. Excessive light can induce additional stress responses or alter receptor turnover. A nonlinear change at high irradiance could reflect several interacting processes rather than a single binding equilibrium.

The expression of CRY2 can be examined at the levels of transcript, translation and protein lifetime. If a blue-light-treated sample contains the same amount of CRY2 mRNA as its dark control but less receptor protein, the balance between protein synthesis and loss has changed. The transcript measurement alone does not show whether translation efficiency has changed. A time-resolved experiment that estimates receptor lifetime after a controlled interruption of synthesis can provide a more specific test of turnover. Since such interventions can themselves perturb cellular physiology, a complementary experiment involving the degradation machinery or an appropriately stabilized receptor variant is valuable.

A comparison of receptor lifetime must be normalized correctly. Suppose a wild-type CRY2 sample begins with an arbitrary abundance of 100 units and falls to 25 units over 40 minutes during a hypothetical turnover experiment. Under an exponential decay model this corresponds to two half-lives, giving a half-life of 20 minutes. A variant that starts with 50 units and falls to 25 over the same interval has a half-life of 40 minutes, despite having the same final abundance as the wild type. Absolute endpoint values alone could therefore conceal a substantial difference in stability. Expressing each time series as a fraction of its own starting value helps separate decay kinetics from initial expression level.

Partial redundancy can also be described using gene dosage. A homozygous loss-of-function mutant lacks the normal function of one gene, but a related gene can still provide overlapping signaling capacity. In a double mutant, both contributions are removed, potentially exposing an effect that is weak in either single mutant. If a transgene encoding CRY1 restores growth inhibition in the double mutant at moderate blue flux but fails to restore normal flowering time under long days, the result is compatible with functional specialization. If CRY2 expression restores both outcomes partially, this indicates overlap rather than strict separation. Such predictions should always be conditioned on correct expression levels and tissues, since failure of a transgene to rescue may reflect its regulatory context rather than intrinsic receptor specificity.

Localization can be tested functionally rather than merely visually. A tagged receptor engineered to accumulate preferentially in the nucleus or cytosol can be compared with a functional unmodified receptor in a mutant background. If nuclear accumulation preferentially restores a transcription-associated response, the result supports a requirement for receptor activity in that compartment for the chosen output. If cytosolic localization restores another component of the response, it indicates that different signaling contexts may contribute. Interpretation requires confirmation that the localization construct has not disrupted FAD binding, activation, protein abundance or the relevant interaction surfaces. A fusion protein observed at one location under one treatment is not a universal map of receptor function under all conditions.

6. The COP1–SPA complex and regulation of HY5 stability

To understand how activated cryptochrome changes seedling form, consider the cell's ability to control how long a regulatory protein survives. Protein abundance depends on both synthesis and degradation. A transcription factor may be produced continuously, yet remain scarce if it is rapidly removed. Conversely, inhibiting its degradation can increase abundance without an immediate increase in transcription of its gene. The ubiquitin–proteasome system provides a regulated mechanism for this type of control.

Ubiquitin is a small protein that can be covalently attached to target proteins. An E1 enzyme activates ubiquitin, an E2 enzyme carries it, and an E3 ubiquitin ligase contributes specificity by recognizing substrates or assembling the transfer reaction. Appropriate ubiquitin chains can target proteins for degradation by the 26S proteasome. Different forms of ubiquitination can have other functions, so the presence of ubiquitin does not universally imply proteolysis. Here the relevant process is the regulation of particular developmental transcription factors through proteasomal turnover.

CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1, or COP1, is a major negative regulator of photomorphogenesis in dark-grown Arabidopsis. COP1 associates with SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA-105 proteins, abbreviated SPA. In relevant complexes, these proteins promote ubiquitination and loss of positive light-response regulators, including ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5). HY5 is a basic leucine zipper transcription factor that participates in the transcriptional program of photomorphogenesis. The basic region contributes DNA recognition, while the leucine zipper contributes dimerization. Its function should be distinguished from that of the chromophore-bearing photoreceptor.

In darkness, the activity of nuclear COP1–SPA-dependent degradation keeps HY5 abundance relatively low. Seedlings consequently maintain the dark developmental program, alongside other regulatory processes. When blue light activates cryptochromes, receptor interactions with components of the COP1–SPA machinery reduce its ability to promote degradation of key targets. The exact interaction mechanisms differ in detail between receptors and experimental conditions, but the common functional outcome is relief of repression. HY5 can accumulate and influence transcription of light-responsive genes. This is a double-negative regulatory arrangement: blue light inhibits a negative regulator of a positive developmental factor.

Consider what would happen if a receptor were activated normally but its interaction with a downstream complex were prevented by a mutation. Its flavin could still absorb light, and oligomerization might still occur, but the COP1–SPA-mediated turnover of HY5 might remain high. A light-response defect would then arise after perception. Conversely, a reduction in COP1 function may promote aspects of light-like development in darkness. Such a phenotype does not mean that COP1 is a photoreceptor; rather, the cell has lost an important negative regulator that normally maintains dark development.

A hy5 mutant tests another point in the causal chain. Even if cryptochrome absorbs blue light and COP1–SPA activity is attenuated, a seedling lacking functional HY5 cannot execute all HY5-dependent transcriptional responses normally. However, HY5 is not the sole positive regulator of light responses. Other transcription factors, receptors and hormone-regulated pathways may still influence elongation. Therefore one should not predict complete loss of blue-light sensitivity in every hy5 assay. The word necessary requires a specified outcome, developmental stage and treatment.

It is tempting to describe photomorphogenesis as a single linear pathway from CRY to COP1 to HY5 to all light-controlled genes. Such a diagram is useful for showing regulatory signs, but the actual system is a network. Phytochromes can converge on COP1-related processes; phytochrome-interacting factors regulate growth; cryptochromes can interact with other transcriptional and hormone-signaling regulators. HY5 itself operates in a context that depends on tissue, developmental age and environmental conditions. A decrease in hypocotyl elongation is an integrated physiological result, not an exclusive reporter for one molecular interaction.

The simplest quantitative description of HY5 abundance uses dH/dt = s − kH, where H is abundance in arbitrary units, s is the synthesis rate and k is an effective first-order degradation constant. If synthesis remains unchanged, the steady state is H = s/k. Suppose s = 12 units per hour and k = 0.60 h⁻¹ in the dark. The predicted abundance is 20 units. If illumination lowers effective k to 0.15 h⁻¹, the new steady state becomes 80 units, a fourfold increase. It takes time to approach that level; the abundance does not jump instantly. This hypothetical calculation shows why a change in turnover alone can amplify the abundance of a regulatory protein without any increase in its mRNA.

Experimentally, measurements of HY5 transcript and protein are complementary. If protein abundance rises rapidly after blue illumination while transcript abundance remains nearly constant, stabilization is a plausible explanation. If both increase, increased synthesis and decreased degradation may operate together. A carefully controlled proteasome-inhibition experiment can test the turnover hypothesis, but inhibitor treatment can alter many other proteins and stress responses. The most convincing interpretation combines biochemical interaction evidence, genetic perturbation, receptor activation and quantitative measurements of target abundance.

The COP1–SPA mechanism illustrates an important distinction between the state of an enzyme and the abundance of its substrate. A regulatory signal may inhibit the ability of an E3 ligase to recognize a particular target without eliminating the entire E3 complex from the cell. Consequently, detection of COP1 protein after blue illumination does not contradict the hypothesis that its activity toward HY5 has been reduced. The relevant question is whether the complex can still recruit, ubiquitinate and promote turnover of the particular positive regulator in its cellular context. Measuring the abundance of the ligase is informative, but a functional assay of substrate stability or ubiquitination addresses the proposed signaling mechanism more directly.

The name constitutive photomorphogenic is historically derived from the phenotype of mutants that show features of light-grown development even when grown in darkness. The name does not imply that the normal COP1 protein promotes the light developmental state. Instead, the mutant phenotype reveals what happens when a dark repressor loses its function. Gene names based on phenotypes frequently invert the apparent sign of the normal gene product. This is especially relevant when reading an examination question that asks whether COP1 is a positive or negative regulator. One should reason from the function of the wild-type protein, not from the phenotype used to name the mutant.

The COP1–SPA assembly recognizes multiple regulatory substrates; HY5 is a particularly useful example because its role in photomorphogenesis is well established. It is possible for illumination to change the relative accessibility of substrate-binding surfaces or alter interactions among components. CRY1 and CRY2 interact with COP1- and SPA-related components in ways that are not identical at every molecular detail. A high-level regulatory diagram can group these actions under suppression of COP1–SPA-dependent degradation, provided it does not assert that every protein interaction is identical between CRY1 and CRY2. This distinction between a shared physiological outcome and distinct molecular routes is an example of mechanistic convergence.

HY5 can bind regulatory DNA sequences and influence transcription, but its accumulation does not guarantee that every light-responsive gene is activated. Some genes are regulated indirectly through other transcription factors, some are repressed by changes in the network, and many require tissue-specific partners. A transcription factor's effect depends on the target regulatory sequence and the molecular context. To connect HY5 accumulation to a particular gene, one may compare that gene's expression in wild type and a hy5 mutant, test whether a functional HY5 transgene restores regulation, and examine whether HY5 can associate with the relevant regulatory region. Together these experiments distinguish a direct regulatory contribution from broader correlations with seedling development.

A useful example of the distinction between necessity and sufficiency arises from a mutant that lacks HY5. If a particular light-induced reporter fails to increase in the mutant, functional HY5 may be necessary for full reporter induction under those conditions. However, merely expressing HY5 in darkness may not fully activate the reporter if other light-dependent factors are also required. Conversely, an artificially stabilized HY5 protein could activate a subset of light-responsive genes in the dark without recreating the complete photomorphogenic program. The receptor network coordinates several branches, so individual regulators can be necessary for certain effects without being sufficient for the entire developmental transition.

The temporal behavior of the HY5 abundance equation can be examined after a shift in degradation rate. Suppose the dark steady state is 20 units, the synthesis rate remains 12 units per hour and the degradation constant becomes 0.15 h⁻¹ after illumination. The solution is H(t) = 80 + (20 − 80) exp(−0.15t), where t is time in hours. After two hours, H ≈ 80 − 60 × 0.741 = 35.5 units. This is an increase but remains far below the predicted final steady state. After six hours, H ≈ 80 − 60 × 0.407 = 55.6 units. These illustrative calculations show that steady-state predictions should not be used to interpret an early time point as if equilibrium had already been reached. The approach can be slower when the new degradation constant is smaller.

In a real plant, synthesis need not remain constant. If blue-light signaling increases HY5 transcription or translation while reducing degradation, the numerator and denominator of the steady-state relationship may both change. Furthermore, protein synthesis and degradation rates may vary over time. The simple equation remains useful because it identifies the minimum information needed to interpret abundance. If only the amount of protein at one time is known, synthesis and degradation cannot be inferred independently. Comparing transcript, newly synthesized protein and the lifetime of existing protein is a way to disentangle their contributions. This is a general analytical principle applicable to many signaling systems involving regulated proteolysis.

An additional control in studies of protein stability is the behavior of an unrelated protein that is not expected to respond to the pathway. If all proteins decline in an extract after treatment, the loss may reflect extraction problems, poor sample quality or general cellular injury rather than targeted degradation. A stable loading control helps detect such problems but does not replace controls for antibody specificity. When comparing HY5 in different genotypes, the same antibody should recognize the intended molecular species in each genotype; a band of an unexpected size can result from cross-reactivity or a truncated protein. The experimental interpretation is strongest when the molecular signal is supported by the expected physiological response.

Phytochrome-related PIF proteins supply a useful contrast to HY5. Many PIFs participate in promoting elongation and dark-associated growth programs. Light can reduce the activity of certain PIFs through interactions with photoreceptors and regulated turnover. Cryptochromes also interact with selected growth-regulatory proteins, including PIFs in relevant signaling contexts. It is therefore possible for the light developmental state to arise from both accumulation of positive regulators and attenuation of growth-promoting factors. These effects need not be symmetrical or mediated by a single E3 complex. The combined shift in the balance of transcriptional activities helps the plant reorganize its development when the environment changes.

7. Seedling photomorphogenesis and the integration of growth signals

The physiological significance of cryptochrome signaling can be understood by comparing resource allocation in an etiolated and an illuminated seedling. In darkness, elongation helps the shoot reach a potentially favorable environment. Upon exposure, a relatively short hypocotyl and expanded cotyledons create a form better suited to photosynthetic establishment. The difference involves altered growth rates and organ-specific responses, not the reversal of an already formed stem. A seedling shifted from darkness to blue light may slow future elongation; its existing hypocotyl does not need to shrink.

At the cellular level, plant expansion depends on wall extensibility, turgor and the regulatory processes controlling cell growth. Cryptochrome affects these processes indirectly by changing transcriptional programs and interacting with other developmental regulatory systems. Hormones such as auxin and brassinosteroids participate in the control of elongation. The physiological response to light can therefore depend on hormone status even when receptor photochemistry is unchanged. A mutant with altered hormone sensitivity could show a changed hypocotyl response without any defect in FAD binding. Distinguishing receptor activation from growth execution prevents misclassification of downstream mutants.

Cotyledon expansion provides a second measure of de-etiolation. A treatment that suppresses hypocotyl extension but fails to promote cotyledon opening might selectively affect one branch of development. Similarly, changes in chlorophyll accumulation or anthocyanin production can report parts of a light-regulated program but are influenced by nutrient status and other environmental signals. Multiple independent response measures provide a richer interpretation than hypocotyl length alone.

Timing is crucial when comparing measurements. A change in receptor oligomerization can occur before measurable organ expansion. A change in protein abundance or transcription may occupy an intermediate timescale. The morphology of a seedling grown for several days integrates these early events and ongoing feedback. For a causal argument, temporal sequence must be combined with interventions; earlier occurrence is consistent with a causal role but does not establish it. For instance, both CRY activation and chlorophyll accumulation may follow illumination, but their temporal order does not make chlorophyll a direct target of cryptochrome binding.

Suppose a blue-light pulse reduces the rate of hypocotyl elongation in wild type. If the response is absent in a receptor-deficient mutant but restored by a functional gene, receptor dependence is supported. If an immediate response also persists in a line with deficient HY5 signaling, that observation may indicate parallel pathways or different temporal components of growth regulation. The appropriate inference is not that HY5 has no function, but that the measured response under those particular conditions is not entirely dependent on HY5.

In natural environments, canopy shade changes the spectral composition and intensity of light. Phytochromes are particularly relevant to the red/far-red ratio, while cryptochrome-dependent responses can change as blue photon flux varies. A plant can receive low blue and low red/far-red signals together. The resulting elongation depends on the integration of several pathways rather than a single wavelength sensor. A controlled experiment that independently varies blue flux and red/far-red ratio can separate contributions that would be difficult to distinguish under an actual canopy.

A seedling's transition from darkness to light involves several anatomical outputs with different biological purposes. Opening the apical hook exposes the shoot tip, expansion of cotyledons increases the potential area for light interception, and restricted hypocotyl elongation reduces continued expenditure on extension. These effects are coordinated but can be separated experimentally. Measuring hook angle, cotyledon area and hypocotyl length in the same treatment allows one to distinguish a broad de-etiolation defect from a selective growth defect. A mutation that affects cotyledon expansion but not hypocotyl length may identify a downstream branch rather than a primary receptor required for every output. Conversely, a receptor mutant that affects multiple light responses is informative about regulatory integration.

The relationship between cell length and organ length is worth making explicit. If a hypocotyl contains a particular number of cells along a longitudinal file, its length depends on the sum of those cell lengths, together with anatomical variation and the arrangement of tissues. A reduction in organ elongation can result from changes in cell expansion, cell number, developmental timing or a combination. In the Arabidopsis hypocotyl, post-germination elongation is strongly associated with cell expansion, making it a useful system for examining growth regulation. Nevertheless, an organ-length measurement does not directly reveal the molecular properties of the cell wall. Microscopy can measure individual cell dimensions, while growth assays and mechanical measurements can investigate additional aspects of the response.

Light-dependent anthocyanin accumulation offers another illustrative developmental output. Anthocyanins are pigments produced through branches of flavonoid metabolism. A light-responsive increase in pigment content can arise from changes in the expression of enzymes involved in biosynthesis, changes in substrate availability or changes in turnover and storage. Cryptochrome-dependent transcriptional regulation contributes to anthocyanin responses in appropriate Arabidopsis assays. The pigment itself should not be confused with the receptor's FAD chromophore. One is a downstream metabolic output in this example, while the other is a cofactor involved in perception. A mutant that changes pigment accumulation may alter light signaling or an enzyme in the pigment pathway; its hypocotyl phenotype and gene identity can help distinguish these possibilities.

To quantify pigment accumulation, a researcher might use absorbance of a plant extract at an appropriate wavelength, accounting for other absorbing compounds and sample size. If the same mass of tissue from wild-type and mutant plants gives different absorbance under matched extraction conditions, the result suggests a difference in pigment content. But optical measurements depend on the mixture of compounds in the extract, and identical fresh masses may have different water contents. Thus careful normalization and chemical specificity are important. The physiological assay becomes more persuasive when pigment changes correspond with gene-expression evidence and the predicted dependence on the receptor. This interpretation does not require treating every pigment biosynthetic reaction as part of the CRY pathway.

Light-controlled growth also depends on whether a plant is experiencing a signal continuously or responding to a transition. A seedling already acclimated to a light environment may have a stable developmental program and no longer show the same acute change as a seedling suddenly transferred out of darkness. Receptor abundance, abundance of transcriptional regulators and hormone status can differ between these conditions. A time series spanning the first minutes, several hours and later developmental stages helps distinguish initial signaling from maintenance. Measuring only a final phenotype can hide a transient response or an early defect that is subsequently compensated by another receptor. A strong Part C question often uses this separation of time scales to test mechanistic reasoning.

The integration of cryptochrome signaling and hormone-controlled growth can be explored through a two-factor experiment. Suppose wild-type and cry mutant seedlings are grown in blue light with and without a hormone-related treatment that reduces elongation. If the treatment reduces hypocotyl growth in both genotypes, the downstream growth machinery can respond even when the normal cryptochrome signal is weakened. This does not necessarily mean that the hormone acts directly downstream of CRY; a parallel growth-inhibitory input could produce the same result. To establish pathway ordering, the experiment must test additional genetic or molecular predictions. For example, comparing hormone-responsive transcriptional markers in the receptor mutant could indicate whether CRY normally alters that signaling branch.

Plant growth under artificial lighting provides an application of these ideas. A change in the blue component of a horticultural spectrum can alter seedling architecture, including compactness and expansion of leaves. However, the appropriate light spectrum depends on species, developmental stage, photosynthetic requirements and cultivation conditions. A plant whose growth is more compact under a high-blue treatment may not necessarily yield more biomass or reproduce sooner. The signal perceived by cryptochromes interacts with the energy captured by photosystems and with other photoreceptors. Consequently, a controlled cultivation experiment should distinguish traits such as stem length, leaf area, total biomass and flowering time rather than treating any one as a universal measure of plant performance. This application follows directly from the physiological distinction between light quality as information and light as an energy resource.

8. CRY2, flowering-related signaling and biological time

An adult plant faces a different light-sensing challenge from that of an emerging seedling. Successful flowering often depends on season, and the duration of illumination across a day provides a cue about the time of year. A photoreceptor detects environmental light, but the significance of that input depends on internal timing. The circadian system supplies a time-dependent context in which illumination can promote or fail to promote a particular developmental outcome.

Arabidopsis is commonly described as a facultative long-day plant because flowering is accelerated under long days relative to short days in suitable conditions. This is not the claim that it can never flower under short days. Genetic background, temperature, developmental age and other pathways influence flowering time. CRY2 has an important role in the promotion of flowering by blue light in appropriate photoperiodic regimes. Its function should therefore be interpreted alongside the timing of light exposure and the operation of other receptors.

CONSTANS, abbreviated CO, is a regulatory protein whose abundance and activity help connect the circadian system to expression of FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT). CO is regulated at several levels, including the timing of gene expression and light-dependent protein stability. In favorable long-day conditions, the temporal coincidence of CO expression and stabilizing light supports accumulation of active CO and promotes FT expression. Cryptochromes contribute to this process in part through their effects on COP1–SPA-mediated protein regulation. Other photoreceptors also modify CO stability in wavelength- and time-dependent ways. This explains why the contribution of cryptochrome to flowering cannot be deduced from total daily blue photon dose alone.

CRY2 can also interact in a blue-light-dependent manner with cryptochrome-interacting basic helix–loop–helix proteins, including CIB1. Such proteins act in transcriptional regulation, linking the light-dependent receptor state to control of flowering-associated genes. This provides a second useful conceptual route from receptor activation to transcription, distinct from a simple diagram in which all CRY signaling is mediated exclusively through HY5. The routes are not necessarily independent in the intact plant; their contributions can overlap and vary with developmental or environmental context.

FT protein produced in appropriate leaf cells can travel to the shoot apex, where it participates in the regulation of floral transition. This connects a light-sensing event in leaves with a developmental change at a distant meristem. Cryptochrome should not itself be called the mobile floral signal. Its role is upstream, in the regulatory network controlling the timing and abundance of flowering-promoting factors. The detailed clock oscillator and photoperiodic coincidence mechanisms are developed in subsequent chapters; here the central lesson is that CRY2 couples blue-light perception to transcriptional regulators that influence reproductive development.

Flowering time can be measured as days to flowering or as the number of rosette leaves at bolting. These are related but not identical measures. A treatment that changes the rate of leaf production could alter days to flowering without changing the developmental leaf number at transition. Recording both measures provides a more informative phenotype. In a CRY2 experiment, compare genotypes grown under both long and short days and hold temperature and spectral conditions constant. A preferential flowering delay under inductive long days supports a photoperiodic role. A general growth delay in all treatments requires a different interpretation.

The timing of blue-light exposure can itself be investigated using defined pulses during the day or night. Equal-duration pulses at different circadian phases may produce different FT expression responses. The relevant comparison is not merely light versus no light; it is the same photic input delivered in distinct internal states. This is a general example of how signal transduction and biological timing integrate environmental information.

The flowering role of CRY2 can be understood through the concept of a time-dependent regulatory gate. A plant receives blue photons whenever they are present, but the molecular partners capable of connecting the signal to floral induction are not necessarily equally available throughout the day. If a partner is abundant at one phase and scarce at another, an identical receptor activation event can have different downstream effects. This is why photoperiod is not simply the total number of photons accumulated over a twenty-four-hour period. The day and night schedule determines when particular light-dependent regulatory processes can coincide with internal molecular events. The detailed oscillator mechanism belongs to the biological clock node, while the external coincidence mechanism belongs to the photoperiodism node.

In the original flowering-time work, Guo and colleagues linked the Arabidopsis late-flowering fha phenotype to CRY2. This connection was important because a mutant initially recognized by a whole-plant developmental phenotype could now be understood in relation to a specific photoreceptor. It was not necessary for a flowering plant to display the same defect in hypocotyl development for the gene to be relevant. Different developmental outputs can reveal different components of the same receptor family's function. Comparing flowering under inductive and noninductive day lengths helps establish whether a mutation particularly affects the seasonal regulation of floral transition rather than causing a general reduction in plant vigor.

The CO-associated mechanism includes post-translational regulation, making it analogous in broad principle to HY5 stabilization. A transcriptional regulator may be synthesized at a particular time of day, but its protein lifetime depends on environmental conditions and the activity of the degradation machinery. If the transcript peaks at a time when light is present and the protein is stabilized, its downstream regulatory effect may be stronger than when the transcript peaks in darkness. This does not mean the timing of transcription is irrelevant. Both transcriptional timing and protein stability contribute to the coincidence between internal and external signals. The exam-level distinction is between the clock-dependent availability of a regulator and the light-dependent persistence of its protein.

The CIB route illustrates a related but distinct use of an activated photoreceptor. In their 2008 study, Liu and colleagues identified a light-dependent interaction between CRY2 and CIB1 and investigated its association with flowering-related transcription. CIB1 belongs to the basic helix–loop–helix class of transcription factors. The basic region participates in DNA recognition, while the helix–loop–helix portion contributes to protein dimerization. CRY2 need not recognize a specific promoter sequence by itself to influence transcription. By associating with a DNA-binding regulatory protein, the receptor can influence a complex that acts at a gene. Further work has shown connections between CIB- and CO-associated mechanisms; they should not be presented as entirely isolated linear pathways.

When interpreting a chromatin-association experiment, distinguish between DNA occupancy by a transcription factor and recruitment of another protein through interactions. If a CRY2-containing complex is detected at a region associated with FT expression, the observation may reflect recruitment through DNA-binding partners. It does not show that CRY2's FAD pocket directly recognizes a promoter motif. If disrupting a partner reduces CRY2 association with chromatin, that supports a role for the partner in recruitment, provided overall CRY2 abundance and activation remain comparable. Loss of a single partner may not eliminate association if other proteins can recruit the receptor. This is a useful example of how partial redundancy can occur both at the receptor level and among downstream regulators.

A late-flowering phenotype can be interpreted using the relationship between gene expression and developmental output. Suppose wild-type plants grown under long days have relatively high FT transcript abundance at an appropriate late-day sampling time, while a cry2 mutant has lower FT transcript and flowers later. This pattern supports a contribution of CRY2 to induction of FT in that environment. However, sampling at only one clock time cannot distinguish reduced expression amplitude from a shift in the timing of its peak. A daily time series is needed to make that distinction. Expressing FT from a suitable promoter independently of the affected pathway could test whether restoring a downstream flowering-promoting signal bypasses part of the receptor defect. Such rescue would support pathway ordering for the floral response without proving that all other CRY2 functions act through FT.

Cryptochromes also contribute light input to the plant circadian system. An entrained rhythm maintains an appropriate phase relationship with a repeated environmental cycle. Blue-light signaling can influence the timing of rhythmic outputs, but a receptor that provides environmental input should not automatically be identified with the oscillator itself. If a mutant changes the response of a rhythm to a blue pulse, it may be defective in photic entrainment while retaining the capacity to oscillate under constant conditions. To evaluate this distinction, compare rhythmic behavior during light–dark cycles and after transfer to constant conditions. A persistent rhythm with altered entrainment indicates a different type of defect from complete loss of endogenous rhythmicity. This concise connection prepares the later biological clock chapter without duplicating its full mechanism.

9. Distinguishing cryptochromes, phototropins and phytochromes

The major receptor classes can be distinguished by three features: their chromophores, their protein architecture and the principal physiological responses they control. Phytochromes possess a bilin chromophore and respond strongly to red and far-red light. Phototropins possess flavin mononucleotide, FMN, associated with LOV domains and contain a protein kinase module. Cryptochromes possess a photolyase-related FAD-binding region with a regulatory extension. The fact that phototropins and cryptochromes both use flavin-based photochemistry and absorb blue wavelengths does not imply they transmit light information through the same mechanism.

Directional bending illustrates this distinction. A seedling exposed to unilateral blue light can grow toward the source because phototropin-dependent signaling contributes to a spatial redistribution of auxin and differential growth. In contrast, cryptochrome-dependent suppression of hypocotyl elongation can be observed under uniform blue illumination without any directional difference in light. Both processes affect the hypocotyl, but one is a directional growth response and the other is regulation of overall elongation. In real seedlings, these responses can interact. A receptor that changes baseline growth may alter the magnitude of curvature even if it is not the principal receptor detecting light direction.

Phototropin also controls rapid physiological outputs including chloroplast relocation and blue-light-dependent stomatal opening. These are useful distinctions from cryptochrome's prominent transcription-linked developmental roles. Nevertheless, an exam question that declares cryptochrome has no influence whatsoever on tropic responses or stomatal physiology would overstate the separation. Receptors act within an integrated network, and some physiological outputs can be affected by multiple signaling pathways. Assign primary mechanisms rather than imposing absolute exclusivity.

For an experiment involving directional curvature and general growth inhibition, use both unilateral and spatially uniform illumination. Measure hypocotyl length and bending angle as separate variables. A phototropin-deficient genotype may be strongly defective in curvature while retaining a substantial response in overall length. A cryptochrome-deficient genotype may show reduced inhibition of elongation while retaining phototropin-dependent directional responsiveness. Double mutants can reveal combined effects. Comparisons must account for the fact that very short or growth-impaired seedlings may have limited capacity to exhibit curvature, even with a normal spatial sensing mechanism.

Finally, distinguish these sensory systems from chlorophyll-containing photosystems. Chlorophyll pigments participate directly in photochemical energy conversion in chloroplast thylakoids. Cryptochrome and phototropin instead participate in signal processing. Changes in gene expression, chloroplast positioning or stomatal aperture may affect photosynthetic performance indirectly, but a regulatory influence on photosynthesis is not the same as operating a photosynthetic electron transport chain.

A comparison of receptor mechanisms can also be made at the level of the initial signal transformation. In phytochrome, absorption by the bilin chromophore changes the distribution of photochemical forms, conventionally described as Pr and Pfr in seed plants. In phototropin, light absorption by FMN in a LOV domain leads to a change coupled to the receptor's kinase activity. In cryptochrome, FAD-dependent photochemistry is coupled to changes in receptor conformation, association and regulatory interactions. These are different molecular strategies for converting photons into biological information. A diagram that labels each receptor simply as light activated is accurate at a broad level but insufficient to distinguish the mechanism in an examination.

The direction of a light beam introduces an additional dimension to receptor function. Uniform illumination can change the developmental state of an entire hypocotyl, whereas unilateral illumination can generate unequal signaling across the organ. The resulting bending response requires some mechanism that preserves or converts spatial information into differences in growth. Phototropin-mediated responses are especially important for that directional process. A cryptochrome-mediated change in growth sensitivity may influence the amount of bending indirectly, because the ability of the organ to bend depends on its growth properties. Therefore an observed change in curvature does not by itself prove that the affected molecule is the primary detector of direction. Testing spatially uniform and unilateral treatments helps distinguish these possibilities.

Chloroplast relocation under blue light offers a physiological example in which the response may be relatively rapid and local. Chloroplasts can change their position within cells to alter exposure to light, with phototropins playing central roles. This is conceptually different from the formation or maturation of chloroplasts during seedling de-etiolation, which involves a broader developmental program. If an experiment measures chloroplast position after a short pulse, it is not measuring the same trait as a study of chlorophyll accumulation over several days. Students should identify the measured output before assigning a receptor family. Different assays can involve the same organelle while testing entirely different signaling processes.

The spectral overlap of CRY and phototropin means that removing blue wavelengths is not a selective molecular perturbation of cryptochrome. Such a treatment simultaneously changes the input to several light-sensitive processes. A blue-blocking filter can therefore reveal the importance of blue light but cannot establish which receptor mediates an effect. Combining the filter experiment with receptor-specific mutants provides a stronger test. Similarly, a light-sensitive response in a plant grown under a nominally red lamp does not automatically exclude cryptochrome if the lamp leaks blue wavelengths or if other signals converge on shared regulators. Measure the spectrum, verify the genotype, and interpret the response in the context of the actual treatment.

10. Experimental reasoning: genotype, wavelength and protein turnover

Consider an illustrative experiment in which seedlings of wild type, cry1, cry2 and cry1 cry2 are grown for five days under darkness, weak blue light, stronger blue light or red light. The hypothetical mean lengths are chosen to demonstrate a pattern, not to reproduce published data. In darkness, all four genotypes measure approximately 12 mm. Under weak blue light they measure 7, 9, 10 and 11 mm respectively. Under stronger blue light the values are 3, 8, 4 and 10 mm. In red light they measure 5, 5, 6 and 6 mm. The data suggest that CRY1 is particularly important under stronger blue light, that CRY2 contributes under weak blue light, and that both contribute to the full response. The similar red-light responses provide evidence against a nonspecific growth defect under all illumination, but they do not prove complete independence of the phytochrome and cryptochrome networks.

A useful normalization is fractional inhibition relative to the corresponding dark treatment: inhibition = (L_dark − L_light)/L_dark. For the wild type under stronger blue light, this equals (12 − 3)/12 = 0.75 or 75%. For the cry1 mutant it equals (12 − 8)/12 = 0.333 or approximately 33%. The difference is about 42 percentage points. Calling it a 42% relative reduction is imprecise; relative to the wild-type inhibition, the loss is approximately 56%. The normalization is particularly useful when genotypes differ in initial dark-grown lengths. However, comparisons of ratios should account for variation in both numerator and denominator.

The double mutant still shows a measurable inhibition under stronger blue light. Several explanations are possible. A different photoreceptor may contribute; the alleles may retain some function; light may indirectly alter metabolism; or experimental differences may contribute. Without additional evidence, it is not valid to assign all residual inhibition to phototropin or any one receptor. A genotype-by-treatment interaction should be investigated statistically using biological replication, and the specific residual pathway can then be tested with additional mutants or complementation.

A second illustrative experiment examines HY5 protein after a transfer from darkness to blue illumination. Imagine the normalized wild-type protein values at 0, 15, 30 and 60 min are 1.0, 1.4, 2.0 and 3.0, whereas the cry1 cry2 values are 1.0, 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3. If transcript abundance remains similar over this interval, the pattern supports a role of cryptochromes in light-dependent protein accumulation. Yet immunoblot data alone cannot distinguish a change in translation from a change in degradation. Direct turnover measurements, proteasome dependence and molecular evidence for interactions with the relevant E3 ligase strengthen the mechanistic interpretation.

The design of a co-immunoprecipitation experiment illustrates another important distinction. If CRY2 is recovered with SPA1 more efficiently from blue-light-treated samples than dark samples, this supports light-dependent association. The assay does not automatically establish direct binding because proteins can be connected through other members of a complex. It is also necessary to normalize for the abundance of both proteins in the input, particularly because CRY2 abundance may change during blue exposure. Controls using nonspecific antibodies and appropriate mutant proteins help exclude nonspecific recovery. Purified-protein assays can further test direct interaction, provided the relevant cofactor and light state are maintained.

Genetic epistasis offers a complementary approach. In a simplified pathway, CRY acts to reduce COP1–SPA repression, which otherwise limits HY5 abundance. If loss of a positive upstream receptor causes a long hypocotyl but loss of a downstream negative regulator causes a short hypocotyl in darkness, a double mutant can help place the regulatory steps in order. If the cop1-like phenotype dominates for the tested output, this is consistent with COP1 acting downstream of CRY in that branch. The inference does not prove direct physical interaction or exclude additional parallel branches. Essential genes and pleiotropic mutant phenotypes may complicate the comparison.

A transcriptional reporter provides a further way to connect receptor activation and gene regulation. A promoter from a light-responsive gene can drive a reporter whose signal reflects promoter activity. If induction after blue illumination is reduced in a cry mutant, the gene's regulation is partly CRY-dependent. To infer direct control by HY5, additional experiments would need to demonstrate HY5 occupancy or functional dependence on a relevant cis-regulatory element. A receptor-dependent reporter alone cannot identify the transcription factor that binds the promoter.

These approaches answer different questions: spectra test wavelength dependence, mutants test genetic requirement, complementation strengthens causal attribution, biochemical measurements test cofactor and protein properties, and interaction and reporter assays test downstream signaling. No single result establishes the entire mechanism. A well-supported model emerges when independent kinds of evidence converge on compatible explanations.

A well-designed experiment on photomorphogenesis requires independent biological replicates. Ten seedlings growing on one plate may share the same medium, position in a growth chamber and local light exposure. Their measurements can describe variation among seedlings, but they do not necessarily provide ten independent replicates of a treatment applied to the whole plate. If all wild-type plates are placed on one side of a chamber and all mutant plates on another, a light or temperature gradient may be confounded with genotype. Randomizing positions and using multiple independently prepared plates across experimental runs reduces such risks. A reported mean without information about variation and replication cannot establish the reliability of an observed difference.

A further issue arises when using continuous illumination to compare genotypes with different developmental rates. Seeds may germinate at different times, so a measurement taken five days after sowing need not correspond to five days of hypocotyl development for every genotype. One can record germination time and align later measurements by developmental age, or include appropriate germination controls. If a mutation changes germination as well as later growth, these are separate effects that should not be silently combined. This distinction is particularly important when a photoreceptor has roles at several developmental stages.

Suppose an illustrative experiment records mean hypocotyl lengths of 12 mm in darkness and 6 mm under blue light for genotype A, while genotype B measures 8 mm in darkness and 4 mm under blue light. Comparing light-treated lengths alone suggests that B is more strongly inhibited. Normalization shows that both have 50% fractional inhibition relative to their own dark controls. The difference in absolute length under light can therefore arise from a difference in baseline growth capacity rather than sensitivity to the blue treatment. However, equal fractional inhibition at one light intensity does not establish identical dose-response curves; additional intensities and time points are needed to compare sensitivity and maximum response.

For a reporter experiment, changes in signal can also reflect properties unrelated to transcription. A fluorescent protein may persist after the promoter becomes inactive, while a rapidly turned-over reporter can track short-term changes more closely. Luminescence can depend on substrate availability and metabolic conditions. If blue illumination affects the growth or energy state of the plant, reporter intensity may change even when promoter activity is similar. Including an internal or constitutive reporter control and comparing transcript abundance can help interpret these effects. The aim is not to discard reporters but to understand exactly what each readout represents. A measured signal is a proxy for a biological variable, and that relationship needs validation.

Biochemical and genetic evidence can be combined to localize a receptor defect. Imagine three mutant proteins: one lacks FAD binding, another binds FAD but fails to oligomerize after blue illumination, and the third oligomerizes but does not associate with an essential downstream partner. All three could produce a similar long-hypocotyl phenotype under blue light. Their biochemical profiles would distinguish failures of primary perception, activation-state formation and signal transmission. Measuring only growth would conceal this mechanistic diversity. On the other hand, a protein that behaves normally in a purified biochemical assay may still fail in the plant because it is unstable, incorrectly localized or expressed at the wrong developmental stage. The functional rescue test connects the biochemical properties to the living system.

An exam question may provide a series of results with increasing mechanistic specificity. First, a mutant shows weak hypocotyl inhibition; second, its CRY protein is present at normal abundance; third, its purified receptor absorbs blue light; fourth, a light-dependent interaction is missing. These observations gradually eliminate some hypotheses, but not all. Normal abundance does not guarantee proper folding, and normal absorption does not guarantee the correct signaling-active conformation. Missing interaction could reflect a damaged binding surface or a failure to reach the appropriate activation state. A measurement of light-dependent oligomerization could distinguish these alternatives. Scientific reasoning advances by asking which observation discriminates the remaining models rather than by selecting a molecular explanation prematurely.

The most effective approach to a complex photobiology problem is to establish the reference condition, identify the manipulated factor, identify the measured variable and follow the direction of the regulatory links. A receptor that inhibits an E3 ligase which degrades a positive regulator has a net positive effect on that regulator's abundance. A mutant that removes the receptor is predicted to weaken that effect, whereas a mutation that removes the E3 ligase can bypass the need for the upstream inhibition in the relevant branch. But a conclusion based on that branch must be limited to the specified output. Parallel photoreceptors, distinct developmental stages and compensatory pathways remain possible. This disciplined reasoning is the common foundation of the genetic, biochemical and quantitative problems in this chapter.

11. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. All blue-absorbing proteins are cryptochromes. Phototropins and photosynthetic pigments also absorb blue wavelengths. Identify chromophore, architecture and functional output. Reasoning: Both receptor classes respond to the same wavelength, making color alone an attractive but insufficient clue. In a matching question, require a consistent domain–cofactor–output combination.
  2. Cryptochrome generates NADPH. Cryptochrome signals; photosynthetic electron transport generates NADPH. Reasoning: FAD is also a redox cofactor in metabolism, which invites confusion with the photosynthetic electron transport chain. Follow the endpoint: transcriptional regulation versus reductant production.
  3. CRY1 and CRY2 act in mutually exclusive pathways. Their functions overlap and depend on flux, developmental stage and response. Reasoning: Textbook shorthand often assigns seedling growth to CRY1 and flowering to CRY2. When a question varies light intensity or uses double mutants, consider overlap before choosing an absolute statement.
  4. Photolyase homology proves DNA-repair activity. Homology identifies ancestry, not identical enzymatic activity. Reasoning: The PHR name and related fold can be mistaken for a functional annotation. Look for actual substrate repair evidence rather than sequence resemblance.
  5. CRY has FMN in LOV domains. The canonical plant CRY PHR binds FAD; phototropin LOV domains bind FMN. Reasoning: Both blue receptors bind flavin derivatives, making their cofactor identities easy to exchange. Pair CRY–PHR–FAD and PHOT–LOV–FMN before reasoning about output.
  6. Phosphorylation proves CRY is a phototropin-like kinase. Phosphorylation can be performed by other kinases and does not establish a kinase domain. Reasoning: Many receptor pathways use phosphorylation, but the presence of phosphorylation does not establish the catalytic identity of a protein. Identify the kinase domain or the kinase responsible.
  7. HY5 is the blue-light photoreceptor. HY5 is a downstream transcription factor; CRY contains the sensory chromophore. Reasoning: HY5 accumulation is a prominent light response, so students may confuse the observed signal with the primary sensor. Ask which protein actually contains the chromophore.
  8. COP1 activates photomorphogenesis in darkness. COP1–SPA promotes turnover of positive developmental regulators in darkness. Reasoning: The mutant-derived COP1 name is misleading if read as a description of its normal effect. Trace the wild-type function: COP1 promotes turnover of positive regulators in darkness.
  9. Blue light must increase HY5 mRNA to increase HY5 protein. Reduced degradation can increase protein abundance even with constant transcript and synthesis. Reasoning: Transcript and protein are often assumed to vary together. Use synthesis minus degradation to distinguish altered protein stability from transcriptional induction.
  10. A long hypocotyl identifies a receptor mutation. A downstream signaling or growth defect can yield the same phenotype. Reasoning: A mutant growth phenotype can arise at many pathway levels. Require molecular identification, complementation and receptor photochemistry before assigning the defective component.
  11. Equal fluence necessarily gives equal response. Activation and recovery kinetics can distinguish different intensity-duration combinations. Reasoning: Equal photon dose seems equivalent if recovery is ignored. In a time-dependent model, compare irradiance, duration and the rate of deactivation separately.
  12. A double mutant with residual response proves CRY is unimportant. Residual response may reflect parallel receptors or retained function; compare effect size and controls. Reasoning: A residual phenotype is sometimes interpreted as absence of genetic importance. Quantify the difference from wild type and test additional receptor or allele explanations.
  13. CRY2 is the mobile florigen. CRY2 acts upstream of FT expression; FT protein is a mobile floral signal. Reasoning: CRY2 influences flowering, making it tempting to identify the receptor with florigen. Trace the output from receptor signaling to FT expression and then FT movement.
  14. Phototropism and photomorphogenesis are interchangeable. Curvature and overall elongation are distinct measurements with overlapping regulatory influences. Reasoning: Both responses involve the hypocotyl, but the geometry is different. Identify whether the measured variable is total length or the angle of curvature.
  15. A light-dependent co-immunoprecipitation proves direct binding. Association may be mediated by other proteins; direct binding needs independent testing. Reasoning: Physical recovery of proteins in one complex can be mistaken for a direct molecular contact. Distinguish complex association from direct binding in experimental questions.

12. Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which cofactor is associated with the PHR of Arabidopsis CRY1?

A. FMN in LOV2; B. FAD; C. Phytochromobilin; D. Heme

Answer: B. FAD is the principal cryptochrome chromophore. FMN in LOV domains characterizes phototropins, and phytochromobilin is associated with plant phytochromes.

2. Part B — HY5 belongs to which functional class?

A. Bilin chromophore; B. DNA photolyase; C. bZIP transcription factor; D. Photosystem reaction center

Answer: C. HY5 regulates transcription downstream of several light-signaling pathways, rather than absorbing light as a photoreceptor.

3. Part B — Which protein is a negative regulator of photomorphogenesis in darkness?

A. COP1; B. FT; C. HY5; D. Chlorophyll a

Answer: A. COP1-containing complexes help remove positive light-response regulators. HY5 is one such positive regulator.

4. Part B — The principal photosensory region of canonical plant cryptochromes is called what?

A. LOV2; B. PAS–GAF–PHY only; C. SH2; D. PHR

Answer: D. PHR denotes photolyase homology region. LOV2 is a characteristic phototropin domain.

5. Part B — Which Arabidopsis cryptochrome is particularly associated with long-day floral promotion?

A. Phot1; B. UVR8; C. Rubisco; D. CRY2

Answer: D. CRY2 is important in photoperiod-dependent flowering, though other receptors and timing pathways contribute.

6. Part B — Which process is typical of de-etiolation?

A. Continued rapid hypocotyl elongation with closed cotyledons; B. Cotyledon expansion and restricted elongation; C. Loss of all chloroplasts; D. Complete cessation of metabolism

Answer: B. Illumination changes allocation from dark extension toward light-adapted development, rather than arresting all growth.

7. Part B — Which statement about CRY and photolyase is accurate?

A. CRY has a photolyase-related fold but can function in developmental signaling; B. They have no molecular similarity; C. Both must repair double-stranded DNA lesions; D. Both are thylakoid electron carriers

Answer: A. Structural ancestry does not require identical catalytic or signaling roles.

8. Part B — Which pair most directly describes phototropin architecture?

A. FAD–PHR and C-terminal extension; B. Bilin and GAF; C. FMN–LOV and serine/threonine kinase; D. Heme and SH2

Answer: C. Phototropin uses FMN-containing LOV domains coupled to a kinase. The FAD–PHR option describes cryptochrome.

9. Part C — In the illustrative dataset, which genotype has the greatest impairment relative to WT under strong blue?

A. cry2; B. WT; C. cry1 cry2; D. No difference

Answer: C. The double mutant measures 10 mm versus 3 mm for WT. Longer length in this assay indicates weaker inhibition of elongation.

10. Part C — HY5 synthesis remains 12 units/h while degradation falls from 0.60 to 0.15 h⁻¹. What is the predicted steady-state fold change?

A. 4; B. 1/4; C. 2; D. 8

Answer: A. Steady-state abundance equals synthesis divided by degradation constant. Values rise from 20 to 80 units, a fourfold change.

11. Part C — A mutant absorbs blue light normally but cannot associate with SPA. What conclusion best follows?

A. The protein necessarily fails to bind its chromophore; B. SPA must be upstream of photon absorption; C. Normal absorption proves normal signaling; D. The defect can be downstream of photochemistry

Answer: D. Preserved absorption and impaired interaction distinguish perception from signal transmission. Other photoreceptor and photosynthetic functions are not established by the observation.

12. Part C — A CRY2 immunoblot signal falls after illumination. Which additional result most directly supports increased degradation?

A. Lower CRY2 transcript at the final time point only; B. Faster CRY2 disappearance when synthesis is experimentally blocked; C. Increased CRY2 transcript after blue light; D. A higher amount of total protein in the blue sample

Answer: B. Comparing turnover while controlling synthesis and loading can distinguish increased degradation from reduced synthesis.

13. Part C — A plant shows normal bending toward unilateral blue light but abnormal overall elongation under uniform blue. Which initial candidate is most consistent?

A. A CRY signaling defect; B. Selective loss of the principal directional phototropin input; C. Loss of both CRY and phototropin signaling; D. A general inability to elongate

Answer: A. The separation of directional bending from overall growth inhibition is consistent with a cryptochrome-linked developmental defect. Further genetic and biochemical tests are needed.

14. Part C — Equal fluences produce different growth inhibition. What is the most defensible explanation?

A. Photon number must have been miscalculated; B. Receptor activation and recovery can depend on irradiance and exposure duration; C. Light has no effect; D. All blue receptors are phytochromes

Answer: B. Equal integrated photon delivery does not constrain the time course or steady-state active receptor fraction.

15. Part C — A cop1-like phenotype dominates a cry1 cop1 double mutant for cotyledon opening in darkness. What does this support?

A. Direct physical binding is proven; B. COP1 is necessarily upstream of CRY photon absorption; C. All light responses use only COP1; D. COP1 is downstream in a relevant branch

Answer: D. Epistasis is consistent with regulatory ordering for the specified phenotype, but does not itself establish direct binding or exclude other branches.

16. Part B — What does the term skotomorphogenesis describe?

A. Photosynthetic electron transport; B. Floral organ identity; C. Dark-adapted seedling development; D. Stomatal closure alone

Answer: C. Skotomorphogenesis is the characteristic developmental program in darkness, including elongation and relatively unexpanded cotyledons. The other processes are not definitions of the dark developmental program.

17. Part B — Which quantity is plotted against wavelength in an action spectrum?

A. An explicitly defined biological response or sensitivity; B. Only FAD concentration; C. Only photon energy; D. The size of the chloroplast genome

Answer: A. An action spectrum is defined by a physiological or photochemical response. An absorption spectrum instead measures absorption as wavelength changes.

18. Part B — Which description best fits an apoprotein?

A. A free chromophore; B. A protein without its associated cofactor; C. A fully assembled photosystem; D. A protein after proteolysis only

Answer: B. The apoprotein is the protein portion without the cofactor. The holoprotein includes its associated cofactor.

19. Part B — What is a characteristic light-dependent activation event in plant cryptochromes?

A. Formation of RuBP; B. Pr-to-Pfr photoconversion; C. Photooligomerization; D. Oxygen evolution

Answer: C. Self-association is an important part of CRY signaling activation. Pr/Pfr belongs to phytochrome, whereas RuBP and oxygen evolution concern photosynthesis.

20. Part B — Which proteins are known inhibitors of CRY activation or self-association?

A. Rubisco small subunits; B. ATP synthase subunits; C. Histone H4 only; D. BIC proteins

Answer: D. BIC proteins can constrain cryptochrome assembly and signaling. The other options do not describe this receptor-specific regulatory role.

21. Part B — Which description of SPA proteins is accurate in the canonical dark signaling mechanism?

A. Bilin chromophores; B. FAD biosynthetic intermediates; C. Chloroplast reaction centers; D. COP1-associated regulatory proteins

Answer: D. SPA proteins participate with COP1 in regulating the turnover of positive light-response regulators.

22. Part B — Which proteolytic machinery is responsible for degradation of suitable ubiquitin-tagged HY5 substrates?

A. Calvin cycle; B. Photosystem I; C. 26S proteasome; D. Rubisco

Answer: C. The ubiquitin–proteasome system regulates target protein lifetime; the photosynthetic components are not protein-degradation machinery.

23. Part B — Which Arabidopsis receptor generally contributes substantially to blue-light responses at lower irradiance?

A. CRY2; B. phyB alone; C. UVR8 alone; D. An auxin efflux transporter

Answer: A. CRY2 contributes strongly in many weak-blue assays. CRY1 and other receptors can also participate, so the assignment is not absolute.

24. Part B — Which statement best describes CRY2 abundance during sustained stronger blue illumination in many Arabidopsis assays?

A. It cannot change; B. It may decline through regulated protein turnover; C. It always increases in direct proportion to flux; D. It is converted into phyB

Answer: B. CRY2 is subject to light-dependent regulation of protein abundance. This does not mean that all activated CRY2 molecules disappear immediately.

25. Part B — Which term refers to light-driven transition out of etiolation?

A. Photorespiration; B. De-etiolation; C. Photolysis; D. Photophosphorylation

Answer: B. De-etiolation includes light-induced changes in seedling morphology and development. The other terms refer to different photobiological or metabolic processes.

26. Part B — Which plant protein is a mobile floral signal associated with leaf-to-apex communication?

A. CRY2 itself; B. COP1; C. FT; D. FAD

Answer: C. FT protein can move from leaves to the apex and promote flowering. CRY2 acts upstream in regulation of floral induction.

27. Part B — CIB1 is a member of which transcription factor family?

A. bHLH; B. Ribosomal proteins; C. Aquaporins; D. Photosystem antenna proteins

Answer: A. CIB1 is a CRY2-interacting basic helix–loop–helix transcriptional regulator. It is not itself a photosensory chromophore.

28. Part B — What does photon flux density describe?

A. Total plant biomass; B. Photon energy alone; C. Length of the hypocotyl; D. Photon arrival per area per time

Answer: D. Photon flux density counts photons delivered per area and time; fluence integrates it over the exposure duration.

29. Part B — What is the most accurate description of plant CRY signaling localization?

A. Confined to the thylakoid lumen; B. Exclusively extracellular; C. Always restricted to cell walls; D. Nuclear regulation is important, with CRY1 also found in cytosol

Answer: D. CRY1 and CRY2 can participate in nuclear signaling; CRY1 also has cytosolic functions. Their principal signaling is not thylakoid electron transport.

30. Part B — Which statement distinguishes CRY from a classical cell-surface receptor for an extracellular ligand?

A. It cannot bind any molecule; B. It requires a membrane-spanning ligand-binding domain; C. Its FAD-containing photosensory region receives photon input; D. Its ligand is ubiquitin

Answer: C. Cryptochrome directly detects light via a bound cofactor, rather than requiring a cell-surface ligand-binding mechanism.

31. Part B — What is the primary function of an E3 ubiquitin ligase in the relevant proteolytic pathway?

A. To absorb blue light; B. To contribute to substrate recognition and ubiquitin transfer; C. To generate photosynthetic NADPH; D. To transcribe FT itself

Answer: B. E3 ligases help determine which proteins are ubiquitinated. They do not act as photoreceptor chromophores or RNA polymerases.

32. Part B — Which feature best distinguishes canonical plant cryptochromes from plant phytochromes?

A. A photosensory region binding FAD rather than bilin; B. Both possess identical chromophores; C. CRY is always a membrane channel; D. Phytochrome is a photosystem

Answer: A. The principal chromophore and protein family differ. Both are photoreceptors but have distinct photochemistry and architecture.

33. Part C — Two lamps provide 1 W m⁻² at 450 and 660 nm. Which has the higher incident photon flux?

A. 660 nm; B. 450 nm; C. Equal; D. Not related to wavelength

Answer: A. At equal power, photon flux is inversely proportional to photon energy and therefore proportional to wavelength. Red flux is about 660/450 = 1.47 times blue flux.

34. Part C — A receptor has k_on I = 0.30 min⁻¹ and k_off = 0.10 min⁻¹ in the two-state model. What is its steady active fraction?

A. 0.25; B. 0.75; C. 0.30; D. 3.0

Answer: B. The fraction is 0.30/(0.30+0.10) = 0.75. A fraction of 3.0 is impossible; 0.25 is the inactive fraction.

35. Part C — After light is removed, an illustrative active receptor pool decays with k_off = 0.20 min⁻¹. What is its half-time?

A. 0.14 min; B. 5 min exactly; C. 3.47 min; D. 20 min

Answer: C. For first-order decay t½ = ln 2/k = 0.693/0.20 ≈ 3.47 min. 5 min is the reciprocal rate constant, not the half-time.

36. Part C — A wild-type seedling grows from 2 to 6 mm, while a mutant grows from 4 to 6 mm over four hours in light. What follows?

A. Identical elongation rates because endpoint lengths match; B. Mutant added more; C. No growth occurred; D. WT added 4 mm and mutant 2 mm

Answer: D. Average growth increments are 1 and 0.5 mm/h respectively. Equal endpoints conceal different initial lengths and growth rates.

37. Part C — A cry mutant has normal red-light inhibition of elongation but reduced blue-light inhibition. What is the best initial inference?

A. All signaling is absent; B. CRY is proven to bind DNA; C. A blue-response defect is plausible; D. No control is needed

Answer: C. The wavelength specificity supports a role in blue signaling, but does not establish a molecular receptor identity or eliminate all alternative growth mechanisms.

38. Part C — A purified receptor binds FAD, but its mutant form cannot restore blue-light inhibition. Which test best distinguishes activation from downstream coupling?

A. Repeat only the FAD absorption measurement; B. Measure CRY transcript abundance alone; C. Compare final hypocotyl lengths at the same single intensity; D. Measure receptor oligomerization and association with signaling partners under blue light

Answer: D. The two molecular assays can localize failure after absorption to receptor activation or subsequent partner interactions.

39. Part C — In a mutant line, CRY2 starts at 50 units and reaches 25 after 40 min; WT starts at 100 and reaches 25. Under exponential decay, which is more stable?

A. Mutant; B. WT; C. Equal; D. Neither can decay

Answer: A. The mutant loses half its starting abundance in 40 min; WT loses three quarters, equivalent to two half-lives of 20 min. Equal endpoints do not mean equal stability.

40. Part C — HY5 protein increases after blue light, but HY5 mRNA is unchanged. Which conclusion is justified?

A. Increased transcription is proven; B. Post-transcriptional regulation is plausible; C. Decreased protein degradation is disproven; D. A direct physical CRY–HY5 interaction is proven

Answer: B. Protein stabilization or altered translation could explain the observation; additional turnover assays distinguish the alternatives.

41. Part C — In a simplified pathway, CRY inhibits COP1–SPA and COP1–SPA degrades HY5. What is the net sign of CRY on HY5 abundance?

A. Negative; B. Positive; C. Zero by definition; D. Unknown because all inhibition is equivalent

Answer: B. Two inhibitory links give a net positive effect on HY5 abundance in this branch. Other regulatory inputs can modify the actual phenotype.

42. Part C — If an E3 ligase variant is present but cannot recognize HY5, which result is most consistent with the direct defect?

A. CRY photon absorption necessarily decreases; B. HY5 transcription must stop; C. HY5 turnover must rise because the ligase is present; D. HY5 turnover through that ligase decreases

Answer: D. A substrate-recognition defect can stabilize HY5 without requiring loss of the ligase or alteration of receptor photochemistry.

43. Part C — A cry2 mutant flowers later in long days but nearly normally in short days. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

A. CRY2 contributes to photoperiodic floral regulation; B. The mutation causes an equal delay independent of day length; C. CRY2 is necessarily the clock oscillator; D. The result proves direct DNA binding by CRY2

Answer: A. The genotype-by-photoperiod interaction supports a role in the long-day response. It does not establish that CRY2 is the oscillator or the mobile signal.

44. Part C — FT transcript is low in a cry2 mutant at a single afternoon sampling point. Which experiment best separates a lower peak from a shifted peak?

A. Measure FT at the original sampling time in more seedlings; B. Measure CRY2 abundance at only the original time; C. Measure FT across a full daily time series; D. Compare final flowering time alone

Answer: C. Sampling throughout the cycle identifies the expression phase and amplitude. A single time point cannot distinguish them.

45. Part C — A CRY2–CIB1 complex is found at FT-associated chromatin. Which claim is not warranted?

A. CRY2 directly recognizes a DNA sequence through FAD; B. The proteins may contribute to FT regulation; C. Recruitment through a DNA-binding partner is possible; D. Additional tests can distinguish recruitment

Answer: A. Chromatin association does not establish intrinsic sequence-specific DNA binding by CRY2. A transcription factor may recruit the receptor.

46. Part C — A plant mutant maintains a circadian rhythm in constant conditions but responds abnormally to blue-light pulses. Which process may be selectively impaired?

A. All endogenous oscillation; B. Photic entrainment; C. The entire clock-controlled output network is absent; D. All photoreceptor responses are necessarily absent

Answer: B. The persistence of a rhythm shows an oscillator can still function. Its alignment to blue-light cues may be altered.

47. Part C — Two genotypes show 12→6 mm and 8→4 mm dark-to-blue hypocotyl lengths. What is the fractional inhibition?

A. 6% and 4%; B. 100% in both; C. 50% in both; D. 0% in both

Answer: C. (12−6)/12 and (8−4)/8 both equal 0.5. Absolute differences reflect baseline length as well as treatment response.

48. Part C — All wild-type plates are on one side of the light chamber and all cry plates on the other. Which change most improves causal inference?

A. Increase light only on the mutant side; B. Measure one seedling per genotype; C. Discard light measurements; D. Randomize plate positions and use independent replicated plates

Answer: D. Position and genotype are confounded. Randomization and replication help distinguish a genotype effect from spatial variation.

49. Part C — A HY5-dependent reporter is induced by blue light in WT, but a mutant promoter lacking a relevant regulatory sequence is not induced. Which result would most strengthen a direct transcriptional model?

A. Evidence for HY5 occupancy or sequence-dependent binding at that region; B. Increased HY5 transcript alone; C. A similar final hypocotyl phenotype; D. A blue-light-dependent change in CRY abundance alone

Answer: A. Binding and functional sequence dependence connect the regulator to the promoter. Reporter induction alone does not prove direct occupancy.

50. Part C — A phot1 phot2 mutant has weak curvature but substantial blue-light growth inhibition; cry1 cry2 shows the converse pattern. What is the best inference?

A. All receptors perform identical functions; B. Directional sensing and overall growth inhibition can depend preferentially on different receptor families; C. CRY produces photosynthetic NADPH; D. Curvature is the same variable as length

Answer: B. The contrasting genotype phenotypes support functional specialization while allowing overlap and integration. Each assay tests a different output.

Trace the sequence from FAD-mediated photon absorption to CRY activation, interaction-dependent control of COP1–SPA, HY5 accumulation and transcriptional responses. Distinguish blue-light growth inhibition from directional phototropism and connect CRY2 to flowering-associated transcription. Interpret mutants in defined wavelength, dose and timing conditions.
