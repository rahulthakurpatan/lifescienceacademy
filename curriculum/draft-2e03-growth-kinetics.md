# Microbial Growth Kinetics: Growth Rate and Generation Time

## 1. The biological question: why growth needs a rate

A newly inoculated microbial culture may look almost unchanged for some time, then become visibly cloudy over a relatively short interval. The cells have not suddenly acquired a new mechanism of reproduction at the moment the cloudiness becomes apparent. The increasing population has gradually reached a density that scatters enough light to be noticed. Each of many cells now contributes descendants to the population. This difference between a small population producing a few new cells and a large population producing many new cells is the central reason microbial growth requires a mathematical description that accounts for the population already present.

Growth has more than one meaning in biology. An individual bacterial cell grows when it accumulates membrane, protein, nucleic acid, cell wall and other cellular material. A population grows numerically when the number of cells increases. A filamentous fungus may produce a great deal of biomass without making a corresponding number of independently countable individuals. Even among unicellular bacteria, a division defect can produce long cells whose total mass increases while cell number changes very little. A mathematical model therefore starts by naming what is being measured rather than treating the word growth as a complete definition.

Let N be the number of cells, or the number of cells per unit volume in a culture whose volume remains constant. Let t denote the elapsed time after an explicitly chosen starting point. If a student measures the culture at two moments, N₁ and N₂ are two observations, not rates. The difference N₂ − N₁ tells us the net change in number, and the quotient (N₂ − N₁)/(t₂ − t₁) tells us the average absolute change per unit time. An absolute change is important when estimating the total production of cells, but it is not generally sufficient for comparing their physiological performance.

Consider two hypothetical cultures in the same environment. Culture A contains 100 cells and rises to 200 in one hour. Culture B contains 10,000 and rises to 20,000 in the same hour. A adds 100 cells and B adds 10,000. B therefore has a hundredfold larger absolute increase, but both populations have doubled. If they are homogeneous populations with negligible loss, the capacity for reproduction per unit of existing population is the same. An absolute difference alone would lead to the mistaken conclusion that B has a hundredfold higher intrinsic rate of proliferation. Normalization to the population size eliminates that distinction.

The term kinetics refers to the quantitative description of change with time. In microbial physiology the relevant question is not just whether conditions permit growth but how rapidly a defined biological quantity changes, over what interval, and under what assumptions. A growth curve is the time-dependent observation; a growth equation is a mathematical model for that observation. Two cultures may show similar changes over a short period even if their later curves differ. The model must be tested within the interval to which it is applied.

Cells are able to multiply because they acquire resources and use chemical energy to make cellular structures. In a growing bacterium, replication produces the genetic material necessary for daughter cells, while biosynthesis increases the material available for partitioning. Binary fission normally increases the number of independently living cellular units. However, it does not create cellular mass from nothing. The two daughters together initially contain approximately the mass of their parent immediately before separation, with ongoing synthesis contributing new material before and after the division event. This is why a count trajectory and a biomass trajectory need not have identical detailed shapes at the level of a single cell.

In the earlier growth-curve model, freshly transferred cells may show a period of adaptation, followed by a period of rapid numerical increase, then growth limitation and eventually a decline in recoverable cells. Kinetic calculations in this chapter are based primarily on the exponential portion. They do not automatically describe the entire period since inoculation. If the graph tells us that an exponential phase starts two hours after inoculation, a rate estimated from that phase cannot be extrapolated backwards to the inoculation time without an additional assumption about the lag phase.

There are two complementary ways to formulate the exponential model. The first uses generations and binary multiplication. It is accessible because it follows the descendants of an initial cell. The second uses a continuous rate proportional to the population, which makes the mathematics suitable for an asynchronous culture with many cells dividing at different times. They are not competing explanations of growth. When their assumptions agree, they predict exactly the same population ratios and doubling times. Understanding their connection removes the need to memorize unrelated equations.

A useful distinction should be maintained throughout this chapter between directly measured quantities and quantities inferred using a model. A count at a given moment is an observation with measurement error. A calculated generation time based on two such counts is an estimate. A fitted specific growth rate is a summary of the change during an interval. A prediction of how many cells will exist later is conditional on the continuation of the estimated conditions. These three activities—measurement, estimation and prediction—have different sources of uncertainty.

## 2. Binary fission as the basis of geometric population increase

Suppose one cell divides into two viable daughter cells and both daughters subsequently divide. Starting from a single founder, the first division gives two cells, the second complete round gives four, and the third gives eight. There are eight cells after three rounds, not six, because every member of the population can contribute to the next round. The same principle applies to a culture containing many founders: if each cell successfully produces two daughters and neither is lost, each completed round multiplies the population by two.

An ideal synchronized population is a convenient starting model. Synchronization means that all cells are at similar stages and divide at approximately the same time. At the initial moment N₀ cells are present. One complete generation later N₁ = 2N₀. Two generations later N₂ = 2N₁ = 4N₀. After three generations N₃ = 2N₂ = 8N₀. Repeating the multiplication n times gives N = N₀ × 2ⁿ. The exponent counts how many factors of two have been applied, whereas the base two represents the number of daughters produced per dividing parent. This is called geometric growth when described in equal discrete rounds.

The net number of additional cells is not N₀ × 2ⁿ; it is N − N₀. For example, four founders undergoing three rounds give 32 cells in total, an increase of 28. Under the special assumption that each division replaces one parent with two daughters and there is no loss, each individual fission event contributes one net cell. Hence the cumulative number of fission events is also 28. It can be obtained by adding four first-round events, eight second-round events and sixteen third-round events. The final number of descendants and the number of division events differ because the original four cells were already present.

A linear growth model instead adds the same number at every interval. If four cells somehow produced four additional cells per generation-equivalent period regardless of their abundance, the sequence would be 4, 8, 12, 16. In the binary growth model the sequence is 4, 8, 16, 32. Both models give the same answer after one period, so a single observation of doubling cannot by itself establish exponential kinetics. The distinction emerges only when several time intervals reveal whether differences or ratios remain approximately constant.

The discrete picture also clarifies why the count of generations is not the same as the count of cell divisions in a culture. A generation corresponds to a doubling of the population under the idealized exponential model. A thousand founders all dividing once give two thousand cells: one population generation has occurred, but one thousand individual division events occurred. Calling the generation number one thousand would confuse the events occurring in parallel with the number of successive rounds experienced by a lineage. This distinction is especially useful when a problem asks separately about descendant number, number of generations and net additions.

A real lineage can show considerable variability. Cells may not receive exactly the same amount of material at division, and physiological state, cell age and environmental fluctuations can influence how soon the daughters divide. A cell may fail to divide or produce a nonviable descendant. The simple geometric expression assumes that these effects are negligible for the chosen interpretation or that their combined effect can be captured by a constant net population doubling rate. It is not a microscopic law that every bacterium must execute at identical intervals.

Even for an ideal synchronized population, the actual numerical trajectory is stepwise. In a complete round, the count remains nearly constant until cells separate, then increases. If the exact same generation time g applies to every cell, the observed count in this special idealization is N₀ × 2 raised to the integer number of completed rounds. A graph of actual discrete counts would contain horizontal segments separated by jumps. The smoothly interpolated expression N₀ × 2^(t/g) agrees with the counts at the full-generation moments but should not be interpreted as the literal number of cells between two perfectly synchronized division events.

The ordinary broth culture differs from this idealization. It typically contains cells distributed across different stages of their own cycles. Some are growing towards division while others have just separated. When the culture is sufficiently large, division events overlap in time and the population trajectory can be treated as smooth. This explains why a mathematical expression containing a fractional generation can describe the behavior of a population even though no individual cell completes a fraction of a division event in the sense of producing a fractional daughter. The fractional generation is an equivalent change in population number on a logarithmic scale.

Worked example 1. A hypothetical culture begins the exponential interval with 7,500 independently countable cells. After four complete population doublings, N = 7,500 × 2⁴ = 7,500 × 16 = 120,000 cells. The net increase is 112,500 cells. Under loss-free binary division, the cumulative event number is also 112,500. If there were deaths, the same net increase could arise from a larger number of fissions accompanied by losses, so the event count could no longer be recovered from the two population sizes alone.

## 3. Generation time and the meaning of fractional generations

Generation time is conventionally denoted by g or t_d in elementary microbial population kinetics. Here g is the time required for a specified population quantity to double during steady exponential growth. A single cell also has an interdivision time, the interval between its birth and its next division. When every cell has the same interdivision time and there are no losses, that interval coincides with population doubling time. In a heterogeneous population the population doubling time is an aggregate property and need not equal the ordinary arithmetic average of observed individual cycle times.

A culture growing exponentially with a generation time of 30 minutes undergoes two equivalent doublings per hour. With a generation time of two hours, it undergoes only half an equivalent doubling per hour. This inverse relationship is fundamental: shorter generation time means faster proportional population growth. A large generation time is not evidence of greater growth simply because the number is larger. It expresses the time cost of multiplying by a fixed factor.

If a population grows through n equivalent generations in elapsed time t, then n = t/g and g = t/n. The relationship follows from the definition: the elapsed time is the number of equivalent generation intervals multiplied by the duration of each interval. In a constant-rate model one can use fractional values of n. For example, 45 minutes at a generation time of 30 minutes corresponds to n = 1.5. The expected population ratio is 2^1.5 = 2√2 ≈ 2.828, not three and not four.

A second example makes the interpretation of fractions more concrete. A population whose number rises by 50 percent has become 1.5 times its starting size. It has not undergone half a doubling, because halfway in time between N₀ and 2N₀ under exponential growth is √2 N₀, about 1.414N₀. Instead the equivalent generation number is log₂(1.5) ≈ 0.585. In general, a fractional increase expressed as a percentage does not translate linearly into a fraction of a generation.

Sometimes a textbook denotes the number of generations per time by k. We will use k = n/t = 1/g, with units of generations per hour if time is expressed in hours. This symbol must not be confused with the natural-logarithmic specific growth rate μ, which has units of inverse time and differs numerically by a factor of ln 2. Different authors use the letter k for different rate constants. An examination problem should therefore be interpreted according to the equation supplied or the quantity explicitly defined rather than according to the letter alone.

The measured generation time belongs to the particular organism and conditions under which the estimate was made. A species may grow rapidly in a nutrient-replete medium at a favorable temperature and much more slowly in another environment. It is scientifically inappropriate to say that a familiar laboratory bacterium always has a fixed short generation time under all circumstances. The genome makes particular biochemical strategies possible, but the environment and current physiology constrain their performance. Temperature, nutrient availability, pH and oxygen can affect rates of biosynthesis or loss; these effects are developed further in the next chapter.

Worked example 2. A population of 2 × 10⁴ cells per millilitre grows with g = 40 min for 3 h 20 min, with no lag or loss and fixed volume. Convert 3 h 20 min to 200 min before dividing by 40 min. The ratio is five generations and N = 2 × 10⁴ × 32 = 6.4 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. Expressing time as 3.20 hours would be incorrect: 20 minutes is one third of an hour. The equivalent calculation is (10/3 h)/(2/3 h) = 5. Both approaches give the same dimensionless exponent.

Worked example 3. A population quadruples over 90 min. The factor four is 2², so there are two equivalent generations. The generation time is 45 min, and k = 60/45 = 4/3 generations per hour. The percentage increase is 300 percent of the initial population, not 400 percent, because the final amount includes the original 100 percent. Population ratio, percentage increase and equivalent generations answer three distinct questions.

## 4. Finding unknown generation numbers using logarithms

Population multiplication is easy to compute when n is known. The inverse problem is more common in an experiment: the investigator measures the starting and ending population and wishes to determine how many equivalent doublings occurred. A logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation. If a positive number R equals 2ⁿ, then n = log₂ R. Taking the ratio of the two observed population values removes the starting scale and leaves the multiplication factor.

Begin with N = N₀ × 2ⁿ. Divide both sides by N₀ to obtain N/N₀ = 2ⁿ. The ratio must be positive. Taking natural logarithms gives ln(N/N₀) = n ln 2, using the logarithmic rule that the logarithm of a power equals the exponent times the logarithm of the base. Rearranging gives n = ln(N/N₀)/ln 2. The same derivation using common logarithms yields n = log₁₀(N/N₀)/log₁₀2. The base of the logarithm is not a biological property of the culture; it is a choice of mathematical representation, and both numerator and denominator must use the same base.

The quotient rule also permits the expression n = [log₁₀ N − log₁₀ N₀]/0.30103 when both numbers use the same population units. With logarithms, scientific notation is useful. For a quantity a × 10ᵇ, log₁₀ of its numerical value is log₁₀ a + b. If the same powers of ten occur in both starting and ending values, they cancel in the ratio. In formal dimensional terms the logarithm should apply to a dimensionless ratio, or to a numerical value expressed relative to a specified reference unit. It is therefore important to express both observations on the same basis before taking logarithms.

Suppose the count increases from 10⁴ to 10⁷ cells per millilitre. The ratio is 10³, or a thousandfold increase. Three is the difference in base-ten logarithms, not the number of binary generations. The latter is 3/0.30103 ≈ 9.966. A common error is to equate an increase of three orders of magnitude with three doublings. Three doublings give only eightfold multiplication. Ten doublings give 1,024-fold multiplication, which explains why a thousandfold change corresponds to slightly fewer than ten generations.

Worked example 4. A constant-volume culture rises from 2.5 × 10⁵ to 1.0 × 10⁷ cells per millilitre during a four-hour exponential interval. The ratio is 40. Because 2⁵ = 32 and 2⁶ = 64, we can predict that n lies between five and six. Numerically n = ln 40/ln 2 = 3.688879/0.693147 ≈ 5.32193. Therefore g = 4/5.32193 h ≈ 0.75161 h, or 45.10 min. A plausibility check is that four hours divided by roughly five and a third gives slightly less than an hour. Inserting the estimated g back into the original expression recovers the observed factor 40 to rounding accuracy.

A logarithm of a ratio smaller than one is negative. If a viable count falls from 800 to 200, log₂(200/800) = −2. This says the population has experienced a net reduction by a factor of four, equivalent to two successive halvings. The negative value is not a negative number of actual divisions. In that interval the growth-only interpretation has failed because net loss exceeds production. Similarly, the value n = 0 corresponds to no net change. Dividing elapsed time by zero to report a doubling time is not a meaningful finite result. These cases motivate a more general treatment in terms of net specific rate.
