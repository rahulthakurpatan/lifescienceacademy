# Site-Specific Recombination: Integration, Excision and Inversion

## 1. DNA rearrangement as a biological solution

DNA is usually introduced as a stable store of information. A cell must copy that information and correct damage without changing the intended sequence. Yet there are situations in which a precise alteration in the arrangement of DNA is advantageous. A bacterial virus can gain a stable place in the host chromosome. A circular chromosome that has become a dimer can be separated into two individual circles. A bacterium can change which surface protein is expressed by reversing a short regulatory segment. In each case, the useful event is not the production of another copy of the whole genome. It is a controlled change in the connections between existing DNA segments. Site-specific recombination is one of the biological solutions to this problem.

Consider two pieces of double-stranded DNA. Each contains a short sequence that is recognized by a particular enzyme. The enzyme brings those sequences together and makes a reciprocal exchange of DNA connections at specified positions. The new connections can put a DNA segment inside a different molecule, release a segment that was previously part of a larger molecule, or change the orientation of a segment within the same molecule. These are called integration, excision and inversion, respectively. The molecular machinery does not need to read the biological meaning of the surrounding genes. It operates on recognizable DNA addresses, and the arrangement of those addresses determines what happens to the larger pieces of DNA.

The term recombination refers to a change in the combination or physical linkage of DNA sequences. It need not imply that two homologous chromosomes exchange entire chromosome arms during meiosis. The term site-specific describes an important property of the recognition process: the enzyme acts on a particular short DNA element, or on a pair of particular compatible elements, rather than locating a partner by extensive sequence similarity. The DNA around the recognition elements can differ greatly in sequence and function. This explains how a phage genome, containing genes quite different from those of its bacterial host, can still join the host chromosome reproducibly at a particular attachment site.

In this chapter, the focus is conservative site-specific recombination. In the elementary reaction, existing DNA phosphodiester bonds are exchanged without an obligatory round of DNA replication or addition and removal of nucleotides. The products contain the DNA material present in the substrates, reorganized into different connections. Conservative does not mean that the genome must remain the same size. For example, a segment that becomes a separate circle is no longer part of its original chromosome, although its nucleotides still exist in the circle immediately after the reaction. If that circle cannot replicate independently, it can disappear from the cell population later. This distinction between the immediate chemical product and its subsequent biological fate is essential.

A student can approach any site-specific recombination problem by separating three levels of reasoning. The first is chemical: which covalent bonds change, and how are new bonds formed? The second is structural: which recognition sites participate, how are they oriented, and are they on one or two DNA molecules? The third is biological: what does the resulting DNA organization do to replication, gene expression, inheritance or survival? Confusing these levels leads to incorrect conclusions. A reaction may be chemically reversible but biologically biased toward one product because the released circle is diluted. A protein may be catalytically competent but unable to recombine sites because a necessary accessory protein is absent. A DNA segment may be inverted without any change in its nucleotide composition, yet the genes inside it may no longer be expressed.

### 2. DNA polarity, recognition sequences and the meaning of an arrow

The starting point is the DNA double helix. Two complementary strands run antiparallel. Each strand has a chemically defined five-prime end and three-prime end. The backbone contains phosphodiester connections between sugars. Complementary bases provide a predictable pairing relationship between strands, whereas the arrangement of bases along a strand supplies sequence information. A protein can recognize a sequence by contacting the exposed chemical groups of base pairs and the shape of the DNA backbone. Consequently, a recombinase can distinguish a suitable site from much of the surrounding chromosome without needing to separate the entire duplex into free single strands.

A recombination site is a DNA segment at which strand exchange can occur. Many sites have two binding regions arranged approximately as inverted repeats, separated by a central region where the DNA strands are cleaved and exchanged. The repeated regions provide docking surfaces for protein subunits. The central sequence contributes to catalytic alignment, compatibility of the reacting partners and orientation. This is a useful general organization, but attachment sites in regulated natural systems may be more complex. For example, the lambda phage attachment region contains additional protein-binding elements beyond its short exchange region. One must not assign the exact dimensions of a simple loxP site to every recombinase family.

An arrow drawn above a site represents the orientation of that sequence relative to the chosen direction along the DNA. It does not represent a replication fork or RNA polymerase movement. It also does not identify which single DNA strand is being cut. Both strands belong to the recognition site. If an oriented site is turned end for end, its arrow changes direction, although the chemical polarity of the individual strands remains the ordinary five-prime to three-prime polarity. The reference strand read from left to right after an inversion corresponds to the reverse complement of the old sequence. Merely reversing the written order of letters without taking complements does not describe a double-stranded DNA inversion correctly.

The canonical loxP sequence illustrates the source of directional information. It has a length of 34 base pairs, with two 13-base-pair inverted repeats flanking an asymmetric eight-base-pair spacer. The outer elements allow Cre protein to bind in the appropriate arrangement. Although those two recognition arms have a symmetry relationship, the central spacer makes the complete site asymmetric. Therefore, the whole site can be assigned a direction. Two loxP sites facing the same way along a chromosome are described as direct repeats; two facing toward or away from one another are inverted repeats. These labels refer to the orientation of entire sites, not merely to the arrangement of the two short binding arms within one loxP site.

To make this distinction concrete, imagine a left flank A, a central segment M and a right flank B. If the two complete sites are aligned in the same direction, the arrangement is A, forward site, M, forward site, B. If the second site is reversed, the arrangement becomes A, forward site, M, reverse site, B. The amount and identity of DNA in the two starting molecules can be the same except for the orientation of the second site. Nevertheless, a single conservative recombination event will generate fundamentally different molecular products. This is why a diagram showing only two boxes labelled loxP, without arrows or equivalent sequence information, is insufficient to predict a unique outcome.

Two sites can also occur on different molecules. In that case, their orientation has to be interpreted together with the topology and the chosen representation of each molecule. An isolated circular DNA has no absolute left or right end. One can rotate the circular map without changing the molecule. A linear chromosome, in contrast, has distinct flanking sequences at its ends. A correct analysis begins by marking identifiable flanks and the orientation of each site, then following the connections through the reaction. Reliance on the position of an arrow on a drawing alone, without tracing its flanks, can give misleading results.

### 3. Why site-specific recombination differs from homologous recombination

Homologous recombination typically involves DNA molecules or regions that share substantial sequence similarity. In bacterial cells, RecA promotes homology search and strand exchange; in eukaryotic cells, Rad51 plays a related role. Such reactions help repair broken DNA, restore missing information using a homologous template and produce genetic variation through exchange between homologous chromosomes. A critical challenge in that process is locating an appropriate homologous partner across a large amount of potentially unrelated DNA. The resulting intermediates can include heteroduplex DNA and Holliday junctions.

Site-specific recombination solves a different recognition problem. The enzymes bind short designated sites and establish a synaptic complex between them. A phage attachment site and its bacterial counterpart need not be surrounded by long homologous regions. The central exchange regions must generally support proper strand pairing and catalytic compatibility, but the DNA beyond these regions can be unrelated. For that reason, absence of extensive homology is not equivalent to absence of sequence constraints. The enzyme recognizes particular sites and the reacting DNA segments must be arranged in a suitable geometry.

The distinction is especially important because a Holliday junction can occur in both homologous recombination and many site-specific recombination reactions. A Holliday junction is a four-way DNA connection, not the name of one exclusive biochemical pathway. In homologous recombination it may arise through strand invasion and subsequent rearrangement of homologous DNA molecules. In the tyrosine recombinase family it is produced after the exchange of the first pair of DNA strands between aligned target sites. The presence of such an intermediate therefore does not establish which type of recombination occurred. Information about the substrate, enzyme and sequence requirements is necessary.

Neither process should be confused with nonhomologous end joining. End joining is a mechanism for reconnecting broken DNA ends, particularly double-strand breaks. Its substrate is a discontinuity that needs repair, and nucleotides can be removed or added before the ends are joined. A conservative site-specific recombinase creates and controls its own reactive DNA ends within an assembled complex. The ordinary reaction is not an attempt to rescue a random broken chromosome. Instead, the controlled cleavage and joining are the planned molecular operations that rearrange the DNA. There may be abnormal situations in which recombinase-associated cleavage creates damage, but such failures are not the normal pathway.

Transposition is another process that can change genomic arrangement. A transposase recognizes the ends of a mobile element and moves or copies that element to a target. Depending on the transposon, a new target sequence duplication or another characteristic product may be generated through DNA repair or replication. Some transposition systems have preferred target sequences, so the word specific on its own is not a complete classification criterion. The classical conservative site-specific reaction considered here is defined by reciprocal exchange at a pair of cognate recombination sites. Certain mobile elements also encode genuine site-specific recombinases for other stages of their life cycles. A student must distinguish the chemistry and purpose of each individual step rather than assigning one label to every activity of the element.

This comparison explains why site-specific recombination is useful for deliberate genome rearrangement. If two recognizable sites are placed around a particular DNA interval, the enzyme can change that interval without conducting a search for long stretches of homologous sequence. The product remains dependent on local chromosomal accessibility and compatible sites. Thus the system is neither a universal sequence editor nor a mechanism that can automatically act at every gene. It is a molecular switch whose input is a specified pair of DNA addresses and whose output follows from the geometry of those addresses.
