## 6. The classical minus thirty-five and minus ten elements

A promoter can be understood initially through two sets of sequence-specific contacts made at different positions along DNA. In a frequently used model of an E. coli σ⁷⁰ promoter, the upstream hexamer is TTGACA and the downstream hexamer is TATAAT, written on the nontemplate strand in the direction of transcription. The former is known as the −35 element and the latter as the −10 element or Pribnow box. Their names are approximate positional conventions: a typical coordinate assignment places the six bases of the −35 element at −35 through −30 and those of the −10 element at −12 through −7. Notice that the numbers name regions rather than the distance between their centers in a strict mathematical sense. These sequences are consensus sequences derived from comparisons among promoters, not fixed sequences that every promoter must possess.

A consensus sequence summarizes a set of aligned biological sequences. For example, if eight out of ten promoters have thymine at an aligned position and the other two have cytosine, the consensus at that position is ordinarily written as thymine. The result describes the most frequent base, not a guarantee that thymine is required for activity. Furthermore, a consensus sequence constructed one position at a time need not correspond to any actual member of the alignment. Certain substitutions may be tolerated only when other positions have complementary properties, so frequency at one position cannot fully capture the relationships among positions.

The historical identification of a conserved promoter region was enabled by the ability to isolate DNA associated with RNA polymerase and determine sequence information. In 1975 David Pribnow reported the sequence of a protected RNA polymerase binding region at an early bacteriophage T7 promoter [3]. The observation linked a physically recognized region to its primary DNA sequence. Comparing multiple promoter sequences allowed recurring features to be identified. The reasoning differs from simply finding short nucleotide words in a genome: the sequences were connected to measured biological behavior. A motif becomes meaningful because promoter recognition or activity has been demonstrated, not merely because it resembles a familiar pattern.

Why do two recognition elements help to locate a starting point? A sequence of six nucleotides is short enough to occur elsewhere by chance. Recognition of two spatially separated features provides additional constraints. The holoenzyme can use the upstream sequence to help orient its binding and the downstream sequence to position the region that must open. In a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter, sigma region four is associated with the −35 element, whereas region two interacts with the −10 region. The physical separation of the domains means that successful recognition depends on their relationship along the double helix. A promoter with individually favorable sequences can function poorly if those sequences are placed at an unsuitable distance.

A simplified probability calculation illustrates why additional constraints are useful. Assume DNA is an independent random sequence with equal probabilities of the four bases. The chance of observing any specified six-base word at an assigned position is 1 divided by 4⁶, or 1/4096. In a hypothetical genome of four million bases, roughly 977 occurrences of that word would be expected in one chosen orientation, ignoring edges. The probability of two specified six-base words at predetermined nonoverlapping positions is 1/4¹², approximately one in 16.8 million. Their expected number in the same simplified genome is about 0.24. These numbers are not estimates of the true number of bacterial promoters, since real promoters tolerate multiple sequences, DNA composition is unequal and occurrences are not always independent. The calculation demonstrates the statistical value of combined recognition constraints rather than providing a literal model of bacterial transcription.

A promoter with one substitution in the −35 hexamer may retain activity if the other recognition contacts remain favorable. Similarly, a promoter with a weak −35 element can be functional when additional elements or regulatory proteins compensate. Some promoters with a near-consensus pair of hexamers nevertheless initiate inefficiently because the subsequent transition to an open complex or promoter escape is unfavorable. Thus promoter prediction from sequence alone involves a hierarchy: identifying candidate recognition sites is a useful first step, evaluating their geometry and context is a second, and measuring correctly initiated RNA provides the functional test.

The AT-rich nature of the familiar −10 sequence is connected to its role in the region of DNA opening. However, the biological significance is not explained simply by saying that adenine and thymine form two hydrogen bonds while guanine and cytosine form three. Base stacking, the DNA sequence context and interactions with the polymerase also affect the energetics. Sigma recognition of particular exposed bases contributes sequence specificity after the strands have separated. The chemical composition of the DNA influences the ease with which opening occurs, while protein recognition determines whether the opened state is stabilized in the appropriate orientation. These are related but distinguishable properties.

## 7. Spacer length, helical geometry and start-site positioning

The spacer is the DNA segment between the two recognition hexamers. With a −35 element extending from −35 through −30 and a −10 element extending from −12 through −7, the intervening positions are −29 through −13. Counting these inclusively gives seventeen base pairs. This is the basis for the common description of a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter as containing a seventeen-base spacer. The distance between the first nucleotide of one hexamer and the first nucleotide of the other is twenty-three base positions, not seventeen. The examination question must be read carefully to determine which distance is being requested.

Spacer length influences the ability of sigma to contact the two recognition elements simultaneously. DNA is a double helix rather than a straight ribbon. As the number of intervening base pairs changes, the next recognition surface is both displaced along the helical axis and rotated around it. The preferred geometry of a protein complex may therefore become less favorable when bases are inserted or deleted between otherwise intact elements. The presence of two consensus hexamers is not enough to establish that their relative placement is suitable.

For a rough geometric model, B-form DNA has about 10.5 base pairs per helical turn and an axial rise of approximately 0.34 nanometres per base pair. An insertion of one base pair changes the relative phase of the downstream element by roughly 360/10.5 degrees, or 34 degrees, and changes the axial separation by about 0.34 nanometres. An insertion of two bases doubles these estimates to about 69 degrees and 0.68 nanometres. Actual promoter DNA can bend, twist and deform in the complex, so these values describe ideal free DNA and should not be confused with measured distances between protein domains. Nevertheless, the model explains why small spacing changes can have substantial functional consequences even without changing any nucleotide within a recognition hexamer.

An insertion of approximately one helical turn restores much of the original rotational relationship but adds considerable axial distance. It would therefore be incorrect to assume that insertion of eleven bases automatically restores wild-type activity merely because the motifs again face nearly the same direction. The sigma regions must still reach their respective DNA elements, and the rest of the promoter must still fit the catalytic cleft. In some regulatory systems long-range protein contacts are influenced by helical phasing, but the core bacterial promoter is additionally constrained by the short distance over which different parts of one holoenzyme must interact.

The spacer is not necessarily biologically inert. Its sequence affects DNA flexibility and can influence the relative ease of forming an initiation complex. An insertion may also introduce a new recognition element or change a local sequence-dependent bend. To investigate length specifically, it is useful to compare several insertion variants of the same length but different base sequences. If their activities are similar to each other and different from the original, a length-related interpretation becomes stronger. If variants of equal length behave differently, sequence-dependent effects must also be considered.

The distance from the −10 element to the transcription start site creates another constraint. When the downstream DNA enters the active center, the template base that occupies the initiating position determines the first nucleotide incorporated into RNA. The geometry of the opened DNA, sequence-dependent nucleotide preference and the concentrations of initiating substrates all help influence the selection. The +1 position is often a purine on the nontemplate strand, corresponding to initiation with ATP or GTP, but the use of a pyrimidine as the first nucleotide is possible. Thus the statement that every bacterial transcript begins with adenine is false.

Start-site selection can be investigated by changing the position of a preferred initiating nucleotide without changing the upstream recognition motifs. An experiment might reveal two RNA products that differ in their first few bases but share the remainder of the transcription unit. This outcome is consistent with use of alternative transcription starts. It is distinct from the presence of two separate promoters, because the same binding arrangement may support initiation at neighboring positions. Additional mapping is needed before concluding which explanation is correct.

Consider a hypothetical promoter with a normal spacer and a start at a defined adenine. A two-base insertion upstream of the −10 element, between the two hexamers, can impair simultaneous recognition without changing the sequence surrounding the start. A two-base insertion between the −10 element and +1 can instead alter the relationship of the start region to the binding site. The two mutations insert the same number of bases but change different geometrical relationships. This distinction is why a mutation description must specify its position rather than merely reporting its size.

## 8. Additional promoter elements and natural exceptions

Although the classical two-hexamer picture is an excellent introduction, some important bacterial promoters use additional recognition surfaces. These extend the range of sequences capable of supporting initiation. An upstream promoter element, known as the UP element, is a region often enriched in adenine and thymine that can interact with the carboxyl-terminal domains of the RNA polymerase alpha subunits. It lies upstream of the usual −35 element, commonly in a region approximately between −40 and −60, although the exact functional boundaries depend on the promoter.

The alpha subunit therefore participates in more than core assembly. Its flexible connection between the amino-terminal assembly domain and the carboxyl-terminal DNA-contacting domain allows it to contribute to promoter recognition at positions upstream of the principal sigma contacts. This arrangement can stabilize the promoter-bound complex or help establish favorable geometry. A promoter that includes an effective UP element may recruit polymerase much more efficiently than an otherwise identical promoter without it.

A classical example comes from studies of the E. coli ribosomal RNA promoter rrnB P1. Ross and colleagues reported in 1993 that an upstream AT-rich element could greatly stimulate transcription and that the alpha subunit participated in recognition [4]. Their findings established an additional direct RNA polymerase–DNA interaction rather than requiring a separate transcription activator for the effect. The distinction between direct recognition by alpha and regulation through an independent DNA-binding protein is important. Both can increase polymerase recruitment, but a mutation removing alpha's DNA-contacting surface and a mutation removing an activator do not necessarily produce identical results.

A second important variation is the extended −10 element. Some σ⁷⁰ promoters contain a short additional sequence, often represented by a TG motif immediately upstream of the −10 hexamer. This feature interacts with another region of sigma and may allow efficient recognition despite a weak or absent recognizable −35 sequence. The familiar elements should therefore be treated as a family of compatible contacts rather than an obligatory checklist. A sequence lacking TTGACA is not automatically disqualified from functioning as a bacterial promoter.

The discriminator is located between the −10 element and the transcription start region. Its sequence can influence the stability and lifetime of the open complex and therefore how initiation responds to changing conditions. This feature is particularly useful for distinguishing initial DNA binding from later kinetic events. Two promoters with identical −35 and −10 motifs may have different discriminator sequences and different lifetimes in the open state. An investigator who measures binding but not productive RNA formation can miss this difference.

The initially transcribed region is also relevant. Once transcription starts, those nucleotides determine the sequence of the short RNA and the nature of its contacts with the active center, the DNA template and other parts of the initiating complex. Changing this region can influence abortive-product formation and escape even if the upstream recognition elements are unchanged. Consequently, the functional boundary of an initiation region extends beyond the two most familiar upstream motifs. A region that is copied into RNA can still affect the initiation process that produces the RNA.

A conceptual example makes the modular nature of promoters clearer. Promoter A has recognizable −35 and −10 elements and no strong additional contacts. Promoter B has a weak −35 element but a favorable extended −10 region. Promoter C contains both conventional elements and an effective UP element. It is reasonable to predict that their binding mechanisms will not be identical, but it is not safe to rank their complete transcriptional output solely from these descriptions. Each promoter still has to open, choose a start, synthesize its first nucleotides and release its upstream contacts. The relative effectiveness of each additional interaction depends on the remaining steps.

Promoter modularity also provides an experimentally useful strategy. One can compare wild-type polymerase and a derivative with altered alpha upstream-contacting ability on templates that differ only in their UP elements. If removal of an UP element reduces transcription strongly with wild-type polymerase but has little additional effect with the alpha mutant, the two changes may affect the same functional interaction. This is a form of genetic or biochemical interaction reasoning. The absence of an additional effect is not absolute proof of direct physical contact, because both mutations might independently drive a process to a floor below which it cannot be measured. Supporting evidence comes from DNA-binding and structural studies, but the comparison itself is a valuable examination-level prediction.

## 9. Alternative sigma factors and the sigma-54 exception

The same catalytic core can function with different sigma factors, allowing a bacterium to direct transcription toward different classes of promoters. Members of the σ⁷⁰ family share broad structural and functional principles but differ in their recognition surfaces and other properties. A housekeeping sigma factor usually supports a large set of genes required for routine growth, while alternative factors direct the transcription machinery toward subsets of genes relevant to specific developmental or environmental conditions. These categories are useful descriptions of dominant physiological roles, not strict divisions that prevent overlap.

In E. coli, σ³² provides a classic example of a factor involved in responses to heat-associated protein damage. σ³⁸, commonly called RpoS, participates in stationary-phase and stress-associated expression, and σ²⁸ contributes to expression of flagellar genes. In Bacillus subtilis, the housekeeping sigma factor is commonly called σᴬ, and alternative factors participate in the temporal and cellular organization of sporulation. These examples demonstrate how a targeting principle is reused in different organisms. They do not imply that all bacteria possess E. coli's particular set of sigma proteins.

Some examination questions present a strain in which a particular alternative sigma is inactivated. If a promoter is recognized mainly by that sigma, its output is expected to decline more strongly than that of an otherwise comparable housekeeping promoter. However, residual transcription may occur through another factor with partially overlapping specificity. The phenotype can be conditional because a promoter that is used only under a particular stress may contribute little under standard laboratory growth. A lack of phenotype in one environment therefore does not prove that a sigma factor is biologically irrelevant.

A distinctive family is represented by σ⁵⁴, encoded by rpoN in E. coli. Its promoter recognition is commonly associated with elements near −24 and −12 rather than the canonical −35 and −10 arrangement of the σ⁷⁰ family. The functional difference extends beyond sequence preference. The σ⁵⁴ holoenzyme ordinarily forms a stable closed promoter complex that requires remodeling by a specialized activator using ATP hydrolysis to achieve the open state. This is a standard exception to the general description of bacterial promoter opening as occurring through the binding and conformational behavior of the σ⁷⁰ holoenzyme without an obligatory separate ATP-driven initiation motor [6].

It is important to distinguish ATP as an initiating nucleotide from ATP as the fuel for an activator. ATP can serve as one of the four ribonucleoside triphosphates used for polymerization, in which case its alpha phosphate becomes part of RNA during an addition reaction or the initiating ATP retains its original triphosphate at the RNA's 5′ end. An ATPase activator, by contrast, hydrolyzes ATP in a separate reaction to support conformational remodeling. The latter can be required even when the RNA sequence does not begin with adenosine. These two roles concern different chemical events and should not be conflated simply because ATP appears in both.

Imagine an assay with an intact σ⁵⁴ holoenzyme and a suitable promoter. A mutation in the activator's ATP-hydrolysis machinery leaves binding of the holoenzyme to DNA largely intact but prevents efficient formation of the melted initiation complex. In a separate extension assay, a preassembled catalytic core may still extend a short RNA normally. This combination of observations identifies the problem as initiation-specific remodeling rather than general RNA synthesis chemistry. A mutation in the active center of core polymerase would be expected to compromise extension itself.

The comparison with σ⁷⁰ also helps clarify what an activator does in other bacterial systems. A transcription activator may improve recruitment, stabilize an intermediate or increase the rate of opening without necessarily using ATP hydrolysis directly. It is inaccurate to infer that because one family uses an ATP-driven remodeling mechanism, every bacterial activator must work the same way. Likewise, the absence of a separate ATPase requirement in a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter does not make initiation energetically costless: association and DNA opening involve changes in free energy, and subsequent polymerization uses energetic nucleotide substrates.

The term alternative sigma factor should not imply a second type of catalytic RNA polymerase. Changing sigma changes the initiation specificity module while retaining the core catalytic architecture. This organization differs from the division of labor among the major nuclear RNA polymerases in eukaryotes, which are distinct multisubunit enzymes. The comparison is useful for preventing a common conceptual transfer error between bacterial and eukaryotic transcription.

## 10. From promoter search to the closed initiation complex

Before any DNA strand can be copied, holoenzyme must encounter a promoter. Molecular motion in the cytoplasm is influenced by diffusion, interactions with DNA, the presence of other macromolecules and the physical organization of the chromosome. RNA polymerase can associate transiently with nonpromoter DNA, and these interactions need not lead to transcription. Encounter with an appropriate sequence allows additional contacts to form and can increase the probability that the complex persists long enough to proceed toward initiation.

It is tempting to imagine RNA polymerase systematically reading every base in the chromosome until it finds the exact consensus sequence. This picture is too mechanical. Proteins recognize DNA through physical and chemical interactions with accessible molecular surfaces, and promoter search involves stochastic encounters rather than an obligatory sequential inspection of all bases. Different search pathways and DNA contexts can influence encounter efficiency. At the level needed here, the central distinction is between nonspecific interactions and formation of a promoter-specific recognition complex. Neither requires the production of an RNA molecule at the moment of initial contact.

The first conventional state in the simplified initiation pathway is the closed complex, often denoted RPc, where R represents polymerase holoenzyme and P represents promoter DNA. The designation closed refers primarily to the base-paired state of the promoter DNA in the starting region, not to the absence of any interaction between the polymerase and the DNA. The holoenzyme has located and associated with the promoter, but the template around the transcription start site is not yet exposed in the arrangement necessary for initiation.

This distinction allows a binding assay to be interpreted without making an unwarranted claim about RNA synthesis. If purified holoenzyme causes a promoter DNA fragment to migrate more slowly during electrophoresis, it may have formed a complex. Such an observation can reveal binding under appropriate controls. It does not, however, directly establish that a transcription bubble has formed or that the first nucleotide has been incorporated. Additional tests are needed to distinguish a closed complex, an open complex and a complex that has already synthesized a short RNA.

The initial recognition event is generally reversible. If the complex dissociates before the next transition, the promoter becomes available for another encounter. Binding therefore competes with the conformational changes that commit the enzyme to subsequent steps. A promoter mutation that increases the rate of dissociation can reduce the probability of opening even if the rate at which polymerase first arrives is unaffected. Conversely, a change that slows opening can reduce productive initiation even when the binding equilibrium appears favorable.

A simple two-state equilibrium provides a starting quantitative description. Let free holoenzyme concentration be R and let promoter occupancy by closed complexes be θ. For binding of one holoenzyme to one independent promoter site, an equilibrium dissociation constant Kd is defined by the product of free concentrations divided by the bound-complex concentration. Under the assumption that free holoenzyme is effectively fixed, the fractional occupancy is θ = R/(Kd + R). Both R and Kd must use the same concentration units. This relationship follows from dividing the bound promoter amount by the sum of bound and unbound promoter amounts.

For a hypothetical promoter with Kd equal to 20 nM and free holoenzyme concentration equal to 20 nM, θ equals 0.5. Increasing the enzyme concentration to 80 nM raises θ to 80/100, or 0.8. A mutant with Kd equal to 80 nM at the original 20 nM enzyme concentration has θ equal to 0.2. These values predict the fraction of sites in a bound state under the simplified equilibrium assumptions. They do not directly predict the fraction of sites producing full transcripts, because the rates of opening and productive escape have not been specified.

This is a useful place to compare affinity with transcriptional flux. High affinity means that the bound state is favored relative to the unbound state at equilibrium. High flux means that many productive transcription events occur per unit time. An enzyme that binds tightly but progresses slowly may occupy a promoter for a long time without producing many RNAs. Another enzyme may spend less time in the bound state but move efficiently through initiation. The distinction between occupancy and flux appears throughout biochemistry and is central to interpreting promoter strength.

The closed complex is best regarded as an ensemble of possible molecular arrangements rather than an inflexible statue. Some complexes contain downstream DNA that has begun to enter the active-site cleft, while others may still be reorganizing interactions. Detailed studies of bacterial initiation support multiple intermediates rather than a universal single closed-to-open elementary event [7]. The two-state representation remains useful at examination level when it is explicitly recognized as a simplified description of a pathway that can contain additional steps.
