# Regulatory checkpoints and response dynamics

## A transcriptional change is not synonymous with altered steady RNA

The flow from DNA to functional protein contains several measurable pools. In a nucleated eukaryotic cell, transcription produces a precursor; processing and export contribute to the availability of mature messenger RNA; translation generates a polypeptide; protein maturation, localisation and degradation influence the final functional pool. The distinction among these stages is important even though they may be physically coupled. Processing can take place while RNA is being transcribed. In bacteria, translation often begins before transcription has finished. A particular molecular perturbation can therefore have effects at more than one stage.

Consider a hypothetical gene for which transcription initiation remains unchanged, but a greater fraction of initiated transcripts terminates before reaching the normal end. An assay detecting a short region near the transcription start site may record little change, while an assay requiring a full-length transcript records a decline. Both observations can be consistent with altered transcriptional completion. If the question refers to transcriptional output, it must specify whether it measures initiation, nascent RNA over a region or completed transcripts.

Changes in RNA processing can produce an analogous distinction without reducing transcriptional initiation. A precursor may be made at the usual rate but yield a smaller number of mature translatable molecules. Alternative splicing can change the ratio of two transcript forms even while total RNA from a shared region remains unchanged. A transcript retained in the nucleus may be present in a whole-cell RNA measurement but relatively unavailable to cytoplasmic ribosomes. Thus measuring total RNA is not always equivalent to measuring the available template pool.

## Solving the RNA balance

Write M for mean RNA molecules per cell and s for molecules produced per cell per minute. If removal is first order with rate constant δ in min⁻¹, the loss flux is δM. Both production and loss are in the same units, and the balance is dM/dt = s−δM. The symbol δ is a parameter and should not be confused with the derivative. At steady state the two fluxes are equal, giving Mss=s/δ.

If RNA is produced at 18 molecules per minute and δ=0.30 min⁻¹, the mean steady abundance is 60 molecules. If production falls to 9 per minute while δ also falls to 0.15 min⁻¹, the steady abundance remains 60. In the second condition the RNA lifetime is longer and the replacement flux is lower. A comparison of RNA abundance alone would miss both changes. A measurement of newly made RNA and a measurement of decay could reveal them.

If transcription is stopped and the loss rate remains constant, dM/dt=−δM. Separating variables and integrating gives ln(M/M₀)=−δt and M=M₀ exp(−δt). The half-life is ln(2)/δ. For δ=0.30 min⁻¹ it is 2.31 minutes; for δ=0.15 min⁻¹ it is 4.62 minutes. After two half-lives one quarter of the initial pool remains. This prediction requires an approximately first-order loss process and no ongoing synthesis during the interval.

A step increase in production has a different initial condition. Suppose the initial steady abundance is 20, and the new steady abundance is 100 with δ=0.20 min⁻¹. The difference from the new steady state obeys first-order decay, so M(t)=100−80 exp(−0.20t). After one half-life, 3.47 minutes, the predicted abundance is 60. After two half-lives, it is 80. A sample collected at the first time point shows a threefold increase rather than the final fivefold increase. The apparent magnitude of induction therefore depends on when the sample is collected.

A slower removal process raises the steady abundance at fixed production, but it also slows the approach to a new steady state in this model. This tradeoff matters biologically. Long-lived transcripts can accumulate and persist, while short-lived transcripts can follow changes in production more quickly. A stable transcript is not necessarily desirable or undesirable; its usefulness depends on the required timing and persistence of the response.

## Protein production and the delay between readouts

Let P be the mean amount of protein and k the rate of production per available RNA per minute. With first-order protein loss constant γ, dP/dt=kM−γP. At steady state Pss=ks/(δγ). Suppose s=10 RNA molecules/min, δ=0.20 min⁻¹, k=3 proteins per RNA per minute and γ=0.05 min⁻¹. Then Mss=50, total protein production is 150 per minute, and Pss=3000. Halving k while retaining the other parameters gives 1500. Doubling γ instead gives the same abundance while retaining the original synthesis flux. A new-protein production measurement separates these alternatives.

If transcription increases rapidly but the protein is long-lived, the protein level may approach its new value slowly. Conversely a rapidly removed protein may respond more quickly to altered RNA availability. The dynamics are coupled: the protein synthesis input kM changes as the RNA pool changes. Treating the protein as an independent single exponential is exact only under additional assumptions, such as an instantaneous change to a fixed RNA pool. It is useful to distinguish an illustrative limiting model from an actual measured time course.

A protein can also change activity independently of its abundance. If total abundance is 1000 molecules but only a quarter are active, the active pool is 250. Increasing the active fraction to three quarters raises this to 750 without changing abundance or synthesis. If an assay measures enzyme activity only, this threefold change can look like increased gene expression. A protein amount measurement and a functional-state measurement are required to distinguish the cases. The regulatory chain may include both gene expression and regulation of pre-existing molecules.

The equations have limits. They assume a defined pool, constant parameters over the stated interval and approximately first-order removal. Growth and cell division can add effective dilution, and changes in cell size can alter a concentration even if the molecule count does not change. Multiple compartments or RNA states can produce non-single-exponential behaviour. A simple model is useful because its assumptions and predictions are explicit; deviations reveal where additional biological details may be needed.
