# Antimicrobial Action, Susceptibility and Resistance Fundamentals

## 1. Why microbial growth can be interrupted without destroying every living cell

A bacterium growing in a suitable environment continually solves several biological problems. It must obtain nutrients, transform them into useful chemical energy, maintain a boundary between itself and its surroundings, copy genetic information, manufacture proteins and ultimately produce a new cell. These problems are connected. A bacterium cannot copy all its DNA if it cannot obtain nucleotide precursors; it cannot maintain its membrane if it cannot synthesize the enzymes responsible for lipid metabolism. A successful division is therefore the visible outcome of many molecular processes that are largely invisible to an observer looking at a culture.

The preceding chapters described how to count microorganisms, explain a population growth curve and understand the cellular changes that accompany environmental stress. In this chapter we introduce a different kind of perturbation. Instead of changing temperature or removing nutrients, suppose a chemical agent interferes with one of the cell's necessary functions. The population may stop expanding even though nutrients and other physical conditions remain favorable. It may also lose the capacity to produce descendants. Understanding why these two results are not necessarily identical is the foundation of antimicrobial physiology.

An antimicrobial agent is a substance that interferes with the growth or survival of a microorganism. The term covers agents directed against different biological groups, including bacteria, fungi and viruses. In this chapter most examples concern antibacterial action because the bacterial envelope, ribosome and metabolism provide especially instructive contrasts with the structures and functions of eukaryotic cells. An antibacterial agent is not expected to inhibit all other groups of microorganisms merely because they are small or infectious. A virus, for example, has no autonomous bacterial ribosome, and an agent directed at bacterial ribosomal translation has no corresponding viral ribosome to bind.

The term antibiotic has a historical connection with substances produced by living organisms that inhibit other organisms. Modern usage also includes many chemically modified and fully synthetic antibacterial drugs; the distinction between natural and synthetic origin does not determine whether a substance acts on a particular target. Antimicrobial activity is a property of the relationship between a substance and a biological system, not a consequence of the substance having been discovered in nature. Disinfectants and antiseptics are also antimicrobial in a broader sense but are not interchangeable with selectively acting drugs. Their appropriate use and safety differ; our subject is the biological principle of inhibition and not the application of chemicals to a patient or environment.

A useful distinction is between a molecular target and a cellular outcome. The target is the molecule, structure or reaction with which an agent interacts in a biologically important manner. The outcome is what eventually happens to the cell or population. These are different levels of explanation. If a compound inhibits a bacterial enzyme that helps produce a wall precursor, the immediate result may be a fall in precursor supply. The next result is defective construction of new wall material. The final population result may be growth arrest or loss of viability, depending on the state of the cells and the nature of the defect. Simply naming the target does not yet explain the outcome.

Selective toxicity means that an agent affects a microorganism more strongly than the host at relevant exposure. The ideal target is essential to the microorganism, sufficiently different from host structures and reachable by the agent. Essentiality explains why interfering with the target matters. Selectivity explains why the agent can distinguish the two biological systems. Accessibility explains why a compound that works on an isolated protein may fail to affect that protein inside an intact microorganism. All three questions must be answered independently.

Peptidoglycan is a particularly clear example. Bacteria typically use this polymer to resist internal osmotic pressure and preserve cell shape. Animal cells do not manufacture peptidoglycan. Interfering with a bacterial-specific wall reaction therefore does not directly inhibit a corresponding reaction in an animal cell. This does not imply that a wall-active agent has no adverse effects on animals: other molecular interactions, immune responses and effects on beneficial microorganisms can still matter. Selectivity is a relative biological property, not a guarantee of harmlessness.

Other targets are less obviously unique. Bacteria and eukaryotes both synthesize proteins, but their cytosolic ribosomes differ in composition and architecture. A chemical can bind preferentially to a bacterial ribosome even though the broad task of translation is shared by both cells. This is selectivity based on molecular differences rather than the complete absence of a process in the host. Mitochondrial ribosomes have evolutionary links to bacterial ribosomes, so some agents that affect bacterial translation can also affect mitochondrial functions. The existence of a preferred microbial target does not establish absolute exclusivity.

One should also distinguish cellular susceptibility from a decision about clinical use. Whether an organism is inhibited in a defined laboratory observation does not, by itself, establish how a drug will behave in a whole organism. The chemical must reach its target at an appropriate exposure, and the host, tissue environment and microbial physiological state all influence the outcome. We will discuss this distinction conceptually without making therapeutic recommendations.

The word resistance is sometimes used loosely to describe anything that survives an antimicrobial exposure. This is scientifically inadequate. A population that continues growing at a concentration that stops a reference population behaves differently from a population that stops growing but remains alive for longer. Both could yield survivors, but they differ in whether the capacity for proliferation has changed or the rate of killing has changed. The distinction becomes especially important when interpreting experiments involving stationary-phase cells, which often behave differently from rapidly dividing cells.

By the end of the chapter, a student should be able to trace each major target from normal function through molecular interference to the observed response; distinguish growth inhibition from killing; interpret a minimum inhibitory concentration, a minimum bactericidal concentration and a zone diameter; and identify which claims about resistance follow from such evidence and which require additional information. These learning goals connect earlier physiology to the subsequent chapter on the molecular origins and population spread of resistance.

## 2. Discovery, evidence and the meaning of selective action

The discovery of an antimicrobial effect begins with a difference between two conditions. If microorganisms grow in an untreated system but not in an otherwise comparable system containing a substance, the substance may have interfered with growth. That simple observation is important, but it leaves several questions unanswered. Has the population died, or has its growth been delayed? Is the chemical the direct cause, or has the surrounding medium changed? Does the chemical affect all microorganisms or a particular subset? Does it interfere with a specific cellular component or damage many structures at once?

Alexander Fleming's 1929 account of the antibacterial action of a Penicillium culture is a useful historical illustration. The observation that bacterial growth was affected in the vicinity of a mold suggested an inhibitory activity associated with that mold. The work described differences in sensitivity among bacteria and the inhibitory activity of culture-derived material. This was evidence of differential antibacterial action, not an identification of the final molecular mechanism. The subsequent understanding of penicillin as an inhibitor of bacterial cell wall construction required additional biochemical and physiological evidence. The paper and its historical context are best understood as a starting point in the development of a mechanistic explanation, rather than a single observation that established everything known about beta-lactams today.

The visible region around an inhibitory source also illustrates a basic physical principle. An agent distributed through a medium can create a spatial concentration gradient. Regions receiving different exposures can support different degrees of microbial growth. Thus, a pattern observed on a surface depends on both biological sensitivity and the distribution of the chemical. Two agents with identical effects at their respective target sites can produce different visual patterns if their movement through the medium differs. This observation prepares us for the later discussion of disk diffusion.

Scientific reasoning must separate a plausible interpretation from proof. If growth stops soon after an agent is introduced, it is tempting to say that every cell has died. Yet growth is measured by increase in population size or biomass, whereas viability is the capacity for continued biological activity and reproductive recovery under appropriate conditions. One measurement cannot automatically stand in for the other. If population mass remains constant, cells could be intact but not dividing, or some might be dying while others continue to divide. If the culture becomes less turbid, cells may have lysed, but other changes in scattering can also affect the observation.

An untreated comparison establishes whether the biological system was capable of growth in the absence of the test agent. An observation without microorganisms helps establish whether a visual signal actually depends on biological growth rather than properties of the medium or agent. These general control principles apply far beyond microbiology. A meaningful comparison also requires that the biological state and observation conditions are comparable; otherwise a difference attributed to a drug might instead reflect nutrient limitation, differences in starting population or different observation intervals. These are conceptual principles of interpretation rather than instructions for conducting a susceptibility assay.

A molecular mechanism is supported more strongly when several independent observations agree. For instance, a decrease in protein synthesis is consistent with interference in translation, but a cell deprived of energy may also show a decrease in protein synthesis without its ribosome being the primary target. If a substance is observed to interact with a ribosomal component and to impair a defined translation reaction, that evidence strengthens a ribosomal interpretation. An isolated biochemical effect alone is also not sufficient to establish what happens in intact cells; uptake, cellular distribution and competing effects may intervene.

A useful way to organize evidence is to distinguish physical, biochemical, cellular and population levels. The physical level asks whether the chemical reaches the relevant compartment. The biochemical level asks which molecular interaction changes. The cellular level asks which physiological function becomes impaired. The population level asks whether growth, viability or both change. An examination question may supply evidence at only one level and ask which conclusion is justified. Do not silently supply the missing levels as if they had also been measured.

Consider an explicitly hypothetical comparison. An untreated population increases in turbidity over time; a second population exposed to an agent shows no increase. These observations establish a difference in the measured optical signal. If independent information establishes that the signal tracks biomass, the second population has failed to accumulate comparable biomass. Nothing yet proves that it has lost viability. A separate observation of the capacity to form descendants would be needed to distinguish a stable arrested population from a population experiencing extensive killing. Even such a measurement describes reproductive recovery under the conditions of observation, not every conceivable definition of cellular life.

The historical development of antimicrobial science therefore led to a progression from observation of inhibition to the identification of molecular targets and the quantitative comparison of microbial responses. The same progression forms a useful approach to NET questions. Begin by describing the observation in precise language, identify the smallest conclusion it supports, and only then connect that conclusion to a plausible mechanism. Where more than one mechanism is consistent with the data, preserve that uncertainty.

## 3. The bacterial envelope as a selectively vulnerable structure

A bacterial cell must keep its cytoplasm physically distinct from the environment while still exchanging nutrients and waste products. The plasma membrane forms the immediate boundary of the cytoplasm. Its lipid bilayer limits the unrestricted passage of many dissolved substances and provides a platform for transport proteins, respiratory components and other functions. Outside the membrane, most bacteria possess a wall containing peptidoglycan. The membrane and wall are not the same structure: the membrane is primarily a selective barrier and an energy-transducing surface, whereas peptidoglycan is a mechanically supportive network.

Peptidoglycan consists of long carbohydrate chains made of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid residues. Short peptide stems attached to the muramic acid residues permit links between adjacent chains. This arrangement converts many individual molecular strands into a continuous mesh. The mesh distributes mechanical stress and reduces the likelihood that the membrane will expand uncontrollably under the difference in osmotic pressure between the cytoplasm and surrounding fluid. The wall is porous to many small molecules; it is not a substitute for the selective permeability of the membrane.

Growth requires that this strong network remain capable of remodeling. If a rod-shaped bacterium were encased in a rigid, permanently closed shell, the shell would prevent elongation and division. Normal growth therefore involves insertion of new wall material and controlled modification of existing links. The synthesis of a new septum during division is especially dependent on this coordination. Wall construction is a dynamic physiological process, not simply the presence of a static polymer at the surface.

At a broad level the building of peptidoglycan requires the formation of precursor units, their delivery to the site of wall construction and the assembly and cross-linking of the polymer. It is important to distinguish joining carbohydrate units along a strand from cross-linking peptide stems between strands. These two operations produce different kinds of bonds and contribute differently to the mechanical structure. Transglycosylation contributes to glycan-chain extension; transpeptidation creates peptide cross-links. An agent that specifically interferes with cross-link formation does not necessarily abolish every earlier step of precursor manufacture.

Penicillin-binding proteins include enzymes involved in peptidoglycan assembly and remodeling. Beta-lactam antibiotics interact with particular penicillin-binding proteins, including important transpeptidases. The beta-lactam ring participates in formation of a covalent acyl-enzyme complex with the active-site serine of susceptible enzymes, inhibiting their normal catalytic activity. The significance of the binding is not merely that a drug occupies a protein. It prevents completion of a wall-building reaction that is ordinarily necessary for production of mechanically effective new material.

During normal growth, the cell's wall synthesis and remodeling activities are balanced. If cross-link formation is impaired while other changes continue, newly produced wall can be defective, and the normal balance of construction and remodeling is disrupted. Mechanical integrity may deteriorate. Under conditions where internal osmotic pressure stresses the envelope, severely damaged cells can rupture. However, not every bacterium exposed to a wall-active compound lyses immediately; growth state, specific targets, stress responses and other processes influence the resulting morphology and viability. Loss of viable cells need not be accompanied by an equally rapid decrease in turbidity.

The difference between an expanding wall and an already existing wall explains an important relationship with growth physiology. An actively elongating or dividing cell depends on continuing wall construction. A cell that has entered a state of very slow growth may make less new wall material. Interference with wall construction may therefore have a different cellular outcome in these two physiological states even if the molecule that binds the drug has not changed its inherited structure. This connection is important when distinguishing resistance from tolerance. A difference in killing rate associated with a physiological state is not automatically evidence of a newly inherited target alteration.

Other wall-active agents interfere with different steps. A glycopeptide such as vancomycin binds to the terminal D-alanyl-D-alanine motif of susceptible peptidoglycan precursors, interfering with their utilization in wall assembly. This is different from directly acylating a transpeptidase. Both kinds of agents can interfere with production of a functional peptidoglycan network, but the immediate molecular interactions differ. A statement that two drugs inhibit the wall therefore does not establish that their molecular targets, penetration properties or resistance patterns are identical.

Envelope organization also determines whether a molecule can reach its target. Typical Gram-positive bacteria possess a substantial peptidoglycan layer outside a single cytoplasmic membrane. Typical Gram-negative bacteria have a thinner peptidoglycan layer within a periplasmic region between the cytoplasmic membrane and an outer membrane. The outer membrane is an additional permeability barrier. It is possible for a compound to interact with a wall component in principle yet fail to reach that component effectively in an intact cell. Vancomycin's limited activity against typical intact Gram-negative bacteria illustrates this distinction between target presence and access.

It is incorrect to infer from the Gram stain alone that every member of a broad group has exactly the same susceptibility. The Gram-positive and Gram-negative envelope models describe common structural arrangements, not universal guarantees about all organisms or agents. Some bacteria have unusual envelopes, and susceptibility can vary among strains. The relevant reasoning is that structural barriers influence the availability of a compound at its molecular site of action. They are one part of the explanation, not an independent substitute for evidence about susceptibility.

Wall-less bacteria illustrate a different principle. Mycoplasmas lack a peptidoglycan wall. A compound whose relevant action consists solely of blocking peptidoglycan assembly lacks that target in these organisms. Their insensitivity to such agents is related to their normal biological organization, rather than a necessary recent acquisition of resistance. This reasoning differs from the Gram-negative example, in which peptidoglycan can be present but the accessibility of certain compounds is restricted. In one case the necessary molecular system is absent; in the other it is present but a barrier alters exposure.

An exam question may contrast a bacterium with a damaged wall and a bacterium with a damaged membrane. A defective wall can allow the membrane to experience greater mechanical stress, whereas a defective membrane can directly disrupt gradients and solute retention even if the wall remains intact. The distinction is causal: the wall normally distributes mechanical force, while the membrane normally maintains chemical differences. An observed terminal rupture does not identify which structure was initially affected.

This chapter therefore treats the envelope as a system in which two necessary but distinct structures cooperate. Once the wall reaction has been understood, we can consider what happens when the membrane itself is the primary site of antimicrobial action.


## 4. Membrane-active antimicrobials and the loss of cellular homeostasis

The membrane is not merely a thin cover surrounding the cell. It allows an organism to preserve a chemically distinct interior while obtaining substances from the exterior. This ability is essential because many reactions of metabolism require particular ionic concentrations, pH ranges, cofactor concentrations and electrical conditions. An enzyme in the cytoplasm cannot operate normally simply because its amino acid sequence is correct; the local chemical environment must also support its folded structure and catalytic function. A membrane that ceases to maintain such an environment can impair many reactions simultaneously.

The lipid bilayer is built from amphipathic molecules, meaning that they have hydrophilic regions and hydrophobic regions. The hydrophobic interior provides a substantial barrier to the free movement of many ions and polar substances. Membrane proteins allow selected solutes to cross this barrier through channels, carriers or pumps. Cells expend energy to establish gradients that are useful for transport and homeostasis. A difference in electrical potential and a difference in proton concentration across the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane together constitute the proton motive force. This electrochemical gradient participates in several important physiological functions, including the synthesis of ATP during respiration and the operation of some transport systems.

When an antimicrobial compound damages the membrane, a consequence can be the uncontrolled movement of solutes across the boundary. If the cell can no longer maintain its ionic composition, molecules that depend on a favorable electrochemical gradient may cease to operate as before. ATP production by gradient-dependent processes can be impaired, pH regulation can become more difficult, and energy-consuming attempts to restore homeostasis can compete with other cellular requirements. Thus, disruption of a membrane can produce widespread secondary effects even though the initial interaction occurs at a limited physical surface.

Not every membrane-active agent causes the same kind of membrane injury. Some interfere with the organization of lipids or interactions between lipids and other envelope components. Some can increase permeability in a more selective way, and others produce defects that permit greater movement of several solutes. The important distinction is between the physical perturbation, such as altered membrane integrity or electrical behavior, and the downstream metabolic consequences of that perturbation. The appearance of an energy deficit does not necessarily mean that an enzyme involved in ATP synthesis was the direct target.

Polymyxins are examples of antibacterial agents whose activity involves interactions with the Gram-negative envelope. Lipopolysaccharide, an important constituent of the outer leaflet of the typical Gram-negative outer membrane, contains lipid A and charged groups that contribute to the organization of that membrane. The interactions of polymyxins with bacterial envelope components can disrupt membrane integrity. For examination purposes, the useful comparison is between an agent whose primary effect is on membrane organization and a beta-lactam whose important immediate interaction is with wall-building enzymes. A detailed molecular model of polymyxin activity is not necessary for this comparison, and the simplistic claim that it merely makes identical holes in every bacterial membrane should be avoided.

Membranes also provide selective targets in organisms other than bacteria. Fungal membranes contain ergosterol as an important sterol, whereas mammalian membranes predominantly contain cholesterol. Some antifungal compounds interact with ergosterol-containing membranes; others interfere with ergosterol synthesis. The distinction is analogous to the difference between damaging an already assembled membrane and preventing production of a constituent needed for its normal function. Fungi and animals both possess eukaryotic membranes, so selectivity depends on biochemical differences rather than the complete absence of membranes from the host. This example is introduced only to clarify selective toxicity, not to provide a catalogue of antifungal treatments.

The membrane is also an example of a cellular target for which visible morphology can be misleading. A cell can lose its capacity to maintain an electrochemical gradient without undergoing obvious rupture. Conversely, a ruptured cell may be the result of a primary wall defect rather than an initial attack on the membrane. A microscope image of disrupted material therefore describes a terminal physical state, but does not necessarily reveal the original molecular event.

Consider a hypothetical set of observations in which a population exposed to an agent shows a rapid change in membrane potential followed by a decrease in nutrient uptake. The sequence is consistent with interference in membrane-associated homeostasis. Nevertheless, correlation and temporal ordering alone do not prove that a specific lipid was bound by the compound. The change in potential might also result from an earlier effect on energy metabolism. The correct conclusion depends on the full evidence supplied. If an examination question asks for the most directly affected function given the loss of electrochemical gradients, transport and energy conservation are reasonable answers; if it asks for the exact bound molecule, additional biochemical evidence would be necessary.

A useful connection with the stress-response chapters is that many environmental challenges also affect membranes. Sudden osmotic stress changes the pressure exerted on the cell envelope, temperature changes membrane fluidity, and acid stress challenges proton homeostasis. An antimicrobial that disrupts membrane function intersects with these normal physiological responsibilities. However, similarity of downstream stress does not make every environmental stressor equivalent to an antimicrobial drug. One must identify the particular interaction and then explain how a previously stable cellular state becomes disturbed.

This completes the physical boundary targets. We now turn to an essential molecular machine inside the cell: the ribosome. The same logic applies there, but the relevant injury is initially a failure to construct new proteins rather than a failure to preserve the cell's envelope.

## 5. Protein synthesis as a target: the bacterial ribosome and selective interference

A cell contains proteins that catalyze reactions, bind DNA, transport nutrients, build structures, sense environmental changes and regulate other molecules. Proteins cannot be maintained indefinitely without replacement, and an actively growing cell must make a large amount of additional protein to provide two daughter cells with the required machinery. Protein synthesis is thus connected to both cellular maintenance and population expansion. An agent that interferes with this process can stop the increase in biomass even if DNA, membrane lipids and many pre-existing proteins remain physically present.

Translation is the process through which the nucleotide sequence of a messenger RNA is read to produce a polypeptide with a defined amino acid sequence. A ribosome brings together messenger RNA, transfer RNAs carrying amino acids and the chemical machinery needed to form peptide bonds. The ribosome must recognize the appropriate transfer RNA, support addition of its amino acid to the growing chain and move along the messenger RNA in a coordinated manner. Translation is therefore not a single chemical step. Different agents can interrupt different events within this common process.

Typical bacterial ribosomes have a sedimentation coefficient of 70S and are composed of a small 30S subunit and a large 50S subunit. A sedimentation coefficient describes sedimentation behavior rather than simple molecular mass, which is why the two numerical values do not add arithmetically to 70. The small subunit is particularly important in reading the messenger RNA and checking correspondence between codon and anticodon. The large subunit contains the peptidyl transferase center, where the chemical reaction joining amino acids occurs. Ribosomal RNA contributes fundamentally to these activities; the ribosome should not be described as though it were only a protein enzyme.

The bacterial and eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes perform related functions but differ in important structural features. Many antibacterial translation inhibitors exploit differences in the shape, chemical environment and accessibility of bacterial ribosomal binding sites. The distinction between a bacterial 70S ribosome and a typical eukaryotic cytosolic 80S ribosome is useful, but knowing only these sedimentation values is not enough to predict the response to every translation inhibitor. The detailed interaction depends on the local structure recognized by the agent. Organellar ribosomes and evolutionary relationships also make it incorrect to claim that all eukaryotic protein synthesis is completely unaffected by every antibacterial translation inhibitor.

Tetracyclines provide a standard example of interference with the bacterial small subunit. They hinder productive accommodation of aminoacyl transfer RNA at the ribosomal A site. In the normal elongation cycle, the A site receives an aminoacyl transfer RNA carrying the next amino acid specified by the messenger RNA. If productive entry of that transfer RNA is obstructed, addition of the next residue is impaired. The ribosome cannot continue the normal sequence of elongation reactions, and the cell's ability to produce proteins decreases. The relevant idea is not that tetracyclines destroy messenger RNA, but that they interfere with the ribosome's use of it.

Aminoglycosides also interact with the bacterial small subunit, but their characteristic effects differ from those of tetracyclines. Interference with decoding fidelity can permit inappropriate interpretation of codons, and effects on other stages of translation may also contribute. Incorrect decoding is especially important as a biological concept: reducing the accuracy of protein production is not identical to simply stopping production. A translated polypeptide with incorrect residues can fail to fold or function properly. Abnormal proteins may contribute to cellular injury. Consequently, two agents that interact with the same broad ribosomal subunit need not produce the same effect on viability.

Chloramphenicol is commonly taught as an inhibitor of peptide bond formation associated with the bacterial large subunit. The large subunit normally positions substrates so that the growing polypeptide can be transferred to an incoming amino acid. Inhibiting this catalytic region impairs elongation even though messenger RNA and aminoacyl transfer RNAs may still exist. In reality, details of inhibition can depend on the translating substrate and sequence context, but the key examination distinction is the interference with the peptidyl transferase center rather than the inhibition of DNA replication or destruction of the cell wall.

Macrolides are another important class associated with the large ribosomal subunit. Their binding near the nascent peptide exit tunnel can impede productive translation in a context-dependent manner. The peptide exit tunnel is the path through which the newly synthesized portion of a polypeptide leaves the interior of the large subunit. Translation requires coordinated movement and continued addition of residues, so interference in this region can impair elongation. It is not necessary to memorize a list of every structural variant of macrolides to understand why a chemical interaction at the tunnel can affect the synthesis of proteins.

These examples illustrate a hierarchy of target descriptions. At the broadest level, all four classes interfere with bacterial protein synthesis. At a narrower level, some act in association with the 30S subunit and others with the 50S subunit. At the most explanatory level for our purposes, one distinguishes interference with incoming transfer RNA, decoding accuracy, peptide bond formation and elongation through the exit tunnel region. A question asking only for the broad target can be answered at the first level, while a question comparing two agents associated with the same subunit requires a more precise mechanism.

An immediate reduction in protein synthesis affects an actively growing population because the rate of production of new cellular components falls. The extent of the effect on survival depends on additional considerations. Previously synthesized proteins can continue to function for some time, although they may gradually be damaged or diluted during attempted growth. Essential proteins that turn over rapidly cannot be indefinitely replaced when translation is impaired. An agent that produces extensive abnormal protein synthesis can have effects that differ from an agent causing mainly an interruption of normal elongation. It is therefore unsurprising that translation inhibitors are not uniformly bacteriostatic or uniformly bactericidal.

The labels bacteriostatic and bactericidal will be examined formally later. For now, remember that they describe a measurable population response and not a complete description of a molecular mechanism. A broad textbook association between a class and growth inhibition is useful but conditional. It does not justify an absolute statement that a given agent can never cause a loss of viability or that all microorganisms respond identically. This is particularly important in questions containing the words always, exclusively or necessarily.

Evidence of a decrease in protein synthesis should also be interpreted at the appropriate level. If all major biosynthetic processes slow following loss of cellular energy, reduced protein synthesis is an expected secondary consequence. It would be incorrect to identify a ribosomal target from that observation alone. Stronger evidence might establish interference with a defined ribosomal reaction, together with a consistent cellular effect. Conversely, an interaction with an isolated ribosome does not ensure activity in an intact cell because the compound must reach the ribosome. The same distinction between biochemical target and whole-cell susceptibility that arose for the wall is therefore relevant to translation.

A final connection links protein synthesis with the next topic. A cell cannot continue translation without a supply of appropriate messenger RNAs, transfer RNAs and amino acids. In addition, the genetic information underlying those RNAs must remain available. It is possible to reduce protein synthesis indirectly by impairing transcription or DNA function. Understanding the difference between those information-processing steps helps prevent confusing a nucleic-acid target with a ribosomal target.

## 6. DNA and RNA synthesis: disrupting the flow of genetic information

The information required to construct many components of a bacterium is encoded in its DNA. DNA replication makes a copy of this information before cell division, while transcription converts particular DNA sequences into RNA molecules. Translation then uses messenger RNA to specify the sequence of a protein. These processes are related but not interchangeable. A bacterial cell that fails to replicate its chromosome may be unable to produce normal daughter cells even if it continues to transcribe genes temporarily. A cell that fails to make RNA may retain its chromosome but gradually lose the capacity to replace short-lived transcripts and other RNA products.

DNA is not a freely unwound linear string inside a bacterial cell. Its double-helical structure and organization constrain the movement of molecular machinery. As a replication fork opens the parental double helix, the DNA ahead of the fork can become overwound unless the resulting torsional stress is managed. After replication, newly copied DNA molecules also have to be physically separated. Topoisomerases are enzymes that modify DNA topology through controlled changes in the continuity of DNA strands and subsequent restoration of those strands. They are therefore essential for coordinating chromosome duplication and organization.

Bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV are type II topoisomerases with important but partly different physiological roles. DNA gyrase can introduce negative supercoils and contributes to the management of topological stress during processes such as replication. Topoisomerase IV is particularly important in separating linked daughter DNA molecules, although its functions are not restricted to this description. Their catalytic cycles involve temporary DNA cleavage and subsequent resealing. In the normal cycle the transient break is a controlled intermediate, not a permanent injury.

Fluoroquinolones interfere with bacterial type II topoisomerases by stabilizing cleavage complexes involving the enzyme and DNA. This can impede the normal completion of the catalytic cycle. When cleavage complexes persist and interfere with DNA-processing machinery, serious DNA injury can result. The immediate interaction is consequently more informative than the broad slogan that the drug prevents DNA synthesis. One needs to distinguish a controlled enzymatic intermediate from an abnormal persistent complex and the later consequences of that persistence.

This target illustrates selectivity through differences between bacterial and host enzymes. Eukaryotic cells also use topoisomerases, because the physical problems created by DNA topology are not unique to bacteria. The existence of the same broad class of enzyme in two organisms does not require identical drug sensitivity. Differences in molecular structure and interaction can permit preferential action on bacterial enzymes. Conversely, the name of an enzyme class does not guarantee that every compound directed toward a member of that class will selectively affect bacteria.

Transcription offers a separate target. Bacterial RNA polymerase reads a DNA template and synthesizes RNA. The enzyme must interact with DNA at appropriate sites, initiate RNA synthesis and then extend the transcript. A disruption at one stage can reduce the production of new RNA without directly inhibiting DNA polymerase or ribosomal catalysis. Rifampicin is a standard example of an agent that interacts with bacterial RNA polymerase and interferes with early transcript synthesis. The biological consequence is a failure to produce normal amounts of newly initiated transcripts. Existing messenger RNAs can still persist temporarily, but many bacterial transcripts have limited lifetimes. Reduced transcription therefore affects later protein synthesis and cellular functions in a time-dependent manner.

A distinction between primary and secondary effects is essential here. Suppose measurements show that RNA production falls first, followed by protein synthesis, with no immediate evidence of a direct effect on purified ribosomes. This pattern is consistent with the reduction in available messenger RNA limiting translation. It would be misleading to conclude that the two affected biosynthetic rates necessarily represent two independent primary drug targets. The processes form a causal chain. The reverse inference also needs caution: an agent that directly stops translation may eventually affect transcription because regulatory proteins and metabolic functions depend on the synthesis of proteins.

Nucleotide supply provides another route by which nucleic acid synthesis can be affected. DNA and RNA polymers are built from nucleotide precursors; an enzyme that polymerizes nucleotides cannot sustain normal synthesis if its substrates become unavailable. An agent that interferes with metabolism of the precursors can therefore reduce the synthesis of nucleic acids indirectly. This concept prepares us for the folate pathway in the next section. It is important not to call every agent that reduces DNA synthesis a DNA polymerase inhibitor. The observed decline in synthesis can arise because the template is damaged, topology is abnormal, the enzyme is inhibited or substrates are insufficient.

When interpreting an examination question about DNA and RNA synthesis, start with the normal function of the named enzyme. DNA gyrase manages topology rather than synthesizing a new DNA strand. RNA polymerase synthesizes RNA rather than reading messenger RNA to make protein. A ribosome catalyzes translation rather than replicating DNA. Once these normal functions are kept separate, the downstream consequences of perturbing one process can be inferred without confusing the primary target with all affected cellular activities.


## 7. Antimetabolites and the dependence of biosynthesis on precursor supply

The cell's information-processing machinery depends on metabolism for a continuing supply of molecular building blocks. Even a structurally normal DNA polymerase cannot replicate DNA if the appropriate deoxyribonucleotide substrates are unavailable. Even a fully functional ribosome cannot construct a particular peptide without the required amino acids and aminoacyl transfer RNAs. An agent that interferes with a crucial metabolic pathway can therefore bring growth to a stop without binding directly to the enzymes that produce the final macromolecules.

This form of antimicrobial action is particularly informative because it demonstrates how a local metabolic disturbance propagates through a network. Consider an intermediate compound that is used to produce several different end products. The cell may continue to use its existing stock of these products for a limited time after synthesis of the intermediate is interrupted. As that stock falls, several otherwise distinct processes may become impaired. The observable phenotype can thus be delayed relative to the immediate chemical interaction. A delay does not contradict the proposed mechanism; it may reflect the time needed to exhaust usable pools of important metabolites.

Folate metabolism provides a standard example of a pathway whose interference can selectively affect bacterial growth. Reduced folate cofactors participate in transfers of one-carbon units during biosynthetic reactions. These reactions help provide building blocks required for synthesis of nucleic acids and other cellular constituents. Tetrahydrofolate and its substituted derivatives are not simply sources of general metabolic energy. They are carriers of chemically usable one-carbon units. The distinction is important because the consequence of disrupting folate metabolism is not explained merely by saying that a cell lacks ATP.

Many bacteria can synthesize folate from simpler precursors. Para-aminobenzoic acid, often abbreviated PABA, is one substrate associated with the synthesis of dihydropteroate by the enzyme dihydropteroate synthase. Sulfonamides are structural analogues of PABA and interfere with this bacterial enzyme. The normal conversion of substrates toward folate production is consequently impaired. In a teaching model of competitive inhibition, structurally related molecules can compete for interaction with an enzyme because they share features recognized by its binding site. A useful biological explanation is that the interaction does not supply the normal product of the pathway in the way that the physiological substrate does.

Dihydrofolate reductase has a different role. It participates in the conversion of dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate, thereby supporting the availability of reduced folates. Trimethoprim preferentially inhibits susceptible bacterial dihydrofolate reductase. Thus, sulfonamides and trimethoprim act at different positions in the wider folate pathway. One affects the production of folate-related precursors, while the other affects formation of the reduced cofactor pool. Both can ultimately compromise biosynthetic reactions that require reduced folates. They should not be described as two names for the same enzyme inhibitor.

An important consequence concerns thymidylate synthesis. Thymidylate synthase uses a reduced folate derivative in the formation of thymidylate from deoxyuridylate. If the appropriate reduced folate pool cannot be maintained, thymidylate supply can become inadequate for continued normal DNA synthesis. The cell's need for purine precursors can also be affected because one-carbon transfer reactions participate in purine biosynthesis. The mechanistic connection is therefore pathway interference, reduced cofactor availability, impaired precursor supply and consequently impaired macromolecular synthesis. This reasoning is more informative than memorizing that an antifolate inhibits DNA.

Selective toxicity is illustrated at two levels in this pathway. Mammalian cells obtain folate from nutritional sources and do not perform the bacterial de novo reaction involving dihydropteroate synthase. An agent directed against that bacterial enzyme lacks a corresponding host enzyme target. Mammalian cells do, however, possess dihydrofolate reductase. Selectivity of trimethoprim is therefore a matter of preferential interaction with susceptible bacterial enzyme forms rather than the universal absence of the enzyme from animals. This contrast is useful because it unites two earlier forms of selectivity: the absence of a pathway and a difference between homologous targets.

It is also important to recognize the limitations of pathway diagrams. A sequence of arrows between metabolites may suggest that each step always depends absolutely on the preceding step. Real metabolic networks can include multiple nutrient sources, alternative reactions and differences in requirements among organisms. The biological effect of interfering with one enzyme depends on whether the organism relies on the affected reaction under the conditions in question. A pathway diagram establishes the normal direction of the represented reactions, but it does not establish the sensitivity of every cell containing a related enzyme. The study of specific resistance mechanisms, including metabolic bypass, belongs to the next chapter.

An examination problem may present a decrease in DNA synthesis together with an unchanged ability of isolated DNA polymerase to catalyze polymerization when all substrates are supplied. This is compatible with several indirect causes of DNA synthesis failure, including impaired nucleotide availability. It does not by itself prove that a folate enzyme is the target. If the problem additionally supplies specific evidence of interference with folate metabolism, the connection between the observations becomes stronger. The disciplined answer should use the full evidence without inventing an unmeasured molecular interaction.

At this stage, the principal categories of antibacterial action have been established. Wall-active agents impair the construction of a supporting structure. Membrane-active agents interfere with the chemical boundary required for cellular homeostasis. Translation inhibitors impair the production or fidelity of proteins. Nucleic-acid-directed agents disrupt the copying, organization or expression of genetic information. Antimetabolites interfere with the chemical supply chains on which those functions depend. All may lead to slower growth, but a common endpoint does not make their molecular actions interchangeable.

## 8. Growth inhibition, viability and the meaning of bacteriostatic and bactericidal action

Microbial growth was previously defined in terms of increase in cell number or biomass. When growth is measured over time, a population can show a positive net rate, little net change or a decline. It is tempting to identify these three trends with three simple cellular states: division, inactivity and death. In reality, a population measurement combines the behavior of many cells. One needs a way to separate the observable net change from the processes producing that change.

Let N represent a viable population count at a particular time. In an idealized population, let b be a per-capita rate of production of additional viable cells and d be a per-capita rate of loss of viability. Over a sufficiently short time interval, the expected change in count can be represented approximately as the difference between additions and losses. In a continuous, homogeneous teaching model this leads to dN/dt = (b − d)N. Both b and d have units of inverse time; their difference is the net specific rate of change of the viable population. The equation is not a complete model of real antimicrobial action, but it exposes a useful ambiguity in interpreting a population curve.

If b is greater than d, the viable population increases. If b equals d, the population may remain approximately constant even though individual cellular events continue. If d exceeds b, the viable population declines. A constant count therefore does not prove that all cells have stopped dividing and that none is dying. Similarly, an increase in viable count does not prove that no individual cell has been lost. The observable is the balance between processes, not a complete record of each cell's fate.

For example, suppose a simplified model has b = 0.4 per hour and d = 0.1 per hour. The net rate is 0.3 per hour. Now suppose a perturbation reduces b to 0.1 per hour without changing d. The net rate becomes zero. A count that stops rising can therefore result from a reduction in production, without a new increase in the rate of death. In another hypothetical population, b = 0.4 and d = 0.4 per hour, also producing zero net change. The two populations have the same net rate but very different underlying rates of turnover. This example is mathematical and illustrative; the values are not an experimentally measured drug response.

The term bacteriostatic describes an antibacterial effect characterized primarily by inhibition of bacterial growth under the relevant conditions. The term bactericidal describes an effect involving a substantial reduction in bacterial viability under defined conditions. These labels concern the effect on the microbial population. They do not refer to whether the chemical was obtained from a living source, whether it is synthetic, whether it affects the cell wall or whether it interacts with a particular ribosomal subunit. Nor do they provide a universal ranking of the usefulness of different drugs.

A bacteriostatic effect can be understood by considering a population whose biosynthetic capacity has been restricted. Cells may no longer produce new daughter cells at the previous rate, while many retain the capacity to resume proliferation if the inhibitory conditions are removed. Under such circumstances the absence of visible population growth is compatible with continued viability. However, bacteriostatic does not mean that every cell must remain alive indefinitely, or that the agent can never cause killing in another biological context. The term is operational and depends on the response being described.

A bactericidal effect requires evidence about viable cells, not merely evidence about the absence of growth. When an agent causes severe and irreversible cellular injury, the count of cells capable of reproductive recovery can fall. Some injury may involve physical disintegration, but killing need not always be accompanied by obvious lysis. A population may contain many intact particles that no longer have the ability to produce descendants. Optical measurements can therefore fail to reflect the decline in viability accurately.

The word irreversible needs to be interpreted carefully. In microbiology, a decrease in a viable count commonly refers to loss of reproductive recovery within the conditions and limitations of the measurement. A cell with a transiently impaired ability to form descendants may be missed by a particular measurement. One must not treat every operational definition as an exhaustive description of life. At the examination level, the key distinction is between the failure to increase while inhibition is present and the loss of the capacity for continued reproductive recovery.

The same agent may display different effects depending on the organism and its physiological state. A target involved in active cell division may be less engaged when a population is not growing rapidly. A target involved in a maintenance function may remain important even in a slowly growing cell. The severity and duration of a perturbation also influence whether impaired function progresses to loss of viability. These relationships explain why statements such as all cell-wall inhibitors kill every bacterial cell immediately or all protein-synthesis inhibitors are purely static are not defensible as general rules.

It is useful to separate the time required for an effect from the magnitude of the effect at an endpoint. An agent might interrupt growth soon after it interacts with a target, whereas the loss of reproductive capacity may occur later. A measurement made early could therefore demonstrate inhibition but not yet demonstrate substantial killing. A different observation time could yield a different conclusion about viability. Comparing results requires attention to the observation interval. This is not a loophole in the definitions; it is a consequence of the definitions referring to measured biological responses.

Growth curves and survival curves are designed to answer related but different questions. A growth curve commonly displays population change during a period in which reproduction may occur. A survival curve displays the remaining viable fraction relative to an initial viable population during a defined challenge. A decrease on the latter graph represents fewer recoverable viable units than at the starting point. The graphs may use similar axes and logarithms, but the biological meaning depends on what is plotted and why.

Suppose a figure has two curves for an untreated population and a challenged population. At the end of the observation, the untreated population is one hundred times larger than its starting value, whereas the challenged population remains at its starting value. The challenged population is one hundred times smaller than the untreated population at that moment, but it has not necessarily undergone a one-hundred-fold loss relative to its own starting count. Confusing comparison with an untreated final value and comparison with the challenged population's initial value is a common error. The former describes suppression relative to the control; the latter describes net survival relative to the beginning of the challenge.

The distinction also explains why a large visible zone of growth inhibition should not be called a region of proven killing. A visual endpoint can establish that visible growth did not occur within that zone under the relevant observation conditions. It does not directly enumerate viable survivors or demonstrate their inability to recover. Such distinctions form the basis of the two concentration endpoints considered next.

## 9. Susceptibility, concentration and the minimum inhibitory concentration

When a substance acts on a microbial population, the response may depend on the amount of substance present per unit volume. Concentration is a physical quantity rather than a biological outcome. A concentration of one milligram per liter means one milligram of the substance in a liter of solution. Numerically, one milligram per liter equals one microgram per milliliter, because both the mass unit and volume unit differ by a factor of one thousand. Correct use of units is necessary when comparing measured values, but converting units does not establish any new biological information.

The same external concentration need not produce the same effect on two organisms. An agent must reach its site of action, interact with the relevant target and impair a function on which the organism depends. Differences in target structure, cellular organization, physiology and other properties can alter the response. Susceptibility describes how readily a microorganism is affected by a particular antimicrobial agent under specified conditions. It is therefore a relationship involving the agent, the organism and the conditions of measurement.

One of the most important quantitative descriptors of growth inhibition is the minimum inhibitory concentration, abbreviated MIC. It is the lowest concentration among the tested concentrations at which visible microbial growth is inhibited under the defined conditions of the observation. This definition contains several important qualifications. It refers to a concentration, not a time. It refers to inhibition of visible growth, not proof that every cell is dead. It is associated with a particular organism-agent relationship, and the reported value depends on the defined measurement conditions.

The word minimum refers to the lowest measured concentration satisfying the specified endpoint, not necessarily the exact molecular threshold at which a single cell ceases dividing. If a concentration series contains discrete values, the result is necessarily limited by that resolution. Suppose a purely hypothetical series of observations uses abstract concentration units, with visible growth at concentrations 1 and 2 and no visible growth at concentrations 4 and 8. The reported MIC on that series is 4 units. This does not mean that the actual transition must occur precisely at 4; it indicates the first observed inhibitory concentration on that particular series. The observations bracket a transition if we assume the response is monotonic.

A concentration below the MIC is not automatically biologically ineffective. There may be a measurable reduction in growth rate or changes in cellular physiology even when visible growth remains possible. The MIC is a defined endpoint rather than a complete concentration-response curve. In an exam problem, distinguish the concentration that first meets the endpoint from concentrations producing partial effects. If a graph shows continuous changes in a measured rate, a separate criterion is required to identify a particular endpoint.

Similarly, a concentration at or above the MIC does not automatically imply that no viable microorganisms remain. A population can be unable to produce a visible increase in biomass yet retain many cells capable of later recovery. A growth-inhibition endpoint therefore cannot substitute for a killing endpoint. This principle remains true whether the MIC is numerically low or high. A low MIC indicates that visible growth is prevented at a relatively low concentration under the measurement conditions; it does not specify the speed or extent of killing.

A useful quantitative comparison is a ratio of MIC values measured for comparable organisms and the same agent. In a synthetic teaching example, population A has an MIC of 2 arbitrary concentration units and population B has an MIC of 8 units. The ratio MIC_B/MIC_A is 8/2 = 4. Population B requires four times the measured concentration to meet the growth-inhibition endpoint in this comparison. The ratio is dimensionless because the units cancel. This is a statement about relative inhibitory susceptibility, not a clinical category assignment or a direct statement about a genetic mechanism.

If a question gives an MIC as greater than the highest tested concentration, the precise value is unknown. For instance, a reported result greater than 8 does not mean the MIC equals 16. It means that the measurement has not located the endpoint within the reported range. Likewise, if visible growth is absent even at the lowest measured concentration, the exact lower endpoint is not established by that series. Such results have bounds rather than precise numerical values. Arithmetic involving a bound must preserve the direction of the inequality.

The interpretation of an MIC also depends on the ability of the untreated comparison to grow. If the organism would not show visible growth even without the agent, the absence of visible growth in its presence cannot establish that the agent caused inhibition. If a nonbiological signal makes the observation difficult to interpret, the endpoint may be uncertain. This is why standardized methods and their quality controls matter. The relevant exam concept is that an endpoint has meaning only when the conditions supporting that endpoint have been established.

MIC measurements are usually discussed for a population, rather than individual molecules or individual bacterial cells. A population can contain heterogeneity, and an endpoint based on visible growth does not directly describe the distribution of individual responses. This is one reason why two populations with comparable MIC measurements may behave very differently when their viable fractions are followed over time. The MIC answers a specific question about visible growth; it is not a complete description of susceptibility, survival or biological variation.

It is also necessary to distinguish a comparative result from an absolute classification. A higher MIC than a reference population supports reduced susceptibility under comparable conditions. Whether a particular result is categorized as resistant in a clinical reporting system requires the appropriate interpretation standard. A raw numerical value without the identity of the organism, the agent and the relevant criteria cannot establish that category. The difference between an observed MIC and an interpreted susceptibility classification will be developed after the bactericidal endpoint and disk diffusion principle.
