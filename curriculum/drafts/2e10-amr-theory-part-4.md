## Fitness costs and compensation: why resistance can persist without direct selection

A resistance determinant is part of a functioning biological system. An altered ribosome must still translate messenger RNA; a modified wall enzyme must still participate in building a mechanically effective wall; an additional protective protein requires cellular resources for its production. These considerations explain why a mechanism that is beneficial in one environment can affect ordinary growth and survival in another.

A fitness cost is a reduction in relative reproductive success associated with a trait in a specified comparison and environment. The term is not interchangeable with energetic expenditure. Synthesis of an additional protein consumes resources, but that expenditure does not establish a detectable fitness cost by itself. The cost must be understood in relation to a reference and a biological outcome, such as relative growth or the contribution of a lineage to subsequent populations.

One possible source of cost is impaired activity of an essential cellular component. Imagine a molecular target that is less susceptible to inhibition because its structure has changed. The same change could alter interactions with its normal substrate or partners. If the ordinary cellular function is carried out less efficiently, the lineage may produce fewer descendants than a comparator under some conditions. The size of this difference depends on the rest of the physiological system, since cells possess regulatory mechanisms and reserves of functional capacity.

Another possible source is the burden of maintaining and expressing accessory genetic information. Replication of additional DNA, transcription and translation all require substrates and energy. If a determinant is expressed, its product can also interact with the normal functions of the cell. A burden is not inevitable for every genetic element, because biological consequences differ among elements, hosts and environments. Some additional genetic activities may provide other benefits, and some determinants may impose little measurable cost in a particular comparison.

Costs are often conditional. The same change can have a different effect when resources are plentiful than when a particular nutrient is limiting. A metabolic activity that is only weakly required under one set of conditions may become important under another. Differences in temperature, pH and physiological state can also affect the relationship between molecular activity and cellular growth. This connects the present chapter directly with the earlier treatment of nutrient yield and stress responses.

Why might a resistant lineage remain prevalent even when the immediate selective condition changes? First, the determinant may have little cost in the new environment. Second, the lineage may still carry other useful traits. Third, its abundance may reflect the history of previous selection and movement, and the timescale of observation may be too short for large compositional changes. Fourth, the initial physiological burden may have been partly offset by later genetic changes. These explanations need not be mutually exclusive.

Compensatory evolution refers to genetic changes that reduce or offset a harmful consequence of an earlier genetic change. To understand the concept, consider an altered protein that performs its essential function less efficiently than the original protein. A later genetic difference may restore some aspect of the ordinary cellular function while the original resistance-associated feature remains. The resulting lineage can retain the earlier phenotype even though its physiological burden has changed. Compensation is therefore distinct from simple reversion.

Reversion means return toward the earlier genetic state at the relevant locus, which may be associated with loss of the resistance phenotype. Compensation instead alleviates a cost without necessarily removing the original determinant. An observed recovery of growth does not distinguish these explanations unless the genotype and resistance phenotype are also examined. The logical point is that fitness and susceptibility are separable measurements.

Compensation need not fully restore fitness to that of the original lineage. It can be partial or conditional. Nor does every later mutation in a resistant lineage deserve the name compensatory. The term identifies a functional relationship: the later change alleviates an adverse consequence associated with the earlier one. A second mutation that has no effect on the earlier cost would not constitute compensation merely because it occurred later.

The combined consequences of genetic differences are not always the sum of their individual effects. The term epistasis describes a situation in which the phenotypic effect of one genetic difference depends on the presence of another. This dependence is intuitive in a cellular network because molecular products interact with other products and participate in shared processes. A change that is useful in one genetic background may have little effect in another. The concept is relevant to understanding why observations in a single lineage should not be treated as universal predictions.

The reviews by Andersson and Hughes (2010) and Melnyk and colleagues (2015) emphasize that costs vary and that compensation, linkage and other biological factors can limit the reversibility of resistance at the population level. The conclusion is not that resistance never disappears. Instead, removal of one selective factor does not logically guarantee an immediate return to a susceptible population. The evolutionary dynamics depend on the existing variation and the environment.

A common examination error is to use the word costly as if it meant lethal. A trait with a fitness cost can be carried by a viable lineage that continues growing and dividing. Its representation may decline relative to a more successful lineage, but it need not disappear instantly. Conversely, the absence of a measurable growth difference in one observation does not establish that the trait is cost-free in every ecological context. The appropriate interpretation is conditional on the measured outcome and comparison.

This discussion completes the bridge from molecular function to persistence of heritable traits. A resistance mechanism affects physiology; its consequences depend on context; the representation of lineages changes through selection and other processes. The same framework helps explain why a population can contain more than one resistance determinant and why the different traits may remain associated over time.

## Multidrug resistance, cross-resistance and genetic linkage

An organism may exhibit resistance to several antimicrobial agents. The phrase multidrug resistance describes a pattern across agents, and its exact formal definition depends on the organism and classification system. The term should not be treated as the name of a single molecular mechanism. Two organisms with similar multidrug phenotypes may possess very different combinations of biochemical processes and genetic determinants.

Cross-resistance occurs when a shared mechanism contributes to reduced susceptibility to more than one antimicrobial. One possible reason is that different agents interact with a shared target or use a similar route of cellular access. Another is that a chemical protective activity recognizes more than one related substrate. A broad-substrate efflux system can also contribute to responses to multiple compounds. These examples share a single causal feature affecting more than one phenotype; they do not imply that all agents are affected equally.

A distinct possibility is the coexistence of separate resistance determinants. One may affect a wall-targeting compound, whereas another may affect a ribosome-targeting compound. The determinants can reside in different genomic regions or be associated within the same genetic element. A multidrug phenotype does not disclose whether its components result from a single shared process or from several separate processes. Understanding this difference is a major objective of mechanism-based reasoning.

Genetic linkage describes an association between genetic features because of their physical relationship and patterns of inheritance. When two determinants tend to be inherited together, changes in the representation of one can be accompanied by changes in the representation of the other. This is a form of co-selection when selection acting on one feature indirectly changes the representation of an associated feature. The second feature need not itself be responsible for the immediate advantage.

It is helpful to distinguish co-selection from cross-resistance by asking where the common cause lies. In cross-resistance, a shared physiological mechanism affects more than one agent. In co-selection involving linkage, distinct determinants may have distinct molecular effects, but their genetic association causes them to be inherited together. Both can lead to correlated phenotypes. The correlation alone cannot identify which explanation is correct.

A further distinction is between a shared genetic element and a single gene. A genetic element can carry several genes, each encoding a different functional product. Conversely, one gene product can have effects on multiple compounds. The number of observed resistance phenotypes therefore cannot be read directly as a count of the responsible genes. Multiple phenotypes might be explained by one determinant, several determinants, or interacting features of the same cellular network.

When different resistance mechanisms coexist in a cell, the integrated phenotype cannot always be predicted by assuming that each acts in isolation. For example, the amount of active drug reaching a target reflects both access and chemical identity. A target-centered difference changes the physiological consequence of that amount. These relationships are connected within a single causal pathway. Observing several candidate determinants does not establish that each makes an equal or independent contribution.

A common misconception is that multidrug resistance means universal resistance to every antimicrobial. The word multiple does not mean all. Each agent has its own target, route of access and chemical characteristics. A phenotype involving several agents therefore still needs to be described with respect to specific observations and suitable interpretive criteria. Clinical classifications such as extensive resistance have specialized definitions that are outside the scope of this chapter.

Multidrug patterns are also not necessarily evidence of recent exposure to every agent involved. A shared biochemical feature may affect several compounds even if only one was relevant to an earlier selective environment. Associated genetic determinants may be inherited together. A population may also acquire a lineage that already possesses a multidrug phenotype. The observed pattern is a record of biological mechanisms and evolutionary history, not a list of every environmental event that has occurred.

For an exam question, separate the possible causal relationships. If one molecular activity affects several distinct compounds, cross-resistance is a plausible classification. If different functional determinants are physically associated and inherited together, co-selection may account for their correlated prevalence. If a multidrug phenotype is seen without genetic or mechanistic evidence, the pattern alone supports neither explanation uniquely.

The public-health significance of these distinctions is that resistance is not merely an isolated property of individual cells. It emerges from the interaction of biochemical mechanisms, genetic inheritance, population dynamics and movement between environments. The physiological chapter does not require specialist epidemiological models or treatment recommendations to establish this principle. The biology itself shows why an observed phenotype needs to be interpreted in its genetic and ecological context.

## Tolerance, persistence and the limits of a resistance interpretation

Students have encountered dormancy and stress survival in earlier chapters. A dormant cell can retain viability while many growth-associated processes are reduced. Some antimicrobial effects depend strongly on the normal activity of the processes they disturb. A cell in a different physiological state can consequently exhibit a different pattern of survival without possessing a heritable change that allows growth at a higher inhibitory concentration.

Resistance, tolerance and persistence all concern responses to antimicrobial exposure, but they are distinguished by the feature of the response that has changed. Resistance is associated with reduced susceptibility to growth inhibition under defined conditions. Tolerance refers to an increased ability of a population to survive exposure over time without necessarily exhibiting an increased MIC. Persistence describes survival by a subpopulation that differs in killing behavior from the majority. These distinctions are emphasized in the consensus statement of Balaban and colleagues (2019).

A tolerant population can remain susceptible to growth inhibition. That observation is possible because stopping division and causing irreversible loss of viability are different physiological consequences. In the preceding chapters, the difference between viable count and optical density helped explain why visible biomass does not directly measure survival. The present distinction applies the same reasoning to antimicrobial responses.

Persistence is often associated with a heterogeneous population. A majority may lose viability relatively rapidly while a minority survives longer. Such heterogeneity can produce a curve with an early steep decline followed by a slower decline. This is commonly called biphasic killing. It is a clue to the existence of different survival behaviors, not proof of a unique molecular mechanism. Other sources of heterogeneity or measurement artifacts can create complicated patterns.

Persistence does not require every surviving cell to be completely metabolically inactive. Reduced growth, dormancy and changes in physiological state can contribute, but the phenotype is defined through the survival pattern rather than through one universal molecular state. Likewise, the presence of a dormant subpopulation does not establish resistance. The essential distinction is whether the cells have a changed capacity for growth under defined inhibitory conditions or merely a changed survival trajectory.

A population can display heteroresistance, a term used for coexistence of subpopulations differing in their susceptibility to an agent. It should not automatically be equated with persistence. A surviving subpopulation may differ in the ability to grow in the presence of an inhibitory agent, or it may instead have a slower killing response with similar growth susceptibility. The observation of rare survivors alone does not separate these possibilities.

Genetic influences and phenotypic states also need to be distinguished carefully. Some inherited differences can affect the tendency to enter a tolerant or persistent physiological state. That does not mean every surviving individual has acquired a resistance determinant or that its descendants must exhibit an increased MIC. Heritable variation can influence survival kinetics without changing the particular growth inhibition endpoint used to define resistance.

A textbook graph often draws susceptibility and tolerance as sharply distinct patterns. Actual biological responses may show overlapping properties. A population may have a changed MIC and altered killing kinetics, or multiple subpopulations may behave differently. The purpose of the categories is to identify measurable aspects of a response, not to impose mutually exclusive identities on all cells.

The most reliable interpretive habit is to state the observation before giving its explanation. A larger growth-inhibition threshold is evidence of reduced susceptibility relative to an appropriate comparator. A slower decline in viable abundance is evidence of altered killing behavior. A heterogeneous survival curve suggests more than one survival behavior. Stable transmission of a phenotype supports heritability, but even then the causal genetic feature may remain unknown. The different conclusions should not be collapsed into a single word.

This approach is useful because it protects against exaggerated conclusions from incomplete data. A surviving population is not automatically resistant; a resistant phenotype does not identify its molecular cause; and a molecular mechanism does not by itself reveal how its determinant spread. These are the central distinctions that students must retain from the entire microbial physiology sequence.

## Integrating molecular evidence with population observations

Imagine a scientific report comparing two anonymized microbial populations. It describes a difference in growth susceptibility, but the authors have not established why the difference exists. At this stage the most scientifically useful response is to enumerate the links in the causal chain and examine the observations that discriminate among them. It is not sufficient to select a mechanism because it is frequently mentioned in textbooks.

If the active molecule is converted into a chemically altered product, inactivation is a plausible primary explanation. If the molecule remains intact but reaches its target in smaller effective amounts, access mechanisms become relevant. If the target responds differently in the presence of comparable active drug, altered recognition or protection becomes plausible. If target inhibition remains comparable but essential physiological output is preserved, altered dependence on the affected process deserves consideration. Each observation concerns a different intermediate consequence.

These are not absolute diagnostic rules. A total chemical measurement may combine intact and altered molecules. A cell-associated measurement may include material bound to surfaces. An isolated molecular target may not reproduce the context of the living cell. A growth endpoint summarizes many cellular processes. When interpreting a question, the stated assumptions determine how confidently alternatives can be distinguished. Scientific reasoning requires sensitivity to what was measured, not simply familiarity with the expected answer.

Evidence of genetic association raises another set of questions. The presence of a determinant may correlate with a phenotype across a collection of cells. Correlation is compatible with a causal contribution, but it can also arise through common ancestry or association with another determinant. A gene may be present but not expressed, or expressed in a context where its product does not affect the agent being considered. The chain from genetic information to functional phenotype cannot be omitted.

The same caution applies to historical inference. The presence of a similar determinant in separate populations may suggest shared inheritance or genetic transfer, but it does not on its own establish the route or time of movement. If the frequency of a trait increases, differential success is a plausible explanation, but sampling, migration, acquisition and changes in measurement definitions can also influence the result. An evolutionary conclusion should identify the process supported by the available evidence.

A particularly important exam principle is that a negative observation has a limited meaning. Failure to detect a particular enzyme does not establish the absence of all chemical inactivation mechanisms. Failure to find a change in a familiar target does not exclude all target-centered differences. Failure to observe an increased MIC does not establish absence of every altered survival behavior. Conclusions must remain proportional to the information in the question.

The central ideas of Unit 2.E now form a coherent framework. Growth measurement explains how abundance and physiological outcomes are observed. Growth kinetics describes how descendants accumulate. Nutrient limitation and environmental factors show why growth is conditional. Division explains inheritance through reproduction. Stress responses explain reversible physiological adjustments and survival. Antimicrobial action introduces molecular vulnerability. Resistance integrates these principles with inherited variation and differential success.

The next unit develops the molecular basis of replication, repair, transcription, translation and gene regulation. Those processes provide the deeper explanation of how genetic information changes, is inherited and becomes a functional protein or RNA. The present chapter has not attempted to replace that material. Instead it has established the biological questions that make the molecular processes meaningful: how a difference in information affects an essential activity, how that activity changes a phenotype, and how that phenotype influences the history of a population.

A student who can explain these relationships without relying on a list of mechanism names has reached the main conceptual goal. Resistance is neither an intentional response of DNA nor a single universal biochemical trick. It is a conditional biological phenotype whose molecular basis, genetic origin, inheritance and population consequences must be considered separately and then connected through evidence.
