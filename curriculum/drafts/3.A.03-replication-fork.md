# Replication Fork Architecture and Core Replication Enzymes

## 1. A chromosome must be copied without reversing its chemistry

A cell that is preparing to divide faces two distinct tasks. It must reproduce the sequence of its genetic information, and it must produce physical DNA molecules that can be inherited independently. Complementary base pairing supplies the principle by which sequence information can be reproduced. Yet complementarity alone does not provide the molecular machinery that separates a stable double helix, selects nucleotides, forms covalent bonds, manages mechanical strain and completes uninterrupted daughter strands. Replication is therefore best understood as a moving assembly of interacting molecular machines rather than as a single chemical reaction.

Imagine examining a small segment of a chromosome just before it is replicated. The two strands are connected by base pairing and stabilized by interactions between adjacent bases. They cannot both be copied efficiently while their bases remain paired to each other. The parental strands must become locally accessible to enzymes. If the entire chromosome were unwound at once, it would expose an enormous quantity of single-stranded DNA to folding, inappropriate interactions and damage. A much more manageable strategy is to expose a limited region of template and convert it rapidly into daughter duplexes. This is what the advancing replication fork accomplishes. The DNA ahead of the fork is parental duplex; the DNA behind it normally consists of two duplex products, each containing a parental strand and its newly made partner.

The fork is a geometric description of the region where a parental duplex separates into two template paths. A replisome, by contrast, is the molecular assembly associated with this region. It includes the activities needed to unwind the duplex and extend complementary strands. Although individual proteins can associate and dissociate, the functional assembly can remain near the junction for a prolonged period. Some accessory reactions occur behind the junction rather than at exactly the same point. A diagram that places all proteins at one coordinate is therefore an instructional simplification, not a claim about their physical positions.

A replication origin is a locus or region from which replication starts, whereas a fork is a moving boundary. A bubble has two boundaries, even though particular replication systems may use only one actively progressing fork. An ordinary bidirectional origin produces two active forks moving in opposite directions. At each fork two complementary daughter strands must be synthesized. The origin may have been activated minutes earlier and may no longer be physically close to the proteins at the junction. This separation between an initiation site and an elongating structure is important when interpreting experiments that distinguish initiation defects from elongation defects.

The daughter DNA is not made by copying one parental strand and then discarding the other. Both original strands are valuable templates. Suppose the parental duplex contains one strand with sequence 5′-AGCT-3′ and its partner written below it as 3′-TCGA-5′. Copying the first makes a complementary strand equivalent in sequence to the second, whereas copying the second makes a strand equivalent to the first. In the absence of errors, both daughter duplexes therefore have the same base-paired sequence as the parental molecule. The inherited material in each duplex is different, however. One daughter contains the first old strand and the other contains the second old strand. This is the semiconservative logic established in the preceding chapter.

To understand why a fork is asymmetric, we must inspect the directional structure of DNA. Numbering a sugar carbon with a prime mark distinguishes it from numbered atoms in a nitrogenous base. In deoxyribose the base is linked to carbon 1′. Carbon 2′ lacks the hydroxyl group present in ribose. Carbon 3′ carries the hydroxyl that can participate in chain extension, and carbon 5′ lies outside the sugar ring and is commonly connected to phosphate. A phosphodiester linkage joins the 3′ oxygen of one sugar to a phosphate attached to the 5′ carbon of the next sugar. Repetition of this arrangement gives a backbone that has distinguishable ends. Calling a strand 5′ to 3′ specifies chemical orientation, not simply the direction in which a drawing is read.

In a normal antiparallel duplex the two backbones have opposite polarity. If one parental strand is 3′ to 5′ from left to right, its partner is 5′ to 3′ in the same spatial direction. Both descriptions are compatible with Watson–Crick pairing. The orientations are not arbitrary labels that can be exchanged to simplify a problem: reversing the labels without reversing the strand would change the chemical identity of its ends. A sequence reported by convention in the 5′ to 3′ direction must be reverse-complemented, rather than merely complemented, when reporting its partner in that same convention. For example, the partner of 5′-ATGCCA-3′ is 3′-TACGGT-5′ when placed below the first strand. Written in the conventional direction it is 5′-TGGCAT-3′. The two answers represent the same physical strand.

Consider a fork moving to the right, with the still paired parental DNA on its right. The upper parental template runs 3′ to 5′ from left to right. An enzyme that reads that template toward its 5′ end can extend a complementary daughter toward the right, in the same direction as fork progression. The lower parental template instead runs 5′ to 3′ from left to right. Its new complement must extend toward the left if it follows the normal polymerization chemistry. This geometrical conflict exists independently of the names or structures of the proteins. Changing the drawing to a leftward moving fork reverses the spatial positions that can support continuous extension; it does not reverse the chemistry of either DNA polymerase.

This is why examination questions that ask whether a particular parental strand is always a leading template are misleading unless the direction of an individual fork is specified. For a bidirectional bubble, a single old strand can be the leading template at one boundary and the lagging template at the other. To solve a problem, first find the boundary between unreplicated and replicated DNA, then mark its direction of movement, then inspect the parental polarity. Only after these steps should one label the new strands. A diagram of a duplex without a fork arrow or another indication of the unreplicated region may be insufficient to assign the labels.

## 2. The chemical reaction that determines polymerization direction

DNA synthesis requires more than free nitrogenous bases. The polymerase uses deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, abbreviated dNTPs. In ordinary DNA replication these are dATP, dGTP, dCTP and dTTP. Each contains the relevant base, deoxyribose and a chain of three phosphate groups on the 5′ position. From the sugar outward the phosphates are called alpha, beta and gamma. The phosphate closest to the sugar is the one retained in the DNA backbone during incorporation. The other two leave together as pyrophosphate. This distinction explains why the labeled phosphate position matters in biochemical experiments: a label in the alpha phosphate can become part of the backbone, while a gamma phosphate label is not retained in that position in ordinary extension.

The other substrate is an existing primer with an available terminal 3′ hydroxyl. The primer is paired with its template, which extends beyond the primer's growing end. DNA polymerase positions the next incoming nucleotide opposite the next exposed template base. The oxygen of the primer's 3′ hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphorus of the incoming dNTP. A new covalent linkage results, and pyrophosphate is released. The incoming nucleotide becomes the new terminal residue, with its own 3′ hydroxyl available for the next round. Thus extension proceeds by repeatedly renewing the same chemical functional group at the growing end.

The reaction may be written schematically as DNA(n) + dNTP → DNA(n+1) + PPi. Here DNA(n) denotes the extendable chain of length n residues; PPi is inorganic pyrophosphate. Hydrolysis of PPi yields two orthophosphate molecules. Under appropriate cellular conditions, consumption of the released pyrophosphate favors the forward overall reaction. The polymerase does not generally need to hydrolyze one separate ATP molecule for each phosphodiester linkage; the activated nucleotide already supplies the chemical potential needed for its incorporation. The additional ATP use by helicases, clamp loaders and some other enzymes supports different tasks.

The active site has to solve a problem of charge. Phosphate groups are negatively charged, and the attacking oxygen must be appropriately oriented and made reactive. Conserved acidic residues and divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium, help coordinate the substrates and stabilize unfavorable charge distributions during the reaction. The standard two-metal-ion model assigns complementary catalytic roles to the metal ions: promoting productive alignment and activation of the primer hydroxyl, and stabilizing the nucleotide triphosphate and leaving group. It is a useful molecular model of phosphodiester formation, not a statement that every polymerase has identical kinetic intermediates or exactly two metal ions in every observable structural state. A mutation that removes a key metal-coordinating carboxylate can leave the protein capable of binding nucleic acids yet severely compromise chemistry.

The polymerase active site is often described using a hand analogy. The palm region contains the catalytic center; other regions help position the primer-template and incoming nucleotide. A fingers region often undergoes conformational changes upon nucleotide binding, whereas the thumb contributes to contacts with duplex DNA. This description captures a functional division of labor without implying that all polymerases are structurally identical. The enzyme can discriminate among candidate nucleotides by recognizing shape and interactions in the active site. Correct complementary pairing favors a geometry suitable for chemistry. Wrong pairing can prevent efficient closure, substrate alignment or reaction. Fidelity therefore depends on more than the number of hydrogen bonds between isolated bases.

After incorporation, the enzyme must shift its relationship to the template so that the next template position reaches the active site. A polymerase that binds and incorporates but cannot translocate efficiently would produce very short extension products. A different mutant could translocate normally yet fail to select the appropriate base. These mechanistic distinctions matter because decreased DNA synthesis is a common final readout for very different molecular failures. Measuring the total incorporated label does not directly reveal which stage failed.

The terminal hydroxyl provides a simple way to understand chain termination. If the last residue lacks a usable 3′-OH, the next incoming triphosphate has no ordinary acceptor oxygen for the phosphodiester reaction. A dideoxynucleotide can therefore serve as an illustrative chain terminator once incorporated. It is important to distinguish absence of a 3′ hydroxyl from the ordinary absence of a 2′ hydroxyl in deoxyribose. The latter is a defining feature of the usual DNA substrate and does not prevent extension. An incoming nucleotide bearing a normal 3′ hydroxyl is not enough to rescue a previously blocked primer end, because its own hydroxyl will only become available for another round after it has been attached.

The same logic explains why a DNA polymerase needs a primer rather than just an exposed template. A template base identifies an appropriate incoming base but does not provide an acceptor 3′ oxygen on the nascent strand. Polymerase binding to unprimed single-stranded DNA is therefore not equivalent to productive synthesis. An experiment comparing otherwise identical single-stranded templates with and without annealed primers tests this requirement. The primer must be oriented so its available 3′ end faces an uncopied portion of template. A short complementary oligonucleotide annealed at the wrong extreme may have no downstream template for extension, even though it is chemically intact and binds strongly.

A primer may be DNA in an artificial biochemical assay, or RNA in the normal initiation of many chromosomal replication events. Its indispensable property for the replicative polymerase is a suitable end and junction. This explains why an externally supplied DNA primer can bypass the need for primase on a simple preprimed substrate while failing to rescue a complete moving fork that needs a succession of new primers. Some biological replication systems use alternative initiation strategies, including protein priming. Such examples should not be interpreted as evidence that the ordinary chromosomal replicative polymerase can simply start DNA chains from freely diffusing dNTPs without an acceptor.

An additional argument connects synthesis direction with error correction. In the normal reaction each new nucleotide arrives already activated at its 5′ end. If a mispaired terminal residue is removed by a proofreading exonuclease, the newly exposed chain end can provide a 3′ hydroxyl. Another incoming activated nucleotide can then participate in the usual extension chemistry. In an imagined reversed system in which the growing end had to carry the activated group, removing the last residue could remove the activation needed for continuing synthesis, unless a separate mechanism restored it. This comparison explains a functional advantage of the established architecture without claiming that hypothetical alternative chemical systems are physically impossible. The experimentally established direction of conventional chromosomal DNA polymerization is 5′ to 3′.

The direction of proofreading must not be confused with the direction of synthesis. Removal of an incorrect terminal nucleotide by a 3′ to 5′ exonuclease is a cleavage reaction rather than backward DNA copying. The activity examines the end that has most recently been extended and removes nucleotides from that end. A 5′ to 3′ nuclease that removes material ahead of a polymerase during primer processing performs another distinct task. The consequences of losing proofreading will be explored in Node 3.A.07; here it is sufficient to recognize that different reactions at different ends can coexist within one protein or multiprotein assembly without violating the direction of polymerization.

## 3. How biochemical evidence separated synthesis from complete replication

A historical difficulty was that visual knowledge of DNA structure did not reveal the proteins responsible for copying the molecule. In the 1950s, Arthur Kornberg and coworkers investigated the formation of DNA in cell-free systems from activated nucleotide substrates. Their work showed that DNA-directed synthesis could be attributed to a purified enzymatic activity and examined through chemical measurements. The enzyme initially characterized from Escherichia coli later became known as DNA polymerase I. The discovery supplied experimental access to the chemistry of DNA synthesis, but it did not establish that this particular enzyme was the main polymerase that copies the bacterial chromosome in vivo.

The distinction between activity in a test tube and function in a cell is fundamental. A purified protein can catalyze synthesis on a convenient artificial template without possessing the speed, processivity or partners necessary to duplicate a complete chromosome. If the substrate is a short, already primed strand, a polymerase with modest processivity may perform extremely well. The same enzyme might fail to sustain rapid, coordinated synthesis across a large double-stranded genome. The bacterial chromosome introduces constraints of DNA opening, torsional strain, repeated priming and assembly of multiple enzyme activities. Thus identification of polymerase activity addressed one essential question while opening several others.

A conceptual reconstruction of such an assay would compare reactions containing template, extendable primer, all necessary nucleotide substrates, appropriate metal ions and enzyme against reactions missing one of these components. Incorporation of a detectable nucleotide into a DNA product provides a readout. A reaction containing inactive enzyme helps establish the background from nonspecific retention of label. Separation of unincorporated nucleotide from macromolecular product is necessary: a detector that measures all isotope in the tube would give a signal whether or not a bond had formed. A time series is more informative than a single final point because it can distinguish the onset and rate of accumulation from the final capacity of the reaction.

If one dNTP is omitted, a heterogeneous template generally permits extension only until the enzyme encounters a template position requiring that missing nucleotide. The effect is therefore sequence dependent. A template consisting of one base repeated over a region may be copied for that region with only its complementary dNTP. This observation is a useful control for a sequence-defined assay and cautions against interpreting the absence of one nucleotide as inevitably producing no extension at all. In real cells, the four substrate pools must support the variety of bases encountered across the genome.

Suppose an extract produces substantial labeled DNA from a primed single-stranded template but little from a closed duplex. This result need not imply that it contains a defective polymerase. It may lack the machinery needed to open the duplex or provide a primer-template junction on it. A second assay using a partially open, fork-like substrate could test the ability to extend once an accessible junction exists. If the missing activity is supplied and synthesis on the duplex increases without changing synthesis on the preprimed single strand, the results suggest an accessory role rather than a direct effect on nucleotide chemistry. Even this conclusion requires assurance that the added preparation does not carry extra polymerase or alter other reaction conditions.

This reasoning illustrates how researchers decompose a complicated biological process into simpler biochemical functions. No single substrate is adequate for testing every replication protein. A strand-separation assay tests whether a duplex can be opened, a primer synthesis assay tests the formation of short initial chains, and a nick-sealing assay tests completion of the phosphodiester backbone. The most informative interpretation compares these specific activities with the behavior of a reconstructed fork. A protein might have normal activity in isolation but fail to interact productively with its partners. Conversely, an accessory factor may appear inactive when offered an artificial substrate that bypasses the task it normally performs.

Genetics added another layer of evidence. Mutations that cause replication to stop under restrictive conditions can help identify proteins needed for replication. A temperature-sensitive variant, for example, allows a protein to function at a permissive temperature and lose function at a restrictive one. Such a change must be interpreted with appropriate wild-type temperature controls because temperature also influences nucleotide metabolism, protein stability and general physiology. The strongest assignment comes when a genetic defect is complemented by a functional gene or protein, and purified versions of the relevant factor demonstrate a particular biochemical role. This combination of genetic and biochemical evidence allows students to infer causality rather than simply associate a protein with DNA.

The historical story also warns against equating the numeral in a polymerase name with its order of action. The enzyme called polymerase I was the first identified in the bacterial system, not necessarily the enzyme that initiates chromosome replication or the major enzyme at every growing fork. The functional assignments of bacterial and eukaryotic polymerases require separate treatment, which will follow in the next nodes. The central lesson at this point is that the synthesis of a complementary chain is only one operation in the production of complete daughter molecules.

## 4. Leading and lagging synthesis are consequences of geometry

Once the direction of chemical extension has been established, the leading-strand solution follows naturally. At a rightward-moving fork, the template oriented 3′ to 5′ toward the junction can be read toward the moving boundary. After an initial primer has supplied an extendable end, newly exposed bases can be incorporated in succession as the parental duplex opens. The growing 3′ end remains close to the point where new template is becoming accessible. Under ideal conditions a long complementary tract can therefore be made without repeatedly creating a new primer. This is the meaning of continuous leading-strand synthesis.

Continuous is a description of the predominant geometry of strand formation, not an assertion that the enzyme never pauses, changes partners, encounters damage or dissociates. A leading polymerase can stop transiently, and special situations may require a new start. A molecular trace containing pauses is compatible with the leading-strand model. What distinguishes the leading strand from the lagging strand is not uninterrupted enzyme attachment at all times; it is that fork movement ordinarily reveals additional template in a direction that the existing nascent end can follow.

The opposite parental template cannot be read toward the same moving junction by an ordinary DNA polymerase. Extension can only proceed away from that junction along the appropriate template polarity. One solution would be to wait until the entire DNA molecule had opened and then copy the second parental strand from the far end, but such a solution would create large stretches of single-stranded DNA and would be unsuitable for many replication geometries. Instead, the fork exposes a finite interval of template. A new primer is made near the recently exposed region, and a polymerase extends that primer away from the junction toward DNA that was synthesized earlier. As more template becomes exposed, another primer is placed closer to the moving fork.

The order of events is sometimes counterintuitive. At a rightward fork, the newest lagging-strand fragment begins farther to the right than its predecessor. Its own synthesis travels left. Consequently, the chronological sequence of fragment initiation moves rightward with the fork even though nucleotide addition on each fragment moves leftward. The 5′ end of a fragment marks where its primer began, and its growing 3′ end moves away from the fork toward the preceding fragment. Adjacent fragments are later processed and joined into one long complementary strand. They are not normally transferred to another DNA molecule.

A convenient diagram can make both new strands appear to move in the same direction if it draws the lagging template bent back into a loop. This represents a spatial rearrangement of the flexible DNA, not a switch in the chemical polarity of polymerase action. A molecular machine can advance overall in one direction even if its subunits act along differently oriented sections of folded substrates. This point will become especially important when discussing the trombone model of lagging-strand synthesis.

In many bacterial systems, textbook Okazaki fragment lengths are roughly one to two kilobases. In eukaryotic nuclear replication they are often approximately one to two hundred nucleotides. These are representative scales, not fixed numerical constants. The intervals vary with the organism, the replisome and the conditions under which products are measured. A question providing explicit lengths should be solved using those values rather than substituted memorized approximations. The smaller fragments of eukaryotic replication imply more frequent priming events per unit length of DNA, all else being equal, but they do not alone tell us how fast a whole chromosome is copied. Fork speed, origin density and the total number of active forks also matter.

To visualize the geometry of a pair of diverging forks, draw a horizontal old top strand oriented 5′ to 3′ from left to right and the old bottom strand oriented 3′ to 5′. At the left boundary the upper template is read toward the left, and its complement can extend leftward continuously. At the right boundary the lower template is read toward the right, so its complement is the continuous product there. The remaining products in each case require fragments. Thus two leading strands and two lagging strands are being synthesized around an ordinary bidirectional bubble. The two leading products are on opposite parental templates. A student who assigns both leading products to the same original strand has ignored the opposing directions of fork travel.

The products eventually form continuous duplexes even though their mode of production differs. A mature double-stranded molecule does not necessarily preserve visible evidence of every primer junction, because the RNA and any unwanted DNA from initiation are removed, the required DNA is filled in, and the remaining nicks are sealed. To discover the discontinuous intermediates one must study DNA during the period of synthesis, not merely after replication has completed.
