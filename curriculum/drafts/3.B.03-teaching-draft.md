# Node 3.B.03 — Prokaryotic Transcription Elongation, Termination and Inhibition

**Unpublished working draft — not a completed lesson.** Scope from the approved 3.B roadmap. Teaching narrative only in the numbered sections; references and supplemental content will be separate.

## 1. From a successfully initiated transcript to a complete RNA

A bacterial cell faces two distinct informational problems each time it transcribes a gene. It must locate the appropriate starting point in an enormous number of potential DNA sequences, and it must then copy the selected information into an RNA molecule of an appropriate length. A promoter solves the first problem, but a promoter cannot by itself specify when the copy is complete. The second problem requires an enzyme capable of maintaining its contact with DNA while incorporating many nucleotides, a mechanism for responding to obstacles during this journey, and a mechanism that releases the finished RNA. These are the subjects of elongation, pausing and termination. They are causally connected rather than independent stages in a diagram. An elongating enzyme is stable enough to copy a long gene, but the same stability creates a problem at its end: a signal must selectively destabilize an otherwise productive complex.

Imagine two neighboring bacterial genes, each with a different promoter. A polymerase that initiates the first gene must not automatically express the second simply because its DNA lies downstream. Sometimes several adjacent genes are intended to be transcribed together as an operon, but even an operon has boundaries. A well-positioned terminator limits unnecessary RNA synthesis and helps separate adjacent transcription units. A terminator that fails may allow the production of RNA extending beyond the normal boundary. Such an RNA is called a readthrough transcript. Depending on the arrangement of genes, readthrough can increase expression of downstream information, interfere with transcription arriving from another promoter, or generate RNA that is degraded without producing useful protein. Thus a change that leaves the coding sequence of every protein intact may still have a substantial effect on gene expression.

RNA polymerase is a DNA-directed enzyme, not an enzyme that makes a duplicate DNA chromosome. It follows one DNA strand in the direction from its 3′ end toward its 5′ end and extends RNA at the RNA's 3′ end. The emerging 5′ region contains information copied earlier, while the growing 3′ region represents the latest information acquired. Once transcription begins, the polymerase does not need a succession of new promoters at every codon. It remains associated with the nucleic acids and repeatedly carries out the same chemical reaction. A start codon is interpreted by a ribosome, not by bacterial RNA polymerase as a general instruction to begin elongation. Likewise, a stop codon does not by itself cause RNA polymerase to release RNA. Transcription signals and translation signals are interpreted by different molecular machines.

This distinction becomes especially important in bacteria, where there is no nuclear envelope separating the usual site of chromosomal transcription from the cytoplasmic translation machinery. A ribosome can engage a suitable ribosome-binding region of an emerging messenger RNA before its 3′ end has been produced. The RNA therefore becomes a shared molecular object: its upstream part may be translated while its downstream end remains in RNA polymerase. The availability of the intervening RNA to other proteins depends partly on whether it is folded or occupied by ribosomes. This connection helps explain why the movement of a protein called Rho can be influenced by the translation status of an RNA even though Rho belongs to the transcription termination machinery. Ribosomal translation is neither required for every bacterial transcription reaction nor possible on every transcript, because many bacterial RNAs are functional without being translated.

A useful learning approach is to distinguish the events by their physical consequences. Elongation adds a covalently attached nucleotide to the RNA chain. Pausing decreases or temporarily interrupts the rate of addition but can leave the RNA and DNA securely associated with polymerase. Termination releases the transcript and dismantles the elongation complex. Inhibition is a perturbation that decreases transcription by interfering with one or more stages; it need not reproduce physiological termination. A drug-treated polymerase that has stopped making nucleotides may remain attached to DNA. The absence of further RNA synthesis is therefore insufficient evidence that termination occurred. This distinction underlies many experimental reasoning questions.

## 2. How experimental evidence separated elongation from initiation and termination

Early biochemical work on transcription established that RNA can be synthesized by an enzyme supplied with DNA and ribonucleoside triphosphates. The crucial observation is not simply that radioactive label enters an acid-insoluble fraction, but that the product depends on the appropriate substrates and a DNA template and behaves as RNA. An experiment without DNA tests whether an observed signal depends on the intended template. An experiment without RNA polymerase establishes the background associated with the remaining reagents. Removal of an essential nucleotide prevents productive synthesis beyond positions that require that substrate. These controls distinguish template-dependent RNA formation from nonspecific association of label with a protein or precipitated material.

A bulk incorporation assay, however, does not tell us which DNA position the enzyme has copied. It reports a summed measure of incorporation. If one reaction produces ten molecules of RNA of one hundred nucleotides and another produces a hundred molecules of ten nucleotides, the total number of incorporated residues may be similar. A measurement proportional to incorporated radioactivity could fail to distinguish them. Electrophoretic separation of RNA products under denaturing conditions resolves this ambiguity by revealing the lengths of the products. With suitable labeling, size markers and reaction design, short products near a promoter can be distinguished from longer intermediates, full-length transcripts and RNA ending at an internal position. The assay thus connects a population measurement to the progress of individual molecular complexes.

It became possible to ask whether termination required a cellular component other than RNA polymerase. In 1969, Jeffrey W. Roberts reported a protein factor from Escherichia coli that promoted specific termination and RNA release in a cell-free transcription system. This finding, published as Termination factor for RNA synthesis in Nature, established that at least some RNA ends depend on a separate protein activity. The protein was named Rho. An important conceptual consequence was that neither the termination position nor the ability to form a short RNA can always be predicted solely by identifying the promoter and the polymerase. An additional factor can participate in determining the final length of the transcript.

The logic of the factor-addition experiment remains useful. Suppose purified bacterial RNA polymerase is supplied with a suitable template and all four substrates. Without an added protein fraction it produces predominantly long RNA. When an active fraction is added, a shorter, reproducible RNA species appears. The obvious hypothesis is that the fraction promotes termination at a specific region. A competing explanation is that the fraction contains an RNase that cuts an already synthesized long RNA. Merely observing the short RNA does not exclude this alternative. Investigators must establish whether RNA was released from an elongation complex in a transcription-dependent manner and whether the short product reflects cessation of chain extension rather than cleavage after synthesis. An RNA degradation control using previously synthesized RNA helps distinguish the possibilities. The result becomes much more convincing when purified factor and defined transcription complexes reproduce the behavior.

The discovery of factor-dependent termination does not imply that all bacterial termination requires the factor. Some sequences allow RNA polymerase to release its transcript without added Rho or other dedicated termination machinery. This is why experiments using a defined set of components are especially instructive. A transcript that terminates efficiently when the only enzyme is bacterial RNA polymerase provides evidence for an intrinsic route; comparison of alternative DNA sequences then identifies features that contribute to its activity. Conversely, factor-dependent release on a template that is otherwise read through shows that a factor can supply a missing termination activity. The two mechanisms can coexist in the same organism and can operate at different transcription units.

Later structural and biochemical studies helped explain how an enzyme that is neither a conventional replicative helicase nor a ribosome can copy DNA while keeping only a small portion locally open. Structures of elongation complexes reveal where template DNA, growing RNA and incoming nucleotides are arranged. A structure is a snapshot: it identifies spatial relationships but does not directly record how rapidly an intermediate forms or disappears. Kinetic experiments complement it by measuring the timing of nucleotide addition, pausing and release. Students should not treat a single static structure as proof of every transition in a catalytic cycle. The strongest mechanistic conclusions combine biochemical, structural and kinetic observations.

## 3. The elongation complex: a mobile, locally opened nucleic-acid assembly

At the end of bacterial initiation, RNA polymerase has produced enough RNA to establish a stable association between the nascent transcript and its DNA template. The initiation-specific arrangement of sigma factor and promoter DNA is reorganized during promoter escape. The core polymerase, containing two alpha subunits, beta, beta-prime and omega, can carry out the subsequent elongation chemistry. Sigma factor commonly dissociates during the transition, although its dissociation is not invariably immediate and complete at every promoter. The important functional difference is that the elongation complex is no longer anchored to promoter recognition in the way the initial complex was. Its stability depends substantially on the interactions of core polymerase with DNA and RNA.

An elongation complex includes downstream double-stranded DNA approaching the enzyme, a short region in which the DNA strands are locally separated, an RNA–DNA hybrid in which the recently synthesized RNA is paired with its template, an exiting RNA segment and upstream DNA that has reformed its double helix. The locally separated DNA region is called the transcription bubble. The RNA–DNA hybrid is not the same structure as the entire bubble. The bubble is defined by the DNA strands being apart; the hybrid is defined by pairing between RNA and one of those DNA strands. In conventional bacterial structural models, approximately eight to nine base pairs of RNA–DNA hybrid lie inside a bubble on the order of ten to fourteen opened DNA base pairs, depending on the complex and how boundaries are defined. These values are useful approximate scales, not a universal fixed counting rule.

The template strand enters the active-center region with its orientation suited to reading consecutive bases toward its 5′ end. The other strand, often called the nontemplate strand, is displaced from the hybrid. The newly formed RNA bases pair with template DNA through ordinary complementary base-pairing interactions. The RNA sequence therefore resembles the nontemplate or coding strand when both are written in the 5′ to 3′ direction, with uracil replacing thymine. Consider a short coding-strand segment written as 5′-GACTAC-3′. The complementary template reads 3′-CTGATG-5′ across the same region, and the RNA is 5′-GACUAC-3′. The six-base example describes sequence identity, not the actual full width of the elongation complex.

One should picture the elongation complex as a moving window rather than a growing wedge. If the polymerase has copied a thousand nucleotides, it has not opened a thousand-base-pair bubble. DNA opens near the leading edge, while the template and nontemplate strands re-pair behind the enzyme as RNA is peeled away from the hybrid. Most of the transcript exits the enzyme as a single RNA strand that can fold or interact with proteins. The same relatively short hybrid is continuously renewed. This arrangement conserves the double-stranded character of the genome while allowing temporary access to one strand for information transfer.

The polymerase surrounds portions of the nucleic acids in a cleft and makes many noncovalent contacts with them. A clamp-like region contributes to holding downstream DNA and the RNA–DNA hybrid in a productive configuration. Separate channels provide routes for DNA, RNA and substrates. At the active center, the growing RNA 3′ end is aligned with the template base and a complementary incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate. These physical arrangements explain why a stable elongation complex does not need to detach and rebind after each nucleotide. Breaking all contacts after every chemical step would expose the transcript to premature loss and drastically reduce the efficiency of copying long transcription units.

Stability nevertheless has limits. A transient change in nucleic-acid position can move the RNA 3′ end away from the catalytic site. A distortion of the hybrid, a physical barrier on DNA or an interaction between the emerging RNA and the enzyme can favor an inactive configuration. If the complex retains the transcript and can return to a productive configuration, it has paused rather than terminated. If the transcript is released and the productive arrangement is dismantled, the enzyme has undergone termination or an alternative form of dissociation. The molecular challenge is to balance stable association against sufficient flexibility for movement and controlled release.

The displaced nontemplate strand is also chemically and biologically relevant. It is not the source of sequence information for the RNA during normal synthesis, but it forms part of the nucleic-acid environment through which the polymerase must move. DNA sequences can affect the stability of the bubble, the kinetics of translocation and the energetics of DNA re-pairing. Consequently, synonymous or noncoding sequence changes can affect transcription even when their impact on the final protein sequence is negligible. It is not enough to know that the active site reads one strand; the entire nucleic-acid assembly influences how easily that reading proceeds.

## 4. The chemistry of extending RNA by one nucleotide

To understand a long RNA transcript, first examine a single chemical step. An incoming nucleotide is not merely a free base. It is a ribonucleoside triphosphate, composed of a base, ribose and three phosphate groups. The four main substrates are ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP. Their bases differ in complementarity to template DNA, but their phosphate-containing chemical groups participate in a shared reaction. A nucleotide that pairs with the template can be positioned with its alpha phosphate near the hydroxyl group at the 3′ end of the existing RNA. The alpha phosphate is the phosphate closest to the ribose; the beta and gamma phosphates lie farther away.

The oxygen of the terminal 3′ hydroxyl participates in a nucleophilic attack on the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphorus. Formation of the new bond connects the previous RNA nucleotide's 3′ oxygen to the phosphate associated with the incoming nucleotide's 5′ carbon. A pyrophosphate group, containing the beta and gamma phosphates, is released. The product is an RNA chain extended by one ribonucleotide residue, with a new 3′ hydroxyl suitable for the next step. In symbolic form, RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi. This equation abstracts away proton transfers and metal coordination but correctly describes the principal substrates and products of chain extension.

Divalent metal ions, normally magnesium in physiological descriptions of RNA polymerase, facilitate catalysis. They help arrange the negatively charged reactive groups, stabilize developing charge in the reaction and assist the departure of pyrophosphate. Acidic residues of the polymerase coordinate metal ions within the active-center environment. The standard two-metal-ion picture is useful because it relates catalysis to electrostatics and geometry rather than attributing the reaction to vague enzymatic energy. The enzyme lowers kinetic barriers; it does not supply a new source of chemical free energy from nothing. Changing a metal-binding residue can therefore strongly impair polymerization even if the protein still associates with DNA.

The incoming triphosphate provides the chemical basis for the net energy change of incorporation. Hydrolysis of released pyrophosphate by cellular inorganic pyrophosphatase helps favor forward synthesis in vivo by decreasing the concentration of a reaction product. This is distinct from saying that RNA polymerase must hydrolyze a separate ATP molecule every time it moves a nucleotide. When a G is incorporated, the substrate is GTP; when a U is incorporated, the substrate is UTP. ATP is required as a substrate specifically where an adenine-containing RNA residue is incorporated. ATP may also be consumed by other proteins in transcription-associated reactions, but their hydrolysis is mechanistically separate from the chemical incorporation reaction.

Unlike DNA polymerase in ordinary chromosome replication, bacterial RNA polymerase can initiate a new chain without an existing nucleic-acid primer. Once a transcript has begun, however, an elongation reaction requires an available 3′ hydroxyl on the growing chain. This is a distinction between the requirements of de novo initiation and those of subsequent phosphodiester formation. A normal growing RNA with a free 3′ hydroxyl can accept another nucleotide; a chain whose end lacks a usable hydroxyl cannot undergo the same extension step. The absence of a primer requirement at the start does not mean phosphodiester bond formation can proceed in the absence of an appropriately positioned nucleophilic group.

RNA polymerase distinguishes ribonucleotides from deoxyribonucleotides and favors correct base pairing. Neither distinction is absolutely perfect. Wrong nucleotides may occasionally be incorporated. A mismatch can distort the geometry of the RNA–DNA hybrid or slow further extension. Some erroneous states are corrected through transcript cleavage, which removes one or more RNA residues and restores a productive 3′ end. Such proofreading does not use the same separate 3′ to 5′ exonuclease mechanism commonly taught for many DNA polymerases. The chemistry of transcription fidelity is integrated with the movement and possible backtracking of the elongation complex. For examination reasoning, distinguish a defective ability to choose a complementary substrate from a defective ability to recover after an incorrect substrate was accepted.

Several experiments can localize the cause of a catalytic defect. A mutant enzyme that binds DNA normally but produces almost no RNA might have a defect in substrate positioning or chemistry. An enzyme that adds a few nucleotides normally but frequently produces shortened products might instead have altered processivity or increased pausing. Measurements of substrate dependence, RNA length, DNA association and reaction time can distinguish these possibilities. No single readout automatically identifies the affected step.

## 5. Movement after chemistry: translocation and the next addition

Catalysis adds one nucleotide, but it does not by itself prepare the active center for every subsequent reaction. After incorporation, the newly extended RNA 3′ end must be repositioned relative to the next template base. Translocation is the change in relative position of the polymerase and nucleic acids that enables the next addition. In a simplified pre-translocated state, the most recently incorporated nucleotide occupies the position associated with the completed addition reaction. In a post-translocated state, the next template position is aligned for an incoming substrate. The enzyme can fluctuate between nearby positions. Correct substrate binding favors the productive state, and catalysis converts the substrate into a covalently incorporated residue.

The Brownian-ratchet description emphasizes that molecular movement involves thermal fluctuations rather than a large deterministic push that must occur at every step. Proteins and nucleic acids continually undergo thermal motion. A suitable arrangement of binding and chemical reactions biases these fluctuations toward net forward progress. When the next complementary nucleotide binds and is incorporated, a forward configuration becomes part of another catalytic cycle. This model explains why it is misleading to think of every nucleotide addition as requiring a separate mechanical ATPase that pulls the DNA through the enzyme.

The active center is surrounded by flexible protein elements, commonly described in structural accounts as the bridge helix and trigger loop. Their motions help coordinate substrate selection, catalysis and changes in active-center configuration. A student need not memorize individual atomic contacts to understand the principle. A correct nucleotide has to enter, be oriented, react and allow the enzyme to proceed. Conformational flexibility makes the sequence possible, while inappropriate conformations can prevent one of those stages. A mutation that changes the tendency of a flexible element to adopt a catalytic configuration may affect elongation speed and accuracy without directly changing its affinity for promoter DNA.

It is useful to distinguish the instantaneous rate of nucleotide addition at a productive site from the average velocity of a polymerase traveling along a gene. The former describes the chemistry and translocation cycle under particular local conditions. The latter includes pauses and other delays accumulated along the entire sequence. An enzyme might add successive nucleotides rapidly whenever active but have a low average velocity because it frequently pauses. A slower catalytic mutant and a pause-prone mutant may take similar amounts of time to reach a distant position but behave differently when observed at high temporal resolution or when assayed on a short, pause-free template.

Translocation also changes the positions of the RNA–DNA hybrid and upstream bubble boundary. As the next DNA base enters the decoding position, pairing must eventually be broken at the rear of the hybrid to allow the older RNA to leave. The RNA is not covalently attached to the DNA, so this separation requires disruption of noncovalent interactions rather than cleavage of a phosphodiester linkage. Simultaneously the DNA strands behind the active center can re-pair. This is an example of coordinated structural transitions: a single change in position connects substrate chemistry, hybrid renewal, strand opening and strand closure. It is the coordination, rather than four independent enzymes performing four unrelated steps, that permits processive transcription.

For a long transcript, the cost of each additional nucleotide is paid through its own substrate. The number of phosphodiester bonds in a newly made RNA of N residues is N minus one, because the first nucleotide begins the chain and every subsequent addition creates one new linkage. If the problem starts with an already existing RNA of length n and ends at N, the number of new covalent linkages is N minus n. Under the standard net-incorporation model without proofreading or abortive synthesis, this also equals the number of NTP substrates incorporated during that extension. Actual cellular consumption can be higher when nucleotides are incorporated and later removed or when initiation produces discarded short RNAs. The distinction between retained residues and total substrate turnover is important whenever a quantitative problem includes repeated failed events.
