# Eukaryotic RNA Polymerases and General Transcription Machinery

## 1. The biological problem: making several kinds of RNA from the same genome

Every nucleus contains a remarkable collection of DNA molecules, but a DNA molecule is not a working copy of every instruction it contains. A cell must select particular stretches of DNA, expose the relevant sequence, copy one strand into RNA, and eventually deliver the RNA to the cellular process that needs it. Consider a cell that is increasing in size. It needs new enzymes to make metabolites, membrane proteins to expand its surface, and thousands of additional ribosomes to support protein synthesis. Each of these needs requires transcription. However, the instructions for a protein and the instructions for a ribosomal RNA have different biological destinations. A protein-coding transcript is an intermediate whose nucleotide sequence will be read by a ribosome, whereas a ribosomal RNA becomes part of the ribosome itself. A transfer RNA does not supply a protein-coding message; it helps the translation machinery interpret one. This distinction between the products explains why it is useful for the nucleus to possess several specialized transcription systems.

The chemical reaction at the center of all these systems is strikingly similar. An RNA polymerase selects an incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate using a DNA template base, joins the nucleotide to the growing RNA, and moves on to another template position. Nevertheless, chemical competence does not explain how the polymerase finds the correct gene. If a polymerase started at arbitrary positions, most RNA molecules would be useless or misleading. Even when it started near an appropriate gene, the product might lack an essential first exon or contain unwanted upstream sequence. A transcription system therefore has two logically distinct responsibilities: specifying an appropriate starting region and carrying out RNA synthesis. Different proteins may be necessary for these responsibilities. Some recognize DNA or stabilize a recognition complex; others help expose the template; the polymerase supplies the catalytic center. These functions cooperate without being chemically identical.

In bacteria, a familiar way to divide these responsibilities is between the RNA polymerase core and its associated sigma factor. The core carries out nucleotide addition and sigma helps select appropriate promoters. The eukaryotic nucleus retains the principle of separating catalytic activity from selective initiation, but implements it in a different and more elaborate way. A typical animal or fungal cell has three distinct nuclear multisubunit RNA polymerases, conventionally numbered I, II and III. Each is associated with its own initiation machinery and transcribes a characteristic group of genes. These are not merely three interchangeable copies of a single enzyme. A mutation in the largest subunit of polymerase II may compromise synthesis of many messenger RNAs even though polymerase I is still producing a ribosomal precursor. Conversely, a defect in polymerase I may reduce ribosome production while the protein-coding genes initially remain capable of transcription.

A common beginner's mistake is to imagine that the polymerase can distinguish a messenger RNA from a ribosomal RNA by reading the meaning of the sequence. The polymerase does not interpret a codon, recognize the future catalytic function of a ribosomal RNA, or count whether the finished product will be translated. Its choice of transcription unit is largely determined by the DNA recognition and recruitment machinery that places it at that site. The consequence can be understood with a conceptual promoter-swap experiment. Imagine that a piece of DNA encoding a short RNA is placed downstream of a functioning polymerase II promoter. If the surrounding sequence and factors allow productive transcription, polymerase II can copy that DNA irrespective of the intended biological use of the RNA. The resulting transcript may differ in its start position, end, or processing from the native RNA. This is precisely why an RNA's biochemical identity is not, by itself, sufficient evidence of which enzyme synthesized it under an engineered condition.

The three nuclear systems also need to be distinguished from transcription in organelles. A mammalian mitochondrial genome has its own transcription apparatus, and photosynthetic eukaryotes have plastid transcription systems. These enzymes cannot be classified as nuclear polymerase I simply because they make ribosomal RNA inside an organelle. The correct classification depends on the enzyme, genomic compartment and biological context. Likewise, a plant can contain additional related nuclear polymerases specialized for particular RNA-mediated regulation. The standard I/II/III scheme is the starting point for nuclear transcription, not an assertion that no other RNA polymerase exists anywhere in a eukaryotic cell.

By the end of this chapter, three complementary questions should become easy to separate. Which polymerase makes the primary transcript of a specified nuclear gene? Which proteins allow that polymerase to be recruited and initiated correctly? Which subsequent biochemical changes connect RNA synthesis to the future fate of the transcript? Distinguishing these questions is particularly important when an examination describes an experiment in which a gene is still present and RNA polymerase is still detectable but mature RNA abundance falls. Such a result could originate in promoter recognition, catalysis, transcript processing or stability. Mechanistic understanding begins by identifying the stage actually tested.

## 2. Catalytic unity and structural specialization

A multisubunit polymerase is an enzyme assembled from several polypeptides, not a mixture of unrelated single-chain enzymes working independently. Its subunits create a coordinated three-dimensional structure. Some contribute directly to the catalytic region, others stabilize its shape, and still others provide interaction surfaces for DNA, RNA or partner proteins. The largest and second-largest subunits of the eukaryotic polymerases are homologous to one another across polymerase classes. They are also evolutionarily related to the major catalytic subunits of bacterial and archaeal RNA polymerases. Homology refers to shared evolutionary origin, inferred from sequence and structure; it does not imply that every regulatory interaction is identical. The conserved center and the divergent surfaces allow an ancient chemical mechanism to operate inside different cellular architectures.

To appreciate the molecular problem, consider a double-stranded DNA template. The two bases at any position normally interact with one another. A DNA-directed RNA polymerase cannot read a template base while that base remains fully paired and inaccessible. Within an active transcription complex a short region of the duplex is opened. One DNA strand reaches the catalytic center and the emerging RNA remains transiently paired to that strand near its growing end. As the complex advances, DNA can re-form behind it and RNA exits through a separate route. The enzyme is consequently more than a catalyst suspended beside a DNA molecule. Its geometry guides the entry and separation of polymers, stabilizes the appropriate nucleic acid configuration and limits access to inappropriate substrates.

For nucleotide addition, the reactive end of the RNA is its terminal 3′ hydroxyl group. The incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate has three phosphate groups. The phosphorus nearest the nucleotide sugar becomes part of the RNA backbone, and the other two leave together as pyrophosphate. Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium ions, participate in the catalytic center. They assist the chemical transformations by coordinating negatively charged groups and favoring productive positioning of reacting atoms. Template-directed base pairing contributes to selection of the correct nucleotide, but the polymerase also imposes geometrical constraints. A correctly paired nucleotide is more likely to adopt the productive arrangement than a mismatched one. The combination of sequence information, substrate chemistry and enzyme architecture accounts for ordered synthesis.

The reaction may be represented schematically as RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi. The symbol n denotes the number of nucleotide residues already present; NTP refers to ATP, CTP, GTP or UTP as selected by the DNA template; PPi is inorganic pyrophosphate. For an RNA of length 100 residues initiated de novo, there are 99 internucleotide phosphodiester linkages. The first initiating nucleotide retains its original triphosphate at the 5′ end in the unprocessed primary transcript. Each subsequent addition produces a pyrophosphate molecule, so idealized production of a single such transcript involves 99 polymerization-associated PPi molecules. Subsequent modification of the 5′ end or hydrolysis of released PPi is chemically separate from this count. The equation is a model for net nucleotide incorporation; it does not enumerate additional ATP hydrolysis required by accessory proteins during promoter opening.

The common two-metal catalytic principle is not the same as the ATP-dependent action of a separate motor. An RNA polymerase can consume ATP as a building block when the next template base requires adenine in RNA, whereas an ATP-dependent translocase can hydrolyze ATP to generate mechanical changes without adding adenine to the transcript. A kinase can transfer the terminal phosphate of ATP to a protein without moving along DNA. These distinct uses of ATP coexist in transcription, and examination statements that treat them as one process obscure the underlying chemistry. A change in ATP concentration can therefore affect multiple reactions simultaneously. To attribute the outcome to a specific protein, the experimental design must discriminate between substrate incorporation, motor activity and phosphorylation.

The catalytic components are also subject to quality control. An incoming nucleotide must be selected and the enzyme must proceed along the DNA. Misincorporation can interfere with further extension, sometimes allowing a polymerase to reverse its position along the nucleic acid and cleave the erroneous RNA segment before resuming. The general principle is relevant to the common ancestry of RNA polymerases, but detailed elongation factors and pause recovery are not necessary to distinguish polymerases I, II and III at initiation. Importantly, RNA synthesis is not DNA replication. A transcription error normally changes one RNA molecule rather than permanently changing a genomic sequence that will be inherited by daughter cells. This explains why both processes require selectivity but are not governed by identical fidelity mechanisms.

A typical mammalian Pol II contains twelve core subunits. The two largest provide much of the conserved catalytic architecture. Several smaller subunits are shared with Pol I and Pol III, whereas others or their related counterparts are characteristic of one enzyme. In a textbook comparison, the exact total number should be tied to the organism and definition of the enzyme complex. For example, yeast Pol I and Pol III are commonly described as containing fourteen and seventeen subunits respectively, and mammalian descriptions of their complexes may use slightly different conventions. Counting general transcription factors as permanent polymerase subunits would artificially increase the number. The functional distinction is therefore more reliable than a memorized number taken out of context: core subunits are integral parts of the enzyme; general initiation factors interact with that enzyme as part of an initiation apparatus.

Specialized subunits of Pol I and Pol III illustrate the modular nature of evolution. Functions that Pol II carries out with separately recruited factors can have counterparts incorporated more permanently into the other polymerases. This does not mean that the Pol I or Pol III enzyme is independent of initiation factors. Rather, the distribution of functional modules differs. The arrangement changes how partners are recruited, how efficiently an enzyme returns to transcription, and how individual genes respond to perturbations. For NET and SET reasoning, a mutation in a component shared by several enzymes can plausibly affect more than one class of transcript, whereas a mutation in an enzyme-specific partner can yield a narrower defect. Even these predictions require experimental confirmation because cells may alter RNA degradation and compensate for synthesis defects.

## 3. How biochemical experiments revealed multiple polymerases

Before enzymes can be assigned distinct biological roles, their physical existence must be established. A crude cell extract may synthesize RNA when supplied with DNA and nucleotides. That observation demonstrates RNA-synthesizing activity but does not determine how many molecular species are responsible. Three different enzymes that catalyze the same reaction can all contribute to a single bulk measurement. Conversely, different activity levels measured under different salt conditions do not by themselves demonstrate distinct enzymes, because one enzyme can have a salt-dependent rate. The discovery of multiple nuclear RNA polymerases therefore required a way of separating catalytic activities and asking whether they retained distinct characteristics after separation.

A foundational study by Robert Roeder and William Rutter in 1969 reported multiple DNA-dependent RNA polymerase forms in eukaryotic organisms. Consider the experimental logic underlying chromatographic separation. A mixture of proteins is passed through a material with which different proteins interact to different extents. As the conditions are changed, individual protein populations emerge in different fractions. Fractions can then be assayed for RNA synthesis using an appropriate DNA template and nucleotide substrates. If several reproducible peaks of catalytic activity emerge at distinct positions, the result supports the existence of physically distinguishable active populations. Additional experiments are required to decide whether the populations are different enzymes, differently modified forms of one enzyme, or an enzyme associated with different partners.

The distinction between activity and abundance is crucial. An assay may record incorporated radiolabeled nucleotide, which provides a measure of synthesis under the chosen reaction conditions. The fraction with the greatest incorporation is not necessarily the fraction containing the most enzyme molecules. It could contain an enzyme with a higher catalytic rate on the supplied template, better substrate access, or fewer inhibitors. To compare catalytic activity per molecule, the investigator must know or estimate the active enzyme concentration. Equally, the area under a chromatographic peak integrates measured activity across fractions, whereas the height reflects the maximum activity in one fraction. Peak height and total recovered activity answer different questions.

Subcellular fractionation supplies a second line of evidence. The nucleus contains an organized nucleolus associated with ribosome production and a surrounding nucleoplasm. If a polymerase activity is enriched in a nucleolar preparation while another activity is enriched outside it, that observation suggests functional specialization. However, enrichment is not absolute proof of localization. The compartments can contaminate one another during preparation, and proteins can dissociate or redistribute when cells are disrupted. A reliable conclusion combines biochemical separation with independent indicators of compartment identity and with evidence concerning the RNA products synthesized in each region. The historical distinction between the nucleolar Pol I and the other nuclear polymerases provided a particularly powerful clue when interpreted alongside RNA synthesis experiments.

An inhibitor can provide an independent chemical fingerprint. In 1970, Lindell and colleagues reported that alpha-amanitin strongly inhibits the nucleoplasmic polymerase II from several animal sources, unlike the nucleolar polymerase I under their tested conditions. Later studies identified the characteristic response of Pol III to higher concentrations in animal systems and used that distinction to connect Pol III with tRNA and 5S rRNA synthesis. These results are important not because one can attach a single universal inhibitory concentration to each enzyme, but because separable enzyme activities responded differently to the same molecule. Sensitivity patterns, physical separation and transcript identity are mutually reinforcing forms of evidence.

There are several ways an experiment could mislead. If the inhibitor reduced all RNA synthesis by destroying the DNA template, it would not be a useful discriminator among polymerase activities. If all nucleotide incorporation were measured in an intact cell over a prolonged period, a reduction in one RNA class might result from secondary depletion of essential proteins rather than immediate direct inhibition of its polymerase. An early-time measurement of nascent transcription, a purified enzyme assay or an appropriate nuclear transcription assay can help distinguish direct and indirect effects. Controls without inhibitor establish baseline activity; a solvent control rules out effects of the carrier; comparable substrates and conditions allow meaningful differences to be interpreted.

Historical biochemical discoveries are sometimes summarized as if one chromatographic experiment immediately revealed which RNA each polymerase made. The more instructive view is a sequence of inferences. First establish that multiple catalytic populations can be separated. Then determine their distinctive properties and localizations. Next associate each population with transcripts produced from particular genes. Finally identify the factors and promoter elements that explain selective recruitment. Each stage answers a different question. A modern experimental problem can recreate this logic without requiring knowledge of every historical apparatus. When given three unknown enzyme fractions, a student should seek independent tests that identify the catalytic populations rather than assign them solely from the order in which they emerged.

## 4. The functional map of nuclear RNA polymerases

RNA polymerase I is principally responsible for synthesis of the large precursor from which most structural ribosomal RNAs are generated. In mammalian cells the primary precursor is commonly described as 47S pre-rRNA, which undergoes processing through intermediates including 45S pre-rRNA. It contains the sequences that become 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNA. These RNAs are separated from one another and from spacer regions through maturation. Budding yeast uses a different conventional precursor designation, 35S, and has 25S rather than mammalian 28S as its large-subunit counterpart. The names reflect sedimentation properties rather than nucleotide counts. A precursor called 47S is not simply a numerical sum of the sedimentation coefficients of its three final components.

The ribosomal RNAs have different positions within the finished ribosome. The 18S RNA is a structural and functional component of the small eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomal subunit, conventionally designated 40S. The 28S and 5.8S RNAs, along with 5S rRNA, occur in the large cytosolic subunit, conventionally designated 60S. Assembly of these subunits ultimately supports the 80S ribosome. The sedimentation coefficient depends on particle mass, shape and interactions with the solvent; the values are therefore not additive in the manner of masses. Students should not infer that a missing 5S RNA changes an 80S ribosome into a 75S particle by subtraction. The important point in this chapter is that the ribosome depends on RNA products made by two different nuclear polymerases.

RNA polymerase II produces the primary transcripts of essentially all ordinary nuclear protein-coding genes. In many cases the transcript is initially a pre-mRNA containing introns and sequences that will be modified or removed. Even the absence of introns does not make a gene a Pol I or Pol III target. Polymerase II also synthesizes numerous non-protein-coding transcripts, including many small nuclear RNAs, many primary microRNA transcripts and long noncoding RNAs. These RNA categories have varied biological functions. In this chapter their relevance is that the statement “Pol II makes mRNA” is a useful first approximation but an incomplete classification rule. A typical noncoding RNA cannot be assigned to Pol III simply because it is not translated.

RNA polymerase III synthesizes nuclear tRNAs, 5S rRNA and several small noncoding RNAs. Examples include U6 small nuclear RNA and, in mammals, the RNA component of the signal recognition particle, 7SL RNA. Although many Pol III products are short, length alone is not a reliable criterion. Some short nuclear RNAs are made by Pol II; a length measurement cannot substitute for promoter and enzyme information. The major practical exception to the shorthand statement that Pol I makes ribosomal RNA is 5S rRNA, which is conventionally transcribed separately by Pol III. Its eventual incorporation into the large ribosomal subunit does not change its transcriptional origin.

The cooperation among these systems can be illustrated with the demand for new ribosomes. Pol I supplies the major precursor rRNA, Pol II supplies messenger RNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and many assembly factors, and Pol III supplies 5S rRNA. tRNAs made by Pol III support the translation needed to produce those proteins. A cell with normal Pol I transcription and defective Pol III transcription could therefore have an abundance of major pre-rRNA without being able to assemble normal numbers of functional ribosomes. A cell with normal major rRNA transcription but reduced synthesis of ribosomal proteins can face a similar imbalance. This network of dependencies explains why a biochemical deficit in one system can eventually alter outputs of the others.

One must also distinguish the enzyme that synthesizes a precursor from the enzymes that produce its mature ends. Pol I is the transcription enzyme for the large rRNA precursor, not the nuclease that separates 18S and 28S sequences. Pol III is responsible for precursor tRNA synthesis, but maturation can involve cleavage, base modification and addition of particular nucleotides by other enzymes. Pol II can recruit proteins that perform RNA processing without itself being a capping enzyme or a spliceosome. Transcriptional origin and maturation mechanism are related through the organization of the nucleus, but they describe different biochemical reactions. These distinctions establish a foundation for later study of RNA maturation.

A useful mental experiment is to imagine a short labeling pulse administered just after selective interruption of a nuclear polymerase. The nascent RNAs directly dependent on that enzyme should respond first if the interruption is rapid and specific. Previously made mature RNA can remain in the cell for a considerable time. A stable mature rRNA pool may consequently appear largely unchanged even while production of its precursor has stopped. Conversely, a short-lived transcript may decline quickly. The duration of labeling and whether the assay measures nascent precursor or mature accumulated RNA can change the apparent result. Thus, a question asking for the immediate effect of enzyme inhibition should not be answered by assuming that every mature product disappears instantaneously.
