## 12. RNA polymerase I and the organization of ribosomal gene transcription

A typical rapidly growing animal cell must make many ribosomes, and each new ribosome contains several ribosomal RNA molecules. The demand for the major rRNAs is unusually high relative to that for the product of most individual protein-coding genes. Nuclear genomes meet this demand partly through repeated ribosomal DNA transcription units. In humans, arrays of these units occur at nucleolar organizer regions on several chromosomes. The active units provide templates for synthesis of the large precursor RNA. The nucleolus is organized in association with ribosomal gene transcription, RNA maturation and ribosomal subunit assembly. It is not surrounded by a lipid membrane like an organelle of the endomembrane system. Its organization instead reflects the concentration and interactions of macromolecules participating in these activities.

A ribosomal DNA repeat is not equivalent to a single mature rRNA sequence. A repeat contains a transcription unit encoding multiple rRNA regions and spacers as well as sequences between transcription units. The primary transcript can include external and internal transcribed spacer segments that must be removed. A single productive transcription event can therefore supply one sequence each for several distinct mature rRNAs. This arrangement differs from a typical protein-coding gene whose primary transcript supplies the information for a particular polypeptide or related alternative products. Understanding the structure of the ribosomal transcription unit is necessary for interpreting both RNA measurements and promoter mutations. A probe within a rapidly removed spacer can report precursor abundance, whereas a probe recognizing the sequence retained in mature 18S RNA can detect a much larger accumulated pool.

Mammalian Pol I promoter recognition involves a core promoter and an upstream control region in a commonly studied rDNA architecture. The precise sequences and functional organization differ among organisms. A characteristic mammalian factor is selectivity factor 1, SL1, which contains TBP and Pol I-specific associated proteins. Its interactions help establish accurate initiation and provide a framework for recruitment of the polymerase machinery. Another factor, UBF, binds rDNA and contributes to organization and activity of transcriptionally competent promoter regions. UBF can influence the shape and accessibility of DNA; it does not replace the catalytic activity of Pol I. A third important functional connection is supplied by RRN3, also called TIF-IA in mammalian nomenclature, which associates with an initiation-competent form of polymerase I and helps connect the enzyme to the promoter-associated apparatus.

This is not simply the Pol II complex with the Roman numeral changed. Although TBP is present in SL1 and TFIID, the associated proteins and the DNA recognition environment differ. The fact that both factors contain TBP cannot establish that the classical Pol II TATA-box geometry is the mechanism at a mammalian rDNA promoter. SL1 contributes promoter recognition in cooperation with other Pol I machinery, whereas TFIID supports assembly at Pol II promoters. Similarly, RRN3 is not a substitute name for TFIIB; it provides a connection important for the Pol I system, while TFIIB participates in Pol II initiation. Functional analogies can be helpful when learning, but a shared general task does not mean the factors are identical or directly interchangeable.

A standard reconstitution question could present a mammalian rDNA promoter and a set of purified proteins. Pol I supplied alone may retain chemical activity on a permissive artificial template yet fail to produce correctly initiated RNA on the natural promoter. Adding promoter-associated factors and an initiation-competent polymerase complex can restore specificity. If selective removal of SL1 lowers accurate promoter-directed transcription, while addition of a competent SL1 preparation restores it, the experiment supports a role for SL1 in the initiation apparatus. It does not show that SL1 itself synthesizes the phosphodiester backbone. To establish that the catalytic function remains intact, the investigator can test the polymerase on a substrate that does not demand the same promoter recognition machinery.

Species-specific differences provide another application of this reasoning. Much of the catalytic chemistry is conserved, but the promoter sequences and factor interactions can evolve. A transcription factor mixture from one organism may fail to initiate on a promoter from another species even when each system works with its own promoter. A carefully designed reciprocal experiment would include both homologous combinations and the two heterologous combinations, using comparable activity and template controls. Failure of both cross-combinations suggests a compatibility constraint. If replacing one component restores accurate transcription, that factor becomes a candidate determinant of specificity. The result would not imply that the polymerase chemistry differs fundamentally between the species. It would indicate that recognition or recruitment interfaces are not fully interchangeable.

The Pol I machinery must also accommodate variable cellular demands for ribosome production. A cell that is growing and synthesizing protein rapidly benefits from abundant rRNA, whereas a nutrient-deprived cell may need to conserve resources. Changes in the fraction of active ribosomal genes, the availability of initiation-competent Pol I, or the frequency of productive transcription can all affect output. A statement that a cell has many rDNA copies does not establish that all copies are simultaneously transcribed. Copy number describes the number of potential templates; active template number depends on the state of the cell and accessibility of the transcription units. This distinction is useful when comparing genomes, cell types or environmental conditions, and it also links the chapter to later study of gene-expression regulation.

For an idealized calculation, suppose a nucleus contains 200 identical rDNA transcription units and 80 are active. If each active unit completes an average of 0.75 precursor transcripts per minute, total precursor production is 80 times 0.75, or 60 molecules per minute. If each precursor is successfully matured into one copy of each of its three major rRNAs, the idealized output is 60 molecules per minute of each type. This would correspond to at most 60 sets of major rRNAs per minute from this source. The number is not 180 complete ribosomes per minute simply because 180 individual rRNA molecules are produced. Each ribosome requires a particular combination of RNA molecules, ribosomal proteins and 5S rRNA supplied separately. Processing losses and limitations in other components can reduce the final number. The calculation connects the stoichiometry of a precursor to its biological function.

A decrease in nucleolar size or change in nucleolar appearance after inhibition of Pol I can be biologically informative, but morphology is an indirect readout. Nucleolar organization depends on multiple ongoing processes, including synthesis and maturation of RNA. An altered nucleolus does not, by itself, identify the defective factor. Combining morphology with direct measurements of nascent pre-rRNA and protein localization gives a stronger interpretation. Similarly, high steady-state rRNA abundance is compatible with either high production or persistence of a previously synthesized pool. The most informative readout for an immediate transcriptional defect is often the newly synthesized precursor rather than total mature rRNA.

## 13. RNA polymerase III: promoter diversity and a shared assembly principle

Pol III offers a particularly instructive example of promoter diversity because some of its best-known gene classes contain transcription control elements within the DNA that will be transcribed. This arrangement appears surprising only if a promoter has been defined exclusively as upstream DNA that is never copied. A control region is functional because proteins recognize it and organize the initiation machinery, regardless of whether the sequence is subsequently incorporated into RNA. The cell can exploit a DNA segment for recognition while also using its nucleotide information in a functional RNA product. Such overlapping requirements can constrain the evolutionary changes that are tolerated in the sequence.

The common feature of several Pol III initiation pathways is recruitment of TFIIIB, a factor containing TBP and additional Pol III-associated proteins. In the commonly described tRNA and 5S systems, TFIIIB includes Brf1 and Bdp1 alongside TBP. TFIIIB is positioned upstream of the initiation region and supports recruitment and productive initiation by Pol III. Other factors help establish its position. The division of responsibilities is important: an internal DNA-binding factor can recognize a sequence downstream of the start, yet the polymerase is ultimately recruited to begin upstream of that internal recognition site. There is no contradiction between these observations. Protein interactions bridge the spatial separation between the recognition elements and the location where catalysis begins.

Three conventional promoter classes illustrate alternative solutions to this recruitment problem. Type 1 promoters are represented by 5S rRNA genes. Their internal control region is recognized by TFIIIA, a gene-class-specific DNA-binding factor. TFIIIA helps recruit TFIIIC, which in turn contributes to positioning TFIIIB upstream of the initiation region. The resulting assembly permits Pol III recruitment and transcription. TFIIIA is not the enzyme that transcribes 5S DNA, and it is not a universal initiation factor for every Pol III gene. Its characteristic association with the 5S promoter class makes it a particularly useful component in experiments designed to distinguish 5S transcription from tRNA transcription.

The name TFIIIA is potentially confusing for a student who has just studied TFIIA. The extra Roman numeral I belongs to the designation of the polymerase system. TFIIA and TFIIIA are not equivalent proteins with a single amino acid change. The first is a Pol II general transcription factor; the second is classically associated with recognition of the 5S gene internal control region in the Pol III system. Similarly, TFIIIB and TFIIB refer to different factors, although Brf1 and certain other components of Pol III machinery have evolutionary or structural relationships to Pol II counterparts. Examination questions frequently test whether the student can read the nomenclature while retaining a clear functional model.

Type 2 promoters are classically represented by tRNA genes. Two internal elements, conventionally designated the A box and B box, provide recognition sites for TFIIIC. Through interactions with other components, TFIIIC helps place TFIIIB upstream of the transcription start. Unlike the 5S pathway, ordinary type 2 tRNA initiation does not require TFIIIA as the specialized internal recognition factor. The A and B boxes are embedded in sequences copied into precursor tRNA. Some of these sequences also contribute to the structure or function of the eventual tRNA. A mutation can therefore have two distinct consequences: reduced transcription because the DNA recognition site is altered, and defective tRNA function because the RNA sequence itself has changed. Which consequence dominates is an empirical question.

Consider a conceptual mutation in a tRNA gene's B box. If the mutation impairs recognition by TFIIIC, TFIIIB positioning and polymerase recruitment may decline. A transcription assay measuring newly produced precursor tRNA could detect this defect. Suppose instead that precursor synthesis remains normal, but the mature tRNA cannot support an expected translational activity. The result would favor a problem in RNA maturation, folding, recognition or another downstream function. The two effects are not mutually exclusive. Because the same DNA segment contributes both to initiation and to RNA sequence, an informative analysis measures transcription separately from the behavior of the resulting RNA. This is an example of why gene architecture matters when designing and interpreting mutations.

Type 3 promoters, represented by mammalian U6 small nuclear RNA genes, use primarily upstream promoter elements rather than the conventional internal A and B boxes. The proximal sequence element and associated recognition complex SNAPc, together with a TATA-containing region in representative vertebrate U6 promoters, help position the relevant machinery. A form of TFIIIB containing Brf2, rather than Brf1, is characteristic of this pathway in mammals. The resulting complex recruits Pol III. This example establishes two important exceptions: not all Pol III promoters are internal, and not all Pol III transcripts arise using the same versions of the associated factors. It also shows why the presence of a TATA motif cannot be used as a unique diagnostic marker for Pol II.

The U6 example connects the three nuclear systems through RNA processing. U6 participates in the spliceosome, while several other major spliceosomal small nuclear RNAs, such as U1 and U2 in the conventional animal model, are transcribed by Pol II. The mature components cooperate in one molecular apparatus even though their transcriptional origins differ. The fact that an RNA participates in splicing does not determine which nuclear polymerase made it. The correct inference follows from the gene and its transcription machinery. Such examples are particularly valuable in examinations that ask for an exception to a broad statement or require matching a set of RNAs with the appropriate polymerases.

The downstream end of a Pol III transcription unit can often be defined by a short thymidine tract on the non-template DNA strand. Copying the corresponding adenine residues on the template produces a uridine-rich RNA end. The termination details belong to the chapter on elongation and termination, but the sequence relationship is worth distinguishing now from the internal promoter. A T-rich termination tract is a different functional element from an A or B box, and neither should be confused with a classical Pol II TATA box. A mutation in the internal promoter may alter the frequency of initiation, whereas a mutation in a termination region can change the length or end position of transcripts. RNA size and quantity together are more informative than either alone.

A comparative depletion experiment makes the shared and specialized factors clear. A preparation capable of initiating transcription from 5S, tRNA and U6 templates is divided into several reactions. Depletion of TFIIIA is expected to strongly affect the 5S system without necessarily eliminating ordinary tRNA transcription. Depletion of TFIIIC is expected to compromise canonical 5S and tRNA pathways, whereas the upstream U6 pathway can use different recognition machinery. Disruption of a component common to appropriate TFIIIB complexes can affect a broader range of Pol III promoters. In each case the inference assumes that removal is selective, that the depleted factor is not indirectly carrying away another component, and that the tested templates use the representative mechanisms described. Re-addition experiments and direct analysis of the remaining complexes are important controls.

A protein called TFIIIC can be present on certain DNA regions without productive Pol III transcription occurring there. The presence of a recognition factor is evidence of an interaction, not necessarily evidence of initiation. The same general principle was established for Pol II occupancy and applies equally here. To demonstrate a functioning transcription unit, it is necessary to establish the appropriate relationship between occupancy, initiation and transcript formation. A cell may also contain several copies of a tRNA gene family, so loss of one copy does not necessarily eliminate every tRNA with the same anticodon. Redundancy and compensatory changes must be distinguished from the molecular effect on the particular promoter being examined.

## 14. Selective inhibitors as evidence for enzyme identity

Alpha-amanitin is an especially important classical biochemical tool for distinguishing nuclear RNA polymerases. Under standard textbook conditions for many mammalian or other animal polymerase preparations, Pol II is highly sensitive, Pol III is inhibited at substantially higher concentrations, and Pol I is relatively resistant over the tested range. The relationship is comparative, not an absolute declaration that all molecules of one class are inhibited at one universal threshold. Polymerases of different organisms and variants of the same enzyme can respond differently. A dose-response experiment should therefore specify the organism, enzyme preparation, concentration range, time and measured outcome. General inhibitor mechanisms and comparisons with bacterial transcription will be developed in the later inhibitors chapter; the present purpose is polymerase identification.

One can derive a simple framework for analysis of an enzyme activity assay. Let V0 be the measured rate without inhibitor and Vc the measured rate at a specified concentration c. If the control is nonzero, relative activity is Vc/V0, and percentage inhibition is 100 times the quantity 1 minus Vc/V0. These expressions normalize results to each enzyme's own baseline. Suppose an untreated preparation gives 240 incorporation units per minute and the treated preparation gives 60 units per minute. The relative activity is 60/240 = 0.25, and inhibition is 75 percent. A second fraction with baseline 40 units and treated activity 30 units is inhibited by only 25 percent even though its absolute treated activity is lower. The comparison should use normalized activity, not simply the smallest final signal.

Consider a hypothetical dataset with three separated animal nuclear polymerase fractions A, B and C, all normalized to 100 percent activity without inhibitor. At a low alpha-amanitin concentration, their respective activities are 96, 8 and 91 percent. At a much higher concentration, the activities are 94, 2 and 12 percent. Fraction B has the characteristic highly sensitive response of Pol II. Fraction C resembles Pol III, because it remains largely active at the low concentration but is strongly inhibited at the higher concentration. Fraction A is the likely Pol I activity because it remains high over the tested range. These labels are reasonable in the stated context, not independent proof of the assignments. Identifying the proteins in each fraction or demonstrating their activity on characteristic promoters would strengthen the conclusion.

A mixture illustrates how the same logic can be used quantitatively. Suppose total incorporation in a crude extract is 100 arbitrary units, falling to 65 units after a low concentration of inhibitor and to 25 units after a higher concentration. Under the deliberately idealized assumptions that low concentration completely inhibits Pol II only, high concentration completely inhibits Pol II and Pol III only, Pol I is unaffected, and activities add independently, the low-dose loss is 35 units attributable to Pol II. The additional loss between low and high concentration is 40 units attributable to Pol III. The remaining 25 units are assigned to Pol I. These results are contributions to the measured activity, not necessarily proportions of polymerase molecules. Different enzymes can have different rates per molecule on the assay template.

Real inhibition curves are seldom perfect step functions. If the low-dose condition leaves 10 percent of Pol II activity and modestly reduces Pol III activity, the subtraction procedure above no longer yields exact class contributions. A more accurate analysis would use measured sensitivity coefficients for each purified enzyme under identical assay conditions. Each observed total would then be modeled as a weighted sum of the underlying activities, with the weights corresponding to residual fractions at each concentration. The central principle is that one measurement generally cannot determine several unknown contributions without further constraints. The simplified problem is useful because the assumptions make the calculation identifiable; in a real experiment, the assumptions must be tested rather than silently adopted.

The concentration that produces a 50 percent reduction in a defined activity is often described as an IC50. It is not automatically the same as the equilibrium dissociation constant of an inhibitor for an enzyme. Activity depends on substrate concentrations, incubation duration, inhibitor binding mechanism and other experimental conditions. A concentration-response curve also does not establish that inhibition is competitive with nucleotide substrates. The molecular interaction of alpha-amanitin with Pol II interferes with efficient transcriptional progression, and the characteristic response is used here as a marker of enzyme identity. It should not be classified as a reagent that simply removes the TATA box, prevents TBP recognition, or directly blocks ribosome translation.

A final distinction concerns direct and downstream effects. If Pol II transcription is strongly inhibited in a cell, production of new mRNAs encoding many proteins can decline. Over time, depletion of these proteins can affect cell metabolism, rRNA synthesis and tRNA use. A prolonged decline in a Pol I product does not establish direct inhibition of Pol I by the original reagent. An acute purified enzyme assay addresses the immediate sensitivity question, whereas a time course in cells includes additional biological dependencies. Students should pay close attention to the duration and system described in an examination rather than apply a classification table without considering experimental context.

## 15. Integrating the systems: transcription, processing and experimental interpretation

The shared features of the three nuclear systems can now be described in a causal sequence. Each contains an enzyme capable of DNA-directed RNA synthesis, a way to recognize appropriate transcription units, and a mechanism that brings the enzyme into a productive relationship with the template. In each case the polymerase's catalytic center supplies nucleotide incorporation, while associated factors determine many aspects of initiation. The systems differ in their typical products, promoter structures, partner proteins, localization and distinctive modes of coupling transcription to subsequent events. The distinction is one of organization around a conserved biochemical task, not three unrelated forms of nucleic acid chemistry.

The collaboration among polymerases is especially evident when considering cellular protein synthesis. The small and large ribosomal subunits contain products derived from the major Pol I precursor, a Pol III-derived 5S component and proteins whose mRNAs are supplied by Pol II. Pol III also supplies tRNAs needed for decoding those mRNAs. Thus, reducing one pathway can limit the performance of a process that requires products from all three. Consider a simplified assembly model in which 100 sets of major rRNAs, 80 copies of 5S rRNA and enough proteins for 90 ribosomes become available in an interval. Assuming one complete set of each category is required per new ribosome and no pre-existing surplus, the theoretical maximum is the minimum of 100, 80 and 90, which is 80 ribosomes. Increasing Pol I output alone would not increase that maximum unless the limiting 5S supply were improved. This stoichiometric model does not describe every regulatory response, but it clarifies the idea of a limiting component in a multi-pathway system.

An equally important integration concerns measurement of RNA abundance. In a simple model with a constant rate s of RNA synthesis, a first-order degradation constant d and RNA abundance R, the rate of change is s minus dR. At steady state the two rates are equal, and R equals s/d. If the initial synthesis rate is 200 molecules per hour and the degradation constant is 0.5 per hour, the predicted steady abundance is 400 molecules. A mutant with synthesis rate 100 molecules per hour and degradation constant 0.25 per hour also has a predicted steady abundance of 400 molecules. Equal mature RNA levels therefore do not imply equal transcription rates. The model assumes a single pool, linear removal, constant rates and no other fluxes. Actual RNA maturation may require separate precursor and mature pools, but the simple calculation is enough to expose a major interpretive trap.

The most useful examination strategy is to associate each experimental measurement with the biochemical state it measures. A footprint measures DNA protection and can suggest binding. An assay detecting exposed DNA bases reports opening under its particular conditions. An RNA incorporation measurement reports synthesis, while mapping primary 5′ ends can report where initiation occurred. A processing-specific assay addresses maturation, and a mature RNA measurement reflects synthesis, processing and removal together. No single readout automatically proves all the preceding events. If two experiments appear contradictory, ask whether they measure different states. The apparent contradiction between high promoter occupancy and low RNA output is a particularly important example.

A reconstitution experiment establishes sufficiency under specified conditions, whereas selective depletion or mutation can test requirement. Reconstitution with a defined set of proteins and a natural promoter is stronger evidence of the capacity of those proteins to support initiation than a binding assay alone. A negative result after removing a factor needs controls for depletion specificity and structural integrity of the remaining machinery. Restoring activity by adding back the factor provides additional support. A mutation that changes a promoter contact while leaving protein stability unchanged is often more mechanistically informative than deletion of an entire protein. Nonetheless, no one experiment settles every question. Different observations must be interpreted together to distinguish direct action, dependence on other factors and indirect consequences.

The general factors do not define the complete explanation of tissue-specific gene expression. A liver cell and a neuron possess much of the same basic Pol II transcription machinery, yet they express different groups of genes. The difference cannot be explained merely by assigning one cell Pol I and the other Pol II. Both require all three major nuclear systems. Gene-specific regulators and the state of chromatin influence where and when the general machinery can be recruited. This is why the next chapter will build on the present molecular vocabulary to explain how activators and repressors affect initiation. The subsequent chapter will follow polymerases after initiation. By keeping these levels distinct, students can reason from a change in one protein or DNA element to a precise prediction rather than memorizing a large, undifferentiated list of transcription factors.
