# Non-coding RNAs: Classes and Biological Functions

## 1. An RNA molecule can be the final functional product of a gene

A gene is not synonymous with a protein blueprint. A stretch of DNA may be transcribed into an RNA that becomes a component of cellular machinery without being translated into a functional polypeptide. This principle is familiar from transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA, but its significance becomes much clearer when we consider regulatory RNAs. In a cell containing many transcripts, a particular RNA can recognize another RNA and alter how much protein that second molecule produces. A different RNA can help a protein locate a chromosome. Yet another RNA can provide an organized molecular surface on which several proteins assemble. These activities are possible because the chemical properties of RNA allow it to carry sequence information and to adopt structures. A protein need not be synthesized from the RNA for the sequence to have a biological consequence.

Consider two DNA loci. Transcription of the first produces a messenger RNA containing a region that is read by a ribosome to form a chain of amino acids. Transcription of the second produces a precursor that is cut into a short RNA and incorporated into a regulatory protein complex. The short RNA guides that complex to the messenger RNA of the first locus. Both loci are transcribed. Only the first is required to make a protein product in this example. The functional relationship between them is a relationship between the products of two genes, rather than a direct physical interaction between their DNA sequences. If the second locus is deleted, the amount of protein encoded by the first may change even when the first gene has an entirely normal coding sequence. A mutation in a noncoding gene can therefore have a strong phenotype without changing the amino acid sequence of any protein directly.

The expression of a protein-coding gene involves several distinguishable variables. Transcription determines the rate of production of new RNA. Processing and transport determine which mature molecules reach the appropriate cellular compartment. RNA degradation determines the duration for which those molecules remain available, and translation determines the rate at which a surviving molecule produces protein. Protein turnover supplies an additional layer of control. A regulatory RNA can influence one or more of these variables. Consequently, measuring the amount of DNA, messenger RNA or protein alone is rarely sufficient to locate the point at which regulation acts. If a mature messenger RNA is equally abundant in two cell types but its protein product is not, a difference in translation or protein stability is a plausible explanation. If the RNA itself is depleted, its transcription or stability may have changed. These are alternative hypotheses that must be distinguished experimentally.

A non-coding RNA, abbreviated ncRNA, is an RNA whose recognized functional product is the RNA molecule rather than a translated protein. This definition is concerned with biological function. It does not mean that the nucleotide sequence contains no AUG, no triplets resembling codons or no open reading frame. Random nucleotide sequences frequently contain short open reading frames. Some transcripts annotated as noncoding have subsequently been shown to encode short biologically active peptides. Other transcripts can possess both RNA-level and peptide-level functions. Such discoveries revise an annotation instead of invalidating the distinction between coding and noncoding functions. The appropriate question is what molecular product accounts for the experimentally observed activity.

A messenger RNA also contains regions that do not encode amino acids. Its untranslated regions may bind regulators, influence localization or control translation efficiency. These regions are part of a coding transcript, not independent noncoding RNA genes simply because they remain untranslated. A regulatory RNA that binds a site within the three-prime untranslated region can itself be a noncoding RNA, whereas the bound region is a regulatory element within an mRNA. A student should distinguish the classification of a complete transcript from the classification of a segment of that transcript. An intron, a promoter and an untranslated region are defined by different relationships to transcription and translation; they are not interchangeable names for noncoding RNAs.

A second distinction separates noncoding DNA from noncoding RNA. A noncoding DNA region may contain regulatory DNA sequences, repeated DNA, an intron, an ncRNA gene or a region with no established function. The existence of such a DNA region does not demonstrate that a stable RNA is produced. Even when a sequence is transcribed, the resulting RNA may be rapidly degraded or may have no demonstrated biological role. Inference from transcription to function is therefore not automatic. A phenotype that follows alteration of a DNA region can reflect a change in a DNA regulatory element, the act of transcription or a functional RNA product. These possibilities are especially important for long noncoding RNAs.

The four principal regulatory categories in this chapter are microRNAs, small interfering RNAs, PIWI-interacting RNAs and long noncoding RNAs. Their names refer to different properties. The first two describe related classes of short guide RNAs distinguished principally by precursor origin and biological context. The third refers to a class of guide RNAs associated with a particular protein subfamily. The fourth defines a heterogeneous set of longer transcripts. No single length, enzyme or cellular location is enough to classify all RNA molecules. A sound classification combines several observations and asks whether they agree with a known mode of biogenesis and function.

## 2. RNA sequence, structure and molecular recognition

A ribonucleotide consists of a ribose sugar, a nitrogenous base and a phosphate group. A chain of ribonucleotides has chemically distinct five-prime and three-prime ends. Its bases can form hydrogen-bonded pairs, notably adenine with uracil and guanine with cytosine. Guanine and uracil can also form wobble pairs in appropriate RNA structures. Pairing can occur between separate RNA molecules or between complementary regions of the same molecule. The latter process generates stems, loops, bulges and more complex structures. Pairing is antiparallel: a sequence written from five-prime to three-prime recognizes its complementary sequence in the opposite orientation. To express both sequences in standard notation, the student must reverse the complement rather than merely substitute each base.

Suppose a guide has the sequence 5′-AUGCCAU-3′. Its complementary partner in an aligned duplex is 3′-UACGGUA-5′, which becomes 5′-AUGGCAU-3′ when written independently in the conventional direction. The two written sequences are not identical, and they are not simple left-to-right complements when both are presented with the same polarity. This elementary rule prevents errors in target prediction, especially when an examination presents a guide and a target sequence as separate five-prime to three-prime strings. An RNA target may contain additional nucleotides on either side of the paired segment, and only an accessible region of the target is required for recognition.

Base pairing provides specificity, but sequence complementarity is not identical to binding under cellular conditions. Hydrogen bonding and base stacking stabilize a duplex, while the formation of an RNA structure reduces some of the conformational freedom of its strands. A target site buried within a stable intramolecular helix must become available before it can pair efficiently with an incoming guide. Nearby proteins may cover the sequence or remodel its structure. Local concentrations and subcellular localization also influence how often molecules meet. A sequence comparison can identify plausible targets; it cannot, by itself, establish that those targets are occupied or functionally regulated in living cells.

Short regulatory RNAs commonly act with proteins rather than as isolated antisense molecules. A protein that binds a guide can stabilize its ends, display certain nucleotides for target recognition and provide surfaces that recruit other factors. This partnership separates the sequence-dependent recognition function from the biochemical activity of the complex. Changing the guide sequence can redirect recognition while the same protein continues to perform its characteristic function. Conversely, a protein mutation can destroy catalytic or recruitment activity while preserving association with its guide. The two mutations may therefore produce distinct experimental outcomes.

The presence of a short RNA in a total RNA preparation does not establish that it has entered a functional complex. It might be a processing intermediate, a passenger strand removed during complex formation, or a degradation fragment. A mature guide associated with an effector protein is more direct evidence of potential function. Even that association does not guarantee that a predicted target is regulated in a particular cell type, because the target must be expressed and accessible there. A useful model of regulation has three components: production of the guide, assembly of the guide with an appropriate protein and encounter with a biologically available target. Loss of any one can prevent the final response.

A long noncoding RNA may use a different strategy. Rather than being reduced to a short guide, it may retain multiple structural domains. One region may associate with a protein, a second may influence RNA localization and a third may stabilize the molecule. In some cases, a comparatively short sequence within a long RNA is necessary for a specific function, while extensive neighboring sequences contribute to folding or stability. Length alone does not establish that a long RNA is a scaffold; this description requires evidence that more than one interaction contributes to the biological activity. Similarly, complementarity is not obligatory for every RNA interaction. A protein may recognize a particular shape or sequence motif, and two molecules may associate indirectly through another protein.

The ribose two-prime hydroxyl group contributes to RNA chemistry and distinguishes RNA from DNA. In appropriate molecular contexts, RNA can even participate in catalysis. This fact provides a connection to ribosomal RNA and the catalytic RNAs examined later. Most regulatory guide RNAs discussed here are not themselves the catalytic enzymes responsible for target cleavage. In an Argonaute-containing slicing complex, the RNA confers target recognition while the catalytic protein hydrolyzes a phosphodiester bond. Separating the catalyst from the specificity determinant is important because the observation of target cleavage does not establish that the guide RNA is a ribozyme.

## 3. How discoveries changed the concept of gene regulation

Classical developmental genetics supplied particularly persuasive evidence that short RNAs could regulate other genes. In the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, cells undergo characteristic developmental events at reproducible times. Mutations in heterochronic genes change the timing of those events, providing an observable phenotype through which investigators can infer the existence of a regulatory system. A cell may execute an otherwise recognizable developmental program at the wrong stage. This distinguishes temporal regulation from the complete loss of the ability to differentiate. The lin-4 gene was identified in this context, long before the modern inventory of small regulatory RNAs existed.

In 1993, work by Lee, Feinbaum and Ambros showed that lin-4 produces short RNAs rather than a conventional protein product. The small RNA sequences exhibited complementarity to regions in the untranslated portion of lin-14 messenger RNA. Complementary genetic and molecular evidence associated the regulation of LIN-14 protein production with these RNA elements. Together, these findings made a compelling case for a new kind of regulatory relationship. Rather than assuming that lin-4 encoded a transcription factor, the model assigned specificity to an RNA that recognizes sequences within another transcript. The fact that the relevant pairing sites lay outside the protein-coding region explained why regulation could change while the amino acid sequence of LIN-14 remained intact.

The developmental context is central to interpreting the evidence. If a negative regulator normally accumulates as development progresses, its target protein can be relatively abundant early and decline later. A mutation that prevents production or action of the regulator can allow the target protein to persist. The cell may then retain a developmental state associated with the earlier period. A mutation that removes functional target sites can produce a related consequence even if the regulator remains normal. These genetic possibilities follow from the same causal model but affect different molecular components. Measurements of the regulator, target RNA and target protein can help distinguish them.

The identification of let-7 in C. elegans in 2000 reinforced the principle that a small noncoding RNA can control the timing of development. Conservation of let-7 sequence across diverse animals subsequently helped establish that this type of regulation was not peculiar to one nematode. Conservation provided evidence of biological constraint and a reason to investigate related functions, but did not prove that every species used the same target or developmental output. The evolutionary argument is that a sequence maintained across lineages is more likely to have a selected function, particularly when independent functional evidence supports that conclusion. A conserved sequence is not equivalent to a completely conserved regulatory network.

A second historical line of evidence concerned the effects of double-stranded RNA. Fire and colleagues reported in 1998 that double-stranded RNA caused strong and sequence-specific interference with gene expression in C. elegans. Purified individual RNA strands were substantially less effective in the tested conditions. This comparison helped distinguish a cellular response to duplex RNA from a simple hypothesis in which added antisense RNA blocks messenger RNA in a one-to-one manner. Potent responses to small quantities of triggering material also suggested that the effect was not adequately explained by a stoichiometric sequestration model alone. Later identification of small interfering RNAs and RNA-processing proteins supplied a molecular account of how the trigger was transformed into sequence-guided regulatory complexes.

These two discoveries illustrate why a historical observation must be separated from the mechanism eventually used to explain it. The early lin-4 study did not establish every later detail of Argonaute function, and the double-stranded RNA study did not identify every downstream enzyme at once. The mature scientific model arose through multiple kinds of evidence: genetic perturbation, detection of RNA products, comparison of precursor structures, protein association and analysis of target responses. A student answering an experimental question should identify which component of a model is established by the particular evidence supplied and which component remains to be tested.

The connection between these histories is that microRNAs and small interfering RNAs both exploit nucleotide sequence to guide protein complexes, although their characteristic precursors and common biological roles differ. A further class of small RNAs associated with PIWI proteins was identified through biochemical and genetic analysis of germline systems. In contrast, the recognition of long noncoding RNA functions, including chromosome-associated transcripts, emphasized that regulatory activity need not be performed by a short guide. These discoveries broadened the concept of gene expression in different directions rather than defining a single universal RNA-regulation pathway.

## 4. Classification requires origin, dimensions, partners and biological role

MicroRNAs, usually abbreviated miRNAs, are short regulatory RNAs commonly about twenty-two nucleotides in animals. Their characteristic source is a defined hairpin embedded within a longer transcript. A specific region of the hairpin gives rise to a mature guide with a reproducible sequence. In many animals, the resulting RNA associates with an Argonaute protein and recognizes target messenger RNAs through partial complementarity, although more extensive pairing and cleavage can occur in suitable circumstances. MiRNAs frequently regulate endogenous cellular genes, making them especially useful for coordinating developmental and physiological processes.

Small interfering RNAs, abbreviated siRNAs, frequently arise from longer double-stranded RNA. A single long duplex can generate multiple small RNA duplexes corresponding to different positions along its length. Typical mature sizes are about twenty-one to twenty-three nucleotides, with additional size classes depending on organism and pathway. They also use Argonaute-family partners and often direct regulation of a highly complementary target. Endogenous siRNAs can participate in regulation and genome surveillance, whereas exogenous double-stranded RNA can generate guides during defense or experimentally induced interference. The distinction from miRNAs is therefore not the simplistic rule that all siRNAs come from outside the cell.

PIWI-interacting RNAs, abbreviated piRNAs, are associated with PIWI-subfamily Argonaute proteins. Many animal piRNAs are approximately twenty-four to thirty-one nucleotides long, although important exceptions exist. Their formation is independent of Dicer and commonly involves single-stranded precursor transcripts rather than a Dicer-processed long duplex. A prominent function is protection of the germline against transposable elements. Some piRNAs also regulate other RNAs or participate in developmental processes. Their defining biochemical partner and distinct origin explain why they are not classified as unusually long siRNAs.

Long noncoding RNAs, abbreviated lncRNAs, are conventionally distinguished from short noncoding RNAs by a length threshold of approximately two hundred nucleotides. This is an operational convention rather than a universal molecular boundary. Many are produced by RNA polymerase II and may be capped, spliced or polyadenylated, but individual molecules differ in processing, localization, stability and function. A long noncoding RNA may remain near its transcription locus, spread along a chromosome or act at other sites. It may associate with proteins, RNAs or chromatin and influence expression or cellular organization. Unlike the short guide categories, lncRNAs cannot be assigned to a single obligatory protein partner or processing pathway.

Other noncoding RNAs reinforce the diversity of the category. Ribosomal RNAs are fundamental structural and catalytic components of ribosomes, while transfer RNAs are adaptors in translation. Small nuclear RNAs participate in the spliceosome, and small nucleolar RNAs can guide chemical modification or assist processing of ribosomal RNA. They are noncoding even though their major roles differ from those of miRNAs, siRNAs and piRNAs. The category is defined by the nature of the functional product, not by the presence of a common regulatory mechanism. Some RNA classifications describe structural or catalytic work, whereas others describe guidance or regulatory activity.

These distinctions should be applied to evidence rather than memorized as rigid slogans. A twenty-two-nucleotide RNA that associates with an Argonaute protein might be either an miRNA or an siRNA. Evidence that it is released from a specific imperfect hairpin supports the former classification; a population of small products distributed across a long duplex supports the latter. An RNA of twenty-eight nucleotides is not necessarily a piRNA because other RNA fragments can have that size. Its PIWI association and Dicer-independent origin provide more informative observations. A transcript of one thousand nucleotides is not automatically a lncRNA because it could encode a protein. Coding potential and the identity of its functional product remain relevant. Classification is strongest when several independent features converge.
