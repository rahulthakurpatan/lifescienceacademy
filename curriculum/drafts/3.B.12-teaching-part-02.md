## 5. MicroRNA genes and the origin of a precisely defined guide

A microRNA begins its biological history as a sequence within a transcription unit. In animals, many microRNA genes are transcribed by RNA polymerase II. Some have their own transcriptional regulatory regions, whereas others occupy introns of host genes. A single primary transcript can contain one hairpin or several distinct hairpins, so one transcriptional event can provide precursors for several mature microRNAs. This arrangement makes coordinated expression possible, but it does not ensure equal mature abundance. Each hairpin has its own processing efficiency, and each resulting guide can have a different lifetime. A measurement of the primary transcript consequently cannot always substitute for a measurement of its mature products.

An intronic microRNA illustrates the difference between the source of an RNA and the identity of its functional product. Imagine that a gene produces a protein-coding precursor messenger RNA containing an intron that includes a microRNA hairpin. Splicing can join the exons to yield a coding mRNA, while the intronic sequence can also supply a regulatory small RNA. The protein and the small RNA are distinct molecular products even though their sequences were initially present within one transcript. They can have independent targets and may participate in unrelated cellular functions. The existence of such arrangements explains why the term noncoding RNA does not require an entirely separate region of the genome with no relationship to a coding gene.

The typical primary microRNA, or pri-miRNA, contains an imperfect double-stranded stem ending in a loop, together with flanking RNA. The stem is generated when different portions of one RNA molecule fold back and pair with each other. It can contain mismatches and unpaired bases. These imperfections are compatible with processing and, in fact, particular structural features can help a processing enzyme distinguish a potential microRNA hairpin from other RNA helices. The important point is that the primary transcript is much longer than the final guide. Its sequence and architecture define where a mature product can be excised.

A cell must convert this precursor into a small RNA whose ends are appropriate for loading into a regulatory protein. Imprecise production could change the target spectrum because target recognition depends strongly on the location of particular bases relative to the guide's five-prime end. A one-nucleotide change in that end can shift the identities of several positions used for recognition. A change in the three-prime end can alter stability, supplementary pairing or the way a guide fits within a protein. For this reason, processing is not merely the removal of excess material: it establishes part of the information needed for target selection.

The mature guide can derive from either arm of the original hairpin. The designations 5p and 3p indicate the arm of origin, not the chemical polarity of the resulting mature RNA; both products possess a five-prime and a three-prime end. In many circumstances one arm becomes the more abundant functional guide and the other is less abundant, but both arms can generate functional products. Arm preference may differ with cell type or precursor features. Thus the label 3p does not mean that the mature RNA is read backward or binds its target in the reverse of normal antiparallel orientation. It records a location within the precursor.

## 6. Canonical animal microRNA maturation

In the usual animal pathway, the initial step of microRNA processing occurs in the nucleus. The enzyme Drosha, an RNase III-family endonuclease, works with an RNA-binding protein known in mammals as DGCR8. The two form the core of the Microprocessor complex. The RNA-binding component helps identify an appropriate hairpin and its surrounding features, positioning the cleavage activity of Drosha relative to the RNA stem. Cleavage of both strands of the stem releases a shorter precursor microRNA, often approximately seventy nucleotides long. The product retains a hairpin structure. Its ends are shaped by staggered cleavage, commonly including a short three-prime overhang.

The cleavage reaction is chemically different from transcription. RNA polymerase originally joined ribonucleoside triphosphates to build the RNA backbone, whereas Drosha hydrolyzes pre-existing phosphodiester linkages. It does not synthesize a new sequence complementary to the precursor. The nucleotides that will eventually form the mature guide were already present in the primary transcript. Their positions become more clearly defined as processing removes flanking regions. This distinction matters in questions that ask whether the mature microRNA carries newly added complementary nucleotides or simply represents a selected portion of an existing transcript.

After nuclear processing, the typical precursor is transported to the cytoplasm. Exportin-5 recognizes appropriate double-stranded RNA structural features and participates in transport through the nuclear pore in a Ran GTP-dependent system. The nucleocytoplasmic gradient of Ran nucleotide states provides directionality for nuclear transport, as discussed in RNA export. Binding of an export receptor to its cargo in the appropriate compartment and release after transport make the process productive. Hydrolysis of GTP is linked to this transport cycle, not to the creation of the phosphodiester bonds of the microRNA itself.

A defect in Exportin-5 can therefore have a different signature from a defect in Drosha. If production of the primary RNA and its initial processing remain adequate, precursor may accumulate in the nucleus and cytoplasmic mature guide production can decline. By contrast, a block in the initial nuclear cleavage can leave less precursor available for transport and may increase the primary RNA pool if its degradation does not compensate. These predictions describe the simplest system; the measured steady-state abundance also depends on the susceptibility of each precursor to degradation. A stable accumulation is possible but not obligatory whenever a processing step is blocked.

In the cytoplasm, Dicer cleaves the precursor hairpin to release a short RNA duplex. Dicer belongs to the RNase III family but is not simply another name for Drosha. It acts at a different stage on a different typical substrate. Its RNA-binding features help define the distance between RNA ends and the cut sites, generating duplex products of characteristic small-RNA lengths. The loop and remaining excess precursor material are removed. The two strands of the duplex contain sequences from opposing arms of the hairpin, and their pairing need not be perfect because the original stem could contain mismatches or bulges.

The released duplex is not necessarily the final regulatory entity. A strand becomes functional when it is incorporated as a guide into an Argonaute-containing complex. The other strand can dissociate or be removed, and can in some cases independently become a guide in another complex. Strand choice is influenced by properties such as the relative stability of the duplex ends, sequence features and the preferences of the Argonaute pathway. It is not determined solely by the physical position of one arm on a conventional hairpin drawing. A student should reason from the actual strand sequence and the evidence of protein loading.

Once loaded, a mature miRNA can be stabilized by its protein partner. Free precursor RNA, free mature RNA and protein-bound RNA can exhibit different degradation rates. In addition, two RNA molecules that differ by a single nucleotide need not be equally efficient at loading or equally stable once loaded. This helps explain why a precursor may be readily detected even when very little biologically active guide exists. It also explains why a mutation in a guide-processing protein can have broader consequences than loss of one miRNA gene: the processing protein may be used by many precursors. In contrast, a mutation in one hairpin might selectively affect one family member, although shared target regulation can complicate the phenotype.

The order of the canonical pathway provides a valuable causal framework. Primary transcript synthesis precedes nuclear processing; the released hairpin is exported; cytoplasmic processing generates a duplex; and an effector-bound guide is established. A question may supply a band corresponding to each molecular form and ask which step is most likely defective. The relevant comparison is between the substrate and product of each proposed step, supplemented by compartmental information and measurements of effector association. A decrease in the smallest RNA alone is insufficient to locate the defective step.

## 7. Exceptions and comparative microRNA biogenesis

Canonical pathways are useful because they provide a default model, not because every organism or precursor follows every step. For example, some animal microRNAs arise through a route in which splicing and subsequent processing of an intron generate a hairpin that bypasses the typical Drosha cleavage requirement. Such precursors are known as mirtrons. They show that the structure required for a later step can be generated by another cellular mechanism. Their mature products can still participate in microRNA regulation. Therefore, the statement that all microRNAs absolutely require Drosha is stronger than the evidence supports.

A different exception is vertebrate miR-451, which can mature by an Argonaute-dependent route without conventional Dicer processing. The short precursor is suited to processing by catalytically active Argonaute, followed by additional maturation of the resulting product. This does not imply that Argonaute replaces Dicer for every hairpin or that every Argonaute can substitute for Dicer. The exception is related to the architecture of a particular precursor and to the catalytic properties of the relevant protein. If a student is given a Dicer-deficient cell in which a certain mature microRNA remains detectable, it is reasonable to investigate noncanonical processing, but persistence of a pre-existing stable RNA must also be considered.

Plant microRNA biogenesis provides a broader comparative example. Plants do not simply reproduce the mammalian Drosha and Dicer arrangement. In a typical plant pathway, the Dicer-like protein DCL1 is responsible for major processing steps of the primary hairpin, largely in the nucleus. Other plant proteins contribute to accurate processing, and small-RNA stabilization can involve methylation of the terminal ribose by HEN1. Mature plant guides function with plant Argonaute proteins. These examples establish that the overall principle of defined hairpin-derived regulatory small RNA can be conserved while details of processing and cellular localization differ.

Target recognition also differs in common cases. Many animal microRNAs recognize sites through a short region near the five-prime end of the guide, with variable pairing elsewhere. Many plant miRNAs exhibit extensive complementarity to their targets and can direct cleavage, although translational inhibition also occurs. It is consequently inaccurate to use the rule that miRNAs never cleave targets or that extensive complementarity always identifies an RNA as an siRNA. Biogenesis is more informative for classification than the mode of target repression alone. The location, sequence and protein partner of the guide must be interpreted within the biology of the organism examined.

A final exception concerns the use of RNA polymerases. Many animal microRNAs are produced from polymerase II transcripts, but examples with different transcriptional arrangements exist. Furthermore, a hairpin may be transcribed as part of a protein-coding gene, in a long noncoding transcript or as an independently regulated RNA. These possibilities differ in how transcription is coordinated with other genes. They do not change the requirement to establish a functional mature guide. In a genetic experiment, deleting a host promoter may therefore influence both the host messenger RNA and an associated miRNA. A targeted change in the hairpin can provide a more focused test if it does not disrupt host transcript processing.

Exceptions are best learned through the logic of the process. A mature RNA needs suitable ends, a sequence that enables recognition and an appropriate effector partner. The most common pathway provides these features through a particular sequence of enzymatic events, while alternative pathways may arrive at a related result through different events. This approach makes the classification robust even when an examination deliberately describes an unusual processing route. It also helps distinguish a true alternative pathway from experimental failure to detect an enzyme that remains necessary.

## 8. The target specificity and cellular functions of microRNAs

The sequence of a mature animal microRNA provides a principal determinant of the set of messenger RNAs it can regulate. A particularly important region, often called the seed, typically spans nucleotides two through seven or eight of the guide, counted from its five-prime end. Pairing with this region can be especially influential in target recognition. Many functional target sites occur in three-prime untranslated regions. Additional sequence and structural features can enhance or reduce the effectiveness of the interaction. Not every seed match is functional and some biologically meaningful sites have other pairing arrangements. This is why a sequence-based list of candidate targets is a starting point rather than a completed map of physiological regulation.

For example, if a mature guide begins 5′-UGAGGUAG-3′, then positions two through eight are GAGGUAG. The fully complementary sequence written in the five-prime to three-prime direction is CUACCUC. An mRNA that contains this motif is a candidate target for the seven-nucleotide seed, subject to the surrounding sequence and other cellular factors. If an alternative guide starts with a different five-prime nucleotide because its processing site has shifted, its seed must be recalculated rather than inferred from the old mature RNA name. The same reasoning applies when considering a mutation in the untranslated region of the target.

Association of a guide with an appropriate Argonaute-containing complex allows a recognized target to undergo post-transcriptional regulation. For many animal microRNAs, the usual outcomes include a reduction in productive translation and a reduction in messenger RNA stability. These outcomes are not mutually exclusive. A cell may show a decrease in protein before the steady-state change in RNA abundance is fully established, or an RNA loss may account for much of the later protein repression. Target cleavage can occur when extensive pairing and a catalytically competent effector permit it, but it is not the default outcome for every animal miRNA target. Detailed downstream silencing pathways follow in the study of integrated gene regulation.

The biological significance of a microRNA is often better understood by considering several targets rather than one. A guide with a common seed can recognize multiple transcripts, and a single transcript can contain sites for multiple guides. This creates many-to-many regulatory relationships. A modest reduction in the expression of several proteins acting in the same biological program may generate a substantial change in cellular behavior. Conversely, loss of one microRNA may produce a mild phenotype because related guides share targets. The apparent size of an individual molecular effect does not directly predict the physiological importance of an entire regulatory system.

Developmental timing provides one well established context for such control. A differentiation program can require proteins associated with an earlier cellular state to decline at the appropriate time. Accumulation of particular miRNAs can contribute to that decline. Tissue-specific microRNA expression provides another context: cells with identical genomes can exhibit different combinations of transcriptional programs and post-transcriptional regulation. A microRNA can therefore affect the expression of a target in one cell type but have little influence in another, even if both carry the same target site in their DNA. Both the guide and the relevant target must be present in the same cellular environment.

The miR-1 family provides a familiar example of microRNAs associated with muscle differentiation and function, while other families are associated with developmental or metabolic processes. These examples should not be reduced to the claim that one small RNA is solely responsible for an entire tissue identity. A tissue is produced by interacting regulatory systems, and the effects of one guide depend on the network in which it operates. A changed microRNA level in diseased tissue may be a contributor to pathology, a response to altered cell physiology, or a consequence of a difference in the relative proportions of cell types in the sampled tissue. Correlation must be tested before it is treated as a causal mechanism.

In plants, a miRNA can influence transcription-factor abundance and thereby change the expression of many downstream genes indirectly. If one miRNA directly regulates an mRNA encoding a transcription factor, hundreds of other transcripts might change without any complementary site for the miRNA. These secondary changes do not convert all affected transcripts into direct microRNA targets. Distinguishing direct and indirect effects is crucial for understanding experimental expression data. A direct target requires evidence relating the guide to the candidate RNA, whereas a downstream effect may be explained through the protein or cellular process regulated by that direct target.

A simple experiment illustrates the difference. A reporter protein can be expressed from a transcript carrying a candidate target region. If introducing a microRNA reduces reporter output and alteration of the relevant pairing site removes that effect, the data support site-dependent regulation. However, a mutation could also alter the baseline stability or structure of the reporter RNA, so the baseline behavior of the wild-type and mutant reporters must be assessed without the added guide. Measuring both reporter RNA and reporter protein distinguishes some alternative explanations. Further confirmation of endogenous regulation requires evidence that the original cellular transcript behaves consistently with the reporter model.
