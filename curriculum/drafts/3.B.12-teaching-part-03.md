## 9. Small interfering RNAs: transforming double-stranded RNA into guides

A long double-stranded RNA presents the cell with a molecular substrate that is different from a typical primary microRNA. Both contain paired ribonucleotides, but a microRNA precursor generally contains a locally defined hairpin embedded within other RNA sequences, whereas a long duplex can contain extensive paired regions formed by complementary strands or by folding of a long inverted repeat. The duplex may derive from a virus, from endogenous transcripts, from a cellular RNA-dependent RNA polymerase in organisms that possess such systems or from experimentally supplied RNA. These different sources converge on a common problem: how can a cell derive sequence-specific information from paired RNA and use that information to recognize related sequences?

In a conventional Dicer-dependent siRNA pathway, the long duplex is processed into a population of short duplexes. Each product represents a region of the original RNA. One precursor can thus yield guides of many different sequences, depending on the positions at which processing occurs. This contrasts with a microRNA hairpin that is generally processed to produce one or two characteristic guide sequences from its opposing arms. An RNA size distribution containing many short molecules is not sufficient to demonstrate the difference. Mapping their sequences back to the precursor is much more informative because it reveals whether products are concentrated at a defined hairpin or distributed across a longer duplex.

Dicer recognizes structural features of double-stranded RNA and cleaves phosphodiester bonds on both strands. Staggered cleavage commonly yields duplex products with short three-prime overhangs. The product length depends on the processing enzyme and pathway. In a standard model, a duplex of approximately twenty-one nucleotides per strand, including short unpaired ends, can be suitable for formation of an effector complex. A diagram that shows twenty-one nucleotides does not imply that every nucleotide is paired; a nineteen-base-pair central region and two-nucleotide overhangs can also give a strand length of twenty-one. When solving quantitative problems, distinguish the number of base pairs in a helix from the number of nucleotides in each strand.

The conversion of duplex RNA into a single guide is essential for target recognition. A nucleotide already base-paired to the passenger strand cannot simultaneously establish the same ordinary Watson–Crick pair with a target strand. Loading and strand separation establish an effector complex in which the guide can interact with another RNA. The protein environment also helps determine which strand is used and how it interacts with potential targets. In some pathways, passenger-strand cleavage aids its removal; in others, duplex unwinding or dissociation contributes to separation. It is therefore important not to identify the entire small duplex with the mature active complex.

The guide typically recognizes a target RNA with extensive complementarity. When the relevant Argonaute is catalytically competent and the guide-target geometry permits it, the protein can cleave the target. The guide itself can remain associated with the protein after the products are released. This creates a molecular distinction between target destruction and guide destruction: cleavage of one target need not consume one guide. It also provides a conceptual reason why the effect of a small quantity of guide can exceed the amount expected from simple one-to-one sequestration. In some organisms, amplification through additional RNA synthesis can further increase the effect. These possibilities are not identical, and the evidence must determine which process operates in a given organism.

Target cleavage occurs at a position defined by the guide-target alignment in a conventional slicing complex. The commonly taught position is the phosphodiester bond between target nucleotides paired with guide positions ten and eleven, counted from the guide's five-prime end. This positional relationship is useful for recognizing a sequence-guided cleavage event. Nevertheless, the existence of extensive pairing is not a guarantee of cleavage. A catalytically inactive Argonaute, an obstructed target or a pairing defect at a functionally important position can prevent efficient slicing. Different pathways may also have different regulatory outcomes. The important distinction is between the origin of an siRNA and the molecular activity of the protein complex with which it becomes associated.

A direct experimental consequence is that mature guide production and target regulation can be separated. A mutation affecting Dicer may prevent normal production of small duplexes from a long precursor. A mutation affecting an essential effector can leave precursor processing intact while reducing the downstream response. In a simplified system, adding a suitable preformed small duplex can bypass the need to cut a long precursor but cannot substitute for an indispensable effector protein. This bypass logic provides a way to order biological events. It is analogous to supplying the product of a blocked metabolic reaction to test whether the downstream pathway remains functional.

## 10. Endogenous and exogenous siRNAs across organisms

The terms endogenous and exogenous indicate where a precursor originates, not whether the resulting guide is a functional siRNA. An endogenous duplex can form when transcripts from complementary DNA strands overlap, when a transcript contains an extended inverted repeat or when an appropriate RNA-dependent RNA polymerase generates a complementary strand. A viral RNA or experimentally supplied duplex can provide an exogenous trigger. In each case, the mature small RNA can use the sequence of the trigger to recognize a related target. An endogenous siRNA need not have a different chemical backbone from an exogenous siRNA.

Plants provide important examples of natural siRNA functions. Viral replication can generate double-stranded RNA intermediates, which supply sequence information to antiviral silencing pathways. Plant cells also generate endogenous siRNAs that participate in regulation of cellular transcripts or repetitive genomic sequences. Plant Dicer-like proteins generate different characteristic classes of small RNA, and their Argonaute partners and target types can differ. Twenty-four-nucleotide plant siRNAs are associated with pathways that direct chromatin-related regulation, including RNA-directed DNA methylation. The detailed recruitment and methylation mechanisms belong to integrated gene regulation; here, the example demonstrates why neither a size cutoff nor a cytoplasmic target model can define all siRNAs.

Some fungi and animals also possess pathways that produce endogenous siRNAs or respond to exogenous duplex RNA. The nematode C. elegans is particularly useful as a genetic model of RNA interference. Its ability to use RNA-dependent RNA polymerases in amplification pathways explains why a simple mammalian model of one precursor yielding only a limited set of guides cannot be transferred uncritically to the nematode. Some secondary small RNAs in nematodes arise through pathways that do not require conventional Dicer cleavage at the secondary production stage. Thus even the common statement that siRNAs require Dicer needs an organism-specific qualification.

In mammalian cells, long double-stranded RNA can activate innate immune responses in many settings. The resulting effects can include a broad reduction in protein synthesis or changes in cell survival. Such responses differ from a specific decrease in one messenger RNA due to an appropriately loaded siRNA. A decrease in a reporter following the introduction of double-stranded RNA does not establish sequence-specific regulation if unrelated reporters decrease similarly. A suitable non-targeting control, measurements of cell viability and comparison of unrelated transcripts help separate the two interpretations. A short duplex can also cause unintended effects, so short size by itself does not prove specificity.

Experimental introduction of a preformed siRNA has become useful for investigating gene function. Reducing the abundance of a protein can provide a phenotype that helps identify its cellular role. The effect is commonly a knockdown rather than a permanent deletion of the DNA sequence. Newly transcribed RNA can restore protein production when the effect subsides. A genetic knockout, by contrast, changes the relevant DNA sequence or genomic function. These interventions may produce different results even when both reduce the same protein because the degree, duration and timing of the reduction differ. A residual pool of protein can support an essential function during partial knockdown, while complete loss can have a stronger phenotype.

Off-target effects are an important limitation of interpreting siRNA experiments. A guide designed to pair extensively with one transcript may also interact through partial complementarity with other transcripts. A change in cellular behavior could therefore arise from the intended target, an unintended target or a general cellular response. Using more than one independently designed guide against the intended gene can strengthen the inference if they yield similar phenotypes. Restoration of the target protein from a version of the transcript that is not recognized by the guide can provide a further test. For a coding-region target, changes to synonymous codons may sometimes preserve the protein while changing the RNA recognition site. These are conceptual experimental controls rather than a guarantee that all unrelated molecular effects have been eliminated.

RNA interference is also of interest in agricultural biology because it connects the sequence of a pathogen or pest-associated transcript to a sequence-guided regulatory response. The effectiveness of such a response depends on whether the target organism can take up, retain and process the relevant RNA and whether the target is biologically accessible. Organisms differ substantially in these capabilities. A successful result in one species is not sufficient evidence that the same approach will work in another. A proper evaluation distinguishes an intended sequence-specific response from effects caused by general stress or altered development. At the curriculum level, this example illustrates the practical significance of natural RNA-regulation systems without requiring specialist application methods.

## 11. Why organisms require another class of small regulatory RNA

The genome of an animal contains numerous sequences derived from transposable elements. Some transposable elements remain capable of moving or generating new genomic copies. Their activity can disrupt genes, alter transcription or contribute to genomic rearrangements. A multicellular organism faces a particularly important challenge when such activity occurs in the cells that give rise to gametes, because changes in their DNA may be transmitted to the next generation. Protection of germline genome integrity therefore requires mechanisms that recognize and restrain potentially harmful mobile elements.

PIWI-interacting RNAs provide a prominent example of RNA-based protection in many animals. PIWI proteins form a subfamily of the Argonaute protein family, and their associated small RNAs are called piRNAs. The name reflects a biochemical relationship rather than an exact numerical size. Many piRNAs are longer than the typical animal miRNA, but their association with PIWI proteins and their mode of formation distinguish them more reliably. The biological significance of the pathway becomes evident when defects in essential components increase transposable-element expression or disrupt germline development.

A transposable element can produce RNA as part of its activity. A regulatory system that recognizes this RNA can reduce the amount available for subsequent steps. Some piRNA-associated pathways also act at the level of chromatin and transcription. In either case, a sequence-dependent mechanism can help direct regulation toward potentially harmful elements without requiring that every transcript of the cell be suppressed. The details of chromatin-directed control vary across species and are treated separately in the chapter sequence on gene silencing. Here the central question is how the piRNA class is generated and why its association with a distinct protein family matters.

Unlike conventional miRNA and primary siRNA pathways, piRNA formation does not require Dicer. In many animal systems, piRNAs originate from long single-stranded precursor RNAs transcribed from genomic regions that can contain numerous fragments of transposable elements. Such regions can provide a record of sequences that the regulatory system may need to recognize. If a precursor contains a fragment complementary to a transposable-element RNA, processing can yield a guide with the potential to recognize that element. The precursor need not adopt a conventional Dicer-recognized long duplex. Endonucleolytic processing, association with PIWI proteins and maturation of the small RNA ends establish the functional products.

The observation that a small RNA survives after Dicer loss is not, by itself, enough to classify it as a piRNA. It could be a stable pre-existing miRNA, a noncanonical Dicer-independent miRNA, a degradation fragment or another small RNA. PIWI association, precursor evidence, length distribution and changes following disruption of the appropriate biogenesis pathway provide a stronger combination. A question describing a thirty-nucleotide RNA in a germline sample, association with a PIWI protein and persistence after Dicer depletion presents a mutually consistent set of observations. The same size information in a total RNA sample without these supporting features is much less informative.

## 12. Primary and secondary piRNA production and biological functions

A simplified primary piRNA pathway begins with transcription of a precursor, followed by cleavage and selection of RNA fragments that become associated with PIWI proteins. Further maturation can define the ends, and in many animal systems the three-prime end is chemically modified by two-prime-O-methylation. The protein-bound product is protected and can participate in sequence recognition. This broad account is sufficient to explain why the pathway uses neither the standard Drosha-to-Dicer sequence of animal miRNA maturation nor the Dicer cleavage of long double-stranded RNA typical of primary siRNAs.

Some piRNA systems use a secondary amplification process often termed the ping-pong cycle. A guide associated with a PIWI protein can recognize a complementary RNA and direct cleavage. One of the resulting RNA fragments can supply the five-prime end of a new guide, provided it is processed and incorporated into an appropriate PIWI-associated complex. If the new guide is complementary to transcripts of the opposite orientation, it can direct another recognition event. In systems where this reciprocal processing occurs, it can enrich the population of guides related to the sequences that are actively expressed.

The characteristic ten-nucleotide overlap between the five-prime ends of opposite-orientation piRNAs can arise from the geometry of guide-directed cleavage. This pattern is not a ten-base-pair length for the entire mature piRNA. It is a relationship between the mapped positions of the ends of two populations of guides. It can be used as evidence supporting a secondary amplification pathway when combined with other biochemical or genetic data. Absence of the pattern does not rule out primary piRNA biogenesis, because not every piRNA is produced through the ping-pong process.

There are important species differences. The Drosophila germline provides a classical model in which PIWI-subfamily proteins and transposable-element-derived guides participate in genome defense. Mammals possess related PIWI proteins and piRNA populations, with functions that can differ among stages of germ-cell development. Not all mammalian piRNAs are directed against transposable elements; later developmental populations can have other RNA targets. C. elegans possesses piRNAs commonly known as twenty-one-U RNAs, illustrating a conspicuous exception to the usual twenty-four to thirty-one nucleotide length range. Therefore, classification by a fixed lower length threshold would incorrectly exclude a recognized piRNA system.

The loss of germline function following mutation of a PIWI protein can arise from several connected consequences. Increased expression of mobile elements may contribute to genomic damage, and defects in regulation of other transcripts may interfere with differentiation. Observing sterility is not sufficient to identify which molecular target is responsible. A stronger causal argument combines evidence of guide loss, altered expression of particular targets and a corresponding cellular consequence. If suppression of the implicated transposable element partially restores the phenotype, the evidence for its contribution becomes stronger, although other defects may remain.

One distinction in comparative biology is that PIWI-interacting RNAs are particularly characteristic of animal systems, whereas plants have their own small-RNA pathways for many related problems of repeat and transposable-element regulation. A plant twenty-four-nucleotide small RNA that guides chromatin regulation is not classified as a piRNA solely because it has a length and broad protective function similar to an animal piRNA. Functional analogy does not establish identical evolutionary origin or identical machinery. A student should connect biological problems across organisms while keeping the classification anchored to the actual protein partners and biogenesis pathways.

The piRNA pathway also illustrates how inherited information can influence protection beyond the conventional inheritance of a protein-coding allele. In some animal systems, maternally provided piRNAs contribute to establishment of transposable-element control in offspring. The resulting difference in early regulatory state can depend on the RNA molecules deposited into an egg as well as on the DNA sequences inherited by that offspring. This should not be generalized to every piRNA system or confused with a permanent change in DNA sequence. It is a useful bridge to the distinction between genetic inheritance and inherited regulatory states, which receives fuller treatment in later chapters.
