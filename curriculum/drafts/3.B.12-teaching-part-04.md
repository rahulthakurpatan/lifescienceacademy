## 13. Long noncoding RNAs and the diversity of longer functional transcripts

The term long noncoding RNA covers a more heterogeneous group of molecules than any one of the three small-RNA classes. The usual length boundary of about two hundred nucleotides is convenient in annotation and analysis, but it does not establish a natural division between two universal biochemical mechanisms. A transcript of two hundred and one nucleotides does not automatically gain a new property that a transcript of one hundred and ninety-nine nucleotides lacks. Rather, the convention allows long transcripts without established protein-coding function to be distinguished from many better defined classes of small functional RNA. To understand an individual lncRNA, one must determine its production, structure, cellular location, binding partners and experimentally supported function.

Many long noncoding RNAs resemble messenger RNAs in aspects of their production. RNA polymerase II can transcribe them, their ends may receive a cap or polyadenylate tail, and introns may be removed by splicing. These features arise from the shared transcription and RNA-processing machinery, not from an obligation to translate every processed RNA. A capped, spliced and polyadenylated RNA can still perform its principal function without being translated. Conversely, not every lncRNA is capped, spliced and polyadenylated in the usual way. Some have unusual forms of end maturation or stabilization. Processing properties help describe a transcript but are not independently decisive evidence of its coding status.

An important classification describes the genomic position of the lncRNA relative to neighboring genes. An intergenic lncRNA originates from a region between annotated genes. An antisense transcript overlaps a gene in the opposite transcriptional orientation. A sense-overlapping transcript has the same transcriptional orientation as an overlapping gene, whereas an intronic transcript may arise within one of its introns. Bidirectional transcription can also generate distinct RNA products from nearby promoter regions. These are genomic descriptions rather than guarantees of function. An antisense transcript does not necessarily repress the corresponding sense transcript, and an intergenic transcript is not necessarily irrelevant to neighboring genes.

For an antisense transcript, the sequence relation can offer a plausible molecular hypothesis. Overlapping portions of the two RNAs might be complementary and might interact if both are present in the same compartment and accessible. Such pairing could influence processing, stability or protein binding. But the transcription of the antisense RNA could also affect the sense gene without the two mature molecules ever meeting. The genomic arrangement can influence transcription through competition for local factors or other effects. Therefore, sequence overlap identifies a relationship that deserves investigation; it does not establish the mechanism by which a phenotype occurs.

Long RNA structure permits the possibility of several distinct interaction surfaces. A long chain can fold locally into hairpins, form longer-range contacts within itself and associate with multiple proteins. Such structural organization may permit regulatory activity even when parts of its primary sequence are not strongly conserved. Some functions require a particular short sequence motif, while others may depend on a higher-order structural arrangement or on the local production of the transcript. These possibilities make evolutionary comparison more complicated than the comparison of a short miRNA seed. Absence of strong conservation over the entire sequence is not conclusive evidence that a lncRNA has no function, but a proposed function still requires direct experimental support.

Cellular location is a particularly useful clue. A transcript enriched in the nucleus is in a position to influence chromatin, transcription, nuclear RNA processing or nuclear organization. A predominantly cytoplasmic transcript may interact with proteins or messenger RNAs in that compartment and influence their activity, stability or translation. Localization does not prove that any one of these functions occurs. It establishes which classes of interaction are physically plausible under the tested conditions. A transcript can also have more than one location, and its distribution may change with cell type, developmental stage or cellular stress. A single localization image is therefore evidence about a specific context, not an invariant property of the molecule.

## 14. Long RNA functions: guide, scaffold, decoy and organization models

One possible lncRNA activity is to guide an interacting factor toward a cellular target. The word guide has a broader meaning here than in the small-RNA pathways. A long RNA may associate with a chromosomal region because it is produced nearby, because it interacts with proteins already present at that region or because another molecular recognition event retains it there. Once localized, it may help bring a regulatory protein to the same place. Unlike a conventional siRNA, a long RNA does not need to be processed into a short guide or loaded into an Argonaute complex to perform this role. The mechanism depends on the molecular interactions of that individual transcript.

A scaffold model proposes that one RNA helps organize several components of a molecular assembly. Different parts of the RNA may provide binding surfaces for distinct proteins. By bringing these proteins into proximity, the RNA could increase the probability that they interact or act on a shared substrate. Evidence for this model must go beyond the detection of several proteins in association with an RNA. If those proteins independently bind unrelated copies of the RNA, the same observations may occur without a single scaffolded complex. Disrupting a defined interaction surface while retaining other aspects of RNA production and localization can help establish whether the interaction is necessary for the biological effect.

A decoy model involves competition for a binding partner. Suppose a cellular protein binds a regulatory site in a messenger RNA and a long noncoding RNA carries another site recognized by the same protein. If the long RNA occupies a sufficient fraction of the protein population, fewer free molecules may be available for other targets. The magnitude of such competition depends on abundance, binding affinity, compartmental localization and the number of available sites. The mere existence of a predicted binding motif does not demonstrate that competition can occur to a biologically significant extent. An RNA present at one copy per cell is unlikely to sequester thousands of freely available protein molecules in a simple one-to-one model, although a localized effect can involve a much smaller accessible pool.

Long RNAs may also contribute to the organization of nuclear bodies. The nucleus contains molecular assemblies that are not necessarily surrounded by lipid membranes. RNA and proteins can form dynamic associations that concentrate selected components in particular locations. A lncRNA that helps establish such an assembly can influence the environment in which other RNAs are processed or retained. The existence of these structures illustrates a function that is difficult to capture in the simple distinction between coding RNA and short antisense RNA. Organization of cellular space can itself be a biologically important role of a gene product.

NEAT1 is a well-established example of a long noncoding RNA involved in the formation of nuclear paraspeckles. Particular NEAT1 isoforms differ in their contribution, with the long isoform playing a central structural role. This makes the example especially useful for considering the relationship between processing and function. Two transcripts from the same locus can have different ends, different lengths and different abilities to support a cellular assembly. Observing an increase in total RNA from the locus may not tell us whether the functionally relevant isoform has increased. Measurements that distinguish isoforms are required when the biological hypothesis depends on their different properties.

MALAT1 is another well-known nuclear lncRNA, associated with nuclear speckles and implicated in regulation of RNA processing and cellular expression programs. It should not be described as an absolutely required component of every splicing reaction or every nuclear speckle. Its effects depend on biological context, and its recognized functions are not identical to those of NEAT1. The two examples demonstrate how distinct long RNAs can be associated with different nuclear assemblies. A general claim that all long noncoding RNAs are structural components of nuclear bodies would go far beyond these observations.

The guide, scaffold and decoy models are explanatory categories, not mutually exclusive labels attached permanently to every transcript. An RNA that helps assemble a complex could also influence the location of that complex. An RNA associated with a nuclear structure could affect the availability of proteins that participate in another process. A particular RNA may perform different functions in different environments. At examination level, the student should ask what evidence supports a specified model in the given scenario. The name of the transcript, length of the transcript or presence of a possible interaction motif cannot independently establish the molecular role.

## 15. Cis and trans activity: the example of XIST

The distinction between cis and trans regulation helps interpret long noncoding RNA activity. In a genetic context, cis generally refers to an effect linked to the allele or chromosomal region from which a regulatory element or transcript originates. Trans activity refers to a product that can influence another genetically separable locus or allele. This distinction is not simply the same as short-range and long-range physical distance. A transcript produced at one position on a chromosome may act over a large portion of that same chromosome and still exhibit cis behavior in the genetic sense. Conversely, a product that diffuses to a nearby locus on another chromosome may act in trans.

XIST, the X-inactive specific transcript, is a classical example of a chromosome-associated long noncoding RNA in mammalian X-chromosome inactivation. In the appropriate developmental context, XIST accumulates on the X chromosome from which it is transcribed and participates in establishment of a repressed state across much of that chromosome. This process contributes to dosage compensation by reducing expression of many genes from one X chromosome in cells with two X chromosomes. The RNA does not encode a protein that travels indiscriminately to silence both X chromosomes. Its localization and associations enable a chromosome-linked regulatory function. Not all genes on the inactive X are equally silenced, and some escape inactivation, so the biological consequence should not be described as absolute shutdown of every gene on that chromosome.

The example demonstrates how a noncoding transcript can influence an entire cellular structure through spatially constrained activity. A chromosome occupies a three-dimensional region within the nucleus; sites distant from each other along its linear DNA sequence may be closer in space. Localization of an RNA near its chromosome of origin can facilitate contact with chromosomal regions through proximity and protein-mediated interactions. This differs fundamentally from the requirement for extensive sequence complementarity between a small guide and every target transcript. The central classification of XIST as a lncRNA rests on its functional RNA product, not on an assumption that it uses the same targeting mechanism as a miRNA.

Dosage compensation also provides a useful connection to inheritance and chromosome biology. Cells of an individual can possess the same DNA sequence at an X-linked locus but differ in which X chromosome is transcriptionally active. In a heterozygous individual, this can generate cell populations expressing different alleles. The resulting mosaicism illustrates that gene expression depends on chromosome state in addition to the inherited DNA sequence. However, X inactivation is not equivalent to deletion of one X chromosome. The inactive chromosome remains physically present, and some of its genes can remain expressed. Detailed establishment and maintenance of the epigenetic state belong to the later gene-regulation unit.

The distinction between cis and trans can be examined experimentally using allele-specific expression. Imagine a diploid cell carrying distinguishable alleles A and B of a neighboring protein-coding gene. A perturbation of a lncRNA locus on chromosome A reduces expression of coding allele A but leaves allele B unchanged. This observation supports a local, allele-linked effect. It does not establish that the mature lncRNA is the causal molecule, because a local DNA regulatory element or the transcriptional process itself could produce an analogous pattern. If expression of the RNA from a distant locus restores function of both alleles, a trans-capable RNA product becomes a plausible explanation. Failure of such rescue does not rule out an RNA-dependent function when the RNA must be produced or retained locally.

## 16. The functional RNA molecule versus its genomic locus

Establishing the function of an lncRNA requires distinguishing three causal possibilities that can produce superficially similar expression data. First, the DNA sequence containing the lncRNA gene might itself be a regulatory element for a neighboring gene. Second, the act of transcribing or processing the locus might influence that neighboring gene even if the mature transcript sequence is not required. Third, the RNA molecule might be the active product. In some cases, more than one possibility contributes. The experimental challenge is to alter one component while preserving the others as much as possible.

Deletion of an entire lncRNA locus removes the DNA sequence, prevents its normal transcription and eliminates its RNA product. If a neighboring gene changes expression, all three explanations remain possible. The deletion is informative because it establishes that the deleted region contributes to the phenotype under the tested conditions, but it cannot establish which molecular property of the region is responsible. Describing a deletion phenotype as definitive evidence that a long RNA acts as a molecular scaffold would therefore be unjustified.

Premature transcription termination can provide a different perturbation. If transcription initiates normally but terminates before production of the full RNA, the experiment preserves more of the original DNA sequence than a complete deletion. It can nevertheless alter transcriptional elongation, RNA processing and the mature product simultaneously. If the phenotype is observed after both deletion and premature termination, the shared loss of full transcription or full RNA production becomes relevant. A promoter-only explanation becomes less compelling if the promoter remains functional, although insertion of a termination element can itself have local effects.

Selective reduction of the RNA molecule offers another type of test. If transcription and the underlying DNA regulatory region remain intact but the functional RNA is depleted, a corresponding phenotype supports an RNA-dependent contribution. The strength of that conclusion depends on specificity, depletion efficiency and whether the relevant RNA population is reached. A nuclear RNA may be particularly difficult to deplete with an intervention that mainly affects cytoplasmic molecules. Failure to see an effect after an attempted depletion may therefore reflect technical failure rather than dispensability of the RNA.

Rescue experiments can strengthen interpretation by restoring a missing functional component. If a phenotype caused by reducing an RNA is reversed by producing a resistant version of the same RNA, that supports a specific role of the transcript. A mutated rescue RNA that retains expression and localization but lacks a particular interaction domain can be used to investigate the role of that domain. However, an ectopically expressed RNA might fail to reach a chromosome-linked site where the endogenous molecule normally acts. In this case, the absence of rescue does not establish that the RNA is nonfunctional. Appropriate localization is part of the causal model, not an optional detail.

A particularly informative examination scenario compares several interventions. Suppose deletion of a locus reduces neighboring gene expression, interruption of transcription also reduces expression, but depletion of the mature RNA does not. If RNA depletion has been convincingly demonstrated in the relevant compartment, an effect associated with transcription becomes a stronger possibility than a function of the mature RNA. If deletion alone causes an effect while neither transcription interruption nor RNA depletion does, the DNA regulatory region becomes a stronger candidate. If RNA depletion reproduces the phenotype and a properly localized resistant RNA restores normal expression, the evidence for a functional RNA product becomes stronger. Each conclusion remains conditioned on the specificity of the interventions.

These distinctions prevent a general misconception about the transcriptome. The number of detected lncRNA transcripts is not equal to the number of experimentally established regulatory functions. Detection, sequence annotation, localization, molecular interaction and causal phenotype represent different levels of evidence. A newly identified transcript may be a promising candidate for further study without yet having an established function. Conversely, a well-characterized lncRNA can have strong evidence for a particular biological role even if many other transcripts of comparable length remain poorly understood. Classification provides a vocabulary for investigation; experiments establish the functions of individual molecules.
