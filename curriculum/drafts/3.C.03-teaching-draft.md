# Ribosome Architecture and the Molecular Basis of Translation

## 1. The problem that a ribosome solves

A cell can copy the information in DNA into RNA without making a protein. Transcription preserves a sequence of nucleotides, even when the original DNA is copied into a chemically different polymer. Translation requires a fundamentally different kind of correspondence. A linear message written using four nucleotide bases must direct the assembly of a linear product written using amino acids. An amino acid does not possess a nucleotide-like surface that can recognize its assigned codon directly. The ribosome solves this difficulty by bringing an informational molecule, a set of adaptors and a chemical reaction into a single organized space. Its structure makes these three activities mutually dependent rather than three unrelated biochemical events.

The messenger RNA provides the sequence. A transfer RNA provides the bridge between a nucleotide triplet and an amino acid, because one end of the folded transfer RNA carries an anticodon while the other can carry an amino acid. The specific amino acid is attached before the transfer RNA reaches the ribosome. The ribosome therefore receives an adaptor that already represents a chemical assignment. Its responsibility is to examine the appropriate message–adaptor interaction, place the amino acid in a suitable position for chemistry and preserve the reading frame during movement to the next triplet. Understanding where these operations occur is more useful than simply learning that the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis.

Consider a cell that must make two proteins containing the same amino acids in different orders. A reaction that joins amino acids indiscriminately would produce many possible sequences and would not distinguish the two genes. The cell must instead create a transient spatial arrangement in which one particular amino acid is presented as the next building block while the growing chain is retained. If the chemical reaction occurred before checking the adaptor, an incorrect amino acid would often be incorporated. If the chain were released after each bond formed, translation would have to find the same growing molecule again. If the message moved by two or four nucleotides instead of three, subsequent assignments would change. These are design constraints imposed by the problem of sequence-directed polymerization.

A ribosome is a ribonucleoprotein particle. The term means that the functional assembly contains RNA and protein rather than being composed exclusively of one class of macromolecule. Its RNA is not a disposable copy of a gene that happens to be present during synthesis. Ribosomal RNA remains part of the molecular machine and provides important surfaces for the recognition and chemical reactions. Messenger RNA enters, is read and can leave; transfer RNAs enter and leave repeatedly; ribosomal RNA is a relatively persistent component of the particle. The distinction between these three roles is particularly important when interpreting an experiment that measures total cellular RNA. A change in RNA abundance is not necessarily a change in the amount of information available as mRNA.

The word organelle is sometimes used broadly for a recognizable functional cellular structure. In that broad sense the ribosome may be called a nonmembranous organelle. Unlike a mitochondrion or lysosome, however, it does not enclose its own membrane-bounded lumen. Substrates and soluble translation factors approach through exposed molecular surfaces. In eukaryotes, ribosomes associated with the cytosolic face of the endoplasmic reticulum have essentially the same cytosolic ribosome architecture as free ribosomes. Their association with a membrane does not turn them into membrane-enclosed particles. It reflects the destination and targeting information of the polypeptide that is being produced.

A useful mental model is to imagine a working bench that accepts a moving strip of information, holds several molecular adaptors at neighboring locations and provides a reaction surface. The analogy has limits. A ribosome is not a rigid bench with externally operated tools. Its components undergo small and large movements as substrates bind, reactions occur and factors interact. Moreover, the position of the information strip is physically coupled to the position of the adaptors through base pairing. Structural change is therefore part of the mechanism. We will use the labels for the major parts as a means of reasoning about this movement, not as a claim that the ribosome is motionless.

In the preceding chapters the genetic code and aminoacylation establish why particular tRNAs represent particular amino acids. Those assignments are prerequisites for this chapter. We now ask how the ribosome uses them. Later chapters examine the individual initiation and elongation factors, termination, inhibitors and regulation. Here the objective is to build a spatial and physical foundation on which all those mechanisms can be understood. The distinction matters because a mutation in a synthetase, a mutation in the decoding center and a mutation in a peptide-bond-forming region can all reduce the quality of protein synthesis, but they disturb different operations.

## 2. How particulate protein synthesis became an experimental question

The existence of proteins does not by itself tell us where the bonds joining their amino acids are formed. During the development of molecular biology, investigators had to distinguish reactions occurring in freely dissolved enzyme mixtures from reactions associated with microscopic cellular particles. Electron microscopy made it possible to visualize objects much smaller than the resolution of a conventional light microscope. In the 1950s, George Palade described small dense particles that were found free in the cytoplasm and associated with membranes of the endoplasmic reticulum. The observation established a structural feature of cells, but appearance alone could not establish its biochemical role. A dense particle could have been a storage aggregate, a site of chemical activity or an assembly with some other function.

A complementary approach was to break cells gently and separate their contents by centrifugation. Particles with different sedimentation behavior could be enriched in different fractions. An experimenter could then ask whether incorporation of a labeled amino acid into material having the properties of protein depended on a particular fraction. Early cell-free studies associated with Paul Zamecnik and colleagues were essential in establishing a biochemical approach to protein synthesis. They permitted investigators to distinguish a particulate component from soluble factors and to test the requirements of the system by combining fractions. The logical advance was to replace an observation of protein production in intact living tissue with a system that could be perturbed and reconstituted.

A well-designed fractionation experiment needs more than a tube that contains many particles and produces a radioactive signal. A label might bind to a particle without becoming part of a newly made protein. It might be attached to a transfer RNA rather than to the completed chain. Investigators therefore need an endpoint appropriate to the chemical question, such as label incorporation into protein after separation from unincorporated amino acid, with appropriate controls. An untreated complete system establishes that the preparation can synthesize product. Omission of the particulate fraction tests whether soluble components alone are sufficient, and restoration of the fraction tests whether activity can be recovered. The addition of a nonfunctional fraction of similar composition can help distinguish a specific activity from a nonspecific stabilization effect. These are general principles for reading historical fractionation studies, rather than a claim that every early investigator used precisely the same control set.

The early microscopy and biochemical experiments answered related but distinct questions. Microscopy located particles and described their relationship to membranes. Cell-free protein synthesis associated an enriched cellular fraction with the ability to produce protein. Neither observation required that every ribosomal constituent be catalytic. In fact, once it was known that ribosomes contained both RNA and proteins, it remained possible that RNA formed a supporting structure while one or more ribosomal proteins acted as the actual catalyst. This was a reasonable hypothesis in an era in which most well-understood biological catalysts were proteins. A later section considers why that interpretation was revised.

The idea of an informational message also needed experimental support. One way of testing whether a preexisting translation apparatus can read a new template is to supply a defined RNA to a cell-free system and examine the product. Nirenberg and Matthaei's 1961 work with polyuridylic acid demonstrated the production of polyphenylalanine in a bacterial cell-free system. The repeated template provided a particularly clear relationship between its repeated codons and the repeated amino acid in the product. For the present topic the key implication is that a ribosomal apparatus does not have to be permanently dedicated to a particular protein. A common translation machinery can respond to different information-bearing RNAs. The full interpretation of codon assignments belongs to the genetic-code chapter.

The historical progression illustrates an important principle of experimental reasoning. Finding a component at a site where a process occurs is evidence of association. Removing it and losing activity suggests necessity, provided that the removal has not also damaged other necessary components. Restoring it and recovering activity strengthens a causal interpretation. Demonstrating an isolated chemical activity under defined conditions is a different level of evidence again. Finally, structural localization of the substrates can show how that activity is organized at molecular resolution. The understanding of ribosomes resulted from converging evidence at all these levels, not from a single observation that made the complete mechanism obvious.

The same reasoning applies to a present-day examination question. Suppose a mutation reduces incorporation of labeled amino acids into protein and cells show fewer intact ribosomes. The observations are consistent with impaired assembly of the machine. They do not establish that the mutated molecule directly participates in peptide-bond chemistry. A specific catalytic claim requires separation of the effects of assembly, substrate binding and chemical activity. The experimental framework introduced by early studies remains useful because it teaches us to distinguish the physical existence of a ribosome from the activity of its individual regions.

## 3. Sedimentation: what a ribosome size designation actually measures

The conventional names 30S, 50S, 70S, 40S, 60S and 80S arose from physical measurements, not from counting constituent proteins or nucleotides. In a centrifuge, a dissolved or suspended particle experiences an effective outward force in the rotating frame. It migrates radially through the liquid while experiencing resistance to that motion. The rate depends on properties of the particle and the solvent as well as the speed of rotation. A larger particle often sediments more rapidly than a smaller one, but the relationship is not a simple mass scale. A compact molecule and an elongated molecule with the same mass may move at different rates because their frictional properties differ.

To separate the effect of the applied field from properties of the particle, the sedimentation coefficient s is defined as the radial sedimentation velocity divided by the centrifugal acceleration. If the radial distance is r and the angular velocity is omega, the acceleration is omega squared times r. A particle moving at velocity u therefore has s = u/(omega squared times r). The SI dimension is time: metres per second divided by metres per second squared leaves seconds. The unit commonly used for biological particles is the Svedberg, named after Theodor Svedberg, with one S equal to ten to the power minus thirteen seconds. This small time unit is not the duration of centrifugation. It is a convenient scale for expressing the normalized velocity.

A 70S particle consequently has a conventional coefficient of 7.0 × 10^-12 seconds under the relevant reference conditions. Suppose, as a hypothetical calculation, that an experiment uses an angular velocity of 4,000 radians per second and examines a position 0.10 metres from the rotation axis. The acceleration there is 1.6 × 10^6 metres per second squared. If the particle has the stated coefficient under these conditions, its local radial velocity is 7.0 × 10^-12 seconds multiplied by that acceleration, or 1.12 × 10^-5 metres per second. This is 11.2 micrometres per second. The velocity changes with radial position if the angular velocity remains fixed, so this value is local rather than an average for the entire tube. The calculation also assumes that the coefficient can be treated as constant in the part of the solution being considered.

The physical origins of this coefficient explain why it is not additive. For a particle of mass m, its effective centrifugal force in a solvent is reduced by the buoyancy corresponding to the displaced solvent. Using a partial specific volume v-bar for the particle and solvent density rho, the effective force is m(1 − v-bar rho) times the centrifugal acceleration. The dimensionless factor in parentheses accounts for the solvent displacement. A moving particle also experiences friction; for the linear low-speed regime, the frictional force is f times u, where f is the translational frictional coefficient. Balancing the forces gives u = m(1 − v-bar rho) omega squared r/f. Division by the centrifugal acceleration yields s = m(1 − v-bar rho)/f. If molar mass M is used rather than mass per particle, m is replaced by M/N_A, where N_A is Avogadro's constant.

This equation relates the measured behavior to the architecture of the particle. The numerator is associated with effective particle mass. The denominator depends on factors such as hydrodynamic size, shape, hydration and solvent viscosity. When two ribosomal subunits associate, their masses add in the ordinary physical sense, provided that we account for other bound or released components. Their frictional coefficients do not have to add. The joined object presents a different shape to the solvent. Its surface and hydration can change, and the relation between the two shapes is not captured by arithmetic addition of separate coefficients. It follows that two particles with coefficients of 30S and 50S can form a particle designated 70S without any loss of mass required by the numbers. The same reasoning explains why 40S and 60S can form an 80S particle.

In a simplified case where the buoyant masses of two particles are equal and their separate friction coefficients differ by a factor of two, their sedimentation coefficients will differ by the reciprocal factor of two. Association can generate a complex with a coefficient greater than either individual component but not necessarily equal to their sum. It is incorrect to assign a universal fraction of the sum to every macromolecular complex. The bacterial and eukaryotic ribosome labels are empirical conventional descriptions, not a numerical formula for predicting a new assembly. Furthermore, the coefficient may differ with experimental conditions. The use of a conventional size class should not obscure the effect of solvent density, viscosity, temperature and macromolecular association.

A second quantitative relationship is obtained by using the diffusion coefficient D. In a dilute solution at equilibrium the Einstein relation gives D = k_B T/f, with k_B the Boltzmann constant and T the absolute temperature. Substitution for f in the sedimentation equation gives s/D = m(1 − v-bar rho)/(k_B T). Replacing the single-particle mass and Boltzmann constant with molar quantities, and using R = N_A k_B, yields M = sRT/[D(1 − v-bar rho)]. This result explains why sedimentation and diffusion together can give information about molar mass even though a sedimentation coefficient alone is not a mass measurement. The assumptions include a well-defined species, suitable dilute-solution behavior and knowledge of the solvent correction. A mixture undergoing reversible association cannot automatically be interpreted by assigning a single coefficient to all components.

The physical lesson also provides a bridge to the examination methods unit. An observation that a ribosomal particle shifts to a smaller sedimentation coefficient might mean dissociation, degradation or an altered shape; a specific mechanism is not established by the number alone. Conversely, a heavier sedimentation class might reflect aggregation rather than a normal translating assembly. If the exam provides complementary evidence such as RNA composition, protein composition, ionic dependence or functional activity, that evidence helps discriminate among explanations. When you see S in a ribosome question, first recognize a sedimentation unit and only then interpret it in the context of the molecule.

## 4. Two subunits: division of labor rather than two independent enzymes

A functional cytosolic ribosome has two unequal subunits. They are often pictured as two rounded objects with a cleft between them, but that simplified drawing should be read as a map of functional interfaces, not a literal shape. The small subunit provides an organized path for the message and an important region for evaluating the interaction between a codon and an anticodon. The large subunit provides the chemical center at which the amino-acid-bearing ends of transfer RNAs are brought together. The space between them accommodates the adaptors, whose elongated three-dimensional structures span the functional distance between the two regions.

This organization solves a problem created by the geometry of transfer RNA itself. The anticodon of a tRNA lies far from the terminal adenosine to which the amino acid is attached. If the two functions occurred at the same tiny active site, the molecule would need to present both ends to one surface in an incompatible arrangement. Instead, the anticodon-containing end is oriented toward the message associated with the small subunit. The acceptor end is oriented toward the large subunit. This spatial separation allows a recognition event at one end of the adaptor to control whether its other end participates in chemistry. It explains how the physical shape of tRNA is related to its role as an adaptor.

The two subunits are not permanently connected. Their association creates surfaces that do not exist in a single isolated subunit in the same way. The initiation process provides a biological setting in which an appropriate message and initiator tRNA become positioned and the complete ribosome is assembled. At the end of protein synthesis the subunits can be separated and reused. A cell therefore does not have to construct an entirely new ribosome each time a polypeptide is produced. Assembly into a functional translating complex and biogenesis of the ribosomal subunits are different processes: the former can recur during successive translation events, while the latter produces the ribosomal particles from RNA and proteins.

Interactions between the two subunits are largely noncovalent. Charged groups, hydrogen bonding, other short-range interactions and the effects of hydrated ions influence their association. Ribosomal RNA contains many negatively charged phosphate groups. Divalent magnesium ions and other ions contribute to the ionic environment that permits appropriate folding and assembly. If a preparation loses magnesium, the electrostatic and structural conditions change and subunit association may weaken. At a suitable magnesium concentration, separated subunits can reassociate. However, excessive magnesium or inappropriate ionic conditions can also alter dynamics and fidelity. Thus maximum structural stability does not necessarily correspond to optimal biological activity.

A functional interpretation must distinguish the major division of labor from absolute independence. The small subunit does not synthesize a protein by itself merely because it can hold a message. The large subunit can support certain peptide-transfer model reactions under suitable conditions, but without the correctly positioned information and adaptors it cannot carry out full sequence-directed translation. At the interface, structural changes can couple events in the two regions. A perturbation on the small subunit can therefore alter chemistry indirectly, while a perturbation on the large subunit may change tRNA accommodation or movement and consequently affect decoding. The labels decoding subunit and catalytic subunit are meaningful shorthand, not proof that each region performs a completely isolated task.

The arrangement is conserved across cellular life even though ribosomes from different organisms differ in size, composition and peripheral structures. This conservation implies strong functional constraints: the orientation of the mRNA, the positions of the adaptor ends and the chemistry of peptide transfer must remain compatible. Changes outside the conserved functional core can modify interactions with organism-specific translation factors and cellular environments. Conservation of an operation does not require absolute identity of every molecule that supports it. This distinction underlies the comparison between bacterial, archaeal, eukaryotic cytosolic and organellar ribosomes.
