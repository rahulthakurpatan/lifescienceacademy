# Ribosome Architecture and the Molecular Basis of Translation

## 1. The problem that a ribosome solves

A cell can copy the information in DNA into RNA without making a protein. Transcription preserves a sequence of nucleotides, even when the original DNA is copied into a chemically different polymer. Translation requires a fundamentally different kind of correspondence. A linear message written using four nucleotide bases must direct the assembly of a linear product written using amino acids. An amino acid does not possess a nucleotide-like surface that can recognize its assigned codon directly. The ribosome solves this difficulty by bringing an informational molecule, a set of adaptors and a chemical reaction into a single organized space. Its structure makes these three activities mutually dependent rather than three unrelated biochemical events.

The messenger RNA provides the sequence. A transfer RNA provides the bridge between a nucleotide triplet and an amino acid, because one end of the folded transfer RNA carries an anticodon while the other can carry an amino acid. The specific amino acid is attached before the transfer RNA reaches the ribosome. The ribosome therefore receives an adaptor that already represents a chemical assignment. Its responsibility is to examine the appropriate message–adaptor interaction, place the amino acid in a suitable position for chemistry and preserve the reading frame during movement to the next triplet. Understanding where these operations occur is more useful than simply learning that the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis.

Consider a cell that must make two proteins containing the same amino acids in different orders. A reaction that joins amino acids indiscriminately would produce many possible sequences and would not distinguish the two genes. The cell must instead create a transient spatial arrangement in which one particular amino acid is presented as the next building block while the growing chain is retained. If the chemical reaction occurred before checking the adaptor, an incorrect amino acid would often be incorporated. If the chain were released after each bond formed, translation would have to find the same growing molecule again. If the message moved by two or four nucleotides instead of three, subsequent assignments would change. These are design constraints imposed by the problem of sequence-directed polymerization.

A ribosome is a ribonucleoprotein particle. The term means that the functional assembly contains RNA and protein rather than being composed exclusively of one class of macromolecule. Its RNA is not a disposable copy of a gene that happens to be present during synthesis. Ribosomal RNA remains part of the molecular machine and provides important surfaces for the recognition and chemical reactions. Messenger RNA enters, is read and can leave; transfer RNAs enter and leave repeatedly; ribosomal RNA is a relatively persistent component of the particle. The distinction between these three roles is particularly important when interpreting an experiment that measures total cellular RNA. A change in RNA abundance is not necessarily a change in the amount of information available as mRNA.

The word organelle is sometimes used broadly for a recognizable functional cellular structure. In that broad sense the ribosome may be called a nonmembranous organelle. Unlike a mitochondrion or lysosome, however, it does not enclose its own membrane-bounded lumen. Substrates and soluble translation factors approach through exposed molecular surfaces. In eukaryotes, ribosomes associated with the cytosolic face of the endoplasmic reticulum have essentially the same cytosolic ribosome architecture as free ribosomes. Their association with a membrane does not turn them into membrane-enclosed particles. It reflects the destination and targeting information of the polypeptide that is being produced.

A useful mental model is to imagine a working bench that accepts a moving strip of information, holds several molecular adaptors at neighboring locations and provides a reaction surface. The analogy has limits. A ribosome is not a rigid bench with externally operated tools. Its components undergo small and large movements as substrates bind, reactions occur and factors interact. Moreover, the position of the information strip is physically coupled to the position of the adaptors through base pairing. Structural change is therefore part of the mechanism. We will use the labels for the major parts as a means of reasoning about this movement, not as a claim that the ribosome is motionless.

In the preceding chapters the genetic code and aminoacylation establish why particular tRNAs represent particular amino acids. Those assignments are prerequisites for this chapter. We now ask how the ribosome uses them. Later chapters examine the individual initiation and elongation factors, termination, inhibitors and regulation. Here the objective is to build a spatial and physical foundation on which all those mechanisms can be understood. The distinction matters because a mutation in a synthetase, a mutation in the decoding center and a mutation in a peptide-bond-forming region can all reduce the quality of protein synthesis, but they disturb different operations.

## 2. How particulate protein synthesis became an experimental question

The existence of proteins does not by itself tell us where the bonds joining their amino acids are formed. During the development of molecular biology, investigators had to distinguish reactions occurring in freely dissolved enzyme mixtures from reactions associated with microscopic cellular particles. Electron microscopy made it possible to visualize objects much smaller than the resolution of a conventional light microscope. In the 1950s, George Palade described small dense particles that were found free in the cytoplasm and associated with membranes of the endoplasmic reticulum. The observation established a structural feature of cells, but appearance alone could not establish its biochemical role. A dense particle could have been a storage aggregate, a site of chemical activity or an assembly with some other function.

A complementary approach was to break cells gently and separate their contents by centrifugation. Particles with different sedimentation behavior could be enriched in different fractions. An experimenter could then ask whether incorporation of a labeled amino acid into material having the properties of protein depended on a particular fraction. Early cell-free studies associated with Paul Zamecnik and colleagues were essential in establishing a biochemical approach to protein synthesis. They permitted investigators to distinguish a particulate component from soluble factors and to test the requirements of the system by combining fractions. The logical advance was to replace an observation of protein production in intact living tissue with a system that could be perturbed and reconstituted.

A well-designed fractionation experiment needs more than a tube that contains many particles and produces a radioactive signal. A label might bind to a particle without becoming part of a newly made protein. It might be attached to a transfer RNA rather than to the completed chain. Investigators therefore need an endpoint appropriate to the chemical question, such as label incorporation into protein after separation from unincorporated amino acid, with appropriate controls. An untreated complete system establishes that the preparation can synthesize product. Omission of the particulate fraction tests whether soluble components alone are sufficient, and restoration of the fraction tests whether activity can be recovered. The addition of a nonfunctional fraction of similar composition can help distinguish a specific activity from a nonspecific stabilization effect. These are general principles for reading historical fractionation studies, rather than a claim that every early investigator used precisely the same control set.

The early microscopy and biochemical experiments answered related but distinct questions. Microscopy located particles and described their relationship to membranes. Cell-free protein synthesis associated an enriched cellular fraction with the ability to produce protein. Neither observation required that every ribosomal constituent be catalytic. In fact, once it was known that ribosomes contained both RNA and proteins, it remained possible that RNA formed a supporting structure while one or more ribosomal proteins acted as the actual catalyst. This was a reasonable hypothesis in an era in which most well-understood biological catalysts were proteins. A later section considers why that interpretation was revised.

The idea of an informational message also needed experimental support. One way of testing whether a preexisting translation apparatus can read a new template is to supply a defined RNA to a cell-free system and examine the product. Nirenberg and Matthaei's 1961 work with polyuridylic acid demonstrated the production of polyphenylalanine in a bacterial cell-free system. The repeated template provided a particularly clear relationship between its repeated codons and the repeated amino acid in the product. For the present topic the key implication is that a ribosomal apparatus does not have to be permanently dedicated to a particular protein. A common translation machinery can respond to different information-bearing RNAs. The full interpretation of codon assignments belongs to the genetic-code chapter.

The historical progression illustrates an important principle of experimental reasoning. Finding a component at a site where a process occurs is evidence of association. Removing it and losing activity suggests necessity, provided that the removal has not also damaged other necessary components. Restoring it and recovering activity strengthens a causal interpretation. Demonstrating an isolated chemical activity under defined conditions is a different level of evidence again. Finally, structural localization of the substrates can show how that activity is organized at molecular resolution. The understanding of ribosomes resulted from converging evidence at all these levels, not from a single observation that made the complete mechanism obvious.

The same reasoning applies to a present-day examination question. Suppose a mutation reduces incorporation of labeled amino acids into protein and cells show fewer intact ribosomes. The observations are consistent with impaired assembly of the machine. They do not establish that the mutated molecule directly participates in peptide-bond chemistry. A specific catalytic claim requires separation of the effects of assembly, substrate binding and chemical activity. The experimental framework introduced by early studies remains useful because it teaches us to distinguish the physical existence of a ribosome from the activity of its individual regions.

## 3. Sedimentation: what a ribosome size designation actually measures

The conventional names 30S, 50S, 70S, 40S, 60S and 80S arose from physical measurements, not from counting constituent proteins or nucleotides. In a centrifuge, a dissolved or suspended particle experiences an effective outward force in the rotating frame. It migrates radially through the liquid while experiencing resistance to that motion. The rate depends on properties of the particle and the solvent as well as the speed of rotation. A larger particle often sediments more rapidly than a smaller one, but the relationship is not a simple mass scale. A compact molecule and an elongated molecule with the same mass may move at different rates because their frictional properties differ.

To separate the effect of the applied field from properties of the particle, the sedimentation coefficient s is defined as the radial sedimentation velocity divided by the centrifugal acceleration. If the radial distance is r and the angular velocity is omega, the acceleration is omega squared times r. A particle moving at velocity u therefore has s = u/(omega squared times r). The SI dimension is time: metres per second divided by metres per second squared leaves seconds. The unit commonly used for biological particles is the Svedberg, named after Theodor Svedberg, with one S equal to ten to the power minus thirteen seconds. This small time unit is not the duration of centrifugation. It is a convenient scale for expressing the normalized velocity.

A 70S particle consequently has a conventional coefficient of 7.0 × 10^-12 seconds under the relevant reference conditions. Suppose, as a hypothetical calculation, that an experiment uses an angular velocity of 4,000 radians per second and examines a position 0.10 metres from the rotation axis. The acceleration there is 1.6 × 10^6 metres per second squared. If the particle has the stated coefficient under these conditions, its local radial velocity is 7.0 × 10^-12 seconds multiplied by that acceleration, or 1.12 × 10^-5 metres per second. This is 11.2 micrometres per second. The velocity changes with radial position if the angular velocity remains fixed, so this value is local rather than an average for the entire tube. The calculation also assumes that the coefficient can be treated as constant in the part of the solution being considered.

The physical origins of this coefficient explain why it is not additive. For a particle of mass m, its effective centrifugal force in a solvent is reduced by the buoyancy corresponding to the displaced solvent. Using a partial specific volume v-bar for the particle and solvent density rho, the effective force is m(1 − v-bar rho) times the centrifugal acceleration. The dimensionless factor in parentheses accounts for the solvent displacement. A moving particle also experiences friction; for the linear low-speed regime, the frictional force is f times u, where f is the translational frictional coefficient. Balancing the forces gives u = m(1 − v-bar rho) omega squared r/f. Division by the centrifugal acceleration yields s = m(1 − v-bar rho)/f. If molar mass M is used rather than mass per particle, m is replaced by M/N_A, where N_A is Avogadro's constant.

This equation relates the measured behavior to the architecture of the particle. The numerator is associated with effective particle mass. The denominator depends on factors such as hydrodynamic size, shape, hydration and solvent viscosity. When two ribosomal subunits associate, their masses add in the ordinary physical sense, provided that we account for other bound or released components. Their frictional coefficients do not have to add. The joined object presents a different shape to the solvent. Its surface and hydration can change, and the relation between the two shapes is not captured by arithmetic addition of separate coefficients. It follows that two particles with coefficients of 30S and 50S can form a particle designated 70S without any loss of mass required by the numbers. The same reasoning explains why 40S and 60S can form an 80S particle.

In a simplified case where the buoyant masses of two particles are equal and their separate friction coefficients differ by a factor of two, their sedimentation coefficients will differ by the reciprocal factor of two. Association can generate a complex with a coefficient greater than either individual component but not necessarily equal to their sum. It is incorrect to assign a universal fraction of the sum to every macromolecular complex. The bacterial and eukaryotic ribosome labels are empirical conventional descriptions, not a numerical formula for predicting a new assembly. Furthermore, the coefficient may differ with experimental conditions. The use of a conventional size class should not obscure the effect of solvent density, viscosity, temperature and macromolecular association.

A second quantitative relationship is obtained by using the diffusion coefficient D. In a dilute solution at equilibrium the Einstein relation gives D = k_B T/f, with k_B the Boltzmann constant and T the absolute temperature. Substitution for f in the sedimentation equation gives s/D = m(1 − v-bar rho)/(k_B T). Replacing the single-particle mass and Boltzmann constant with molar quantities, and using R = N_A k_B, yields M = sRT/[D(1 − v-bar rho)]. This result explains why sedimentation and diffusion together can give information about molar mass even though a sedimentation coefficient alone is not a mass measurement. The assumptions include a well-defined species, suitable dilute-solution behavior and knowledge of the solvent correction. A mixture undergoing reversible association cannot automatically be interpreted by assigning a single coefficient to all components.

The physical lesson also provides a bridge to the examination methods unit. An observation that a ribosomal particle shifts to a smaller sedimentation coefficient might mean dissociation, degradation or an altered shape; a specific mechanism is not established by the number alone. Conversely, a heavier sedimentation class might reflect aggregation rather than a normal translating assembly. If the exam provides complementary evidence such as RNA composition, protein composition, ionic dependence or functional activity, that evidence helps discriminate among explanations. When you see S in a ribosome question, first recognize a sedimentation unit and only then interpret it in the context of the molecule.

## 4. Two subunits: division of labor rather than two independent enzymes

A functional cytosolic ribosome has two unequal subunits. They are often pictured as two rounded objects with a cleft between them, but that simplified drawing should be read as a map of functional interfaces, not a literal shape. The small subunit provides an organized path for the message and an important region for evaluating the interaction between a codon and an anticodon. The large subunit provides the chemical center at which the amino-acid-bearing ends of transfer RNAs are brought together. The space between them accommodates the adaptors, whose elongated three-dimensional structures span the functional distance between the two regions.

This organization solves a problem created by the geometry of transfer RNA itself. The anticodon of a tRNA lies far from the terminal adenosine to which the amino acid is attached. If the two functions occurred at the same tiny active site, the molecule would need to present both ends to one surface in an incompatible arrangement. Instead, the anticodon-containing end is oriented toward the message associated with the small subunit. The acceptor end is oriented toward the large subunit. This spatial separation allows a recognition event at one end of the adaptor to control whether its other end participates in chemistry. It explains how the physical shape of tRNA is related to its role as an adaptor.

The two subunits are not permanently connected. Their association creates surfaces that do not exist in a single isolated subunit in the same way. The initiation process provides a biological setting in which an appropriate message and initiator tRNA become positioned and the complete ribosome is assembled. At the end of protein synthesis the subunits can be separated and reused. A cell therefore does not have to construct an entirely new ribosome each time a polypeptide is produced. Assembly into a functional translating complex and biogenesis of the ribosomal subunits are different processes: the former can recur during successive translation events, while the latter produces the ribosomal particles from RNA and proteins.

Interactions between the two subunits are largely noncovalent. Charged groups, hydrogen bonding, other short-range interactions and the effects of hydrated ions influence their association. Ribosomal RNA contains many negatively charged phosphate groups. Divalent magnesium ions and other ions contribute to the ionic environment that permits appropriate folding and assembly. If a preparation loses magnesium, the electrostatic and structural conditions change and subunit association may weaken. At a suitable magnesium concentration, separated subunits can reassociate. However, excessive magnesium or inappropriate ionic conditions can also alter dynamics and fidelity. Thus maximum structural stability does not necessarily correspond to optimal biological activity.

A functional interpretation must distinguish the major division of labor from absolute independence. The small subunit does not synthesize a protein by itself merely because it can hold a message. The large subunit can support certain peptide-transfer model reactions under suitable conditions, but without the correctly positioned information and adaptors it cannot carry out full sequence-directed translation. At the interface, structural changes can couple events in the two regions. A perturbation on the small subunit can therefore alter chemistry indirectly, while a perturbation on the large subunit may change tRNA accommodation or movement and consequently affect decoding. The labels decoding subunit and catalytic subunit are meaningful shorthand, not proof that each region performs a completely isolated task.

The arrangement is conserved across cellular life even though ribosomes from different organisms differ in size, composition and peripheral structures. This conservation implies strong functional constraints: the orientation of the mRNA, the positions of the adaptor ends and the chemistry of peptide transfer must remain compatible. Changes outside the conserved functional core can modify interactions with organism-specific translation factors and cellular environments. Conservation of an operation does not require absolute identity of every molecule that supports it. This distinction underlies the comparison between bacterial, archaeal, eukaryotic cytosolic and organellar ribosomes.


## 5. The bacterial 70S particle and its constituent RNAs

The classical bacterial ribosome is described as a 70S particle assembled from a 30S small subunit and a 50S large subunit. Escherichia coli provides the conventional reference system for many textbook descriptions. It is important to specify this reference because bacteria are diverse and not every species has exactly the same set of ribosomal proteins. The conserved organizational principles are much more important than memorizing a universal protein count.

The principal RNA of the 30S subunit is 16S ribosomal RNA. The size designation refers to the conventional sedimentation behavior of this RNA species, not to the coefficient of the complete small subunit. In E. coli the 16S RNA contains approximately 1,540 nucleotides. A linear molecule of that length would be unsuitable as a compact recognition machine if it did not fold. Complementary regions within the RNA form helices through base pairing. Other regions form loops, turns and junctions that help arrange the helices in three dimensions. Ribosomal proteins stabilize this organization and help produce the mature small subunit. The resulting surface contains regions involved in message binding, tRNA recognition and contacts with the large subunit.

The large bacterial subunit contains two principal RNA species, 23S rRNA and 5S rRNA. In E. coli the former is approximately 2,900 nucleotides long and the latter about 120. The 23S RNA is much more than a structural filler: it contains the conserved peptidyl-transferase center and important regions participating in tRNA positioning. The smaller 5S RNA contributes to the organization of the large subunit and its interactions with ribosomal proteins. One should not infer that the smallest RNA must have the least biological importance. An interaction network can depend critically on a short component that helps position or stabilize larger structures.

The conventional E. coli count of small-subunit proteins is about 21, while the large subunit contains roughly 33 distinct proteins, with some variation in counting conventions and species. Older and newer descriptions may differ because proteins have been renamed, some structural proteins occur in multiple copies and certain components vary among organisms. The examination distinction worth retaining is that the small subunit contains 16S rRNA and associated proteins, whereas the large subunit contains 23S and 5S rRNAs and associated proteins. If a question specifies an organism and asks for a precise protein count, the organism-specific description should take precedence over an approximate generalization.

An interesting feature of bacterial rRNAs is that many are synthesized as parts of longer precursor transcripts before maturation. In E. coli, 16S, 23S and 5S rRNAs can be produced from common ribosomal RNA operons, with processing generating individual functional molecules. This coordinates their supply. The term operon describes the organization of genes and their transcription, while the assembled ribosome is the product of expression and maturation. An rRNA precursor is not a 70S particle. The RNA must undergo processing and association with proteins before the resulting structure can carry out its full role. These maturation mechanisms were covered in the RNA-processing part of the curriculum and need not be repeated here in molecular detail.

Why are rRNAs so extensive when the most immediately recognizable decoding event involves only three bases of an mRNA? The short codon–anticodon duplex is part of a much larger mechanical problem. The ribosome must position the message, admit an adaptor, maintain the growing chain, allow movement and interact with soluble factors. A recognition surface alone would not provide a stable pathway for the rest of these activities. The large folded RNAs provide extensive geometry on which localized sites can be organized. The significance of an individual nucleotide is thus dependent on the three-dimensional context created by many other nucleotides and associated proteins.

The bacterial particle also illustrates that a single cellular component can be both highly conserved and selectively targetable. A conserved rRNA region may have closely related functions in organisms with very different genomes, yet small changes in neighboring residues or in associated protein surfaces can change the binding of an external molecule. That distinction will be useful when considering why a drug can preferentially interfere with bacterial protein synthesis without acting equally on the eukaryotic cytosolic machinery.

## 6. The eukaryotic 80S cytosolic ribosome

The conventional eukaryotic cytosolic particle is 80S, made from a 40S small subunit and a 60S large subunit. Its overall organization is recognizable as the same basic molecular solution that we encountered in bacteria. The small subunit binds the message and supports decoding; the large subunit provides the peptide-transfer center and the path taken by the newly growing polypeptide. The different S values describe a different physical assembly, not a change in the number of nucleotides read per codon or the direction of protein synthesis.

The 40S subunit contains 18S rRNA as its principal RNA. This RNA has evolutionary and functional relationships to bacterial 16S rRNA. The 60S subunit contains the conventional 28S rRNA of animals, 5.8S rRNA and 5S rRNA. Some organisms have other conventional names for the largest cytosolic rRNA; budding yeast, for example, is usually described as having 25S rather than 28S rRNA. The precise sequence and length may differ, but the relationship to the bacterial large-subunit rRNA is conserved. A student encountering a 25S label in a yeast problem should therefore not classify the particle as a bacterial ribosome simply because the expected mammalian 28S label is absent.

The 5.8S rRNA makes base-pairing and structural contacts with the large rRNA. It is evolutionarily related to part of the larger bacterial rRNA rather than being an unrelated innovation performing an entirely independent reaction. In typical eukaryotic cells, the major 18S, 5.8S and 28S molecules are processed from a precursor produced by RNA polymerase I, while the 5S molecule is produced separately by RNA polymerase III. A consequence is that a disturbance to polymerase I can reduce the supply of several components simultaneously, even if the 5S transcription system remains intact. Conversely, the continued production of 5S alone cannot guarantee that complete large subunits will be assembled.

Eukaryotic ribosomes include a substantial number of proteins, often approximately eighty distinct proteins in the complete cytosolic particle, depending on species and counting conventions. Many proteins are evolutionarily related to components of archaeal ribosomes, whereas others show broader or more restricted relationships. The RNA also includes expanded regions outside the ancient conserved core. Expansion segments can provide additional structural surfaces and interaction possibilities without replacing the core mechanism of decoding and peptide transfer. It is not scientifically justified to assume that all such additional material is needed merely to read a triplet that a bacterial ribosome can also read.

The term cytosolic ribosome is useful even for ribosomes associated with the rough endoplasmic reticulum. The cytosolic surface of the ER allows a translating ribosome to interact with a membrane protein-conducting machinery. The protein chain may enter the ER lumen or become integrated into its membrane, depending on targeting and topology information. However, the ribosome doing the synthesis is still an 80S ribosome of the cytosolic type. This distinction allows one to classify the particle correctly even when the product ends up in a compartment that does not itself contain free cytosolic ribosomes.

Ribosome assembly in a typical eukaryotic cell requires coordination across intracellular compartments. Many ribosomal proteins are made by already functioning cytosolic ribosomes. They are transported into the nucleus, where they participate in assembly with rRNAs and associated factors. Subunit precursors are then exported and mature into functional cytosolic particles. This is a particularly informative example of biological continuity: functioning ribosomes help produce the protein components required for new ribosomes. The cell does not generate an active ribosome merely by transcribing a single gene. The problem of making a ribosome is distinct from the problem of using a ribosome to translate an mRNA.

An examination problem may report an accumulation of a nuclear large-subunit precursor together with a reduction in the amount of mature 60S subunits in the cytoplasm. Such a pattern suggests a defect in maturation or export rather than immediately proving a defect at the catalytic center. To support a more precise conclusion, it would be useful to examine processing intermediates and the distribution of assembly components. If isolated mature particles that do reach the cytoplasm retain normal activity, the evidence for a biogenesis defect becomes stronger. This reasoning depends on separating the existence and localization of a subunit from the biochemical events performed by an assembled translating ribosome.

## 7. Conserved core and variable architecture across cellular lineages

The labels prokaryotic and eukaryotic are useful in introductory cell biology, but the first label must not be confused with a single evolutionary lineage. Bacteria and archaea both lack a typical membrane-enclosed nucleus, yet their ribosomes have important molecular differences. Archaeal ribosomes generally belong to the 70S class with 30S and 50S subunits, but many archaeal ribosomal proteins and aspects of their translation apparatus are more closely related to those of eukaryotes than to their bacterial counterparts. An archaeal particle can therefore have a conventional sedimentation class resembling that of bacteria while differing in molecular features that determine factor interactions or sensitivity to particular inhibitors.

This is a useful example of the difference between classification by measured physical behavior and classification by evolutionary history. The numerical coefficient cannot reveal every aspect of molecular composition. Two different structures could have similar sedimentation coefficients if their effective masses and frictional properties compensate for their differences. Conversely, structures performing homologous functions could have substantially different sedimentation coefficients as a result of changes in peripheral regions, RNA lengths or protein content. A numerical resemblance is a clue that must be interpreted together with molecular evidence, not a complete account of ancestry.

Small-subunit rRNA sequences are especially valuable for evolutionary comparisons because these molecules are widely present, perform essential functions and contain both conserved and variable regions. Conserved sequences help establish relationships and allow comparisons among organisms; variable regions provide information useful for distinguishing lineages. Bacterial and archaeal surveys frequently use 16S rRNA sequence data, whereas eukaryotic microbial studies commonly use 18S rRNA markers. The functional reason these molecules can be compared is that they belong to homologous translation systems. The methodological reason variable regions are informative is that changes have accumulated without destroying all essential functions.

Even here it is important to separate relatedness from identity. Highly conserved sequences can indicate that organisms share an ancient molecular apparatus, but they do not prove that two modern organisms belong to the same species or have identical physiology. Different genes may evolve at different rates, and a short fragment may not contain enough informative changes to resolve closely related organisms. In the context of ribosome architecture, the main point is that rRNA is both a structural and functional constituent of the particle and a source of evidence about evolution. The full theory of phylogenetic reconstruction belongs to the evolution and bioinformatics units.

The structural conservation of the ribosomal core is also relevant to hypotheses about the early history of life. A catalytic center organized principally by RNA is consistent with the possibility that RNA performed both informational and catalytic functions early in evolution. This possibility is often discussed under the RNA-world hypothesis. The existence of present-day RNA catalysis provides evidence that RNA is chemically capable of such a role, but does not directly demonstrate the exact chronological origin of a modern ribosome or the complete sequence of ancient evolutionary events. A careful answer distinguishes a supported present-day molecular mechanism from a historical model inferred from comparative evidence.

## 8. RNA folding, ribosomal proteins and the importance of assembly

Ribosomal RNA has a long negatively charged backbone and a sequence capable of forming many local and long-range interactions. The primary sequence is the linear arrangement of nucleotides. Secondary structure describes the pairing of residues, often represented by stems and loops. Tertiary structure describes the spatial packing of these elements and contacts that bring otherwise distant parts of the sequence close together. All three descriptions are necessary because a functional site may contain nucleotides that are far apart in the primary sequence yet adjacent in the folded particle.

To understand why a mutation can have an effect far from its position in a linear sequence, imagine that two segments of rRNA must pair to hold a third segment in an appropriate orientation. A single nucleotide substitution that weakens a critical pair may change the geometry of the entire region. The substituted nucleotide might not contact the message, tRNA or growing polypeptide directly. Nevertheless, the change could reduce the quality of decoding or the efficiency of peptide transfer because those activities depend on the structure supported by the mutated interaction. Conversely, a compensatory change in the pairing partner might restore the helix and rescue the function even though the original nucleotide sequence has not been restored. This is a powerful experimental test of a structural hypothesis.

The ribosomal proteins contribute to the stability, assembly and function of the RNA framework. Many have surfaces or extended regions that contact rRNA. Positively charged amino-acid side chains can help interact with the negatively charged phosphates of RNA, although the complete binding specificity also depends on shape, hydrogen bonding, other chemical interactions and the local ionic environment. Some proteins contribute to the positioning of particular RNA regions or interact with translation factors. Other proteins participate in interfaces that change during translation. Calling the ribosome a ribozyme does not mean that ribosomal proteins are dispensable for its ordinary cellular function.

Assembly imposes a different set of constraints from those of the final translation reaction. If a protein stabilizes an early folding intermediate, the final structure may never form properly when that protein is missing. An experiment that examines the absence of peptide synthesis after deleting the protein cannot by itself identify the protein as a direct catalyst. The same absence of activity would result if the substrate-binding architecture failed to assemble. This distinction is especially important when reasoning from mutations affecting essential macromolecular complexes. A component may be essential for the overall activity without being one of the atoms directly responsible for chemical transformation.

The role of magnesium illustrates the balance between electrostatic stabilization and dynamic behavior. Several adjacent phosphate groups repel one another if their charges are not sufficiently screened. Positively charged ions can stabilize folds that would otherwise be unfavorable. Some metal ions are associated closely with particular RNA environments; others contribute more diffusely to the surrounding ionic atmosphere. The dependence of RNA folding and assembly on ion concentration is therefore not surprising. But an experimentally observed dependence on magnesium does not by itself show that a magnesium ion directly catalyzes formation of the peptide bond. Structural stabilization, ligand binding and direct chemical catalysis are different mechanistic possibilities.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which a bacterial lysate is divided into three samples. The first is maintained under conditions that preserve intact 70S particles. The second is treated in a manner that lowers available magnesium and produces more separated 30S and 50S particles. The third is returned to the original ionic conditions, after removal of the perturbation, and is found to recover a substantial proportion of the 70S population. The result is consistent with a reversible, ion-dependent association process. To determine whether the recovered particles are functional, a translation assay is still necessary. Recovery of a sedimentation peak and recovery of biochemical function are related observations but not logically interchangeable.

Now suppose a second sample has been exposed to an RNA-degrading enzyme rather than an ionic perturbation. Returning the ion concentration to its original value may not restore the original RNA sequence or structure. An association defect can be reversible because the separated macromolecular components remain intact. A cleavage event can be irreversible under the same conditions because covalent bonds have been broken. The distinction helps explain why a failure to recover intact particles after a treatment can indicate damage rather than simple dissociation, although additional evidence is needed to identify which component was damaged.

Ribosomal proteins themselves are synthesized as polypeptides on existing ribosomes, so growth places a substantial demand on the translation apparatus. A cell that increases its protein synthesis capacity must allocate nutrients and energy to rRNA production, ribosomal protein production and assembly. This investment is useful when conditions support growth but can become wasteful when nutrients are scarce. The later chapter on translational regulation will examine how cells coordinate these demands. For now, the architecture makes clear why the amount of one component alone cannot determine the amount of functional ribosome: the relevant units must be made in compatible proportions and assembled correctly.


## 9. The message channel and the geometry of the reading frame

An mRNA is not held in the ribosome as an entire folded molecule. Only a limited region can occupy the message channel at any one time. The small subunit helps guide the chain through this region, and the relative positions of its bases make successive triplets available to tRNA anticodons. A codon is a property of a sequence as read in a particular register. An arbitrary set of three adjacent nucleotides is not necessarily the codon that the translating ribosome is currently interpreting. The initial positioning of the message establishes the register from which subsequent triplets are selected.

The orientation of the message is conventionally written from its 5′ end to its 3′ end. A ribosome progressing through an ordinary open reading frame reads in that direction. If the message is drawn with the 5′ end on the left, the start region is upstream of the stop region. A common source of confusion is that a structural picture may show the same molecule with the 5′ end on the right. The correct interpretation depends on labeled ends rather than on the left or right side of a diagram. The ribosome does not acquire a different biochemical direction because an illustrator rotates the picture.

The mRNA and tRNA anticodon strands pair antiparallel to one another. When the codon is written in the usual 5′ to 3′ orientation, its complementary anticodon beneath it must be read in the opposite direction if the bases are aligned in a pairing diagram. For instance, consider a message segment containing 5′-GCU-3′. A fully Watson–Crick-complementary anticodon can be written as 3′-CGA-5′ when placed directly beneath that sequence. Written independently in the conventional 5′ to 3′ direction, this anticodon is 5′-AGC-3′. Naturally occurring tRNAs may exploit wobble at the third codon position, so a sequence exercise must specify whether it is asking for strict complementarity or a set of possible biological decoders.

The small subunit holds neighboring codons in positions related to the binding sites occupied by the adaptors. If a growing chain is associated with a tRNA paired to one codon and the next adaptor is paired with the immediately downstream codon, their chemical ends can be brought into neighboring functional locations on the large subunit. The juxtaposition depends on the correct register. An incorrectly positioned message can lead to the wrong adaptor being selected even if the local codon–anticodon pairing at that moment is chemically correct. In other words, accuracy at the level of a single triplet is not identical to accuracy of the reading frame.

There are three kinds of sequence information that students often confuse when moving from transcription to translation. A transcription promoter helps specify where RNA polymerase initiates RNA synthesis. A translation initiation region helps position a ribosome on a message. The coding region specifies the succession of amino acids, beginning at an appropriate start codon and normally ending at a stop codon. A mutation in a promoter might reduce the amount of mRNA; a mutation in a translation initiation region might reduce the number of ribosomes that successfully begin on an unchanged amount of message; and a substitution in the coding region might change a residue without substantially changing RNA abundance. These are not necessarily exclusive effects, but the distinction provides a framework for designing experiments.

In many standard bacterial examples, a sequence upstream of the start codon can pair with a complementary region near the 3′ end of 16S rRNA. This is the Shine–Dalgarno interaction. It helps align the start region with the appropriate location on the small subunit. The interaction is a useful illustration of how a structural RNA can contribute to message positioning through complementary base pairing. It is not a universal requirement for every bacterial message. Leaderless messages and other initiation arrangements are important exceptions. In typical eukaryotic cytosolic cap-dependent initiation, the small subunit and its associated factors use a different route to identify a start site. Detailed comparisons of those routes are reserved for the next two nodes.

The message channel is also a selective environment. A stable secondary structure in the mRNA can present a physical obstacle to the progression of a translating complex, and interactions between the ribosome and the message can help disrupt some local base pairing as the coding sequence is fed into the decoding region. A folded segment of mRNA outside the ribosome is not equivalent to the accessible triplet presented to a tRNA inside it. A question describing an altered RNA structure therefore requires us to ask where the structure lies, whether it affects initial recruitment or later passage and whether the observed protein output can be attributed to either step. The existence of a normal coding sequence does not guarantee normal accessibility of that sequence.

The message is a temporary substrate rather than a permanent part of the ribosome. After a translating ribosome finishes, the same message may be read by another ribosome if the RNA remains intact. The ribosome can likewise move on to a different message. The independence of these two reusable components gives a cell flexibility. A fixed number of ribosomes can produce many different proteins by changing the distribution of ribosomes among available messages, rather than constructing a specialized translating particle for every gene. Some biological situations involve specialized ribosome populations or differential interactions, but the general architecture of a ribosome is not a separate genetic codebook for each protein.

## 10. Three binding sites and the changing roles of tRNAs

The three conventional tRNA-binding sites of the translating ribosome are the aminoacyl site, the peptidyl site and the exit site, abbreviated A, P and E. The names describe characteristic functions during the translation cycle. The A site generally accepts the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA during elongation. The P site commonly holds the tRNA carrying the chain that has been synthesized up to that point. The E site provides a location from which a deacylated tRNA can depart. These descriptions refer to particular states of a cycle, not permanent chemical definitions of everything that can occupy a site.

A critical point is that a tRNA binding site spans both subunits. The anticodon end is positioned near the message on the small subunit, while the acceptor end interacts with the large subunit. Thus asking whether the A site is on the small or the large subunit without further qualification can produce a misleading answer. The decoding center is associated mainly with the A-site codon–anticodon region of the small subunit. The A-site acceptor end, when fully accommodated, is positioned in the vicinity of the large-subunit peptidyl-transferase center. Both statements can be true because they concern different parts of the same adaptor.

A tRNA carrying a single amino acid is called an aminoacyl-tRNA. A tRNA bearing a polypeptide is called a peptidyl-tRNA. A tRNA with no amino acid or peptide attached is deacylated or uncharged. These terms describe the chemical state of the acceptor end. They must not be treated as permanent names for the individual tRNA molecule. The same molecule can pass through these states, leave the ribosome and be charged again for use in another cycle. In contrast, the anticodon and identity features are attributes of that tRNA species.

At the start of ordinary translation the specialized initiator tRNA is positioned in the P site, paired with the selected start codon. This is the key exception to the elementary rule that new charged tRNAs enter the A site. Ordinary elongator tRNAs are delivered through the A-site pathway, but initiation uses the P-site positioning of the initiator to establish the chain's first residue and leave the next codon available for the next aminoacyl-tRNA. Students who memorize the site names without considering the start state often put the initiator in the A site and subsequently find that the geometry of peptide transfer no longer makes sense.

Once a chain-bearing tRNA occupies the P site and an appropriate aminoacyl-tRNA is accommodated in the A site, the two acceptor ends are positioned for the next reaction. The chain is transferred from the P-site tRNA to the amino acid attached to the A-site tRNA. This creates a peptidyl-tRNA at the A site and leaves a deacylated tRNA at the P site, at least in the classical description immediately after peptide transfer. Thus the statement that the P site always contains peptidyl-tRNA and the A site always contains aminoacyl-tRNA is false. The names describe characteristic roles, while the chemistry changes the occupants' states.

During translocation, the message advances through the small subunit relative to the ribosome by one codon and the tRNAs move to the next classical sites. The new chain-bearing tRNA reaches the P site, and the deacylated tRNA moves toward the E site before dissociating. The newly exposed A-site codon is available for selection of another aminoacyl-tRNA. Because the message and anticodons remain paired during critical parts of movement, coordinated movement helps preserve the established reading frame. A loss of coordination can disrupt the relationship between the message and the peptidyl-tRNA and lead to a stalled or incorrectly translated product.

The actual transition between classical sites is not always a simultaneous rigid shift of every atom. The acceptor end and the anticodon end can move relative to their respective subunits in a sequence of intermediate states. A tRNA whose anticodon is still in the A-site region on the small subunit can have its acceptor end moved toward the P-site region on the large subunit. This is conventionally called an A/P hybrid state, where the first letter refers to the small-subunit position and the second to the large-subunit position. The complementary deacylated tRNA can occupy a P/E hybrid state. Such hybrid states illustrate why the ribosome is a dynamic two-subunit assembly rather than a fixed row of three rigid slots. Detailed translocation energetics and factor mechanisms are the subject of Node 3.C.06.

The E site has often been overlooked in elementary diagrams because attention is concentrated on selection and peptide bond formation. Nevertheless, removal of the used adaptor is necessary for continued operation. A tRNA that remained indefinitely associated would interfere with the orderly turnover of the binding sites. Its release must occur in a context compatible with maintenance of the message register and the positions of the other tRNAs. The exact occupancy and departure timing can vary with the organism and experimental state, so it is not necessary to assert that every translating particle contains three tRNAs at every moment. A diagram showing one, two or three occupied sites may all be valid if the state of the translation cycle is specified.

A release factor provides another important exception to the idea that every A-site occupant is a tRNA. At a normal stop codon, the translating ribosome admits an appropriate protein release factor instead of a standard aminoacyl-tRNA that would add another amino acid. The result is hydrolysis of the linkage attaching the polypeptide to its tRNA. The overall architecture of the ribosome accommodates both chain extension and chain release by positioning different substrates and factors in related regions. The stop mechanism and recycling factors are examined in Node 3.C.07; their structural basis already becomes intelligible from the spatial logic of A and P sites.

## 11. The small-subunit decoding center

A ribosome has to distinguish a correct adaptor from many competing tRNAs. Hydrogen bonding between the codon and anticodon is necessary for this task, but a short duplex in solution does not provide the complete discrimination observed during ordinary translation. Many incorrect tRNAs can make some favorable contacts with a codon, and some mismatched base pairs can persist for a short time. The cell therefore uses a decoding center that evaluates structural features of the codon–anticodon interaction in an organized environment.

In the classical bacterial system, several conserved residues of 16S rRNA are especially important in monitoring the geometry of the short duplex. The residues often referred to by E. coli numbering as A1492, A1493 and G530 help form the decoding environment. Their interactions with the codon–anticodon helix contribute to recognition of appropriate pairing, particularly at the first two codon positions. The third codon position has greater scope for permitted wobble interactions. The numbering is a reference convention and should not be assumed to correspond to the same numerical position in all organisms. Homologous residues in another rRNA can have different sequence numbers while performing a related function.

This recognition mechanism explains why the ribosome is not simply a passive holder of RNA molecules. A correct codon–anticodon interaction favors particular conformational arrangements within the small subunit. Those arrangements influence the progression of the incoming tRNA through selection and accommodation. The ribosome therefore couples the geometry of a short nucleotide duplex to changes in the behavior of a much larger macromolecular assembly. Near-cognate tRNAs can be rejected at more than one stage. The detailed roles of elongation factors, GTP hydrolysis and kinetic proofreading will be developed in the elongation chapter; the present architectural principle is that the small subunit provides a physical recognition environment capable of transmitting a local event to the rest of the machine.

The ribosome does not ordinarily identify the amino acid attached to a tRNA by comparing that amino acid directly with the codon. This is a crucial limitation. An aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase has already established the normal correspondence between the tRNA's identity and its amino acid. If a tRNA with the correct anticodon has been experimentally mischarged, correct codon pairing need not prevent incorporation of the wrong amino acid. Other substrate features can influence translation efficiency, and severely abnormal substrates need not behave identically to natural ones. Nevertheless, the ribosome is not a second complete aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase that verifies the standard code assignment of every incoming amino acid.

This separation of responsibilities creates two experimentally distinguishable error classes. A decoding error can occur when an incorrectly paired tRNA is accepted despite carrying the amino acid appropriate to its own identity. A charging error occurs when a tRNA carries the wrong amino acid for its identity and then pairs correctly with a codon. Both can produce an amino acid substitution in the product, but the causal routes are different. Exam questions can distinguish them by examining the chemical identity of the charged tRNA before it encounters the ribosome or by testing decoding with a controlled supply of correctly charged tRNAs.

A hypothetical study of a mutant small subunit can make this distinction explicit. Imagine that the supply of aminoacyl-tRNAs is prepared and verified before translation begins. The mutant particles produce more substitutions at codons that differ from the accepted anticodon pairing by a single base, while the same preparation of tRNAs gives fewer substitutions with wild-type particles. Because the charging supply was held constant, a defect in synthetase specificity is not a satisfactory explanation for the difference between the two reactions. The result points toward a ribosome-associated change in discrimination or an effect on the way the ribosome interacts with its delivery factors. To localize the defect more precisely, additional tests of binding and accommodation would be needed.

An important conceptual qualification concerns the difference between an accurate ribosome and an infallible ribosome. The biological recognition system strongly favors appropriate substrates but is not perfect. The measured error frequency depends on the codon, the competing tRNAs, the cellular conditions and the experimental definition of an error. A single textbook number should be regarded as an approximate scale rather than a universal probability for all positions. It is more valuable to understand which parts of the architecture make errors less likely and how a perturbation can alter the competition between substrates.

## 12. The large-subunit catalytic center and peptide-bond chemistry

The large ribosomal subunit holds the chemical ends of tRNAs close enough for the reaction that extends a polypeptide. To understand this reaction, begin with the chemistry of the linkage produced during tRNA charging. The carboxyl group of the amino acid is attached through an ester linkage to the terminal adenosine of a tRNA. Once a growing chain has formed, its carboxyl terminus remains attached through a related ester linkage to the tRNA that carries it. This linkage has a different chemical character from the amide bonds that already join the amino acids within the chain.

During the ordinary extension reaction, the amino group of the incoming amino acid attacks the carbonyl carbon of the ester linkage connecting the existing peptide to the P-site tRNA. The reaction converts that acyl linkage into a peptide bond connecting the existing chain to the incoming residue. The tRNA that originally held the chain is released from its covalent attachment, and the extended peptide remains attached through the ester linkage of the incoming tRNA. The general reaction is therefore a transfer of the chain from one adaptor to another, not the addition of a free amino acid to an unattached protein.

For a simple example, consider a dipeptide made from an initial methionine followed by alanine. Methionine is initially attached to the initiator tRNA. The amino group of the appropriately positioned alanine, attached to an elongator tRNA, participates in formation of the new bond. The resulting Met–Ala peptide is attached to the alanine-bearing tRNA through the carboxyl group of alanine. The methionine-bearing tRNA becomes deacylated. If a third residue is subsequently incorporated, the next bond is formed using the carboxyl end of alanine as part of the growing chain. Thus translation extends a polypeptide from its amino terminus toward its carboxyl terminus.

The direction of extension is not an arbitrary convention. At each step the free amino group of the new residue becomes connected to the carboxyl end of the previously synthesized chain, while the carboxyl group of the incoming residue remains linked to the new carrying tRNA. The growing chain's original amino terminus is not repeatedly used as the site of addition. This makes it possible for the early part of the sequence to emerge from the ribosome while later residues are still being synthesized. The physical relationship between synthesis direction and exit direction has consequences for folding and targeting that will be developed in later chapters.

A common error is to imagine that a molecule of water is directly removed from two free amino acids at each ribosomal bond-forming step. That description may be useful as an overall formal comparison of free amino acids and the resulting peptide, but it does not represent the immediate ribosomal substrates. The reactants at the peptidyl-transferase center are aminoacylated or peptidylated tRNAs, and the reaction is an aminolysis of an activated ester. The prior activation of amino acids by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases is essential to understanding why the ribosome can extend the chain without directly hydrolyzing an ATP molecule for each new peptide bond.

The peptidyl-transferase center organizes the reactants in a geometry favorable for this acyl-transfer reaction. The large-subunit rRNA provides the main chemical environment that positions the terminal regions of the two tRNAs. Correct positioning reduces the configurational freedom of substrates that would otherwise have to encounter each other in an appropriate orientation. Interactions involving substrate groups and the local RNA environment can assist the chemical transformation. The detailed contributions of proton movement, substrate hydroxyl groups and local waters have been investigated extensively; for examination purposes, the established conclusion is that the large-subunit RNA organizes the catalytic center and that the reaction transfers the peptide to the incoming amino acid.

It is equally important not to confuse catalysis with energy supply. A catalyst accelerates a reaction by providing a more favorable route through the relevant transition states. It does not create chemical free energy from nothing or change the equilibrium constant of the same overall reaction. The energetic investment in aminoacyl-tRNA formation contributes to the activated substrate state. GTP-dependent factor cycles help delivery, selection and movement through translation. These processes are coupled in the complete system, but a GTP molecule is not simply burned in the catalytic center to form each amide bond. Identifying the particular operation associated with each nucleotide hydrolysis is more informative than treating translation as a single undifferentiated energy-consuming event.
