# Node 3.C.04 — draft teaching manuscript, part 2

## From sequence complementarity to experimental evidence

<!-- teaching:historical -->
The idea that ribosomal RNA recognizes an initiation region grew from a molecular question: if a bacterial message contains numerous potential coding triplets, what allows the ribosome to find the relevant one? In 1974, John Shine and Lynn Dalgarno reported sequence information from the 3′ end of E. coli 16S ribosomal RNA. They noted a relationship between that sequence and nucleotides present near known initiation regions. The discovery was important because a possible recognition signal in the message could now be explained by a defined molecular partner in the ribosome. The proposal was testable: changing one partner should impair recognition, and restoring complementarity through a change in the other partner should restore at least part of the activity.

A sequence comparison, however, is not by itself a complete functional demonstration. Two RNA sequences may be complementary because of coincidence, and a complementary segment may be unavailable in the folded molecule. The decisive issue is whether a molecular system responds to complementary versus noncomplementary versions of the sequences. One informative strategy uses the logic of a compensatory mutation. A mutation in the message reduces complementarity to the normal 16S rRNA. A separately engineered ribosomal RNA changes the complementary segment so that the mutated message can again pair with it. If activity depends on this matching relationship, the double-mutant combination should perform better than either mismatched combination.

Hui and de Boer used this principle in a specialized ribosome system reported in 1987. They altered the ribosome-binding region of a message encoding human growth hormone and made complementary alterations in the 16S rRNA of a subpopulation of E. coli ribosomes. Preferential translation of the matched message by the modified ribosomal population supported the role of the interaction. This is an elegant case because the predicted pairing partner belongs to the ribosome rather than to another freely diffusing recognition protein. The conclusion is not that every bacterial gene uses this strategy, but that complementary rRNA–mRNA recognition can be a direct determinant of bacterial translation initiation.

To understand the evidence, consider four conceptual combinations. The normal message with the normal ribosome is the reference combination. The altered message with the normal ribosome tests whether disrupting the message sequence compromises initiation. The normal message with the altered ribosome asks whether changing the ribosomal partner compromises recognition of the original signal. The altered message with the altered ribosome tests whether restored complementarity reverses the defect. A mere decrease in one of the single mutants could be attributed to an incidental change in folding or ribosome integrity. Recovery specifically in the matched combination provides a stronger causal argument.

A good experiment must still control for other changes. The modified ribosomal RNA must be expressed and incorporated into functional subunits. If it fails to assemble, poor translation would not establish a recognition defect. The messages should be present at comparable concentrations, because a reduction in abundance would reduce protein output even if the ribosomes recognize the remaining molecules perfectly. A second, independently translated reference message can indicate whether the cell has suffered a global defect in translation. Finally, protein accumulation is an integrated outcome: differences in protein degradation, transcript stability and ribosome availability can influence it. A direct initiation-complex assay helps connect the final result to the proposed molecular step.

Consider a hypothetical teaching dataset with four combinations assayed at equal RNA abundance. Relative outputs are 100 for normal message plus normal ribosome, 12 for altered message plus normal ribosome, 15 for normal message plus altered ribosome, and 78 for altered message plus altered ribosome. Relative to the low output of 12, restoration to 78 is a 6.5-fold increase. The restored activity is 78 percent of the normal reference. Neither the residual activity of the mismatched pairs nor the incomplete recovery of the matched pair invalidates the hypothesis. Some recognition may survive through other RNA features, and the altered partners need not recreate every favorable interaction of the normal complex. The pattern of specificity, rather than exact equality, is the informative feature. These numbers are illustrative and are not the reported results of the historical study.

A further line of evidence comes from experiments that reconstitute bacterial initiation with defined molecular components. In a cell-free setting it is possible to ask whether purified subunits, mRNA, aminoacylated initiator tRNA, GTP and initiation factors are sufficient to generate an assembly capable of proceeding to the first peptide bond. The usefulness of this approach is that a candidate component can be omitted and restored. If removing IF2 strongly impairs productive initiation while replacing it rescues the reaction, the observation identifies a functional requirement under the specified conditions. It does not necessarily establish that IF2 has only one role; the same factor might influence tRNA binding, subunit joining and the transition to elongation.

The historical development of the initiation model illustrates a general principle of molecular biology. Structural compatibility suggests a mechanism, selective mutations perturb it, compensatory mutations test specificity, reconstitution determines molecular sufficiency, and functional measurements connect an assembled complex to its biological task. No single experimental technique simultaneously proves all these claims. A student should therefore recognize which question each experiment answers and avoid treating a diagram or sequence correlation as a complete explanation of molecular causation.

## RNA accessibility and the energetics of recruitment

<!-- teaching:rna-folding -->
Although SD recognition is often presented as pairing between two freely available strands, the message is usually already folded to some degree. Folding is a consequence of intramolecular interactions among nucleotides. A segment of the initiation region can participate in a stem, and those same bases may need to pair with ribosomal RNA. The two possibilities compete: a base engaged in one stable helix generally cannot simultaneously form the same Watson–Crick interactions with a different strand. The physical challenge is therefore not merely to form a favorable new duplex but to gain access to the bases needed for that duplex.

A simple equilibrium model helps separate the effects. Suppose an unbound message can occupy an open state O and a folded state F. Let the standard free energy of the folded state relative to the open state be ΔGfold = GF − GO. The equilibrium ratio of folded to open molecules is exp(−ΔGfold/RT), where R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. The fraction in the open state is consequently 1 divided by 1 plus exp(−ΔGfold/RT). A negative ΔGfold favors the folded state and decreases the open fraction. This sign convention matters: a more negative folding free energy means that more molecules occupy the folded state, not the open one.

At 310 K, RT is approximately 2.58 kJ per mole when R is 8.314 J per mole per kelvin. If ΔGfold is −5.16 kJ per mole, the folded-to-open ratio is exp(2), approximately 7.39. The open fraction is therefore 1/8.39, or about 0.119. If a mutation weakens folding so that ΔGfold becomes zero, the two-state model predicts an open fraction of 0.5. The open fraction increases by roughly 4.2-fold. It does not automatically follow that protein output will increase 4.2-fold, since initiation may be limited by another step, and the ribosome may itself help disrupt some structures.

This calculation assumes equilibrium, two well-defined RNA conformations, and no appreciable depletion of either population by ribosome binding. Cellular RNAs can adopt more than two structures, may fold as they emerge from RNA polymerase, and can undergo structural transitions at rates comparable to translation. The equation is therefore a conceptual tool for interpreting changes in accessibility rather than a universal quantitative model of bacterial initiation. A kinetic barrier may keep an RNA in a less favorable state for a significant period. In that case even an accurate equilibrium calculation will fail to predict the initial behavior.

A second simple model describes binding of an accessible message to the small subunit. For the reversible reaction R + M ⇌ RM, the dissociation constant is Kd = [R][M]/[RM]. Concentrations are expressed in the same units, and Kd has concentration units. If free ribosomes are approximately fixed and the message is dilute, the fraction of accessible message bound is [R]/(Kd + [R]). When the free ribosome concentration equals Kd, this fraction is one half. When [R] is nine times Kd, it is 0.9. The model is valuable because it illustrates saturation: once most accessible messages are occupied, increasing ribosome concentration has a progressively smaller effect on occupancy.

Suppose free 30S subunits are present at 60 nM, and two otherwise similar accessible initiation regions have Kd values of 20 nM and 180 nM. The first has a predicted bound fraction of 60/80 = 0.75; the second has a bound fraction of 60/240 = 0.25. Under the simple assumptions, the first binds three times as large a fraction of message as the second. If the stronger-binding site progresses slowly to 70S assembly, however, its protein production need not be three times greater. Binding occupancy describes how often molecules are together. Productive flux describes how frequently they pass through the complete reaction pathway.

Opening and binding can be combined as a qualitative energetic argument. For example, if formation of a recognition helix provides favorable free energy but requires disruption of a pre-existing intramolecular stem, the overall association involves both contributions. A stable stem can offset the advantage of favorable SD pairing. It is generally misleading to quote an isolated SD duplex energy as if it were the free energy of the entire initiation process. Contacts with ribosomal proteins, placement of the start codon, tRNA binding, conformational rearrangements and subsequent factor-mediated steps also contribute.

An important experimental distinction is between the structure of naked RNA and the structure present in a translating cell. A computational prediction of a stable hairpin identifies a plausible candidate but does not show whether the hairpin persists in vivo. RNA structure probing can indicate which positions are exposed or constrained, and comparison of mutant RNAs can test whether predicted structural changes actually occur. Controls involving equivalent transcript abundance, identical protein sequences and independent binding assays help isolate the effect of RNA conformation on initiation.

The distinction between accessibility and sequence recognition clarifies why two reporters with identical SD motifs may behave differently. One motif may be buried in a folded leader and the other exposed; one may be positioned properly relative to the start codon and the other displaced. A comparison that changes both sequence complementarity and structure at once is ambiguous. A more informative series changes an element expected to affect recognition, then makes additional changes that restore folding without restoring the original sequence, or restores sequence complementarity while preserving another structural feature. Each comparison addresses a separate mechanistic possibility.

These principles also show why initiation efficiency cannot be predicted simply from the number of G and C residues upstream of a start codon. Guanine and cytosine may contribute stable RNA pairing, but that stability could occur in a helpful intermolecular recognition helix or in a competing intramolecular stem. The same chemical propensity can have opposite biological consequences depending on molecular arrangement. A useful analysis follows the actual partners and conformations instead of assuming that a particular nucleotide composition is always favorable.

## The initiator transfer RNA and its special identity

<!-- teaching:initiator -->
Once the message is positioned, the cell must choose a tRNA that establishes the first residue of the chain. In typical bacteria this is a distinct initiator tRNA for methionine, often written tRNAfMet or tRNAf. The corresponding elongator methionine tRNA is written tRNAMet. The superscripts and subscripts used in textbooks vary, so the biologically important distinction is not a particular typographical convention. One molecule is specialized for the beginning of translation, while the other inserts methionine at appropriate internal codons during elongation.

Both types of tRNA usually possess a CAU anticodon written in the 5′-to-3′ direction. Its antiparallel alignment with an AUG codon is 5′-AUG-3′ opposite 3′-UAC-5′. Because both tRNAs can recognize the same triplet, a selection system that examined only the anticodon could not consistently discriminate the initiator from the elongator. The cell must recognize structural identity elements elsewhere in the tRNA molecule. This is a particularly clear illustration of the fact that a tRNA carries information beyond its anticodon: its body determines interactions with enzymes, translation factors and the ribosome.

In the E. coli model, a characteristic mismatch at the top of the initiator tRNA acceptor stem and consecutive G–C base pairs in the anticodon stem contribute to its specialized behavior. The acceptor-end structure helps recognition by methionyl-tRNA formyltransferase and influences routing through the translation machinery. The anticodon-stem features contribute to efficient recognition of the initiator during assembly at the P site. These are useful representative structural principles, not an assertion that every bacterial species has identical sequence elements. The tRNA is recognized by a combination of features, and their relative importance can differ across organisms.

Methionyl-tRNA synthetase initially attaches ordinary methionine to both the initiator and elongator species in the typical bacterial system. The reaction begins by activating methionine with ATP, forming enzyme-bound methionyl-AMP and pyrophosphate. The activated methionyl group is transferred to the terminal adenosine of the tRNA's CCA end, producing methionyl-tRNA and AMP. Hydrolysis of pyrophosphate makes the activation process strongly favorable overall. The enzyme does not attach an already formylated methionine in the usual pathway. The specialized initiator form is produced after charging by a second enzyme.

The enzyme methionyl-tRNA formyltransferase transfers a formyl group to the alpha amino group of methionine attached to initiator tRNA. Its usual donor in bacteria is 10-formyltetrahydrofolate. The products are N-formylmethionyl initiator tRNA and tetrahydrofolate. The amino acid remains esterified to the terminal ribose of the tRNA; the formylation reaction modifies its amino group, not the bond through which the amino acid is attached to tRNA. A useful notation for the overall transformation is Met-tRNAf + 10-formyl-THF → fMet-tRNAf + THF.

Formylation converts the free alpha amino group into a formamide. This chemical change has consequences for recognition and reactivity. The first amino acid of a nascent chain must act as the donor of the acyl group during formation of the first peptide bond. Its own amino group does not need to act as the attacking nucleophile in that event. The incoming amino acid at the A site supplies the free amino group that attacks the ester-linked carbonyl of the P-site donor. N-formylmethionine can therefore serve as the first donor even though its alpha amino group is blocked. The chemistry is different for an elongating amino acid, which normally requires a free amino group to accept the growing chain.

The formyl group also promotes appropriate selection by bacterial initiation machinery, particularly through interactions with IF2. Formylation and the structural features of the initiator tRNA help channel the molecule toward initiation rather than the normal elongation pathway. In the usual bacterial system EF-Tu delivers elongator aminoacyl-tRNAs to the A site, whereas the initiator uses the initiation apparatus to enter the P-site region. This routing avoids the conceptual error of placing the first aminoacyl-tRNA in the A site and requiring an empty P-site chain to be extended. The initial donor is installed directly in the appropriate position.

It is important not to make the formyl group an absolute criterion for initiation in every organism or experimental condition. Certain bacteria and bacterial mutants can initiate with unformylated initiator tRNA, often with altered efficiency and fitness. Eukaryotic cytosolic and archaeal initiator tRNAs are generally unformylated. Organelles descended from bacterial ancestors may use formylated initiators, with lineage-specific variation. A correct answer therefore distinguishes the canonical bacterial mechanism from an assertion about all cellular translation.

The first amino acid installed by initiation is not necessarily the first residue found in a purified mature bacterial protein. Peptide deformylase can remove the formyl group from the nascent chain. Methionine aminopeptidase may then remove the terminal methionine, depending in part on the identity of the following amino acid and the local substrate context. These processing reactions are distinct from codon recognition. A mature protein beginning with alanine could therefore have been synthesized from an AUG start codon that initially specified formylmethionine. Absence of N-formylmethionine from a mature product does not prove that bacterial initiation occurred without formylation.

A useful comparison involves three separate failures. If methionyl-tRNA synthetase fails to attach methionine to either species, both initiation and internal methionine insertion can be compromised. If formyltransferase is defective, charging may remain normal while the proportion of formylated initiator declines. If an initiator identity element is disrupted, the molecule may be charged and even formylated but still participate poorly in P-site assembly. These distinct defects can produce the same final symptom of reduced protein synthesis. Measurements of charging, formylation and initiation-complex formation are needed to identify the earliest affected step.

The relationship between one-carbon metabolism and initiation supplies a genuine cross-unit connection. Tetrahydrofolate derivatives carry one-carbon units used in several biochemical pathways, including the formylation reaction. A disturbance that reduces the availability of 10-formyl-THF can influence production of formylated initiator tRNA. However, folate metabolism also contributes to other essential reactions, including nucleotide biosynthesis. A growth defect after altering folate metabolism cannot therefore be assigned specifically to translation initiation without suitable controls. The relevant lesson is the biochemical connection, not the assumption that every effect of a folate perturbation acts through one mechanism.
