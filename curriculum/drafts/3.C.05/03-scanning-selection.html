7. Recruitment and the physical meaning of scanning

Two preparations now converge: a small ribosomal subunit carrying initiation factors and initiator tRNA, and a messenger whose cap-associated proteins can support recruitment. The cap-proximal region must be accessible enough for the messenger to engage the small subunit. In the commonly illustrated mammalian mechanism, interactions involving eIF4G and eIF3 facilitate that encounter. The RNA does not enter the ribosome as a complete folded messenger ribonucleoprotein particle. A portion of the leader engages the channel that accommodates single-stranded messenger RNA, while other regions of the transcript and its bound proteins remain outside. This is why recruitment involves both protein-protein contacts and the physical condition of the RNA itself.

The interaction between eIF4G and eIF3 is an example of how recruitment can be achieved indirectly. The cap is recognized by one protein, the small subunit by other proteins, and the scaffold links them through a series of contacts. No direct Watson–Crick pairing between the cap and ribosomal RNA is required. The mechanism is different from the usual bacterial arrangement in which a messenger ribosome-binding sequence can pair with a complementary portion of 16S rRNA. Recruitment to the eukaryotic leader generally establishes an entry region rather than specifying the final initiation triplet. Consequently, productive cap binding can occur even when the message has a mutated AUG and cannot make the intended protein.

After engagement, the preinitiation complex inspects the leader in the five-prime to three-prime direction. This movement is called scanning. It is not equivalent to the movement of an elongating 80S ribosome, because the small subunit is still locating the place at which a stable reading frame will be established. An elongating ribosome normally advances by one codon at each translocation event. During scanning the candidate positions are not restricted to a preselected triplet register. The initiator anticodon can in principle encounter a potential start at successive nucleotide positions. Thus, if AUG begins at nucleotide 37 of a leader, the scanning apparatus need not have already decided that nucleotides 1 to 3 constitute a codon. No peptide is being synthesized as the standard scanning assembly moves across the untranslated sequence.

The open configuration of the small subunit provides an arrangement conducive to RNA movement and inspection. Initiation factors help maintain this configuration and permit the initiator tRNA to sample possible start sites without becoming fully locked into the P site at every candidate. The aminoacylated initiator tRNA is already present in the ternary-complex-associated assembly; it does not need to be recruited anew each time a new triplet reaches the decoding region. This arrangement brings the search criterion along with the searching apparatus. A mutation that prevents initial association of the ternary complex can therefore impair start-site recognition even if the messenger itself has an ideal cap and an excellent nucleotide context around its AUG.

RNA structures encountered during scanning are dynamic. Thermal motion can open and close short regions, and proteins can stabilize or disrupt them. ATP-dependent remodelling assists the scanning machinery in dealing with structures that would otherwise impede progress. It is tempting to imagine that every nucleotide traversed corresponds to a fixed ATP hydrolysis step. This is not a generally valid interpretation. Different leaders present different structural obstacles, and simplified reconstituted systems can scan relatively unstructured RNA under conditions in which the usual ATP-dependent accessory factors are dispensable. The energy requirement of the natural pathway must therefore be discussed in relation to the messenger and its physical environment rather than as a universal integer per base.

A common terminology question concerns the names of messenger-bound complexes. The expression 43S preinitiation complex usually identifies the prepared small subunit before messenger engagement. Once it binds RNA and scans, some accounts describe the messenger-bound assembly as a scanning 48S complex, while others reserve the name 48S initiation complex for the stable start-selected state. In an examination the most reliable distinguishing features are messenger association, start-codon pairing and the presence or absence of the large subunit. If a question shows a small subunit already positioned at a start codon with the initiator tRNA in the P site, 48S is the conventional answer. If the small subunit lacks the messenger but carries the initiating machinery, 43S is the appropriate answer. Neither state is an 80S ribosome.

The spatial placement of a start codon relative to the cap matters because the small subunit needs to engage an adequate stretch of RNA. A candidate triplet immediately next to the cap may be less effectively recognized in a standard scanning system, even if its local nucleotide sequence would otherwise be favourable. This is a geometric limitation rather than a different assignment of the genetic code. When designing synthetic expression constructs, placing the initiation codon after a suitable accessible leader is a way to provide the machinery with room to operate. The precise minimum spacing is context dependent and should not be treated as an invariant number across eukaryotic organisms and all experimental systems.

Scanning gives eukaryotic cells a way to use information distributed along an RNA leader. A candidate start located before the main coding region may capture some complexes, whereas an inhibitory structure may prevent others from reaching either start. Translation output is thus influenced by the order in which the scanning complex encounters these features. Two messages with identical principal coding sequences can produce different amounts of protein because their leaders differ. The coding sequence tells the ribosome which amino acids to join after initiation, but the leader helps determine how often the opportunity to translate that coding sequence is realized.

8. The Kozak context: why identical AUG triplets differ

AUG is a strong signal for initiation in the canonical system because its three nucleotides can pair with the anticodon of methionyl initiator tRNA. However, there may be multiple AUG triplets in a single RNA. An accurate cell cannot treat every occurrence as equally suitable, and the initiation machinery responds to features outside the triplet itself. Studies of vertebrate messenger sequences and targeted changes around start codons led to identification of a favourable sequence environment often called the Kozak context. An often quoted vertebrate consensus is 5′-GCCRCCAUGG-3′, where R represents a purine, either adenine or guanine. Expanded versions of the consensus include additional flanking residues. These are statistical and functional descriptions of favourable context, not an invariant compulsory sequence.

The notation for the positions around AUG is especially important. The A of the AUG initiation codon is assigned position +1, U is +2 and G is +3. The nucleotide directly preceding A is −1; there is no position zero in this convention. A purine at −3 and a guanine at +4 are particularly influential features in many vertebrate messengers. To identify them in a sequence, first underline the actual candidate AUG, then count three positions upstream from A and one position downstream from the final G. The +4 base is the first nucleotide of the next coding triplet if this AUG is selected as the start. The −3 base is in the preceding leader unless an upstream coding region overlaps the position.

For example, consider 5′-GCCAUGGCU-3′. Immediately before AUG are the bases G, C and C, so the −3 position is G; immediately after AUG is another G, occupying +4. These two positions provide favourable features. In 5′-UCCAUGUCU-3′, the −3 position is U and the +4 position is U. Both features are less favourable in the usual vertebrate context. Neither example changes the identity of the AUG itself. The difference is in the probability that a scanning complex will commit to it. If the second sequence is used as an internal site of a longer RNA and a better downstream start is available, some complexes may proceed to the downstream site. Context affects site selection without changing the standard codon assignment.

There is no requirement that an entire consensus sequence be perfectly matched for an AUG to function. A naturally occurring message may have a strong signal at one important position and a weaker signal at another. The behaviour can depend on the surrounding leader and on the initiation machinery of the cell. The Kozak context should not be treated as a promoter, because it is read as part of a messenger during translation rather than as a DNA regulatory site for RNA polymerase. Nor should it be equated with the bacterial Shine–Dalgarno sequence. The latter helps position a start through complementary interaction with ribosomal RNA; the Kozak context influences whether an encountered start is accepted within a scanning pathway.

One useful way to understand the effect of flanking bases is to distinguish the three-base recognition event from the total stability of the start-selected assembly. An initiator anticodon may form complementary base pairs with an AUG, but the mRNA occupies a channel within a ribonucleoprotein complex that contacts neighbouring nucleotides as well. Favourable context can make the transition to a committed conformation more likely. A mismatch at the central triplet weakens the central recognition event, whereas a change outside the triplet can alter the surrounding interactions. Both may influence initiation efficiency, but the genetic consequences of the mutations differ. An alteration at −3 does not itself replace the initiating amino acid or shift the open reading frame. An alteration at +4 may additionally change the second amino acid if the coding sequence is translated from that AUG.

The possible coding effect of a +4 mutation creates an experimental design problem. Suppose a researcher changes G at +4 to U and sees less activity of a reporter enzyme. The result might be caused by weaker initiation, but the altered nucleotide can also change the second amino acid, potentially affecting enzyme activity or protein stability. To test initiation specifically, it is useful to quantify the amount of newly synthesized protein as well as its enzymatic activity, or to use an independent start-site occupancy assay. Depending on the codon, one may not be able to change its first nucleotide synonymously. Careful comparison with a noncoding-context mutation at −3 provides another route to isolating initiation effects. A reduction in reporter activity should not automatically be equated with a reduction in initiation frequency.

The context recognized most efficiently is not identical in all eukaryotic lineages. Yeasts, plants and animals show characteristic biases in sequences around start sites. The vertebrate consensus remains the standard illustrative example for examinations, but a question specifying an organism with different preferences should be interpreted using its stated evidence. The conserved principle is that initiation depends on the triplet and its environment, not that every eukaryotic gene must carry an identical run of bases. This distinction also prevents a common bioinformatics mistake: a sequence that fails to match a strong vertebrate consensus cannot be excluded automatically as a genuine initiation site in another species.

9. Choosing the start: scanning arrest and commitment

When a scanning complex encounters a suitable initiation site, the initiator anticodon can pair with the candidate triplet in the decoding region. The resulting interactions favour a rearrangement of the small subunit and associated factors. The open, mobile state is replaced by a closed state in which the initiator tRNA is more fully accommodated in the P site. In simplified structural terminology the less fully accommodated state is sometimes called P-out and the committed state P-in. These names describe alternative positions or modes of binding of the initiator tRNA, not two separate P sites on the same ribosome. The key educational point is the switch from an arrangement that supports inspection to one that retains the selected codon and tRNA in a stable relationship.

eIF1 helps oppose inappropriate commitment while the small subunit is searching. A suitable start promotes rearrangement of its interactions and eventual displacement from the committed complex. This contributes to the connection between correct start recognition and the nucleotide cycle of eIF2. eIF5 assists the hydrolysis reaction, and the selected state favours release of inorganic phosphate. As a result, the stable interaction between eIF2 and the initiating tRNA is lost and eIF2 can leave in its GDP-associated form. The ribosome must retain Met-tRNAi even as the transport factor leaves. The base-paired tRNA and its interactions with the small subunit provide the arrangement that makes this transfer possible. Simply dissociating eIF2 from an uncommitted complex would not necessarily yield a usable start-selected particle.

This mechanism gives direction to what would otherwise be a reversible series of encounters. During scanning, short-lived interactions with unsuitable triplets can be abandoned. At a suitable start, the rearrangements and nucleotide transition favour persistence at that position. It would be excessive to describe the system as perfectly irreversible: biological complexes can dissociate and initiation sometimes fails even after substantial assembly. Nonetheless, the use of nucleotide-driven transitions permits distinct functional states to be linked in an orderly direction. A strong initiation site is one at which productive commitment occurs with relatively high probability under the relevant conditions, rather than one at which every individual scanning complex must stop with absolute certainty.

The selected AUG is positioned at the small-subunit P site. The following coding triplet is positioned for eventual decoding in the A site of the assembled ribosome. This arrangement defines the phase of subsequent translation. Consider an RNA segment containing 5′-AUGGCUAAAUAA-3′. If initiation occurs at the displayed AUG, the next two sense triplets are GCU and AAA, which encode alanine and lysine, followed by UAA as a termination signal. The initial chain produced from these three sense codons is methionine-alanine-lysine before any maturation. If a different AUG upstream established an overlapping reading frame, the same letters might be divided differently and the apparent UAA might not be a stop in that frame. Termination is frame dependent just as ordinary decoding is.

Start recognition does not itself catalyse the first peptide bond. At this point the complex still needs to recruit the large subunit, rearrange or release initiation factors and expose the A site for an elongator tRNA. An experiment could detect a stable start-selected small-subunit complex even when no protein is produced. This is a central concept for experimental reasoning. A loss of output may reflect failure before start selection, failure to join the large subunit, or failure to proceed after an apparently complete initiation complex. Exam questions often provide an observation about the accumulated intermediate; the correct interpretation is the step immediately downstream of that intermediate, provided the preceding reactions can still supply it.

A defect in fidelity can have consequences beyond changing total protein abundance. If an inappropriate start in the same reading frame is used upstream of the normal start, the resulting protein may contain an amino-terminal extension. If initiation occurs at a downstream in-frame AUG, the chain may lack an amino-terminal segment. If the selected start establishes another frame, the product may have an entirely different sequence and terminate at a different stop codon. These consequences depend on actual sequence relationships and must not be inferred merely from the relative order of AUG triplets. A sequence analysis that labels the candidate AUG positions and computes their differences modulo three can establish whether the predicted coding frames coincide. Protein size and sequence measurements can then test the prediction.

The transition from open to closed states is a useful example of a broader biological principle: the same molecular apparatus can perform sequential tasks by changing shape. A receptor can respond to a ligand through a conformational change, an enzyme can close around a substrate, and a ribosomal preinitiation complex can close around a selected codon. The analogy does not imply that these proteins have identical structures or use the same energy source. It illustrates how molecular recognition can be coupled to a later event without requiring a separate machine to inspect every molecule after recognition. Here the inspection event establishes the starting point from which the entire polypeptide will be generated.
