10. From 48S to an elongation-competent 80S ribosome

At a selected start the small subunit possesses the messenger and the correctly positioned initiator tRNA, but the catalytic machinery of the large subunit has not yet been assembled around them. An additional factor, eIF5B, promotes the joining of the 60S subunit. This protein is itself a GTPase and is distinct from eIF5, the factor that stimulates the GTPase reaction of eIF2. The similarity of their names is a source of frequent mistakes. eIF5 acts primarily in the transition associated with the ternary complex and recognition of the start; eIF5B acts prominently during subunit association and the final maturation of the initiation complex. There are thus two functionally distinct guanine nucleotide-dependent transitions in the canonical pathway, rather than two interchangeable proteins acting at one step.

The small and large subunits make a specific set of contacts when they join. The initiator tRNA must fit into a position compatible with both the decoding region of the small subunit and the peptide-bond-forming region of the large subunit. eIF5B supports this arrangement and promotes productive subunit joining. This does not mean that eIF5B supplies the large subunit with its ribosomal proteins or manufactures a ribosome. Both subunits are pre-existing particles derived from ribosome biogenesis or recycling. The initiation factor facilitates their association at the proper site and time. Its activity couples an already established relationship between messenger and initiator tRNA to construction of the catalytic assembly.

eIF5B bound to GTP is competent to act in the joining pathway. Once the subunits associate productively, GTP hydrolysis and rearrangements promote release of eIF5B and eIF1A. This allows the ribosomal A site and surrounding factor-interaction surfaces to become available for elongation. The complete 80S particle at this stage has an appropriately placed initiator tRNA, and the next coding triplet is available to be read by an incoming aminoacyl-tRNA. Its messenger is aligned and its essential initiation factors have been displaced or rearranged to permit the next phase. The initiator tRNA remains associated with the P site rather than leaving along with the protein that brought it to the ribosome.

Consider a mutant factor that binds GTP and can promote association of the two subunits but cannot readily hydrolyse its nucleotide or depart. Sedimentation could reveal an abundance of 80S-sized particles even though protein synthesis is low. This is consistent with the formation of structurally associated but functionally incomplete particles. In contrast, a mutant that cannot interact productively with the 60S subunit might permit the accumulation of start-selected 48S complexes and reduce 80S formation. The two mutations affect the same named factor but are predicted to produce different molecular intermediates. The important question in either case is whether the complex reaches the stage in which the affected activity becomes necessary.

Subunit joining has a useful historical comparison with bacterial translation. Bacterial IF2 is related evolutionarily to eukaryotic eIF5B. Both are guanine nucleotide-dependent factors involved in initiation and subunit association. Eukaryotic eIF2, despite sharing a numeral with bacterial IF2, is a separate type of factor that performs the principal ternary-complex delivery function in the standard eukaryotic pathway. Correspondence between names is therefore not a reliable method for identifying evolutionary relationships. Functions can be divided among several factors in one lineage even when another lineage organizes related tasks differently. A comparison of the molecular work performed is more informative than simply aligning columns by factor number.

A purified 80S particle isolated from a cell is not necessarily an actively translating ribosome. Some 80S particles may lack a productive messenger or contain stalled or otherwise inactive assemblies. The designation 80S describes the particle and its sedimentation behaviour, not a guarantee of peptide synthesis. For functional identification one needs information about messenger association, tRNA placement, factor occupancy or the capacity to synthesize peptide. A preparation with a large 80S peak and little newly synthesized protein cannot be interpreted as evidence of enhanced initiation merely from the peak size. It is the relationship among multiple measurements that supports an assignment of molecular state.

At the boundary between initiation and elongation, no elongator aminoacyl-tRNA has yet been accommodated in the A site in the standard starting arrangement. The initiation process has created a P-site donor and an available A-site codon. When the first elongator tRNA enters, the machinery begins the repeating cycles considered in the next chapter. This boundary gives a precise answer to an otherwise ambiguous question about the end of initiation: the end is not the detection of the first methionine residue, nor merely the presence of a large subunit; it is the establishment of a complete ribosomal assembly that is able to enter the elongation cycle.

11. Productive mRNA organization and repeated initiation

The main protein-coding sequence of a messenger can be translated more than once. After one ribosome moves away from the start, another small subunit may engage the RNA and initiate independently. The cell therefore does not require a new transcript for every newly synthesized polypeptide. This is a major amplification step in gene expression. The cap-associated machinery and the proteins interacting with the three-prime end of a messenger can influence repeated opportunities for recruitment. In a simplified closed-loop model, cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein on the tail contacts eIF4G, which in turn interacts with cap-associated eIF4E and RNA-remodelling factors. The model identifies a potential route through which a feature far from the initiation codon can affect its utilization.

The RNA need not remain in the same shape throughout translation. The messenger is flexible, and proteins can associate with and dissociate from it. An interaction between proteins at its two ends can create transient proximity without producing a permanently circular topology. It is therefore better to think of the closed loop as a functional organization than as a ring of fixed circumference. A diagram that draws the messenger as a circle represents contact or proximity between its ends. It does not imply that the first nucleotide is joined by an ordinary phosphodiester bond to the last nucleotide. True covalently circular RNAs are different molecular entities.

Poly(A)-binding protein, often abbreviated PABP, can enhance the efficiency of initiation through its interactions with eIF4G and other components. It can also contribute to the stability and organization of the messenger. The influence of the tail on protein accumulation is consequently not restricted to one measurable variable. A longer-lived messenger provides more time for ribosomes to begin translation, while a messenger with more efficient recruitment may produce protein at a higher rate even when its lifetime is unchanged. In many biological systems both effects are relevant. A comparison between tailed and untailed messages should therefore distinguish RNA abundance from the rate of protein production per available transcript.

A useful experiment to investigate the relationship between the cap and the tail uses four otherwise comparable messages: capped with a tail, capped without a tail, uncapped with a tail, and uncapped without a tail. Measuring only the first and last conditions combines two changes and does not reveal whether one feature depends on the other. The full four-condition design permits evaluation of the separate effects and of their interaction. If the benefit of adding a tail is much larger in a capped RNA than in an uncapped one under otherwise matched conditions, the two features may cooperate. This is consistent with a protein bridge that links the ends, but it does not prove the physical arrangement by itself. A mutation specifically disrupting the relevant protein interaction would test the proposed link more directly.

There are distinct ways of expressing cooperation quantitatively. For an additive model, the expected increase caused by applying both changes is the sum of their individual increases above a common baseline. For a multiplicative model, the expected relative fold change is the product of the two individual fold changes. The same dataset can appear synergistic on one scale and additive or independent on another. If a question asks about cooperation, inspect the model it supplies. For example, a baseline of ten protein units, a cap-only value of twenty and a tail-only value of thirty give an additive expectation of forty and a multiplicative expectation of sixty. An observed value of fifty exceeds the additive expectation but not the multiplicative one. The arithmetic cannot identify a biological interaction without stating which interpretation is intended.

A highly translated messenger may carry several ribosomes simultaneously. Their presence is consistent with repeated initiation, but the number of ribosomes on a message also depends on how long each remains there. A slow elongating ribosome occupies the message for longer, potentially increasing ribosome density even if initiation has not become more frequent. On the other hand, a severe initiation defect can cause a decline in ribosome occupancy as previously initiated ribosomes complete translation and depart. This simple relationship between entry and residence time provides a bridge to the later chapter on polysomes. At the initiation stage it warns us that the presence of many ribosomes is not a direct measurement of the rate at which new chains are being made.

The repeated recruitment of ribosomes imposes a requirement on soluble initiation factors. A molecule of eIF2 that remains trapped in the GDP-associated state or an eIF5B molecule that remains bound to an assembled ribosome cannot support the same number of subsequent initiation events as efficiently as a factor that is recycled normally. Factor availability therefore depends on both total abundance and turnover through its functional cycle. The messenger itself can be reused and the ribosomal subunits can be recycled, but each new chain requires its own initiator tRNA molecule and a fresh set of nucleotide-dependent transitions. Reuse of the apparatus reduces the cost of constructing new molecular machinery, not the requirement for chemical energy in each productive initiation event.

12. Leaky scanning, alternative start sites and noncanonical entry

The canonical scanning mechanism often selects the first suitable AUG encountered from the five-prime end, but it does not invariably select the first AUG. When an upstream triplet lies in a poor context, some scanning complexes continue downstream instead of committing to it. This behaviour is called leaky scanning. The word leaky refers to the passage of a fraction of complexes through a potential start site, not to loss of messenger RNA from the nucleus or an opening in the ribosomal structure. A single messenger can thus support initiation at two different sites if both are accessible and neither captures every eligible complex. The relative production of the resulting proteins depends on the recognition efficiencies of the sites and the probability of reaching them.

If two alternative AUG triplets are in the same frame and there is no intervening stop in that frame, initiation at the upstream site may produce a longer polypeptide with an amino-terminal extension. Initiation at the downstream site can produce a shorter version sharing the same carboxyl-terminal sequence. The extra sequence may contain a targeting signal or affect stability, so the difference can be biologically important even when most of the residues are identical. If the two sites are in different frames, however, their products are not simply long and short versions of the same protein. The difference must be established by reading the coding triplets from each site. A difference of three, six or nine nucleotides between the positions of the initiating A residues preserves the frame; a difference of four or five nucleotides does not.

A short upstream open reading frame, abbreviated uORF, may start and terminate before the principal coding region begins. Some scanning complexes bypass its start, while others initiate and translate the upstream sequence. When translation of the uORF ends, the possibility of a small subunit remaining associated with the RNA and acquiring the components needed to initiate again is known as reinitiation. Reinitiation differs from leaky scanning because the ribosome has actually translated an upstream coding sequence before the next initiation event. A scanning complex that bypasses an upstream AUG has not yet completed an initiation and termination cycle. Detailed regulation by uORFs belongs to the later regulation chapter; the distinction between the two physical routes is essential for understanding why mutating an upstream AUG can increase translation of the principal coding region.

In some contexts a triplet differing from AUG by one nucleotide, such as CUG, GUG or UUG, can function as an initiation site. Initiation at a near-cognate codon is generally less efficient in the ordinary pathway and is influenced by context and factor activity. Importantly, recognition of such a site by the conventional methionyl initiator tRNA need not insert the amino acid normally encoded by that triplet during elongation. The identity of the delivered amino acid depends on the tRNA and initiation route used. Some specialized alternative routes differ in this respect, but they should not be treated as the standard rule. For an examination question in which the conventional initiator tRNA is explicitly specified, use its attached methionine when reasoning about the first residue.

Not every messenger needs to recruit the ribosome by the ordinary five-prime cap mechanism. Internal ribosome entry sites, or IRES elements, provide a different route in certain messages, particularly well-established examples in viral RNAs. An internal RNA region can recruit ribosomal components and initiation factors through its sequence, structure and associated proteins, reducing or removing the dependence on the standard cap-binding step. This is a mechanistic exception to the usual entry route, not an exception to the need to establish a productive reading frame. Different IRES elements use different subsets of the translation apparatus, and some still require scanning over a portion of the RNA. The term IRES alone does not specify exactly which factors are required.

Cap-independent initiation and internal initiation are related but not identical descriptions. A message may recruit machinery near its five-prime end by a route that does not depend strongly on eIF4E and then scan; that would be cap-independent but not necessarily internal entry. Conversely, a naturally capped message can contain an element that supports internal initiation under appropriate conditions. The existence of a cap does not prove that every protein synthesized from the RNA depended on that cap. The physical entry site, the chemical features needed for recruitment and the factor requirements should be treated as separate questions. This separation is useful in interpreting experiments where cap recognition is inhibited but a subset of messages remains translated.

An RNA structure that supports internal recruitment may be beneficial under conditions in which conventional entry is less efficient. However, it is not valid to assume that any protein whose synthesis persists during stress must be translated by an IRES. Alternative explanations include changes in messenger lifetime, residual activity of cap-dependent initiation, changes in elongation or differential sensitivity of particular messages to limited factors. Demonstrating internal entry requires evidence about the initiation mechanism itself. The present chapter establishes the principle and the essential controls; the detailed viral strategies and wider regulatory programmes belong to later curriculum topics.
