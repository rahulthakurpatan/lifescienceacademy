## From a translated chain to a functional protein

When a ribosome releases a newly synthesized polypeptide, it has completed a remarkable chemical task: it has joined amino acids in the order specified by a messenger RNA. Yet the ability of that chain to carry out a biological task is not determined by sequence alone. A receptor may need to receive a chemical group before it can recruit a partner. A digestive enzyme may need to lose a short inhibitory segment before its active site becomes useful. A secreted protein may need a carbohydrate chain to survive passage through the endoplasmic reticulum. In each case the genetic message specifies the original amino acid sequence, while an additional cellular process establishes the chemical form that performs the task.

Consider two cells carrying identical copies of a gene for a metabolic enzyme. Both cells can synthesize the same amino acid sequence at similar rates. If one cell experiences a nutritional signal that activates a protein kinase, a fraction of its enzyme molecules may acquire covalently attached phosphate groups. The other cell may contain mostly unmodified molecules. The two populations can now differ in catalytic activity even though neither transcription nor translation has changed. This distinction gives cells a way to respond over seconds or minutes without waiting for new RNA and protein synthesis.

A post-translational modification, commonly abbreviated PTM, is an alteration of the covalent chemical structure of a protein after a residue has been incorporated into a polypeptide. Many modifications happen after the chain has been released, but some begin while the chain is still attached to the ribosome. The word post-translational describes modification relative to formation of the encoded polypeptide rather than imposing an absolute chronological rule for termination. N-terminal acetylation and N-linked glycosylation provide important examples of processes that may be cotranslational. It would therefore be incorrect to draw a universal boundary at the moment a release factor hydrolyses peptidyl-tRNA.

Three phenomena must be distinguished. Folding rearranges the spatial positions of atoms in the polypeptide, largely through noncovalent interactions. Binding a regulatory ligand may change protein conformation without adding a covalent bond. Modification changes chemical connectivity, for example by linking a phosphoryl group to a hydroxyl oxygen. These events can influence one another. A folded domain may expose a modification site; attachment of a phosphate may change the favored conformation; binding of a regulatory partner may prevent a modifying enzyme from reaching a residue. Nevertheless, a change in conformation alone does not demonstrate a PTM. Formation of a disulfide bond is a covalent oxidative event, although its significance for folding was addressed in the preceding node.

Some modifications add groups from cellular donors. Some alter groups already present, and some hydrolyse peptide bonds to release segments. A mature insulin molecule, for example, is not simply a folded precursor with a different shape: specific peptide bonds have been broken. The excised sequence cannot be replaced by a simple reverse reaction, because synthesizing a peptide bond within the appropriate chain would require a specialized biosynthetic process. In comparison, phosphatases can remove a phosphate from a modified protein and allow the same molecule to enter another cycle of phosphorylation. The biological meaning of a modification therefore depends on its chemistry and the lifetime of its product.

The idea that one gene produces one chemically uniform protein is a useful initial simplification but inadequate for cellular biology. A population of polypeptides having identical sequence may contain an unmodified form, a phosphorylated form, an acetylated form or a molecule carrying several groups simultaneously. Each chemically distinct molecular form is a proteoform. Alternative splicing or sequence variants may also contribute to proteoform diversity, but those are separate from changes introduced chemically into an existing chain. A protein's abundance measurement can conceal major differences in the proportions of its proteoforms.

Imagine a particular enzyme is present at a total concentration of ten micromolar. If phosphorylation completely inhibits its activity and eighty percent of the molecules are phosphorylated, only two micromolar is available in its active unmodified state. In another condition, twenty percent phosphorylation leaves eight micromolar active. Provided that substrate availability and the catalytic properties of the active molecules are identical, the maximal catalytic rate would differ fourfold even though the total amount of protein was unchanged. This illustrates why a western blot against the total enzyme and a direct activity measurement answer different biological questions. The former measures abundance, the latter measures function under the assay conditions.

The chemical identity of the modifying group cannot by itself predict whether the modification activates or inhibits a protein. Phosphorylation activates glycogen phosphorylase in a classic example, but phosphorylation can inhibit glycogen synthase. Attachment of carbohydrate may assist folding, protect a surface from degradation or provide a recognition element. It may also be unimportant for the catalytic activity measured with a purified protein in vitro while being essential for correct delivery inside the cell. To determine a modification's role, one must identify its site, examine its structural environment and connect the chemistry with the cellular outcome.

A useful way to organize every example in this chapter is to ask what substrate is modified, what atom participates in the linkage, what donor supplies the chemical group, which enzyme catalyses the reaction, where that enzyme can encounter the protein, and what consequence follows. A seventh question asks whether a reverse enzyme or another removal pathway exists. This sequence gives a common intellectual framework for studying diverse modification types without memorizing them as disconnected names.

## Chemical foundations and site selection

The amino acids of a protein present different reactive chemical groups. Serine and threonine contain alcoholic hydroxyl groups, while tyrosine has a phenolic hydroxyl group on an aromatic ring. All three can participate in oxygen-linked phosphorylation. Asparagine contains a carboxamide side chain, whose nitrogen forms the attachment point of a typical N-linked glycan. Lysine carries a terminal amino group on its side chain. At physiological pH this group is usually positively charged when free, although its precise protonation depends on its local environment. It can receive an acetyl group, a methyl group or a small protein such as ubiquitin. Arginine has a guanidinium group and can be methylated. Cysteine bears a thiol group, capable of participating in thioester formation with fatty acids or thioether formation with isoprenoid groups.

In addition to side chains, the termini of a polypeptide are chemically distinct. The alpha amino group at the amino terminus can be acetylated; a terminal glycine can accept a myristoyl group through its amino nitrogen if the proper enzyme and sequence are present. This amino group is not the epsilon amino group of a lysine side chain. Both may participate in acetylation, but the enzymes recognizing the two sites and the biological consequences can differ profoundly. The carboxyl terminus likewise becomes important in certain maturation processes, including recognition of a terminal motif for prenylation. Whenever an examination problem specifies the identity of the modified residue, determine whether the functional group belongs to the side chain or to a terminus.

Covalent bonds are not made efficiently merely because two molecules collide. A reaction can require activation of a donor, correct alignment of reacting groups and stabilization of a transition state. Cellular modifying enzymes supply specificity and lower activation barriers. A kinase typically uses ATP as a phosphoryl donor; an acetyltransferase commonly transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA; a methyltransferase usually uses S-adenosylmethionine. Glycosyltransferases use activated sugars, and many lipid-transfer enzymes use activated lipid donors. The cell invests metabolic resources in producing these donors. A modification is consequently connected to metabolism even when its biological consequence appears to involve signaling rather than energy production.

For a transfer reaction, it is helpful to distinguish the chemical group that becomes attached from the remainder of its donor. ATP does not generally remain attached to a substrate after a conventional protein kinase reaction. The terminal phosphoryl group is transferred and ADP is released. When an acetyl group is transferred from acetyl-CoA to a lysine amino group, CoA is released. When methyl transfer occurs from S-adenosylmethionine, the product S-adenosylhomocysteine is formed. Correct donor accounting avoids a frequent examination error in which an entire donor molecule is incorrectly treated as the modification.

Specificity involves more than chemical compatibility. A protein containing fifty serines does not necessarily acquire fifty phosphates. An enzyme may recognize a local sequence motif near one serine. Other interactions can recruit that enzyme through a remote docking region, raising the local concentration of substrate near the catalytic site. In a folded protein, a suitable residue may be buried and inaccessible even when its sequence resembles a preferred motif. A region may become exposed after ligand binding or another modification. Protein association with a scaffold may bring an enzyme and substrate into proximity, whereas separation by an organelle membrane can prevent them from meeting.

The recognition sequence for typical eukaryotic N-glycosylation offers a particularly clear illustration. An asparagine followed by a non-proline residue and then serine or threonine is a candidate for glycan attachment. This motif, frequently written N-X-S/T with X not proline, provides a useful predictor. It is not sufficient to guarantee occupancy. The segment must reach the ER lumen and be accessible to the luminally oriented glycosylation machinery. A cytosolic enzyme may carry several sequences matching the motif without acquiring conventional secretory-pathway N-glycans. A sequence near a transmembrane segment or a protein terminus may also be less efficiently modified depending on context. A mutation that changes N-X-T to N-P-T therefore eliminates the standard motif, while the reverse change creates a candidate rather than proof of attachment.

Localization is itself a determinant of chemical specificity. A plasma membrane protein has extracellular portions and cytosol-facing portions. During synthesis along the secretory pathway, the extracellular portions face the lumen of the ER and later the Golgi. Conventional N-linked glycans can therefore occur on these portions. The cytosolic tail of the very same protein may be phosphorylated by a cytosolic kinase or ubiquitinated by cytosolic machinery. The protein crosses a membrane but its enzyme accessibility is not uniform. A problem that states only that a protein is membrane-associated gives insufficient information to decide the glycosylation potential of every region.

A modification may be stoichiometric, affecting nearly every molecule of a certain protein, or substoichiometric, affecting only a fraction. Occupancy is the fraction of the molecules bearing the particular mark at a defined site. For a population containing P unmodified molecules and M modified molecules, site occupancy is M divided by the sum P plus M. Occupancy is distinct from the concentration of M, which changes with the total abundance of the substrate. If a treatment halves the total protein but leaves half of its molecules modified in both conditions, occupancy remains constant while the amount of modified protein decreases by half.

Consider a cell containing two micromolar of a target protein. An assay calibrated to distinguish the forms detects 0.6 micromolar carrying a particular phosphate. Occupancy is 0.6 divided by 2, or thirty percent. After stimulation, the total protein increases to four micromolar and the amount of phosphorylated protein increases to 1.2 micromolar. A phospho-specific signal may have doubled, yet occupancy has not changed. This example explains why normalizing a site-specific measurement to a measurement of total protein is often necessary before concluding that the balance of modification and removal has changed.

A modifier may alter charge, hydrogen bonding, steric shape, hydrophobicity or affinity for recognition proteins. Acetylation of lysine eliminates its ordinary protonatable side-chain amino group by forming an amide. Methylation of lysine, unlike acetylation, does not ordinarily neutralize the positive charge of its side chain at physiological pH. Phosphorylation introduces a bulky polar group that is substantially negatively charged under many cellular conditions. Lipid addition may strengthen association with membranes. Carbohydrate addition creates a hydrated surface with possibilities for lectin recognition. The student should relate each effect to the actual group rather than treating all PTMs as equivalent switches.

The term writer is sometimes used for an enzyme installing a mark, eraser for an enzyme removing it and reader for a protein recognizing it. This vocabulary is useful as long as it is not mistaken for a universal mechanism. Some modifications do not possess a direct eraser, and some act by changing the substrate's intrinsic catalytic properties rather than through a dedicated reader. A phosphatase removes a phosphate, whereas an enzyme that recognizes a phosphotyrosine docking site does not necessarily modify it. An enzyme could possess more than one activity, but substrate recognition and covalent catalysis must still be distinguished experimentally.

A chemical change is not necessarily an intentional regulatory event. Oxidation or nonenzymatic glycation may occur as a consequence of cellular conditions. Some such changes are repaired or have regulatory roles, whereas others represent damage. Glycation refers to nonenzymatic reaction with reducing sugars and must not be confused with enzyme-directed glycosylation. In the remainder of this chapter the primary emphasis is on controlled, biologically purposeful modifications, while chemically related nonenzymatic reactions provide useful contrasts.

## Discovery of reversible phosphorylation

The biochemical discovery of reversible phosphorylation transformed understanding of enzyme regulation. Glycogen phosphorylase was known to participate in mobilization of stored glycogen. Its reaction uses inorganic phosphate to cleave certain glycosidic bonds and produce glucose-1-phosphate. A key question was how a cell could alter the activity of this enzyme rapidly in response to a physiological demand. Allosteric effects of metabolites provided one possible explanation, but they did not explain every difference observed between preparations of the enzyme.

Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs investigated conversion between forms of muscle glycogen phosphorylase in the 1950s. Their work established that the conversion involved an enzyme-mediated phosphorylation reaction and that the phosphate could subsequently be removed. A second enzyme, phosphorylase kinase, catalysed addition of phosphate using ATP. The modified state and its catalytic behavior could be distinguished from the unmodified state. This was a direct demonstration that changing covalent protein chemistry could regulate enzyme function reversibly. The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1992 recognized Fischer and Krebs for these discoveries.

The experimental logic is more important than memorizing the prize date. An increase in enzyme activity upon adding ATP could initially be interpreted in several ways. ATP might bind noncovalently to the enzyme and change its behavior. It might serve as a substrate for a separate reaction producing an activator. Alternatively, part of ATP might become covalently incorporated into the target. Testing activity after removal of free ATP helps distinguish persistent modification from an effect requiring continuously bound soluble nucleotide, although retention of a tightly bound ligand remains another possibility. Detecting phosphate associated with a purified target under conditions disrupting noncovalent binding provides stronger evidence of covalent modification. Enzyme-mediated removal of that phosphate accompanied by reversal of the functional change supports the regulatory cycle.

An educational reconstruction of a suitable experiment would compare purified phosphorylase incubated with ATP and active phosphorylase kinase against an otherwise identical mixture without kinase. A further control would contain a kinase lacking catalytic activity. Measuring phosphorylase activity under matched substrate conditions tests function, and measuring phosphate incorporation into the isolated substrate tests chemical change. If ATP is labeled at its terminal phosphate, radioactivity associated with purified phosphorylase after separation from free ATP would support transfer of that group. A phosphatase treatment followed by a second measurement could test whether the attached mark and functional effect are lost together. This is a hypothetical teaching design, not a reproduction of any one historical dataset.

Even such a result must be interpreted carefully. A phosphatase preparation might contain additional enzymes or cause an unrelated alteration if it is impure. A target preparation containing another phosphorylated protein could show radioactivity in the same fraction. Appropriate purification, controls and independent detection of the modified site strengthen the conclusion. An increase in activity and an increase in phosphorylation do not on their own establish causation if both can be caused by a third factor. Reconstituting the reaction with defined components helps narrow the possible explanations.

The muscle glycogen example also illustrates the distinction between allosteric and covalent regulation. Adenosine monophosphate can influence muscle glycogen phosphorylase through reversible noncovalent binding. It need not become attached to the enzyme's amino acids. Phosphorylation instead changes the covalent chemical state. A phosphorylase molecule can be subject to both kinds of influence, so observing the existence of one does not exclude the other. Describing the phosphorylated a form as generally more active and the b form as responsive to allosteric regulation is more accurate than assuming that the b form is entirely inactive under every condition.

The same physiological situation can require different proteins to respond in opposite directions. During mobilization of glycogen, stimulation of phosphorylase contributes to breakdown, while inhibitory phosphorylation of glycogen synthase reduces synthesis. If phosphorylation always activated enzymes, this coordinated response would be impossible. The phosphoryl group instead changes a particular structural and regulatory environment in each enzyme. The biological outcome depends on that environment. This principle extends far beyond carbohydrate metabolism, including regulation of translation initiation, membrane receptors and cell cycle proteins.
