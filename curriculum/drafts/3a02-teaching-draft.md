# DNA Replication Origins and Initiation

Node 3.A.02 — Unit 03, Fundamental Processes — Subunit 3.A, DNA Replication, Repair and Recombination.

## 1. Why copying a chromosome requires a decision about where to begin

A chromosome is a continuous DNA molecule that contains many thousands or millions of chemically similar nucleotides. Nothing in the chemical reaction that joins one nucleotide to another automatically tells a DNA polymerase that it has reached the beginning of a chromosome replication cycle. A polymerase can recognize an appropriate template and a primer, but it cannot ordinarily create an entire new strand simply by encountering an arbitrary intact segment of double-stranded DNA. The cell therefore needs an initiation system that selects an appropriate position, makes the two parental strands accessible, assembles proteins that separate DNA continuously, supplies primers, and connects those primers to a processive copying apparatus. The origin of replication is the chromosomal region at which these events are organized.

The need for an origin is not a consequence of DNA being linear or circular. A circular bacterial chromosome has no physical beginning or end, yet it normally initiates copying at a defined site. A linear eukaryotic chromosome has physical ends, but its ends are not sufficient as starting positions because copying the entire molecule from those sites would be slow and difficult to coordinate. Instead, both systems establish local starting regions. The cellular initiation apparatus interprets molecular information at these regions, creating an opportunity to begin the physical process of copying. This distinction separates the geometry of a molecule from the biological organization of its replication.

Three terms are useful from the outset. An origin is a region at which replication begins. A replicator is a cis-acting DNA element that supports the initiation of replication in a particular context. An initiator is a trans-acting molecular factor that recognizes or acts on that element. The term cis means that the relevant sequence acts on the same DNA molecule; trans means that the relevant product, usually a protein, can act on other DNA molecules in the same cell. These definitions are particularly clear in model organisms with recognizable origin sequences, although the distinction between a replicator and the exact start of DNA synthesis can become less sharply defined in complex eukaryotic genomes.

A mutation in an origin sequence and a mutation in an initiator gene may both prevent chromosome duplication, but their genetic logic differs. Imagine two separate DNA molecules in a cell. One has a functional origin and the other has a damaged origin that can no longer recruit the required machinery. Providing additional normal initiator protein can potentially rescue a defect caused by an abnormal initiator, because the protein can diffuse and act on both molecules. It cannot necessarily restore the molecular information missing from the damaged origin. Conversely, a plasmid carrying a normal origin does not automatically confer replication competence on a different DNA molecule with no functional origin. The relationship between cis elements and trans factors will reappear when studying promoters and transcription factors, but here the outcome is DNA replication rather than RNA production.

The word replicon refers to a DNA segment replicated under the control of an initiation event. In the simplest bacterial example, a circular chromosome copied from a single origin can be treated as one replicon. Eukaryotic chromosomes are divided functionally into many replicating regions. The boundaries between adjacent regions may change between individual cells because neighboring potential origins do not always initiate at the same times or with the same efficiencies. An origin is thus not the entire replicon: it is the place from which a replicating domain develops.

Initiation also solves a counting problem. In a normally cycling diploid eukaryotic cell, each chromosomal DNA region should be duplicated once between successive mitoses. If a region starts replication prematurely, repeats replication after it is copied, or never acquires a functional fork, the daughter cells may inherit abnormal amounts of genetic material. The chromosome copying machinery is therefore subject to restrictions that are separate from its ability to synthesize a chemically correct DNA strand. A perfectly accurate polymerase cannot compensate for a chromosome region that is copied twice or not copied at all. This is why replication initiation is a mechanism of genome maintenance as well as a biochemical mechanism.

Bacteria also coordinate initiation with growth and chromosome segregation, but the simple eukaryotic statement that replication occurs only during S phase must not be transferred to bacteria. In rapidly growing bacteria, a new round can begin on already duplicated origins before the preceding round has finished. The relevant control is appropriate initiation of available origins in the context of the growth cycle, rather than a requirement that the cell contain exactly one replication bubble at any moment. We will use Escherichia coli for a concrete bacterial mechanism and budding yeast and animal cells for contrasting eukaryotic mechanisms.

## 2. The physical distinction between recognizing DNA and opening it

DNA in its usual cellular form consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide chains. Base pairing connects complementary bases across the duplex, whereas stacking interactions between adjacent bases contribute substantially to the stability of the helix. The sugar-phosphate backbones remain chemically continuous during ordinary initiation. Local separation of the strands therefore does not mean breaking the phosphodiester chains. Instead, base-pairing and stacking interactions must be disrupted over a limited region so that the two chains become accessible to proteins.

A short region of relatively low duplex stability may open more easily than its surroundings under otherwise comparable conditions. A/T-rich stretches are frequently associated with bacterial DNA unwinding elements because their sequence-dependent duplex stability can favor opening. However, the statement that adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds while guanine pairs with cytosine through three does not provide a complete thermodynamic explanation. Neighboring sequence, ionic conditions, stacking interactions, protein binding and DNA topology all influence whether a region opens. A random A/T-rich segment is not necessarily an origin. Recognition requires the appropriate arrangement of signals and factors, and an accessible unwinding region must be positioned relative to those signals.

The local opening of DNA involves an energetic cost. In a simplified model, imagine an origin that exists in equilibrium between a closed state and an open state. If the free-energy difference for opening is positive, the open state has lower equilibrium occupancy. A protein that binds preferentially to the open configuration can shift the population toward that state because formation of favorable protein-DNA contacts changes the overall energy balance. Protein binding can also bend or constrain a DNA segment so that the free-energy cost of opening decreases. Real initiator systems involve multiple proteins, nucleotide-dependent transitions and nonequilibrium steps; the two-state model only illustrates how specific interactions can make local strand separation favorable.

A quantitative example clarifies the scale of energetic effects. Suppose two hypothetical origin variants differ in the free energy of opening by 2.0 kilojoules per mole at 310 kelvin, with all other factors identical. For an equilibrium between closed and open forms, the ratio of open-to-closed equilibrium constants for the easier-opening variant and the harder-opening variant is exp(2000 divided by RT), where R is 8.314 joules per mole per kelvin. RT is approximately 2577 joules per mole, giving a ratio of about 2.17. A modest change in energy can therefore have an appreciable effect on the relative tendency to open. It does not follow that the easier-opening origin fires exactly 2.17 times as often: binding, helicase recruitment, competition and regulatory state may limit actual initiation.

The origin region also experiences the consequences of DNA topology. In a covalently closed DNA molecule, the linking number is a topological invariant unless a strand is broken and rejoined or the topology is otherwise altered. Linking number equals twist plus writhe. Negative supercoiling means that the linking number is lower than that of a relaxed reference molecule. Local unwinding can relieve some of the energetic strain associated with negative supercoiling. This is one reason that appropriately supercoiled bacterial origin substrates often support initiation more effectively than relaxed versions in cell-free assays. In cells, topological constraints may also occur within chromosomal domains; they do not require the entire chromosome to behave as an unrestricted simple circle.

For example, consider an ideal closed duplex of 1050 base pairs with 10.5 base pairs per helical turn in its relaxed B-DNA reference state. Its relaxed linking number is approximately 100. If the actual linking number is 95, the linking difference is minus 5 and the superhelical density is minus 5 divided by 100, or minus 0.05. This underwound molecule can redistribute deformation between twist and writhe. An origin that opens roughly one helical turn changes the twist locally, but linking number does not spontaneously increase by one merely because the strands separate there. The remaining molecule must adjust unless an enzyme changes topology. This is why opening DNA is a mechanical event embedded in the surrounding chromosome rather than an isolated breaking of a few hydrogen bonds.

Opening is not yet productive DNA replication. An open complex can close again if the exposed strands are not stabilized. In addition, a limited region opened by an initiator cannot by itself supply the continuous strand separation needed as forks advance. A specialized ATP-dependent motor, the replicative helicase, must be installed in the correct configuration. Once active, helicases can convert the energy of nucleotide hydrolysis into directed movement and strand separation. Single-stranded DNA-binding proteins help protect and organize the exposed templates. Finally, primer synthesis provides an accessible 3-prime hydroxyl group from which DNA polymerase can begin covalent extension. These are distinct stages, and an experiment that measures only one of them cannot automatically establish the success of the others.

It is also useful to separate three kinds of molecular evidence. A DNA-binding assay may show that an initiator recognizes an origin. A strand-accessibility assay may show that the duplex has opened locally. Incorporation of labeled deoxyribonucleotides may show that DNA synthesis is occurring. Each reports a different property. A mutant could retain the first property while losing the second, or retain both while losing the third. In examination problems, arranging experimental observations in their causal sequence often identifies the defective step more reliably than memorizing the name of a protein.

## 3. From an origin hypothesis to molecular evidence

The semiconservative nature of DNA replication explains how parental sequence information is transmitted to daughter DNA molecules. It does not identify a specific starting position. To establish that replication begins at particular sites, researchers needed evidence about where DNA synthesis starts and how replication intermediates develop. Mapping replication bubbles, examining the movement of forks and determining which DNA sequences allow autonomous replication helped transform the origin concept into an experimentally testable mechanism.

A classical genetic strategy uses a DNA molecule that cannot maintain itself autonomously in a selected host. If a fragment of chromosomal DNA confers the ability to replicate and persist extrachromosomally, the fragment is a candidate replicator. This experiment requires more than simply counting colonies after introducing DNA. A selectable marker can be expressed transiently from DNA that will later be lost, and the introduced sequence may integrate into the chromosome rather than replicate independently. Physical examination of recovered DNA and controls lacking the candidate fragment are therefore crucial. A successful autonomous replication assay demonstrates that the fragment supports replication in the tested context, not that every equivalent chromosomal copy must fire during every cell cycle.

Stinchcomb, Struhl and Davis isolated a yeast chromosomal replicator, ARS1, in 1979. This was important because it established a way to recognize eukaryotic chromosomal replicator activity experimentally using yeast. The activity was attributable to a DNA sequence carried on a plasmid, making the origin amenable to genetic alteration and functional analysis. Such experiments made it possible to ask which parts of a candidate DNA segment were necessary and how proteins recognized those parts. They also encouraged a distinction between a sequence sufficient for autonomous plasmid maintenance under suitable conditions and the probability of initiation at the same sequence in its normal chromosomal environment.

Bacterial origins were likewise investigated using DNA substrates containing defined chromosomal origin sequences. In 1981, Fuller, Kaguni and Kornberg described enzymatic replication of the E. coli chromosomal origin using a cell-free system. Dependence on intact oriC and DnaA supported the idea that initiation was organized by a specific origin and particular proteins rather than indiscriminate polymerase activity. Their original enzyme system was an important intermediate step toward biochemical reconstruction of the machinery; it should not be interpreted as evidence that every activity detected in a complex extract is an obligatory direct component of the minimal modern initiation reaction.

The strength of biochemical reconstruction is that components can be removed and restored. If an extract deficient in a protein cannot initiate replication and addition of a functional form restores synthesis under comparable conditions, the restoration links that protein to the process. Yet direct biochemical action must still be distinguished from indirect support. For instance, a factor might maintain a substrate's topology or improve assembly without being the motor that separates DNA. Experiments with purified components, DNA binding and structural intermediates help clarify the causal order.

A similar transition occurred in eukaryotic origin biology. In 1992, Bell and Stillman reported ATP-dependent recognition of budding yeast origin DNA by a multiprotein complex, named the origin recognition complex, or ORC. This supplied a molecular connection between a genetically identified origin and a protein assembly able to recognize it. It did not mean that ORC itself was the motor that unwound DNA during S phase. Subsequent genetic and biochemical work distinguished origin recognition, helicase installation and helicase activation. The distinction ultimately explained how cells can prepare replication sites in one cell-cycle phase without immediately producing DNA.

One informative design uses a matched normal and mutant origin with a purified recognition complex. If binding occurs to the normal sequence but decreases after a substitution in the recognition region, the substitution may disrupt an important recognition signal. A second experiment should determine whether the sequence also affects loading of the helicase. A third can measure DNA synthesis after activation. Binding alone does not establish biological origin function, because a site that recruits a recognition complex can still fail at the next stage. Conversely, a mutation that changes firing without measurably altering binding may affect a different aspect of origin architecture.

In 2009, biochemical and structural studies of budding yeast origin licensing showed that the MCM2-7 helicase core is loaded in paired hexameric assemblies around double-stranded DNA. Work by Remus and colleagues, and an independent study by Evrin and colleagues, provided direct support for a configuration in which two helicase cores are positioned for future bidirectional replication. Their observations explain a feature that would otherwise be puzzling: a helicase can be associated with DNA before replication has begun, because the initially loaded form is not equivalent to the active strand-separating helicase at an elongating fork.

These milestones illustrate three complementary styles of evidence. Genetics discovers sequences and factors needed for normal replication. Biochemistry determines what those components can accomplish and under which molecular conditions. Physical and structural methods reveal the intermediates created during assembly. A convincing mechanism emerges when the different observations fit together, not merely when a named protein is found close to an origin.

## 4. The architecture of the E. coli chromosomal origin

The principal replication origin of the E. coli chromosome is called oriC. It is a compact region whose minimal functional form is roughly 245 base pairs under the conditions of classic plasmid experiments; boundaries and efficiency depend on the experimental context. Its organization includes several binding sites for the bacterial initiator DnaA, a region that favors local duplex opening, and sites that interact with regulatory and architectural proteins. The sequences are arranged in an order and orientation that allow the origin to assemble a coordinated nucleoprotein complex rather than just bind a collection of independent molecules.

DnaA recognizes short DNA sequences traditionally referred to as DnaA boxes. A commonly taught consensus for the strong E. coli DnaA-binding motif is the nine-nucleotide sequence TTATCCACA. The actual origin contains sites with different sequences and affinities. A consensus summarizes shared preferences; it is not an assertion that each functional site has precisely the same sequence. In classic diagrams the strongest sites and three A/T-rich thirteen-base-pair repeats are emphasized because they illustrate the division between recognition and opening. More complete origin descriptions identify additional lower-affinity binding sites and strand-specific interactions that are important for coordinated initiation. A student should recognize the simplified map without mistaking it for an exhaustive list of every nucleotide contact.

The DNA unwinding element, abbreviated DUE, is positioned near the assembly region. Its sequence contributes to its ability to open under the influence of DnaA, DNA-bending proteins and favorable topology. DnaA also interacts with exposed single-stranded DNA within this element, helping stabilize the opened state. The DUE is not a promoter, a primer or a gene encoding a replication protein. It is a functional DNA region within the origin. Mutating it may interfere with opening even if the main DnaA-binding sites remain able to recruit protein.

Consider two hypothetical oriC alleles. In the first, several essential recognition boxes are altered while the unwinding region remains unchanged. The initiator may bind poorly, so the coordinated assembly needed for opening cannot form. In the second, recognition remains strong but the unwinding element is changed in a way that raises its opening cost. The origin may accumulate bound DnaA without forming a stable open complex. The final consequence can be similar, reduced replication initiation, but the molecular intermediates distinguish the variants. A genetic assay that detects only final chromosome duplication would not by itself identify which defect occurred.

The spatial arrangement of sites matters as much as their presence. Protein molecules bound to adjacent DNA sequences may interact only if their positions and orientations are compatible. Inserting a short segment between sites can alter the helical phase of the DNA and consequently the relative positioning of the bound proteins. In an ideal B-DNA model, adding about five base pairs rotates downstream features by nearly half a helical turn, whereas adding ten or eleven base pairs rotates them by approximately a full turn. Real oriC is bent and distorted during assembly, so the simple helical model does not predict an exact rescue for a given insertion. It nevertheless explains why altering spacing can change origin behavior without deleting a recognizable motif.

The E. coli origin is not a universal blueprint for bacterial chromosomes. Many bacteria use DnaA-related initiation systems, but the organization of binding boxes, auxiliary DNA sequences and associated factors varies between species. Some bacterial chromosomes are linear, and bacteria with multiple chromosomes require appropriate coordination of different replication units. The terms oriC and DnaA are useful in comparative biology, but the particular E. coli map is a model with organism-specific details. Its general lesson is that a cis-acting initiation region integrates DNA recognition, structural change and regulated access to the helicase.
