## 5. CMG helicase, RPA and the physical environment of the replisome

The name CMG is constructed from three components: Cdc45, the MCM2–7 complex and GINS. The MCM ring contains six different but related subunits, and its motor activity depends on ATP. Cdc45 and GINS contribute to the formation and functioning of the active helicase rather than serving as alternative DNA polymerases. The ring encloses predominantly the leading-strand template at a normal fork and translocates along that strand in its 3′ to 5′ direction. The other parental strand is excluded from the central channel during the usual strand-separation model. The result of movement is the continued availability of two single-stranded templates.

This molecular architecture is useful for understanding fork polarity. At the rightward fork drawn in the preceding section, the upper strand has the 3′ to 5′ orientation required for movement of CMG toward the right. The same strand is read by the leading-strand DNA polymerase. The helicase is ahead of the polymerase in the direction of overall fork progression, although both are associated with the replisome. There is no contradiction between the direction of helicase movement on the template and the direction of new DNA synthesis: they are directions measured on different molecular strands.

The energy used by CMG is not simply the energy stored in DNA base pairs. ATP binding and hydrolysis drive conformational changes in the helicase motor that support directed movement. Base pairing and base stacking stabilize the duplex; the motor progressively overcomes this stability while moving along the parental template. An ATPase-defective helicase can therefore bind DNA yet fail to unwind it productively. Conversely, thermal denaturation of purified DNA can expose a single-stranded template without ATP, but it does not reproduce the controlled, local movement of a replication fork in a living cell.

Once the parental strands separate, they are vulnerable to intramolecular folding and reannealing. Replication protein A, abbreviated RPA, binds exposed single-stranded DNA in eukaryotic nuclei. It is a heterotrimeric protein complex rather than a DNA polymerase. Its binding helps organize and protect single-stranded regions, reducing inappropriate interactions with complementary sequences and helping coordinate enzymes that need access to the template. Bacterial SSB performs an analogous general function, but the two names should not be interchanged as though they refer to identical protein complexes.

The lagging-strand template is particularly likely to expose extended single-stranded intervals because its new DNA is made in successive fragments. RPA cannot simply form an impenetrable shell around the template: primase and polymerase need local access. Protein binding therefore has to be dynamic, involving exchange and displacement as synthesis proceeds. A cell can contain abundant RPA yet have an abnormal fork if that protein is unable to interact properly with newly exposed DNA or if its normal turnover at the fork is impaired.

A distinction of experimental importance is that a large RPA signal can indicate an accumulation of single-stranded DNA without specifying its cause. It can arise when helicase activity becomes uncoupled from polymerase activity, during repair processes, or when DNA ends are processed. To infer an elongation defect, the signal should be localized to replicating DNA and interpreted alongside nucleotide incorporation and appropriate cell-cycle controls. Conversely, a failure to detect RPA might reflect reduced exposure of single strands rather than proof that replication is proceeding normally.

The mechanical problem does not end at the fork. DNA in a chromosome has constrained rotational freedom because it is bound to proteins and organized into loops and domains. Removing helical turns at one site creates a tendency toward torsional strain elsewhere. In a simplified topological picture, unwinding produces excess positive supercoiling ahead of the progressing fork if the DNA cannot freely rotate. Topoisomerases provide controlled passage or rotation of DNA strands to relax such strain. Topoisomerase I can relax supercoiling by transiently breaking one DNA strand. Topoisomerase II can pass one duplex through a transient break in another duplex and is particularly important for resolving interlinked daughter molecules.

If a fork progresses through a topologically constrained interval of 10,500 base pairs and the relaxed helical repeat is approximately 10.5 base pairs per turn, the initial DNA contains about 1,000 helical turns over that interval. Complete strand separation must accommodate changes associated with these turns through relaxation, rotation, or interwinding of daughter molecules. This estimate is not a prediction of exactly one thousand enzymatic turnovers: topoisomerase classes alter topology differently, and DNA can distribute torsion among multiple structures. It establishes the scale of the physical problem that accompanies copying even a modest stretch of chromosomal DNA.

## 6. The three principal nuclear replicative DNA polymerases

The eukaryotic fork does not employ a single replicative polymerase for every stage of synthesis. The central division of labor involves DNA polymerase alpha (Pol α), DNA polymerase delta (Pol δ) and DNA polymerase epsilon (Pol ε). Their properties complement one another. Pol α is associated with the primase responsible for beginning new chains. Pol δ and Pol ε are much better adapted to extensive DNA synthesis and possess intrinsic proofreading exonuclease activities. The typical nuclear replication system uses Pol ε predominantly for leading-strand elongation and Pol δ predominantly for lagging-strand elongation.

The assignment is useful because it describes the main traffic of nucleotide incorporation in normal chromosomal replication. It must not be interpreted as an absolute rule that every leading-strand nucleotide in every situation is synthesized by Pol ε or that Pol δ can never extend a leading-strand primer. In particular, Pol δ can contribute to establishment of leading-strand synthesis and can take over synthesis under some circumstances. The cell's dependence on the structural functions of a protein can also differ from its dependence on the catalytic activity of that protein. The phrase predominantly distinguishes a normal physiological allocation from a chemically obligatory exclusion.

Pol α has a polymerase catalytic site that extends DNA but works as part of a complex containing primase activity. Primase makes RNA directly on a single-stranded DNA template, without the pre-existing DNA 3′ terminus required by the DNA polymerase active sites. The RNA primer is transferred within the complex to the DNA polymerase component, which adds a limited stretch of DNA. The resulting starter provides a DNA 3′ end that can be recognized by an elongating DNA polymerase. Typical primers have approximately ten RNA nucleotides and a few tens of DNA nucleotides, but their exact lengths depend on the biological system and experimental conditions.

Why should a cell use a relatively low-processivity polymerase to start chains rather than allow the most processive enzyme to begin at once? The issue is not that Pol δ or Pol ε cannot bind an RNA–DNA hybrid under any circumstance. The issue is that their DNA polymerase reactions need a pre-existing, appropriately positioned primer terminus. Primase can establish that starting geometry. Pol α extends it to generate a useful intermediate, after which a more processive polymerase takes over. Polymerase switching is thus a coordinated handoff between different stages of DNA synthesis rather than redundant competition among equivalent enzymes.

Pol α lacks the intrinsic 3′ to 5′ proofreading exonuclease characteristic of Pol δ and Pol ε. If Pol α contributes a short DNA stretch, it is reasonable to ask how the cell limits the mutational burden associated with this less accurate synthesis. One important feature of lagging-strand maturation is that Pol δ can displace DNA at the beginning of a previous fragment together with the RNA primer, and the resulting displaced material can be cleaved. Not every nucleotide initially made by Pol α is necessarily removed under all conditions; retained primer-associated DNA and subsequent correction processes must be considered. The small proportion synthesized by Pol α and the action of other enzymes help limit the consequences of its lack of intrinsic proofreading.

Pol δ is strongly stimulated by its association with PCNA, the eukaryotic sliding clamp. Its catalytic activities include extending a primer and proofreading at the growing terminus. Its ability to synthesize DNA while displacing a downstream strand is central to the maturation of lagging-strand fragments. A mutation that selectively compromises strand displacement could therefore cause a defect in primer removal and fragment joining even if the mutant enzyme is able to extend an isolated primer on a simple template. A test that measures only incorporation of labeled dNTP into such an isolated primer would miss the distinction.

Pol ε associates with the CMG-centered replisome and is positioned to synthesize the leading strand. In addition to its catalytic region, it contains structural features involved in replisome organization. In some experimental yeast contexts, a catalytically impaired form of the leading-strand machinery may permit DNA synthesis through compensating activities while disrupting the normal division of labor. This is a useful illustration of the difference between removal of an entire protein and selective mutation of an active site. Removing the whole complex may eliminate both catalytic and structural contributions, producing a more extensive phenotype than inactivating one activity.

Other DNA polymerases are present in a eukaryotic cell but are not normally responsible for the bulk of nuclear chromosome duplication. For example, Pol β is associated principally with repair synthesis, whereas Pol γ is the principal mitochondrial replicative DNA polymerase in animals. The presence of a DNA polymerase in a nuclear extract does not establish that it is a major replicative enzyme. Function must be assigned from biochemical properties, interactions, genomic evidence and the consequences of selective perturbation. A polymerase name is therefore a useful clue, but not a substitute for mechanistic reasoning.

The evolution of eukaryotic replication proteins illustrates that analogous functions can be performed by related or different molecular structures. Eukaryotic Pol α, δ and ε belong to the B family of DNA polymerases; the principal chromosomal Pol III of E. coli belongs to the C family. The shared tasks of priming, processivity, proofreading and coordinated elongation do not require the enzymes to have identical names or evolutionary origins. This distinction will help when comparing nuclear replication with bacterial and archaeal systems.

## 7. Evidence for polymerase division of labor

Assigning a particular polymerase to a specific strand is experimentally harder than demonstrating that an enzyme can synthesize DNA in a test tube. Both Pol δ and Pol ε can extend suitable primers. Detecting either enzyme at replicating DNA indicates an association with replication but does not reveal which of two neighboring parental strands it is copying. The spatial separation between leading and lagging templates is small, and both are being processed as part of an organized molecular complex. An experiment that distinguishes their catalytic contributions must exploit a feature that differs between the two synthesis modes.

One informative feature is the direction of a replication fork relative to a sequence whose orientation is known. Consider a reporter positioned on one side of a well-characterized bidirectional origin. If that origin fires efficiently, the usual direction of replication through the reporter is predictable. The two orientations of the reporter place complementary sequences into different template contexts. Reversing its orientation can exchange whether a particular diagnostic base belongs to the leading or lagging template without altering the general biochemical identity of the polymerase being examined.

Certain engineered polymerase variants produce characteristic biases in the errors they make. Such a variant can be used as a genetic marker for the synthesis performed by the affected polymerase. In a simplified example, suppose a polymerase is unusually prone to insert G opposite template T compared with inserting C opposite template A. Because complementary sequences reverse the identity of these potential mismatches when analyzed in a defined reporter, mutation spectra can supply information about which parental strand was copied by the error-prone enzyme. The evidence concerns differences between classes of mutations, not merely an increase in their total number.

Pursell and colleagues reported evidence in 2007 supporting a major role for Pol ε in leading-strand replication in budding yeast. Nick McElhinny and colleagues in 2008 used a diagnostic Pol δ mutator allele to examine replication orientation and obtained results supporting a primary lagging-strand role for Pol δ. Together the studies support the familiar model of strand specialization in normally functioning yeast replication forks. These studies are especially instructive because they ask about the cellular context of the polymerases, instead of equating activity on a purified template with the normal physiological assignment.

For conceptual interpretation, consider a hypothetical pair of orientation-dependent reporter experiments. In a Pol ε variant the diagnostic error appears eighty times in the orientation predicted to reveal leading synthesis and ten times in the opposite orientation. In a Pol δ variant the corresponding numbers are twelve and seventy-two. These invented values illustrate a pattern of complementary strand preferences. They are not measurements from the historical studies. Under comparable conditions, the ratio of diagnostic events in the preferred and alternative orientations would be eight for the first variant and six for the second, consistent with substantial but nonexclusive contributions.

The ratios cannot automatically be interpreted as exact proportions of DNA synthesized by the two enzymes. The probability of a diagnostic mutation depends on intrinsic polymerase bias, sequence context, proofreading, mismatch correction and the survival and detection of mutant cells. A mutation may be erased by a different enzyme before becoming a heritable substitution. A reporter can also be replicated from a different origin in a subset of cells. Controls should establish fork orientation, reporter comparability and a well-characterized diagnostic error bias. The correct conclusion is a predominant association supported by the pattern, not an exact census of nucleotides inferred from uncorrected mutation counts.

Biochemical approaches provide a complementary test. Reconstituted fork systems allow investigators to alter the availability of specific proteins and observe the structures and quantities of DNA products. They can reveal that a protein interaction stimulates processive synthesis or that a particular enzyme is needed to complete a defined intermediate. Such systems deliberately simplify the cell, so a finding that a particular polymerase is sufficient in an artificial reaction does not prove it is the exclusive enzyme in intact chromosomes. The strongest mechanistic account combines experimental approaches with different limitations.

The classic simian virus 40 replication system also illustrates this point. It was invaluable for dissecting eukaryotic DNA synthesis using host cell proteins, but the viral initiator-helicase and the set of requirements in that system are not identical to those of a normal cellular chromosome. Historical conclusions drawn from viral replication must be interpreted in the context of the substrate used. Modern models of nuclear replication incorporate cellular genetic evidence and the architecture of the CMG replisome.

## 8. PCNA and RFC: maintaining a processive DNA polymerase

A polymerase can add many nucleotides during a single encounter with its template or dissociate after adding only a few. The average number of nucleotides incorporated per binding event is called processivity. It is not the same as catalytic speed. A polymerase that incorporates fifty nucleotides per second while bound may nevertheless make little DNA overall if it dissociates repeatedly and rebinds slowly. Increasing the time spent attached to DNA can improve the effective output of a replication system without accelerating the active-site chemical reaction.

PCNA, proliferating cell nuclear antigen, forms a ring built from three related positions occupied by identical subunits in the usual eukaryotic homotrimer. When the ring is around duplex DNA, it can move along the DNA rather than simply remaining at a fixed sequence. A polymerase associated with the ring is less likely to escape into solution while it is extending a primer. The advantage is especially striking for Pol δ. PCNA is not an enzyme that synthesizes a phosphodiester bond; it changes the physical relationship between the polymerase and its DNA substrate.

A closed protein ring cannot ordinarily be placed around an internal segment of a very long DNA molecule simply by threading it from one end. Replication factor C, RFC, is the ATP-dependent clamp loader. It recognizes a suitable primer-template junction, interacts with PCNA and opens the clamp. ATP-dependent structural transitions permit loading of the ring around DNA. Subsequent ATP hydrolysis and changes in RFC allow release of the loaded clamp, which can then associate with the elongating polymerase. Loading requires recognition of the appropriate DNA structure rather than any randomly available double-stranded region.

The directionality of synthesis is relevant even to a clamp-loading experiment. The productive junction contains a recessed 3′ primer end adjacent to template available for extension. An intact blunt duplex with no exposed template beyond the primer terminus does not provide the same initiation substrate. Nor does adding PCNA solve the problem of a missing 3′ hydroxyl. The clamp supports an already feasible polymerase reaction; it does not change the chemical requirement for a primer.

PCNA has another important function as a platform for binding several proteins that act on newly synthesized DNA. Among these are factors involved in primer removal, ligation and chromatin assembly. Their interactions are coordinated in time and space. It is misleading to imagine that all these proteins must occupy the same PCNA surface simultaneously in a fixed permanent complex. Binding sites may be used sequentially, and more than one clamp may be present in a replicated region. PCNA's physical persistence on DNA helps organize the transition from copying to processing.

Suppose an isolated polymerase incorporates an average of twenty nucleotides before dissociating. In a simplified model with an effective clamp it incorporates one thousand nucleotides before dissociating, while the intrinsic incorporation speed remains unchanged at forty nucleotides per second. Copying a hypothetical ten-kilobase template would then require approximately five hundred binding events without the clamp and ten events with it. If each renewed productive association costs two seconds and the first association is counted, the model gives 250 seconds of incorporation time plus 1,000 seconds of association time without the clamp, versus 250 plus 20 seconds with it. The large difference arises without changing the speed of chemistry.

In an actual cell there are more variables, including clamp retention, loader kinetics, protein concentration and the distribution rather than the mean of binding times. The model nevertheless identifies the essential point: a clamp defect may manifest as short synthesis products, reduced fork progression or excessive polymerase dissociation even when the active-site chemistry is normal. A useful biochemical test compares extension on a short substrate that requires little processivity with synthesis on a much longer substrate. If the mutant extends short primers nearly normally but produces truncated long products, a processivity problem becomes a plausible explanation.

An important parallel with bacterial replication is the β sliding clamp and its loader. Both systems use a ring around DNA and an ATP-dependent loading mechanism, but their proteins and subunit arrangements differ. E. coli's β clamp is a homodimer, whereas typical eukaryotic PCNA is a homotrimer. Their similar ring-like architecture is a response to the same physical requirement: maintaining a mobile attachment to a long polymeric substrate without obstructing the movement needed for synthesis.
