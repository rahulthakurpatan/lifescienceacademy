1. Beyond the chromosome: what makes DNA a replicon?

A bacterial cell may contain a large chromosome and several much smaller DNA molecules. When the cell divides, its chromosome must be copied because it carries genes required for ordinary cellular existence. The smaller molecules present a different problem. They may encode functions useful in a particular environment but absent from many other members of the species. Nevertheless, when a lineage retains such a molecule for hundreds of generations, its persistence cannot be explained merely by its initial presence. Every generation consumes some of the supply of molecules by distributing them between daughters. Continued inheritance therefore requires production of new copies and successful allocation of those copies. These two processes, replication and segregation, are the foundations of the biology of extrachromosomal replicons.

A replicon is a unit of DNA whose copying is controlled by an initiation system. Its boundaries are determined by the region copied from a functional origin under the relevant replication program. A small bacterial plasmid with one origin is a straightforward example: activating that origin normally results in copying the complete plasmid. A mammalian nuclear chromosome, in contrast, contains many origins and can be understood as containing numerous replication units. Two different meanings of independence must be kept separate. A plasmid can initiate replication from its own origin, independently of initiation at the bacterial chromosomal origin, yet still depend on bacterial polymerases, nucleotide metabolism and cellular energy. It is autonomous as an initiation unit, not autonomous as an organism. Even an element that encodes its own polymerase relies on an environment containing the precursors and conditions required by the enzyme.

The molecular distinction is between the replicator and the initiator. A replicator is a DNA sequence or collection of sequences necessary for regulated initiation. It acts in cis, meaning that its activity is associated with the molecule on which it resides. An initiator is a molecule, often a protein, that acts on the replicator. Many initiators can diffuse within the cell and therefore act in trans. Some origins instead depend on RNA molecules produced locally, and local synthesis can make a diffusible molecule function largely on the DNA from which it arose. An origin is more than an arbitrary stretch of sequence: it is a site where biochemical events convert intact parental DNA into a substrate that can accept new nucleotides. The distinction predicts an important genetic result. A defective initiator gene may be complemented by a functional gene elsewhere, whereas deleting the only required origin usually cannot be rescued just by placing another origin on an unrelated molecule.

Consider two otherwise identical circular DNA molecules. One has a functional origin and an appropriate initiator; the other lacks its origin. Both may initially be detected inside a cell. At the next division, either molecule may be inherited by a daughter. After repeated divisions, however, the nonreplicating molecule is diluted out of the expanding lineage, even if no enzyme destroys it. If the original molecule is integrated into a chromosome, chromosomal replication can preserve the inserted sequence without requiring the original plasmid origin. In such a case the sequence persists, but not as an independently copying plasmid. This distinction is the reason a genetic marker alone is insufficient evidence of plasmid replication. The physical form of the DNA and the source of its copying must also be established.

A plasmid is typically a separately maintained genetic element, most often double-stranded DNA in bacteria. Plasmids are frequently circular, but linear plasmids also occur. Their size ranges widely, and the distinction between a very large plasmid and a secondary chromosome can become biologically complicated. The usual textbook contrast is functional: the primary chromosome carries a large complement of core genes, while a plasmid often carries accessory genes and has an independent maintenance system. These are useful tendencies rather than universal definitions. Some secondary replicons contain essential functions, and certain plasmids become indispensable to a host under particular conditions. Neither length nor circular geometry alone provides a reliable classification.

Extrachromosomal DNA is not restricted to bacterial cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells also maintain mitochondrial DNA, and photosynthetic eukaryotes maintain plastid DNA. These are organellar genomes whose location outside the nucleus distinguishes them from nuclear chromosomes. They should not be treated as merely large bacterial plasmids: they constitute part of the hereditary system of their host cell and encode selected organellar functions. Viral DNA may also exist as a separate replicating molecule inside a host, although viruses depend on infection or another association with cells rather than ordinary division as independent cells. In each case the question is not simply where DNA is located but how its own copying is initiated, executed and coordinated with the biological system that contains it.

It is useful to distinguish stable replicons from extrachromosomal DNA fragments. A fragment produced by breakage, an excised DNA segment without an active origin, or a newly entered DNA molecule that fails to establish replication may be extrachromosomal in location without being a self-maintaining replicon. Conversely, the same sequence may be an autonomous replicon in one state and part of a chromosome in another state. An experiment must therefore distinguish the presence of a molecule from its persistence and persistence from demonstrable independent initiation. This prevents a common error in interpreting a short observation period as proof of long-term maintenance.

Historically, the vocabulary emerged from genetic observations in bacteria. Joshua Lederberg introduced the term plasmid in 1952, before modern molecular characterization of the many elements now placed in that category. Jacob, Brenner and Cuzin subsequently articulated the replicon model, separating a genetic element's ability to serve as a substrate for replication initiation from the factors that trigger that event. These ideas were powerful because they converted inheritance patterns into testable molecular predictions. Genetic observations that independently inherited elements could be retained or lost became questions about which DNA sequences were required for initiation and which gene products controlled their activity. The modern plasmid concept and its terminology developed through further genetic and biochemical evidence rather than appearing fully formed in a single experiment.

Three experimental outcomes are especially informative. First, a DNA element might remain present during proliferation as a molecule of its original size and structure. This supports stable maintenance but does not by itself identify its initiation mechanism. Second, an origin alteration might prevent DNA synthesis while a control alteration elsewhere has no effect. This localizes a required sequence, provided both molecules have comparable initial entry and detection. Third, an initiator defect might be rescued by functional initiator supplied separately. This supports a role for a trans-acting product, although rescue must be interpreted in light of any effects on host growth. The logic of combining physical evidence, sequence-specific perturbation and functional complementation will recur throughout the chapter.

2. Plasmid architecture and the requirements for stable maintenance

A simple map of a plasmid divides its sequence into regions with different functions. At minimum, a replicating plasmid must possess a way to begin DNA synthesis and obtain the machinery that completes it. A regulatory region determines how often new initiation events occur. Additional regions may promote partition, resolve multimers or encode functions that alter the host phenotype. Such maps are circular in many familiar illustrations, but the map represents sequence order and does not necessarily describe the physical conformation of the molecule in a living cell. A tightly supercoiled circular DNA molecule may appear compact rather than ring-shaped; the circular map preserves the fact that the end of the sequence joins its beginning.

The origin of vegetative replication, often denoted oriV, must be distinguished from the origin of transfer, oriT, found in transferable plasmids. The former participates in copying DNA for intracellular maintenance. The latter is recognized during DNA transfer between cells. A transferable molecule can undergo strand cleavage and replacement synthesis associated with transfer, but the presence of oriT does not by itself ensure ordinary vegetative replication in a recipient. This distinction explains why transfer competence, replication competence and stable inheritance are separable properties. A plasmid may replicate without transferring, may require other functions to transfer, or may fail to persist after arriving in a host whose replication machinery is incompatible with its origin system.

Many plasmid origins contain binding sites for an initiation protein encoded by the plasmid. The protein recognizes particular bases or DNA structures rather than binding to every position with equal probability. Repeated binding sites, called iterons in several plasmid systems, may help assemble an initiation complex and also participate in copy-number regulation. Other sequences provide a region that can be opened, enabling priming. Because base stacking, local sequence, supercoiling and protein binding all influence opening, it is inadequate to explain origin activation solely by counting hydrogen bonds. AT-rich regions often facilitate opening, but the complete origin is a coordinated recognition and assembly site, not merely any sequence rich in adenine and thymine.

Not every plasmid encodes a dedicated initiator protein. ColE1-family replicons illustrate initiation through a locally transcribed RNA precursor that becomes a primer for DNA synthesis. This immediately reveals why the term origin should not be confused with a promoter. A promoter directs transcription initiation; an origin directs DNA replication initiation. A transcription event can be an essential step at a replication origin, but not every promoter produces an RNA capable of establishing DNA replication. The fate of the RNA depends on its sequence, its interaction with the DNA template and its processing. The specificity of an RNA-dependent origin lies in this chain of events.

The energy for polymerization is provided by the incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates. Their incorporation releases pyrophosphate, and cellular pyrophosphate hydrolysis favors the overall forward reaction. DNA polymerases extend a pre-existing hydroxyl group at the growing 3′ end, making the new strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction. A replisome may additionally require ATP-dependent helicase activity, nucleotide-dependent clamp loading, topological relaxation and primer processing. Consequently, the origin is necessary but not sufficient. A correct initiation event cannot yield a complete daughter molecule if elongation is blocked or if the necessary precursors are unavailable. This is why a replication defect may arise from a host mutation even when all plasmid DNA sequences remain intact.

Host range is the set of hosts in which an element can be maintained under specified conditions. It reflects multiple barriers rather than a single recognition reaction. A plasmid may encode its own initiator but still require a host helicase, polymerase or transcriptional system capable of interacting with that initiation complex. It must also operate in the host's physiological environment and avoid rapid loss. The inability to detect a plasmid after many generations in a new host may therefore reflect failure of initiation, defective elongation, unstable segregation, or reduced survival of plasmid-containing cells. Determining which mechanism is responsible requires observations of replication intermediates and lineage behavior, rather than a single final count of cells carrying a marker.

Plasmid maintenance differs from the synthesis of an RNA transcript or a protein. The product of a transcription event is normally not a new copy of the DNA sequence that directs subsequent transcription. A successfully replicated plasmid, however, contributes additional origin-containing molecules to the very population that can initiate future replication. Without regulation, this positive feedback could produce disproportionate accumulation of DNA and impose a cost on the host. Conversely, if initiation is too infrequent relative to cellular proliferation, copy number falls. Stable plasmids solve the problem by coupling initiation probability to the abundance of molecules or to other features of cellular growth. Copy-number control is thus a feedback problem, not just an enzyme-speed problem.

Segregation is distinct from copying because DNA molecules do not automatically occupy different future daughter cells simply by being synthesized. Replicated circular duplexes may remain topologically linked or physically clustered. Topoisomerases can separate linked molecules, recombination-associated resolution systems can convert multimers into independently segregating units, and partition systems can position copies. A plasmid with a high abundance of separate copies can tolerate imperfect positioning more readily than one with only a few copies, but actual intracellular distributions are not necessarily random. Experimental interpretation must distinguish the number of origin sequences, the number of full genome equivalents and the number of physical particles that can independently segregate.

3. Theta replication: opening a duplex and copying around a circle

The first major strategy used by circular double-stranded replicons is theta replication. The name refers to an intermediate whose outline can resemble the Greek letter theta: an unreplicated part of the parental circle connects two junctions that are also connected by the two growing daughter duplexes. It is not a special chemical reaction different from ordinary DNA polymerization. Instead, it is a geometric arrangement of template exposure and fork progression. A student who understands the architecture of a bacterial chromosome replication fork already possesses most of the molecular vocabulary required to understand this mechanism.

Imagine an intact closed circular duplex with one origin. An initiation complex recognizes the origin and destabilizes a short region of double-stranded DNA. The separation creates two single-stranded templates with opposite orientations. In a conventional bidirectional theta system, two replication forks are established at the edges of the origin region and move away from one another along opposite arcs of the circle. Helicases enlarge the accessible region of template, single-strand binding proteins reduce reannealing and inappropriate secondary structures, primases generate suitable primers, and polymerases extend the resulting 3′ ends. The detailed proteins vary with the plasmid and host, but the direction of nucleotide addition is conserved.

At either fork, the parental strand oriented 3′ to 5′ in the direction of movement permits leading-strand synthesis in the same overall direction as that fork. The opposite parental strand requires lagging-strand synthesis in segments. A primer is placed near the newly exposed region, extended away from the fork, and later replaced or processed so that adjacent DNA fragments can be joined. This coordination explains an apparent puzzle: both daughter strands become complete even though polymerases share a single chemical direction of synthesis. It is the orientation of templates and the arrangement of initiation events, not reversal of polymerase chemistry, that accommodates antiparallel DNA.

At a pair of bidirectional forks, a parental strand that serves as the leading-strand template at one fork serves as the lagging-strand template at the other. To verify this, draw one parental strand as clockwise 5′ to 3′. A fork moving clockwise encounters that strand in the wrong orientation for continuous 5′ to 3′ daughter synthesis, while the fork moving anticlockwise encounters the same strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction. The two forks thus use opposite leading templates. This relationship is a valuable exam strategy: label template polarity first, then infer synthesis directions, rather than trying to memorize which side of a diagram is the leading strand.

Some theta replicons use only one progressing fork. The origin may produce a replication bubble with one boundary that remains near the initiation region while the other advances around the molecule. There are still duplex replication intermediates rather than a long displaced tail of single-stranded DNA. The term theta therefore does not mean that every origin launches two active forks. Directionality must be inferred from evidence about the positions and movement of active junctions. One cannot simply count the two boundaries of a DNA bubble and conclude that both move at the same speed.

Because parental strands are intertwined, moving a replication fork around a covalently closed circle changes topological constraints. Without strand passage or an equivalent relaxation mechanism, separation at one position tends to create overwinding ahead of the fork or intertwining behind it. Topoisomerases resolve these constraints by transiently breaking and rejoining DNA strands. Type I topoisomerases act through transient single-strand cleavage, whereas type II enzymes pass a duplex through a transient double-strand break and can resolve DNA catenanes. In bacterial systems, DNA gyrase is important for controlling supercoiling, and topoisomerase IV is especially important for decatenation. The contribution of each enzyme depends on the particular organism and replication system.

As replication approaches completion, the remaining unreplicated arc becomes shorter. In a bidirectional system the two forks converge; in a unidirectional system the advancing fork returns to the completion region. Processing of residual primers, sealing of nicks and physical separation of daughter duplexes complete the production of two closed circles. Each daughter duplex normally contains one parental and one newly synthesized strand. The daughter circles may still need to be disentangled if they are catenated. Replication completion is therefore not identical to physical separation. An experiment that counts completed DNA sequence copies but ignores topology could report a successful replication event even when inheritance remains impaired.

The distinction between DNA geometry and the molecular history of its strands is fundamental. A theta intermediate can be drawn as three arms between two branch points. Counting visible arms gives information about structure, not necessarily about whether all arms are old or new. Each replicated duplex consists of an old template paired with a newly made strand; the unreplicated duplex contains both old strands. Under the idealized situation in which half of a circular genome has been copied, the total amount of DNA in the intermediate is one and a half genome equivalents, not two. There is still only one connected replicating structure until termination and resolution. A two-dimensional image must therefore be interpreted with a distinction between molecule count, mass and sequence copy count.

Classic evidence that helped establish this way of thinking came from John Cairns's 1963 autoradiographic study of bacterial chromosomes. Radiolabel incorporated into DNA made long DNA molecules visible indirectly through photographic tracks. The observed circular and branched patterns supported a model in which a circle was copied progressively rather than broken into many independently duplicated pieces. This evidence concerned a bacterial chromosome, not every plasmid, but it supplied a physical language in which related circular replication intermediates could be recognized. The presence of an intermediate is not by itself a measurement of how rapidly its forks move. Comparing stages or differently labeled newly synthesized regions is required to infer direction and kinetics.

A direct contrast with a rolling circle is possible before learning the latter in detail. If one observes a circular duplex with an internal duplicated arc and no extended displaced tail, theta replication is a plausible interpretation. If one observes a circle with a long extending strand or duplex tail, a displacement-based mechanism becomes plausible. However, branch structures can arise from recombination, incomplete processing or molecule breakage. A reliable conclusion combines topology with strand composition, origin dependence and the time order of intermediates. A single attractive drawing is never sufficient evidence to identify a mechanism in an unfamiliar system.

4. Distinct routes into theta replication

Although theta replication has a recognizable geometry, its origins can establish replication through different molecular routes. One useful comparison is between a protein-dependent origin such as that of many iteron-controlled plasmids and an RNA-dependent origin represented by ColE1. Both ultimately require DNA synthesis from an accessible 3′ hydroxyl group, yet they create that substrate differently. This comparison shows why the geometry of an elongating intermediate should not be used to infer the identity of the initiation factor.

For a representative protein-dependent theta plasmid, origin-binding protein accumulates in the cell, binds specific origin elements and promotes local DNA opening with assistance from host factors. A host helicase can then be recruited, primers generated and the replicative polymerase assembled. Iterons provide a recognizable platform for origin binding, while an adjacent unwinding region supports exposure of template. The precise order and role of host proteins differ among plasmid families. It is therefore safer to learn the common logic, recognition, opening, priming and fork assembly, rather than attribute the exact bacterial chromosomal oriC mechanism to every plasmid.

In the ColE1 family, transcription from a particular promoter produces RNA II, which can interact with the DNA template at the replication origin. A stable RNA–DNA hybrid is formed as the RNA anneals to its complementary DNA strand. RNase H participates in processing the RNA to form a usable primer terminus. DNA polymerase I extends that primer, and host machinery subsequently establishes continued replication. The striking feature is that a dedicated plasmid-encoded Rep initiation protein is not required for the canonical ColE1 system. This is a clear exception to a statement that is otherwise tempting in textbook summaries: plasmid initiation is often controlled by a Rep protein, but it is not universally dependent on one.

The initial RNA–DNA hybrid is sometimes called an R-loop. In this structural arrangement the RNA pairs with one DNA strand while the other DNA strand is displaced locally. The hybrid stabilizes a particular arrangement near the origin, allowing RNA processing to generate the substrate for DNA polymerase. An ordinary messenger RNA generally separates from the DNA template and does not serve this role. RNA II must form the correct structure and persist in the correct location. Therefore, transcription alone is not proof that replication initiation has occurred. The experimental distinction would require detection of the processed primer or its conversion into an extending DNA product.

RNA I provides negative regulation in ColE1-type systems. It is a short antisense RNA whose sequence is complementary to a region of RNA II. Interaction between the two RNAs interferes with the conformational pathway required for RNA II to form the productive RNA–DNA hybrid. In simple terms, RNA I intercepts the primer precursor before it reaches the state that supports replication. When plasmid abundance rises, the cell produces more copies of RNA I, increasing the likelihood of such inhibition and reducing the average initiation probability per plasmid. The Rop or Rom protein, where present, facilitates the inhibitory RNA interaction. Neither RNA I nor Rop should be described as a DNA polymerase inhibitor acting at every replication fork; the central control is directed at formation of a competent initiation substrate.

This regulatory system predicts different effects for changes in different regions. If RNA II loses the ability to establish an origin hybrid, successful initiations decline even if RNA I is absent. If RNA I is produced normally but can no longer recognize the appropriate RNA II structure, inhibition may weaken and copy number may increase, assuming the rest of the origin remains functional. A defective host primer-processing activity may reduce productive initiation despite normal transcription. The direction of the phenotype depends on which biochemical step fails. One should not interpret every mutation near an origin as a simple reduction in replication: removing a negative regulatory element can have the opposite effect from damaging the priming element itself.

The genetic analysis of ColE1 copy-number variants provides a way to separate feedback from basal priming. Suppose a mutation changes an antisense RNA without affecting the complementary origin sequence, and another mutation changes the target RNA region. If the first change reduces inhibition of the original target, while a compensatory target change restores inhibition, the sequence specificity of the interaction is supported. Such compensation is more informative than the observation that an altered plasmid simply reaches a high abundance. A high abundance could also result from changed degradation, host growth or segregation. Measurements of RNA interaction and initiation intermediates help connect the genetic effect to a direct molecular mechanism.

In iteron-containing plasmids the feedback mechanism can involve limiting availability of active initiator, origin titration or inhibitory pairing of origin complexes, sometimes termed handcuffing. In a titration model, a finite pool of active initiator is distributed among many binding sites, reducing the free fraction as origin abundance increases. In a handcuffing model, interactions between protein-bound origin regions can reduce productive initiation. Different systems use different combinations of autoregulation, active-state switching and origin interactions. A diagram showing origin pairing should therefore be treated as a model for appropriate examples, not a universal mechanism shared by all theta plasmids.

In both regulatory examples, increasing the number of replicons changes the probability of future replication. The controlled variable is average initiation activity, not necessarily the rate at which polymerase adds nucleotides once a fork is established. This distinction matters in experiments. If the duration of replication of each individual molecule is unchanged but the number of initiation events decreases, an initiation-control model is supported. If initiation frequency is unchanged but completion takes longer, altered elongation or resource availability becomes more plausible. An observed fall in final copy number can arise from either mechanism, making time-resolved measurements essential for causal interpretation.
