12. Viral DNA replicons: matching a copying strategy to the viral genome

Viruses illustrate how the general chemical requirements for DNA synthesis can be satisfied in dramatically different biological settings. A virus does not simply divide into two daughter cells. Instead, during a productive infection, viral genetic material is copied within a suitable cellular environment and genome copies may become incorporated into newly assembled particles. The viral genome may have a different physical form inside a particle from its actively replicating form inside a cell. Thus a linear viral genome can become circular before copying, and a single-stranded viral genome can generate a double-stranded intermediate. Understanding the transition from the entering genome to the replicative form is essential for choosing a correct mechanism.

Bacteriophage lambda provides a classical illustration of changes in replication geometry. Its genome in the mature particle is linear double-stranded DNA with complementary single-stranded cohesive ends. These ends can associate after the DNA reaches the bacterial interior. Sealing the backbone discontinuities produces a circular DNA molecule. The physical transition from a linear sequence in a particle to a circle inside a cell allows the circular form to be replicated without ordinary linear end-completion problems. This circularization does not by itself increase DNA copy number. It reorganizes the substrate from which copying can subsequently occur.

During the early productive replication phase of lambda, circular molecules can be copied through theta-type intermediates. Viral origin recognition functions, including the O protein, participate in establishing initiation, and viral P protein helps recruit bacterial helicase machinery. Other essential activities are supplied by the host. The detailed regulation of the viral developmental program is beyond the present chapter, but the biochemical division of labor is instructive. Viral genes help define which DNA molecules initiate copying, while host replication proteins supply major catalytic and structural functions. Dependence on cellular proteins does not make the viral genome a mere passive segment of the host chromosome; the viral origin defines its own replicating template during productive replication.

Later in the classical lambda productive program, replication produces long concatemeric DNA through rolling-circle-type replication. The advantage of a concatemer in this biological context is that it provides repeated genome-length units as substrates for genome packaging. Each repeat contains the information needed for one mature genome. A packaging-associated cleavage system recognizes appropriate sites in the repeated DNA and generates unit-length DNA with the required ends. Synthesis of the long DNA and cleavage into genome units are different processes. If DNA synthesis were normal but packaging-associated cleavage failed, long products could still accumulate without a corresponding production of mature genome-containing particles.

The transition from theta-type replication to production of concatemers illustrates that a replicon need not use one fixed geometry throughout its biological existence. Early amplification can increase the number of circular templates, while later replication creates long substrates appropriate for packaging. The precise molecular control of this transition has been investigated experimentally and should not be reduced to a simplistic universal switch. Classical kinetic studies of lambda DNA distinguished the early multiplication of circular molecules from later extension of rolling-circle structures. The important conclusion for examinations is the association of early theta intermediates and later concatemeric products in this representative system, not an assumption that all DNA viruses follow the same sequence.

A hypothetical numerical example distinguishes concatemer length from product count. Let a model viral genome be 40 kilobase pairs, and let a continuous double-stranded DNA product contain five complete head-to-tail repeats, for a total length of 200 kilobase pairs. If the product contains all required processing sites and is partitioned perfectly into genome-length units, the theoretical number of units is five. This is not the actual yield of infectious particles. Cleavage, packaging, particle assembly and other biological requirements may lower the number of viable products. If a 210-kilobase product contains only five complete repeats and an additional incomplete segment, one cannot simply round the quotient 210/40 up to six. The amount of information in a complete genome and the physical number of complete genome units constrain the interpretation.

The single-stranded DNA bacteriophage phiX174 illustrates a different starting substrate. A single-stranded viral genome cannot undergo conventional theta replication as a double-stranded circle immediately upon entry. It first acquires a complementary DNA strand, using host activities to establish a double-stranded replicative form. This form permits both expression of viral information and further DNA copying. Viral gene A protein participates in origin-specific nicking and rolling-circle replication of the replicative form. The strand produced or displaced in the relevant replication phase can ultimately supply single-stranded viral genomes. The cycle therefore includes a double-stranded intermediate even though the genome in the mature viral particle is single-stranded.

This example helps resolve an apparent paradox. If a virus carries only one DNA strand, how can it use ordinary DNA-dependent RNA polymerase to produce transcripts from a normal double-stranded promoter? Formation of a complementary strand can generate the necessary double-stranded DNA context. Equally, producing a DNA complement is a replication event but does not automatically create a second genome-containing viral particle. The viral system must produce the correct strand type and perform additional steps to generate mature progeny. The distinction among genome synthesis, replicative form and packaged genome is more informative than a broad classification of the virus as single-stranded.

Consider what would be observed if complementary-strand synthesis of an entering phiX174-like genome were blocked. The entering single-stranded molecules might remain detectable briefly, yet the double-stranded replicative form would be reduced and subsequent amplification impaired. In a different conceptual perturbation, the initial double-stranded form could accumulate if later nick-dependent replication were impaired. These distinct structural phenotypes identify different stages of the copying pathway. They also demonstrate why the presence of a viral genome at the beginning of an observation is not evidence that productive DNA replication has taken place.

Not all viral DNA replication can be reduced to either theta or rolling-circle initiation. Adenovirus supplies a textbook example of protein-primed replication of linear double-stranded DNA. A viral precursor terminal protein serves as a primer through covalent attachment of the first nucleotide, and a viral DNA polymerase extends the growing strand. The protein primer provides a solution to the problem of initiating synthesis at the end of a linear template without relying on a conventional upstream nucleic-acid primer. The essential lesson is that initiation requires a suitable chemical group for the first linkage, but the way this group is provided varies. Once a nucleotide is linked and extension proceeds, subsequent nucleotide addition obeys the ordinary directionality of DNA synthesis.

Some small double-stranded circular DNA viruses provide still other examples of theta-like replication using a combination of virus-specific initiation functions and cellular replication proteins. Simian virus 40, commonly used as a classic model of eukaryotic DNA replication, uses its large T antigen to recognize its origin and promote DNA unwinding and works with host replication machinery. A circular genome and bidirectional fork movement make its replication geometry comparable in broad outline to theta replication of bacterial circles, but the protein machinery reflects the eukaryotic host environment. This example reinforces that similar geometries can be implemented by different sets of enzymes.

RNA viruses are outside the central scope of this DNA replication chapter. Retroviruses, which pass through a DNA intermediate, use reverse transcription at an important stage of their cycle. Reverse transcription differs from DNA-dependent DNA replication because an RNA template initially directs DNA synthesis. Although integrated retroviral DNA may subsequently be copied as part of a chromosome, it is misleading to classify the entire replication cycle as ordinary plasmid theta replication. The appropriate classification follows the relevant substrate and stage, not simply the fact that DNA is detectable somewhere in the life cycle.

13. Quantitative reasoning across replication geometries

The geometry of an extrachromosomal replicon influences how much template each progressing fork must traverse. Consider a circular duplex of length L base pairs with one active origin and two forks that begin at the same time and advance at constant speeds v1 and v2 measured in base pairs per minute. If the forks travel along opposite directions around the circle and copying is complete when the total distance equals L, the completion time T satisfies v1T+v2T=L. Hence T=L/(v1+v2). If only one fork advances, T=L/v under the equivalent assumptions. These equations describe the time required to traverse the template and do not include initiation delays, fork pauses, primer processing or physical decatenation.

For a hypothetical 12,000-base-pair circle with two forks moving at 600 and 400 base pairs per minute, completion occurs after 12 minutes, since the combined speed is 1,000 base pairs per minute. The two forks travel 7,200 and 4,800 base pairs, respectively. They do not meet opposite the origin because their speeds differ. In a separate unidirectional model with one 600-base-pair-per-minute fork, traversal requires 20 minutes. These values do not imply that introducing a second origin necessarily yields the same effect as introducing a second fork. The position and firing time of multiple origins determine how much each fork travels. The derivation applies to the specified single-origin system.

In theta replication, the physical length of duplex regions changes as parental DNA is copied. Let f be the fraction of the initial genome sequence that has already been replicated on both strands. The initial parental molecule contains L base pairs, and an additional fL base pairs of daughter DNA are synthesized when both complementary strands over that fraction have been completed. The total duplex DNA content is therefore L(1+f) base pairs, neglecting short single-stranded regions and primer effects. If a circle of length 8,000 base pairs is 75 percent replicated, the total DNA content of the connected intermediate is equivalent to 14,000 base pairs. There is not yet necessarily a pair of separate 8,000-base-pair molecules. DNA mass and number of physically distinct molecules tell different stories.

Rolling-circle products are quantified differently. For a first-strand polymerase that synthesizes at v nucleotides per minute for a time t, the amount of newly synthesized first-strand DNA is vt nucleotides under a constant-speed approximation. If the circular template contains L nucleotides, the quotient vt/L gives the number of template lengths traversed. The number of complete repeats is the integer part of that quotient when synthesis is uninterrupted from an appropriate starting position. Additional complementary-strand synthesis is needed before a displaced single-stranded product becomes fully duplex. For example, a hypothetical 6,000-nucleotide circle copied at 500 nucleotides per minute for 30 minutes yields 15,000 newly synthesized nucleotides, corresponding to two complete template lengths and one-half of a third. A measurement in nucleotides must not be labeled as base pairs unless duplex formation is established.

These examples illustrate a broader principle about molecular quantities. A single genome equivalent of a double-stranded DNA circle of L base pairs contains approximately 2L nucleotide residues. Replication of one intact duplex into two intact duplexes requires incorporation of approximately 2L new deoxyribonucleotide residues, ignoring replacement events and primer processing details. For a 5,000-base-pair plasmid, producing an additional duplex copy therefore involves approximately 10,000 newly incorporated deoxyribonucleotide residues. A reaction that has incorporated only 5,000 residues could have copied one full strand or partially copied both strands. Without structural or strand-specific information the incorporation count cannot discriminate those possibilities.

DNA dosage can also be inferred from a mass ratio, subject to explicit assumptions. If a cell has one complete 4,000,000-base-pair chromosome and twenty complete 5,000-base-pair plasmids, the plasmid DNA contributes 100,000 base pairs and the total cellular DNA represented by these molecules is 4,100,000 base pairs. Assuming similar average mass per base pair, plasmid DNA accounts for approximately 2.44 percent of the total. A mass fraction is not equal to the fraction of molecules. The twenty small plasmids greatly outnumber the single chromosome in physical copy number, despite accounting for a small proportion of DNA mass. Cells with multiple chromosomes or replication intermediates require a modified denominator.

Sequence-specific quantification introduces its own assumptions. A hypothetical measurement reports a plasmid marker to chromosomal reference ratio of 12. If each complete plasmid contains one marker and each cell has one reference locus copy, the inferred average abundance is twelve plasmid genome equivalents per cell. If the same cells actually contain two copies of the reference locus on average, the inferred abundance is twenty-four. Neither calculation guarantees that the plasmids are structurally intact or separate particles. Fragments carrying the marker, integrated sequences and tandem repeats could contribute to the signal. The most defensible interpretation reports the directly measured ratio and then states the conditions under which it estimates complete plasmid copies.

When quantitative amplification is used as a conceptual measurement, an ideal doubling of amplified product per cycle relates a difference in threshold cycle to a ratio of starting template abundance. Under matched efficiencies and calibration, a difference of three cycles corresponds to an eightfold difference in starting quantity. If the plasmid marker reaches the threshold three cycles earlier than a single-copy reference, the idealized ratio is eight marker copies per reference copy. In real measurements, primer efficiency, sample quality and calibration matter; threshold differences cannot be used uncritically. The method estimates target sequence abundance rather than the physical topology or inheritance competence of the DNA.

14. Experimental discrimination and integration of the major strategies

Mechanistic evidence is strongest when different measurements address different features of the same replication process. Physical visualization can reveal a branched circle or a tail. Sequence-specific measurements can determine which regions are present at increased abundance. Label incorporation can identify newly synthesized DNA. A controlled perturbation can connect a particular origin or protein to a change in replication. None of these alone necessarily establishes the entire mechanism. Their combination can distinguish a structural intermediate that participates in copying from an unusual DNA form that accumulates for an unrelated reason.

Consider a hypothetical time-resolved dataset. At the first observation, a preparation contains closed circular molecules. At an intermediate observation, many molecules contain an internal duplicated arc and two branch points. At a late observation, the predominant products are separate duplex circles of the original length. The data are consistent with theta replication. A second preparation starts with similar circles, but early products include nicked circles and extended single-stranded tails, followed later by displaced circles and completed duplex products. This sequence of structural states is consistent with rolling-circle replication. The order of appearance is important: a nick detected after all products have accumulated could instead be caused by sample handling, whereas a nick associated with initiation and followed by extension supports a role in the mechanism.

Electron microscopy and other structural approaches can be interpreted using the same caution. A replication bubble is a physical structure in which DNA has been duplicated over a region. A recombination intermediate may also have branch points, and a broken DNA molecule can display an unusual contour. To distinguish these cases, one asks whether new DNA label is present in the expected arms, whether structures depend on the relevant origin and whether the branch points progress over time. A static image cannot reveal by itself the polarity of synthesis or whether a structure leads to viable daughter molecules. Those conclusions require complementary evidence.

Electrophoretic mobility is another useful conceptual tool. DNA molecules of the same sequence length can migrate differently because of their topology. A supercoiled circular plasmid, an open circular plasmid containing a nick, and a linear DNA molecule of the same length often occupy different positions in a gel. The exact order depends on experimental conditions, so one should avoid a universal claim about which topology always migrates fastest. A linear DNA size marker calibrates linear molecules, not necessarily the apparent mobility of a compact circular form. A change in band position can therefore reflect topology rather than changed genome length. Conversely, a double-length product can arise through recombination or replication; a length measurement alone does not establish its origin.

Two-dimensional DNA gel analysis offers a standard example of evidence linking molecular mass with geometry. In an idealized analysis of a defined DNA fragment, replication intermediates can separate according to size and structural shape. Fragments containing an internal active origin may produce a bubble-like pattern; fragments through which a single fork passes can display a Y-like pattern. The presence or absence of a pattern depends on the position of the origin relative to fragment boundaries and on the abundance of intermediates. A Y pattern does not necessarily mean that the entire genome has only one replication fork. It may simply mean that one fork is traversing the particular DNA fragment being examined. The inference must match the scale of the measurement.

Genetic complementation provides a different kind of evidence. Suppose a hypothetical replicon contains an origin O and an initiator gene I. Mutation of I prevents new DNA synthesis, but a functional I gene supplied separately restores synthesis. Mutation of O is not rescued by the same source of functional I. The pattern is consistent with a diffusible initiator acting on a required local DNA sequence. However, control experiments must show comparable initial DNA amounts, cell viability and host replication. If the origin mutation destabilizes the entire DNA molecule or prevents detection of the marker, the apparent lack of complementation could have another explanation. Good experimental reasoning separates the observation from the interpretation and identifies a plausible alternative.

In organellar DNA studies, a decrease in abundance is likewise insufficient to locate a defect. If newly synthesized DNA falls before existing genome abundance falls, a defect in synthesis becomes plausible. If nascent synthesis is near normal but pre-existing genomes disappear more rapidly, increased turnover becomes plausible. If the average organellar DNA per cell falls because a population contains more cells with low organelle mass, the observed change may reflect cell composition. Normalization to a nuclear reference and an independent measure of organellar content can help distinguish these levels, but each normalization has assumptions. No single ratio universally establishes the activity of a specific DNA polymerase.

The diverse mechanisms can now be organized around a shared set of questions. What is the template at the moment of initiation? What event provides an extendable primer end? Which strand or strands are copied at the moving junction? How is the product completed and physically separated? What ensures that enough copies persist in the appropriate biological setting? For theta plasmids, these questions lead to duplex opening, priming, fork movement, circle completion and feedback with segregation. For rolling-circle plasmids, they lead to nick-dependent initiation, displacement, termination and complementary synthesis. For mammalian mitochondrial genomes, they lead to RNA-associated initiation, temporally separated strand synthesis and maintenance of multiple nucleoids. For viruses, the answers depend on the replication form and on the relationship between genome synthesis and particle production.

These questions also clarify the relationship to the next chapter on replication fidelity. The current chapter has concentrated on generating enough complete copies and recognizing distinct mechanisms of initiation and progression. But a complete physical copy can still contain an incorrect nucleotide. Increasing replication frequency may increase the number of copying events without changing the error probability per nucleotide, and a stable copy-number controller does not prove that every copy is genetically accurate. The next logical step is to ask how polymerases select correct nucleotides, how proofreading removes some mistakes, and how mismatch repair corrects errors that escape the initial copying reaction. Quantity, molecular completeness, inheritance and sequence fidelity are related but distinguishable properties of a replication system.
