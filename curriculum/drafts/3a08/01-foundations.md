# 3.A.08 — DNA Damage: Sources, Lesions and Consequences

## 1. The difference between a stable genome and an indestructible molecule

A cell inherits an arrangement of nucleotides, not an abstract string of symbols. The distinction matters because every nucleotide is a material object with bonds that can be broken or modified. A chromosome can preserve a sequence across many generations even though individual molecules within that chromosome undergo chemical reactions. Such persistence is a property of the biological system, rather than proof that DNA itself is chemically inert. Accurate copying, chemical protection, removal of damaged components and controlled cellular responses contribute different parts of that persistence. To understand why these activities are necessary, first imagine DNA in the absence of any copying error. Even a polymerase that always selected the correct incoming nucleotide would be insufficient to maintain the genome if the parental molecule were allowed to decay between successive divisions.

DNA normally consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. Each nucleotide contributes a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and a nitrogen-containing base. Adjacent sugars are connected by phosphodiester bonds, whereas each base attaches to its own sugar through an N-glycosidic bond. Bases face toward the interior of the double helix, where stacking and hydrogen bonding stabilize the association of the strands. Complementary pairing means that the two strands encode overlapping information. In a normal G:C base pair, the presence of guanine specifies cytosine on the opposing strand. If a chemical reaction removes one guanine without altering the other strand, the original information is not necessarily lost from the duplex. It remains represented by the cytosine, although it is no longer represented by an intact guanine on its own strand. This asymmetry gives cells an opportunity to restore information, provided that the complementary strand remains available and unaltered.

The bonds involved in these structures have distinct biological roles. A phosphodiester bond provides physical continuity along the strand; a glycosidic bond anchors a base at its position; noncovalent interactions position the bases relative to one another. A lesion may interfere with one role without immediately interfering with all the others. For instance, removal of a base can leave both phosphodiester bonds associated with the sugar intact. The sequence then has a missing chemical letter but the chain has not been severed. By contrast, a single-strand break interrupts the chain but can leave every neighboring base chemically unmodified. A covalent bridge connecting opposite strands introduces yet another difficulty: the strands may remain continuous, but their necessary separation becomes obstructed. These examples explain why chromosome damage cannot be adequately characterized using only the number of base pairs present.

The word lesion refers to a chemically or structurally abnormal feature in a DNA molecule, including a modified base, a missing base, an abnormal covalent bridge or a broken backbone. A mutation is a persistent change in genetic sequence or chromosome structure relative to the relevant starting state. The two concepts overlap in their consequences but are not interchangeable. If cytosine becomes uracil within DNA, the molecule contains an abnormal base at that site. If repair restores cytosine before that site is copied incorrectly, the original base sequence can be maintained. If replication instead produces a thymine-containing daughter duplex after the lesion has been copied, a C:G to T:A substitution may become established. Conversely, a fixed T:A pair can be chemically normal even though it differs from the inherited C:G pair. A chemical assay that detects uracil would not necessarily detect the fixed substitution.

A third term, mismatch, describes inappropriate pairing between opposing nucleotides. A G:T pair can arise when a polymerase inserts thymine opposite guanine, or when a chemically modified cytosine becomes thymine while remaining opposite its original partner. Both outcomes produce the same pairing arrangement, but their histories differ. A mechanism that simply removes the nucleotide on one specified strand works only if that strand has been identified appropriately. During replication, a mismatch can indicate an error in the new strand. During chemical deamination, the altered nucleotide can be on either strand. The geometric abnormality alone does not reveal the complete history of the molecule.

There is also an important distinction between a potentially mutagenic lesion and a blocking lesion. A potentially mutagenic lesion changes the information that an enzyme may extract from the template; a blocking lesion prevents the enzyme from continuing efficiently. These categories are not mutually exclusive. One altered base might pair readily with a wrong nucleotide while a bulky chemical adduct might almost completely inhibit an ordinary polymerase. The outcome depends on the enzyme, the lesion's position, the surrounding sequence and the availability of alternative processing mechanisms. Thus a compound that causes very extensive DNA damage may produce comparatively few recovered mutations if severely damaged cells fail to survive. Survival, the frequency of chemical lesions and the frequency of inherited mutations must be considered as distinct observations.

In a mature non-dividing cell, a lesion may never be encountered by a replication fork during the time of observation. Nevertheless, the cell uses genes to produce RNA, and an RNA polymerase may encounter that lesion. Failure of RNA production can impair the supply of a needed protein. In a rapidly dividing cell the same lesion may additionally obstruct chromosome duplication. This is why the effect of DNA damage depends on cell state and on the functions occurring at the damaged locus. A base change in an inactive region may have little immediate physiological effect while a blocking lesion in a heavily used essential gene may matter before there is any inherited mutation.

A beginner can therefore approach DNA damage through three successive questions. First, which chemical structure has changed? Second, what does that alteration do to the DNA molecule as a substrate for enzymes? Third, what outcome follows if the altered substrate persists? Each question concerns a different scale of explanation: chemistry, molecular function and biological consequence. The distinction becomes especially useful when comparing radiation, hydrolytic reactions and oxidative stress, because their sources may differ even when some of their molecular products overlap.

## 2. Why endogenous DNA damage is unavoidable

A chromosome exists in a hydrated, chemically active environment. Water is indispensable for enzymes, solute transport and the stability of cellular structures, but it can also participate in chemical reactions involving DNA. Cellular metabolism produces reactive molecules, including oxidants and naturally occurring methyl-group donors. Some interact with DNA without the deliberate involvement of a DNA-modifying enzyme. Temperature, pH, availability of metals and local molecular architecture affect the rates of individual processes. The important conclusion is not that every chromosome deteriorates rapidly under every condition, but that none of these reactions has a universally zero probability.

An apparently spontaneous DNA lesion may have a well-defined chemical origin. In this context, spontaneous means that the event can occur without a specifically applied external damaging treatment. Hydrolysis can proceed through reactions involving water; deamination can change an amino substituent on a base; intracellular oxidants can alter bases or sugars. These reactions have chemical activation barriers, so they occur at characteristic rates that depend on conditions. At ordinary cellular temperatures the reactions are slow at an individual site, but the number of susceptible sites is enormous. A small probability multiplied by billions of chemical targets can correspond to a substantial number of events in a population of molecules.

Tomas Lindahl's work established the biological importance of intrinsic DNA instability. Measurements of decay reactions in DNA and subsequent investigation of repair systems showed why the preservation of genetic information requires continuous molecular maintenance. His 1993 review synthesized evidence that the primary chemical structure of DNA is not permanently stable. The key reasoning is that a process can be infrequent at a single nucleotide yet impose a major cumulative burden when considered over an entire genome and across a lifetime. This reconciles the familiar observation of stable heredity with the experimentally demonstrable vulnerability of DNA.

A measurement made with purified DNA can reveal spontaneous chemical change without requiring cells to replicate. For example, if DNA is kept under a defined set of conditions and released bases are detected over time, the resulting increase indicates that nucleotide chemistry changes even when a DNA polymerase is absent. A suitable comparison requires samples that began with equivalent DNA, a consistent method of measuring the chemical product and control of conditions such as temperature and pH. A background measurement taken at the initial time helps distinguish pre-existing damage from new damage. Such an experiment establishes that chemical loss is possible and can be quantified. It does not by itself establish how many lesions accumulate in a living chromosome, where DNA-associated proteins and enzymes alter the situation.

The cellular abundance of a lesion is determined by formation and disappearance. Suppose lesions form continuously and are removed soon after they form. A measurement taken at one instant can find relatively few lesions even though many have formed and been removed during the preceding hours. In a different cell, the formation rate might be lower but the removal process defective, giving a higher instantaneous abundance. Without knowing the time dependence or the removal capacity, it is not legitimate to interpret an increased abundance as evidence that formation necessarily increased. Part C problems commonly test this distinction through time-course measurements, mutant comparisons or different recovery periods.

It is also necessary to distinguish endogenous source from intracellular site. An oxidant can originate inside the cell during metabolism, or an external stimulus can initiate intracellular oxidant production. In both cases the immediate DNA lesion may be chemically similar. Conversely, one source such as ionizing radiation can generate several classes of damage. A classification by origin therefore answers a different question from classification by lesion type. The first helps explain exposure and prevention; the second helps explain template behavior and repair requirements. Neither is sufficient on its own.

In bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts, genetic material resides close to biochemical processes that involve electron transfer. This makes redox chemistry relevant to genome stability, but proximity is not enough to predict actual damage rates. Protective systems, DNA packaging, local metal availability, metabolic conditions and repair capacity differ among compartments and organisms. A photosynthetic cell additionally experiences light-dependent reactions, while its nuclear DNA may also be affected by environmental ultraviolet exposure. These comparisons support a general biological principle: the sources and processing of DNA damage reflect the physical and biochemical environment in which a genome is maintained.

A normal cell also has intentionally produced DNA intermediates. During replication and repair, enzymes can transiently generate nicks or gaps. During specific biological processes, regulated DNA breaks serve important purposes. Their existence should not be confused with indiscriminate injury. A controlled intermediate is normally created at a defined stage and subsequently processed in a coordinated way. An accidental or persistent intermediate becomes hazardous when this coordination fails. Thus even the boundary between an ordinary intermediate and a pathological lesion can depend on context, timing and whether the appropriate downstream process is functioning.

## 3. Classifying DNA lesions by the molecular target

Classification begins with whether the base, sugar, phosphodiester chain or relationship between strands has changed. A modified base such as uracil can remain attached to an intact sugar and an unbroken chain. An abasic site has lost its base while retaining the sugar initially. Damage to the sugar can destabilize the backbone, and cleavage of the backbone produces a strand discontinuity. These classes are connected chemically: a base modification may destabilize a glycosidic bond, base loss may make the sugar vulnerable to further reaction, and subsequent sugar damage can generate a strand break. A sample can therefore contain both an initiating lesion and downstream products.

The double helix can also acquire covalent links not present in normal DNA. Adjacent pyrimidines on one strand can become linked after absorption of ultraviolet radiation. A bulky adduct can project away from its attachment point and interfere with the fit of the duplex or of an enzyme acting on it. An interstrand crosslink joins the two complementary strands and can prevent their local separation. A DNA–protein crosslink joins DNA to a protein, creating an obstacle whose properties differ from those of a small altered base. Although all can interfere with enzyme progression, the geometry and size of the obstacle are different and the processing required to remove it is not identical.

Structural classification should be combined with an understanding of the coding face of the base. Polymerases distinguish nucleotides through several features, including the geometry and chemical interactions of the nascent pair. A modification that changes the donor or acceptor pattern can create an alternative pairing preference without producing a large deformation in the free duplex. An apparently small lesion may thus be unusually mutagenic. Conversely, a modification outside the coding face may primarily alter stability or interactions with proteins rather than immediately specify a different partner. Inferring mutagenic risk from the size of a chemical group alone is therefore unreliable.

A final distinction is between local and clustered damage. One isolated altered base can often be processed while the opposing strand remains intact. Several lesions located near one another may compromise both strands of the same region. Removing two nearby lesions independently can then create intermediates whose combination is more disruptive than either original site. Local clustering is particularly relevant to the physical deposition of ionizing radiation energy. A large number of well-separated lesions and a small number of clustered lesions are not equivalent burdens, even when a simple assay reports the same total number of damaged bases.

These categories establish the language needed for the subsequent chemical examples. The first reactions to examine are hydrolysis and deamination, because they demonstrate particularly clearly that the molecules responsible for storing information can change in the absence of an external irradiation or deliberate mutagen exposure.
