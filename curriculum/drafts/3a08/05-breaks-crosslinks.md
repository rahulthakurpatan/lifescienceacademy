## 11. Single-strand breaks: loss of continuity within one DNA chain

A single-strand break, often abbreviated SSB, interrupts one of the two covalent polynucleotide chains in a duplex. The other chain remains continuous at the corresponding position. This distinction separates a single-strand break from an abasic site: a missing base does not necessarily interrupt the chain, whereas a break necessarily interrupts the linkage between successive nucleotides. It also separates a simple nick from a gap. A nick is a discontinuity between two adjacent nucleotides, while a gap contains one or more missing nucleotides. A gap necessarily has discontinuities at its boundaries, but its chemical and informational properties differ from those of a nick with no missing residues.

Complementary hydrogen bonding can hold the two sides of a nicked strand in position against the intact partner. A chromosome containing an SSB therefore need not fall into two separate double-stranded fragments. In an isolated linear duplex under conditions preserving base pairing, the nick may be relatively difficult to recognize by fragment length alone. In a denaturing environment, however, the interrupted strand separates into shorter pieces while the continuous complementary strand retains its length. The contrast between native and denaturing observations is a useful way to reason about the structure of the damaged DNA.

A covalently closed circular DNA molecule provides another instructive example. When both strands are covalently continuous and the duplex is topologically constrained, a molecule can retain supercoiling. A nick in one strand permits rotation around the remaining continuous strand and can relax superhelical stress. The resulting open circular form has a different conformation from the compact supercoiled molecule. Electrophoretic separation can reveal this conversion even though the number of base pairs remains essentially unchanged. The precise migration order depends on gel and DNA conditions, but in commonly used agarose gel conditions an open circular plasmid tends to migrate more slowly than the compact supercoiled form. A linearized plasmid of the same sequence is a separate structural form and often migrates between them. Comparing bands therefore requires knowledge of topology as well as molecular length.

One cannot assume that all strand breaks have ends suitable for immediate joining. Ordinary DNA ligases join appropriately juxtaposed 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate ends using a chemically activated intermediate. A break generated by sugar oxidation may instead contain a blocked 3′ terminus or another abnormal end structure. The problem is then not simply that the ends are spatially separated; their chemistry may be incompatible with ligation. Additional processing can be required before joining. The exact reactions differ with lesion type and are taught in the subsequent repair nodes. At this stage the principle is that a gel reporting broken DNA cannot distinguish a simple ligatable nick from every chemically complex break.

Single-strand discontinuities also arise during normal cellular reactions. On the lagging strand of a replication fork, DNA is synthesized as fragments that eventually become covalently linked. Temporary nicks are therefore expected intermediates. Topoisomerases use transient strand cleavage to regulate DNA topology; their normal catalytic cycles restore continuity. An enzyme-linked break becomes a problem when the reaction is interrupted before restoration. DNA repair similarly creates transient interruptions to remove damaged constituents and insert replacements. A sample undergoing active repair may contain a temporary excess of nicks even when the initiating lesions are being processed successfully.

The most important biological consequence of a persistent single-strand break may appear when a replication fork reaches it. Consider a replisome moving along a duplex toward an interruption in one parental template. As the parental strands separate, the continuity of one template can no longer support the same molecular arrangement. Depending on the strand affected and fork configuration, progression can generate a broken or collapsed replication structure, including a one-ended double-strand break. This phenomenon explains why a lesion that seemed structurally modest in non-replicating DNA may become severe during S phase. The replication machinery does not simply copy across a physical discontinuity as though it were an ordinary altered base.

An SSB on the template strand of an actively transcribed gene can also interfere with RNA production, particularly if the discontinuity has abnormal ends or creates a gap. The exact response depends on lesion location and the transcription system. An interruption elsewhere in the duplex may exert less immediate effect on that transcription event. A lesion's consequence cannot therefore be predicted solely from its classification: its strand identity and genomic position are relevant variables. This principle connects strand integrity to the functional use of DNA without requiring a detailed description of transcriptional regulation.

## 12. Double-strand breaks and loss of local template continuity

A double-strand break, or DSB, interrupts both polynucleotide chains within a local region so that duplex continuity is lost. The cuts need not occur exactly opposite each other. Closely staggered cuts can produce single-stranded extensions, and cuts may generate blunt ends or ends with complex chemical structures. A diagram representing two aligned straight breaks is useful for teaching the basic definition, but it should not imply that all actual DSBs possess identical ends. The spacing of the cuts and the chemistry of the termini influence how the broken molecule can subsequently be processed.

In a linear chromosome, a DSB separates the continuous duplex into two broken portions. The complementary strand that would normally supply local information is itself interrupted. This makes a DSB qualitatively different from an isolated base lesion in an otherwise intact duplex. A homologous copy, such as a sister chromatid after replication, may provide an alternative source of information. Alternatively, the broken ends may be joined directly if the cellular machinery can identify and process them. Neither restoration route can be assumed simply from the detection of a break. The availability of homologous DNA, cell-cycle state, end chemistry and competition among processing pathways affect the possible outcomes.

Some DSBs are generated directly, for example when local ionizing energy deposition damages both strands. Other DSBs arise secondarily. Two nearby single-strand breaks may together disrupt duplex continuity; processing nearby damaged bases can generate opposed incisions; a fork encountering a persistent nick can produce a one-ended break. In each case the measured DSB is a product of a different sequence of events. This distinction is essential when interpreting a time course after an external exposure. A delayed increase in DSBs may reflect processing of earlier lesions rather than new primary DSB formation by the original external source.

A conventional double-stranded restriction enzyme cut provides a clean conceptual comparator. The enzyme recognizes a particular sequence and cleaves defined phosphodiester bonds to generate products with characteristic ends. A radiation-induced break may occur at a less predictable position and may be accompanied by nearby damaged bases or abnormal sugar residues. Thus two samples can contain the same number of double-strand breaks and still differ substantially in how readily the breaks can be restored. Counting the breaks does not fully describe their molecular complexity.

When a chromosome is broken, end association becomes a problem. Joining the original partners appropriately can restore continuity, but inappropriate joining can connect DNA segments that were not previously adjacent. Loss of sequence during processing can produce a deletion; connection of unrelated chromosomal segments can produce a rearrangement. An exchange between broken chromosomes can lead to a translocation. Such outcomes are mutations at the chromosome level if the altered structure becomes established. The original DSB was a lesion, and the rearranged chromosome is one possible stable genetic result of processing it. The distinction is the same in principle as that between cytosine deamination and a fixed C-to-T substitution, even though the scale of the damage is much larger.

A break may also leave a chromosome fragment without the structures required for normal segregation. In eukaryotic cells, the centromere enables interaction with the chromosome segregation machinery. A fragment lacking a functional centromere may fail to segregate normally. Unprotected broken ends can also participate in abnormal chromosome interactions. This links the DNA integrity problem to the chromosome architecture and cell division concepts from Unit 2. A damaged chromosome need not produce only a local sequence error; it can alter the inheritance of much larger regions of genetic material.

The biological burden of a DSB cannot be reduced to a universal probability of lethality. Some organisms and cell states efficiently restore substantial amounts of broken DNA; others are more sensitive. Even in one cell, a break at an essential or structurally difficult locus may have different consequences from a break elsewhere. A cell may delay division, restore continuity, survive with a rearrangement, undergo permanent arrest or die. The observed outcome represents a response of the entire system, not a simple property of the broken phosphodiester bonds.

## 13. Bulky adducts, intrastrand crosslinks, interstrand crosslinks and DNA–protein crosslinks

A DNA adduct is a chemical group covalently attached to DNA as a result of a reaction. An adduct may be relatively small, like a methyl group, or sufficiently bulky to alter the shape and flexibility of the double helix. Some environmental compounds undergo metabolic activation to generate reactive derivatives that attach to nucleobases. A well-known example in discussions of carcinogenesis is the formation of bulky guanine adducts by reactive metabolites of certain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The parent compound and the ultimate DNA-reactive derivative should not be conflated: metabolic transformations can influence whether an exposure creates a DNA lesion at all. This provides a bridge between metabolism and genetic toxicity.

A bulky adduct can interfere with enzyme progression even when the backbone is intact and the original base remains present. To act on DNA, a polymerase must position the template within an active site and coordinate its movement relative to incoming nucleotides. An attached chemical group can make an otherwise ordinary base difficult to accommodate. The adduct can also disrupt pairing or stacking, changing how the local DNA segment is recognized. In some cases a bypass polymerase can copy across it with an altered probability of misincorporation. In other cases progression stops. Therefore, the structure of an adduct determines a range of possible consequences rather than providing a simple answer about which base will ultimately replace the original one.

An intrastrand crosslink joins two sites on the same strand. A cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer is a photochemical example; certain chemical agents create other types of intrastrand adducts. Cisplatin is a standard example from molecular biology: its DNA lesions include predominantly intrastrand platinum crosslinks, especially between adjacent purine residues, as well as less frequent interstrand crosslinks and other adducts. The important lesson is the geometry of linkage, not the clinical use of the compound. Adjacent guanines joined by a platinum-containing adduct differ chemically from adjacent thymines joined by a cyclobutane ring, even though both lesions restrict same-strand nucleotide positioning.

An interstrand crosslink joins the complementary chains to one another through an abnormal covalent linkage. The ordinary double helix is stabilized by noncovalent interactions between the strands, which can be disrupted locally to allow replication and transcription. An interstrand covalent bridge cannot be separated simply by unwinding the helix or breaking ordinary hydrogen bonds. This creates a direct mechanical obstacle to processes requiring strand separation. Importantly, such a molecule may retain a continuous sugar-phosphate backbone in both strands. A test measuring intact fragment length could therefore fail to reveal the very lesion that prevents the molecule from functioning as a template.

The reason interstrand crosslinks are difficult to process is that both templates are involved in the same chemical structure. Excision of one modified base alone may not suffice to separate the strands and restore normal information. Multiple coordinated reactions may be required to release the linkage and restore the affected region. The repair details belong to later chapters; here the structural explanation is sufficient to predict that an interstrand crosslink can block replication and transcription severely. A student who understands this physical constraint can infer the effects of an unfamiliar crosslinking agent even without memorizing a specific chemical name.

Crosslinks can also connect DNA to proteins. A DNA–protein crosslink may arise when a reactive chemical links a protein to DNA or when a normally transient covalent enzyme–DNA intermediate persists. Topoisomerases provide a useful example of the latter possibility. They normally create controlled cleavage intermediates in which the enzyme is covalently linked to DNA, followed by restoration of the strand. If that intermediate persists, the attached protein and the interrupted backbone can together form an obstacle. A large protein adduct differs from a small base adduct in both steric properties and the possible requirements for processing. This illustrates how enzyme malfunction and chemical injury can generate related structural problems.

The distinction between a trapped topoisomerase intermediate and a simple inhibitor of catalytic activity deserves attention. A compound that prevents an enzyme from performing its normal catalytic reaction need not necessarily increase the amount of the covalent cleavage intermediate. A compound that stabilizes such an intermediate can increase DNA-associated cleavage complexes even if the net physiological outcome is reduced topoisomerase function. Both may disrupt DNA metabolism, but their primary molecular effects differ. An experimental result showing accumulation of protein-linked breaks is more directly consistent with persistent cleavage complexes than with a nonspecific reduction in enzyme abundance alone.

The effect of an interstrand crosslink on DNA separation suggests a useful experimental prediction. If DNA strands would ordinarily separate under denaturing conditions, a region containing a covalent interstrand bridge may remain associated even when normal base pairing is disrupted. Similarly, crosslinking can reduce the migration of DNA in a break-sensitive assay because the damaged strands cannot move as freely. A reduced migration signal is not necessarily evidence of less damage; it can reflect a different lesion class that opposes migration. An experiment that introduces a controlled comparison of break formation and crosslink-associated retention can help distinguish these possibilities, provided the analysis remains sensitive to other causes of reduced migration.

The general relationship between lesion structure and cellular consequences can now be seen clearly. Small miscoding modifications change the information presented at a nucleotide position. Missing bases remove that information from one strand. Strand breaks disrupt physical continuity. Bulky adducts obstruct protein movement. Same-strand links restrict neighboring residues, and interstrand links obstruct strand separation itself. These categories are useful precisely because they lead to different mechanistic predictions even when they all fall under the general heading of DNA damage.
