## 14. How DNA damage affects replication, transcription and cell physiology

To copy a chromosome, the replisome must expose single-stranded DNA and repeatedly present template nucleotides to polymerases. A chemical change can affect any of these requirements. An interstrand crosslink prevents normal local strand separation, whereas a small miscoding lesion may permit separation but alter nucleotide selection. A polymerase-blocking lesion can arrest synthesis while a helicase continues to unwind DNA for some distance. This uncoupling can produce exposed single-stranded regions that must be protected from inappropriate reactions. Thus a single chemical lesion can generate a larger structural disturbance through the activities of the replication machinery.

The choice between accurate synthesis and arrest depends on the substrate and the enzyme. A high-fidelity replicative polymerase has a constrained active site that helps reject unsuitable base pairs, but this precision also makes some damaged templates difficult to copy. A specialized translesion polymerase may have an active site that accommodates a damaged template more readily. Its activity can permit replication to resume, but the accuracy of bypass depends on both the enzyme and the lesion. Some bypass polymerases are relatively accurate for specific lesions, and their effects cannot be summarized as uniformly error prone. Crucially, synthesis past a lesion does not necessarily remove it from the parental strand. The distinction between tolerating damage and chemically restoring DNA will be developed in Node 3.A.10.

A stalled replication fork is not synonymous with a double-strand break. Stalling refers to an inability to continue replication efficiently at that time, and the underlying DNA structure may initially remain recoverable. If the obstacle is processed, synthesis may restart without losing substantial sequence. Persistent or improperly handled arrest can generate additional problems, including gaps, broken replication structures and incomplete chromosome duplication. This sequence explains why a treatment can produce rapid inhibition of DNA synthesis but only later produce a break-associated marker. The earlier lesion and the later break are mechanistically connected without being identical.

Replication stress also has causes other than chemical lesions. Limited nucleotide supply, difficult DNA sequences, torsional stress and conflicts between replication and other processes can impede fork progression. Inhibition of DNA synthesis alone is therefore insufficient evidence of DNA damage. For example, depletion of an essential nucleotide precursor can reduce incorporation of labeled nucleotide without producing a particular base adduct. Measuring the relevant precursor pools, examining lesion-specific endpoints and testing recovery can help discriminate these possibilities. This distinction becomes important in exam problems in which a drug decreases DNA synthesis but its direct target has not been established.

The eukaryotic DNA damage response provides a way to link physical substrate abnormalities to cell-cycle regulation. ATM and ATR are protein kinases commonly associated with responses to double-strand breaks and replication stress, respectively. At a DSB, the recognition and signaling machinery can activate ATM-dependent responses; at a stressed replication fork, accumulation of RPA-coated single-stranded DNA helps recruit and activate ATR signaling. These are useful principal associations rather than exclusive rules, because processing of DSBs can also create single-stranded DNA and the signaling systems interact. The kinases participate in signaling pathways that can restrain cell-cycle progression and help preserve genome integrity. They do not chemically reverse a thymine dimer merely by phosphorylating a target.

A checkpoint-mediated decrease in division can be protective. If a cell divides before chromosome duplication or repair has been completed, damaged structures may be segregated abnormally. Temporarily restraining progression can provide an opportunity to process lesions. A cell unable to activate a checkpoint might therefore appear to continue division more readily immediately after damage yet display greater chromosome instability later. Short-term proliferation and long-term reproductive viability are not equivalent endpoints. Conversely, persistent arrest is not proof that all lesions remain; the regulatory response can have a different time course from chemical repair.

Transcription creates a parallel requirement to read DNA, even in a cell that is not dividing. RNA polymerase moves along a template strand and synthesizes complementary RNA. A blocking lesion in the transcribed strand can interfere with elongation and reduce production of full-length transcripts. A lesion in the opposite strand may have a different direct effect, although a large deformation or an interstrand crosslink can obstruct both. The position of the lesion relative to promoter recognition and the transcribed region also matters. A lesion downstream of the transcription termination site may have little direct effect on a particular transcript, while the same lesion within an essential coding region can be physiologically important.

Some damaged bases can alter RNA nucleotide selection without creating a permanent DNA mutation. If an RNA polymerase reads an oxidized guanine incorrectly, an altered RNA molecule may be generated. Such transient errors can change a protein produced from that transcript, a phenomenon called transcriptional mutagenesis. This differs from a fixed DNA mutation because the altered RNA is not necessarily copied into the genome and usually has a limited lifetime. Multiple transcripts may nevertheless be affected while the DNA lesion persists. In a long-lived non-dividing cell, the physiological significance of lesions can therefore extend well beyond inherited mutations.

A cell with persistent DNA damage may experience altered gene expression, delayed proliferation, permanent arrest or cell death. In multicellular systems, apoptosis can eliminate a severely compromised cell, reducing the chance that an abnormal cell lineage will expand. Survival with a fixed mutation is a different outcome from death, and not every surviving mutation is harmful. Its effect depends on location, gene function, dominance and the biological environment. Cancer development is a useful example of the importance of genome stability, but a single lesion does not automatically cause cancer. Damage must be considered in the context of mutation fixation, cellular selection and multiple other biological requirements.

Comparisons across organisms highlight the distinction between resistance to DNA damage and resistance to cell death. A microorganism that survives substantial irradiation may do so through effective protection, restoration of genetic information, efficient protein maintenance or a combination of traits. Its survival does not necessarily imply that no lesions were formed. Likewise, plants exposed to sunlight can deploy protective pigments and repair systems without making their DNA intrinsically immune to UV photochemistry. An observed resistance phenotype is a system-level outcome. Identifying its cause requires measurements at appropriate molecular and cellular levels.

## 15. Quantifying lesion formation, disappearance and mutational probability

A useful first approximation treats lesion production as a collection of rare events. Suppose a DNA population contains N susceptible sites and each site has probability p of suffering a specified lesion during a defined interval. If the sites have equal probabilities, the expected number of affected sites is Np. For example, a hypothetical genome contains 2 × 10⁶ susceptible bases and the probability of modification of each base in an interval is 3 × 10⁻⁶. The expected number is 6 lesions per genome. An expectation of six does not mean that every genome has exactly six lesions. It is the average predicted across many equivalent genomes. Independence is not required to calculate the expectation by summing probabilities, but it is required for the simple independent-site probability distribution used below.

Counting units is essential. A double-stranded genome containing 3 × 10⁹ base pairs contains approximately 6 × 10⁹ individual nucleotide residues. If the probability is quoted per nucleotide rather than per base pair, the appropriate target count changes. For a lesion restricted to guanine, only the number of guanine residues should enter the formula. Suppose a hypothetical haploid duplex has 4 × 10⁶ base pairs and its GC content is 50%. The duplex contains 8 × 10⁶ nucleotide residues, of which 2 × 10⁶ are guanines and 2 × 10⁶ cytosines, because the number of G residues equals the number of C residues in a complete ordinary duplex. If the probability of a lesion per guanine is 2 × 10⁻⁶, the expected count is four, not sixteen. The latter would incorrectly treat every nucleotide as an equally susceptible guanine.

If events are rare and approximately independent, the number of lesions can often be approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean λ. The probability of observing zero events is e^(−λ), and the probability of at least one is 1 − e^(−λ). For a hypothetical DNA fragment with mean 0.5 blocking lesions, the probability of an undamaged fragment is e^(−0.5) ≈ 0.607, while the probability of at least one blocking lesion is about 0.393. For a mean of two lesions, the corresponding probabilities are about 0.135 and 0.865. Longer fragments tend to have a greater probability of encountering a lesion if the per-length rate is constant. However, clustered radiation damage, sequence-specific photochemistry and heterogeneous chromatin accessibility violate the simple uniform independent-event assumptions. The calculation is an illustrative model, not an exact description of every genome.

A second model considers changes in lesion burden with time. Let L represent the mean number of a particular lesion per genome and F represent a constant formation rate in lesions per genome per hour. If disappearance is approximately first order with rate constant k in h⁻¹, the change is dL/dt = F − kL. The first term supplies new lesions; the second removes a fraction of the existing burden per unit time. At steady state the mean burden is constant, so 0 = F − kL and L = F/k. If F = 80 lesions per genome per hour and k = 0.4 h⁻¹, the steady-state burden is 200 lesions per genome. Doubling the formation rate at fixed k doubles the burden. Halving k at fixed F also doubles the burden. A single measurement of 400 lesions cannot distinguish these two explanations.

When an external pulse of damage ends and new formation is negligible relative to the excess being studied, the model becomes dL/dt = −kL. Separating variables gives dL/L = −k dt. Integrating between the initial time and time t gives ln(L/L₀) = −kt, hence L = L₀e^(−kt). Here L₀ is the initial excess burden and t is in hours. The half-life follows by setting L/L₀ equal to 1/2, giving t½ = ln(2)/k. If a lesion burden falls from 120 to 30 per genome in four hours, the remaining fraction is 0.25. Under the model, k = −ln(0.25)/4 ≈ 0.347 h⁻¹ and the half-life is about two hours. The model assumes a constant fractional disappearance rate and no substantial ongoing excess production, dilution or selective cell loss. If these assumptions fail, the fitted constant cannot be identified uniquely as a repair rate.

The presence of a nonzero background changes the interpretation of recovery curves. Suppose an untreated population has a stable mean of 20 lesions and a treated population has 180 immediately after exposure. The initial excess is 160. If two hours later the treated population has 60, its excess is 40, so the excess has fallen to one quarter of the initial value. Assuming the background remains stable and excess removal follows first-order kinetics, the half-life is one hour. Calculating from the total values 60 and 180 without subtracting background would give a different answer. Background subtraction is justified only when the untreated comparison accurately represents the background under the relevant conditions.

The probability of mutation fixation can also be represented using a deliberately simplified conditional model. Suppose a cell experiences an average of 100 potentially miscoding lesions. If 90% are accurately repaired before copying, an average of ten persist to the next relevant stage. If 20% of persistent lesions direct incorrect nucleotide incorporation, an average of two miscoding events occur. If half of those lead to fixed substitutions in the surviving lineages being modeled, the expectation is one fixed substitution. These values are hypothetical, and the stages are conditional rather than independent alternatives: the probability of miscopying applies to lesions that persist, not to the whole original lesion population. Real outcomes depend on cell lineage, lesion position, repair after misincorporation, survival and selection. A low mutation count cannot by itself demonstrate a low lesion formation rate.

Radiation-related calculations require a different physical unit. Using h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, a photon of wavelength 260 nm has energy (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸)/(260 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 7.64 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or about 4.77 eV using 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A 365 nm photon has energy about 3.40 eV. The ratio is 365/260 ≈ 1.40, not the square of that ratio. The number of photochemical lesions cannot be calculated from photon energy alone, because the fraction absorbed and the probability of lesion formation after absorption are not specified. This is why simple physical calculations can clarify trends while leaving biological outcomes uncertain.

For ionizing radiation, a hypothetical sample of mass 0.002 kg absorbing 0.004 J receives an absorbed dose of 0.004/0.002 = 2 Gy. The calculation states energy per mass; it does not specify how many lesions were generated. If an exam independently provides a measured yield of lesions per genome per gray and assumes linearity over the given range, multiplication by dose can predict the expected lesion count. Without the measured yield or an appropriate model, conversion from gray directly to DSB number is unjustified. The physical and biological quantities have different dimensions and must not be substituted for one another.

## 16. How experimental measurements distinguish lesion chemistry and consequences

Direct chemical analysis can identify modified nucleosides using separation and detection methods. A lesion-specific signal is most informative when the identity of the detected compound is validated against a suitable standard. For example, detection of 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine can provide evidence of oxidized guanine residues, but the interpretation must account for possible oxidation during isolation and processing. An internal standard and matched preparation can improve comparability. Such a measurement does not directly tell whether a replication fork encountered the lesion or whether a mutation resulted. Those questions require separate measurements of replication or sequence outcomes.

Antibodies that recognize particular UV photoproducts can provide a different type of information. A CPD-specific signal measured shortly after irradiation can be compared with the signal after a recovery interval. Loss of signal is consistent with removal of the recognized photoproduct, provided that detection efficiency, DNA recovery and population composition remain comparable. The signal is not necessarily a linear count of all DNA damage types. A CPD antibody will not automatically measure 6–4 photoproducts, oxidative bases or strand breaks. Use of more than one validated lesion-specific reagent can distinguish different kinetic profiles after the same treatment.

Gel-based methods examine DNA movement through a matrix and can reveal fragment size or topology. With circular DNA, conversion from supercoiled to open circular or linear forms can report physical strand discontinuities under appropriate conditions. With large chromosomal DNA, methods adapted for separating large fragments can help assess loss of duplex continuity. In a denaturing gel, single-strand fragments can reveal interruptions that do not divide the intact duplex in a native gel. Each result must be interpreted relative to the molecular form expected under the experimental conditions. Migration speed alone does not uniquely identify a lesion.

The comet assay provides a cell-by-cell view of DNA migration after lysis and electrophoresis. DNA from individual nuclei forms a head region and, when sufficient DNA can migrate, a tail region. The standard alkaline assay is sensitive to strand discontinuities and alkali-labile sites; it does not identify each original lesion separately. A lesion-recognizing enzyme can convert some otherwise poorly detected base modifications into detectable breaks. If the enzyme-treated sample displays greater migration than a matched sample without the enzyme, the difference can support the presence of enzyme-sensitive lesions. It is not valid to equate that difference with a specific absolute lesion count without appropriate calibration and knowledge of enzyme specificity.

Neutral comet measurements should not be described as universally and exclusively specific to DSBs. The conditions and physical basis of migration matter. Similarly, DNA crosslinks may reduce migration by restricting the movement of strands, even when damage is greater. This makes interpretation of a reduced comet tail particularly sensitive to the experimental context. A meaningful comparison of crosslinking includes a break-inducing reference condition and a group with both effects so that inhibition of migration can be distinguished from an absence of strand breaks. Severe cell death can also produce extensively fragmented DNA, complicating the interpretation of a large migration signal as direct primary damage.

The phosphorylation of histone H2AX near DNA damage-associated sites provides another useful marker in eukaryotic cells. Gamma-H2AX foci can indicate activation of a response associated with DSBs and other relevant stresses. However, a focus is a region of signaling, not a direct visual image of one broken phosphodiester bond. Foci may overlap, persist after some molecular changes have been resolved, or arise in contexts including replication stress. It is therefore inappropriate to convert every focus count into an exact DSB count without validating the relationship in the system. Combining a signaling measurement with a physical or chemical damage assay gives a stronger basis for conclusions.

Measurements of DNA synthesis can show that genome duplication has slowed, while transcription measurements can show reduced production of RNA. Sequence analysis after growth can reveal substitutions and rearrangements that have become established. Colony formation or long-term proliferation can assess the ability of cells to produce descendants. These endpoints answer complementary questions. A chemical lesion might be abundant without an increase in surviving mutations if most damaged cells die; a mutation may persist after the chemical lesion has vanished; a checkpoint may reduce proliferation without direct inhibition of polymerase by a damaging compound. A well-designed experiment therefore matches each measurement to a specific hypothesis and avoids using a downstream proxy as though it measured every upstream event.

For an integrated hypothetical experiment, compare two genetically matched cell populations differing in one protective function. Measure a specific lesion at the earliest comparable time and again after recovery, monitor DNA synthesis, and examine survival and mutations among surviving descendants. Equal initial lesion measurements followed by different recovery kinetics suggest a difference in processing or persistence. A slower return of synthesis together with more persistent blocking lesions supports a mechanistic link but does not exclude a checkpoint contribution. A higher mutation frequency among survivors may reflect lesion bypass or selection rather than a greater initial formation rate. Independent biological replicates, matched untreated controls and appropriate normalization allow these alternatives to be investigated rather than assumed.

The next stage of studying genome stability is to ask how each substrate is recognized and restored. The chemical taxonomy developed here supplies the reason that different repair mechanisms exist: a misplaced chemical group can sometimes be reversed directly, a damaged base can be removed and replaced, a bulky lesion may require removal of a short region, and a broken duplex or covalent link between strands presents a different structural problem. These are not arbitrary categories to memorize. They follow from the physical structure of DNA and from the need to preserve its information while maintaining a usable molecular template.
