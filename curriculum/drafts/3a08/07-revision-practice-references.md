## 17. Revision: connect the structure of a lesion to its consequences

| Lesion | Immediate molecular change | Potential consequence | Typical repair association (introduced in the next nodes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| AP site | Base absent; sugar initially present | Missing template instruction, possible cleavage | Base excision repair |
| C deamination | U:G mismatch | C:G → T:A transition | Base excision repair |
| 5-methyl-C deamination | T:G mismatch | C:G → T:A transition | Appropriate mismatch-recognizing glycosylases |
| A deamination | Hypoxanthine | A:T → G:C transition | Base excision repair |
| 8-oxoG in DNA | Altered guanine pairing | G:C → T:A transversion | Base excision repair |
| O6-methylG | Altered pairing face | G:C → A:T transition | Direct reversal where available |
| N7-methylG | Destabilized glycosidic bond | AP site formation | Base excision pathways |
| N3-methylA | Modified adenine | Polymerase arrest | Base excision pathways |
| CPD | Adjacent pyrimidines covalently joined | Arrest, possible UV-associated mutations | Photolyase where present; nucleotide excision repair |
| 6–4PP | C6–C4 linkage between neighbors | Helical distortion and arrest | Specific photolyase where present; nucleotide excision repair |
| SSB | One backbone interrupted | Nick/gap; secondary replication-associated break | Single-strand break repair |
| DSB | Both backbones interrupted locally | Fragmentation, rearrangement risk | End joining or template-guided repair |
| Interstrand crosslink | Covalent bridge between strands | Strand separation impeded | Coordinated crosslink processing |
| DNA–protein crosslink | Protein covalently linked to DNA | Steric obstruction, possible break | Context-dependent processing |

### High-yield distinctions and examination traps

1. An AP site is not automatically a backbone break or genetic deletion. The sugar can initially remain attached.
2. Depurination removes a base; deamination chemically changes a base. These are not synonyms.
3. A lesion and a fixed mutation are different endpoints. The latter can persist after the lesion disappears.
4. Cytosine deamination initially yields U:G; 5-methylcytosine deamination yields T:G.
5. A normal T:A pair is not a chemically damaged pair merely because it is a substitution relative to a reference.
6. 8-oxoG in DNA can cause G:C → T:A; oxidized nucleotide precursors can produce different errors.
7. O6-methylG can direct T insertion, whereas N7-methylG tends to destabilize the glycosidic bond.
8. A classical CPD joins adjacent pyrimidines on one strand, not complementary bases across the helix.
9. UV photoproducts are not limited to T–T dimers; UVA can also produce CPDs.
10. Ionizing radiation can generate several lesion types; DSBs are not its only product.
11. Equal absorbed dose does not imply equal lesion complexity.
12. A stalled fork need not have collapsed; bypass does not necessarily remove the lesion.
13. An increased AP-site or break signal can reflect a repair intermediate.
14. A low comet tail signal is not universal evidence of low damage; crosslinks can impede migration.
15. DNA damage signaling is not itself chemical repair; a γ-H2AX focus is not necessarily one physical DSB.

### Formula and active-recall sheet

- Expected lesions: E[N] = sum of site-specific probabilities; for identical sites, Np.
- Independent rare events: P(0) ≈ e^(−λ); P(≥1) ≈ 1 − e^(−λ).
- Formation and disappearance: dL/dt = F − kL; steady-state L = F/k.
- First-order excess disappearance: L = L₀e^(−kt); t½ = ln(2)/k.
- Photon energy: E = hc/λ; λ in metres.
- Absorbed dose: D = energy absorbed/mass; 1 Gy = 1 J kg⁻¹.

Recall without looking: Which bonds change during depurination? Which normal partner remains after C deamination? Why can O6 and N7 methylation have different consequences? How do native and denaturing gels distinguish an SSB? Why might equal initial damage yield different mutation frequencies? Explain how a persistent crosslink can impair transcription without fragmenting both strands. Finally, predict whether a damage marker, mutation assay or long-term survival assay is most directly relevant to each question.

### Mastery checklist

You should be able to classify lesions from a diagram; derive the potential substitution from a specified base-pair intermediate; distinguish hydrolysis, oxidation, alkylation and photochemistry; contrast UV with ionizing radiation; explain why a nick can be particularly hazardous during replication; interpret lesion-specific and indirect assays with controls; solve quantitative questions with explicit assumptions; and predict which lesion property creates the need for a particular broad repair strategy.

## 18. Part B — Conceptual questions (original)

**B1.** Which event produces an AP site directly? (A) Breaking a hydrogen bond between paired bases (B) Hydrolyzing a base–sugar glycosidic bond (C) Joining two DNA ends (D) Replacing one complete base pair with another.  
Answer: B. The base departs, but the sugar and backbone can initially remain. A disrupts pairing without removing the base, C restores continuity, and D describes a substitution.

**B2.** The first product of cytosine deamination in a G:C pair is: (A) G:U (B) A:T (C) G:A (D) a DSB.  
Answer: A. The complementary G is unchanged; T:A may appear only after miscopying and fixation. No backbone cleavage is required.

**B3.** Deamination of 5-methylcytosine yields: (A) uracil (B) thymine (C) hypoxanthine (D) xanthine.  
Answer: B. The methyl group remains at C5. Unmethylated C gives U, A gives hypoxanthine, and G can yield xanthine.

**B4.** Hypoxanthine in DNA can arise from: (A) adenine deamination (B) guanine oxidation (C) thymine dimerization (D) cytosine methylation.  
Answer: A. The product can favor pairing with C; oxidation of G may instead yield 8-oxoG.

**B5.** Which is a nonradical ROS? (A) Hydroxyl radical (B) Superoxide radical (C) Hydrogen peroxide (D) A hydrated electron.  
Answer: C. H₂O₂ has no unpaired electron, although it can generate radicals in metal-mediated reactions. A and B are radicals; D is not an example of a nonradical oxygen species.

**B6.** A characteristic substitution arising from 8-oxoG in a G:C pair is: (A) G:C → T:A (B) G:C → A:T (C) A:T → G:C exclusively (D) deletion of every oxidized G.  
Answer: A. A can be inserted opposite oxidized G and subsequently direct T insertion. B is a characteristic possibility for O6-methylG; neither C nor D is a compulsory consequence.

**B7.** Which alkylated base can favor inappropriate thymine incorporation? (A) O6-methylguanine (B) N7-methylguanine exclusively (C) 5-methylcytosine in normal pairing (D) thymine glycol.  
Answer: A. Its altered coding surface is important; N7 methylation instead destabilizes base attachment. The other choices do not describe this lesion.

**B8.** Which property best distinguishes an SSB from an AP site? (A) A missing phosphodiester continuity (B) presence of water (C) number of purines (D) presence of chromatin.  
Answer: A. A strand discontinuity is necessary for an SSB but not for an AP site.

**B9.** The usual UV CPD links: (A) two adjacent pyrimidines of one strand (B) A and T across the duplex by a hydrogen bond (C) two distant phosphate groups (D) two proteins.  
Answer: A. It is a covalent same-strand lesion; ordinary base pairing and the other listed linkages are not CPDs.

**B10.** A 6–4 photoproduct involves: (A) a C6–C4 linkage between successive pyrimidines (B) removal of every C6 atom (C) two phosphodiester cuts (D) normal purine pairing.  
Answer: A. Its geometry differs from the four-membered ring of a CPD.

**B11.** Which unit is used for absorbed ionizing radiation dose? (A) Gy, J kg⁻¹ (B) mol L⁻¹ (C) base pairs s⁻¹ (D) mutations per genome.  
Answer: A. Absorbed dose is energy per mass; the other units describe different quantities.

**B12.** Which lesion most directly prevents strand separation through a covalent connection between complementary chains? (A) interstrand crosslink (B) isolated U:G mismatch (C) N7-methylG (D) a simple nick.  
Answer: A. The other lesions do not inherently join the two chains covalently.

**B13.** A nick in a covalently closed circular DNA molecule can produce: (A) an open circular relaxed form (B) obligatory loss of all bases (C) an RNA molecule (D) an interstrand crosslink.  
Answer: A. Strand discontinuity releases the topological constraint. It need not change molecule length or produce the other listed outcomes.

**B14.** A DNA ligase generally needs which termini to join a simple nick? (A) juxtaposed 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate (B) two unmodified bases with no backbone (C) two 3′-phosphates only (D) two 5′-OH ends only.  
Answer: A. Other end structures may require processing before ligation.

**B15.** A blocking lesion in the transcribed strand of a gene may directly cause: (A) impaired RNA polymerase elongation (B) obligatory chromosome doubling (C) immediate Mendelian segregation (D) loss of every transcript.  
Answer: A. The effect depends on locus, lesion and enzyme; B–D are unjustified generalizations.

**B16.** Which statement about lesion bypass is correct? (A) It always removes the lesion (B) It can permit synthesis despite a persistent lesion (C) It necessarily causes a mutation (D) It is identical to photoreactivation.  
Answer: B. Tolerance is distinct from removal; accuracy depends on the polymerase and substrate.

**B17.** MutT in E. coli primarily illustrates: (A) sanitation of oxidized nucleotide precursors (B) severing both DNA strands (C) histone phosphorylation (D) pyrimidine dimer formation.  
Answer: A. It acts on oxidized nucleotide substrates before incorporation rather than directly repairing a chromosomal CPD.

**B18.** Which is a valid example of an endogenous damage source? (A) reactive products of ordinary cellular metabolism (B) only laboratory UVC lamps (C) only external gamma radiation (D) only inherited sequence variation.  
Answer: A. Endogenous chemistry occurs within the organism; B and C are external examples, D is not a chemical source.

**B19.** Which is most directly consistent with the appearance of a fixed mutation? (A) a changed canonical base pair inherited after replication (B) an increased H₂O₂ concentration alone (C) one AP site in an isolated molecule (D) a transient mismatch that is accurately repaired.  
Answer: A. The other choices do not establish a stable sequence change.

**B20.** A standard alkaline comet assay primarily responds to: (A) strand breaks and alkali-labile sites (B) only point mutations (C) exclusively CPDs without modification (D) only protein concentration.  
Answer: A. The assay is not a direct sequencing method or a universal lesion-specific measurement.

### Assertion–reason (B21–B25)

Use A: both statements true, reason explains assertion; B: both true, reason does not explain assertion; C: assertion true, reason false; D: assertion false, reason true.

**B21.** Assertion: A base can be lost without immediate strand cleavage. Reason: The base–sugar bond is distinct from the phosphodiester backbone.  
Answer: A. The chemical independence of these linkages explains why AP sites need not be breaks.

**B22.** Assertion: 5-methylcytosine deamination is challenging to recognize. Reason: The product thymine is normally present in DNA.  
Answer: A. The normal chemical identity of thymine complicates discrimination of T:G compared with U:G.

**B23.** Assertion: A photolyase can reverse a particular UV photoproduct in organisms possessing it. Reason: Human placental mammalian cells possess conventional DNA photolyases.  
Answer: C. The assertion is true in the specified organisms, but placental mammals lack the conventional photolyase pathways.

**B24.** Assertion: Every blocked replication fork is already a DSB. Reason: A fork can stall while duplex continuity is initially recoverable.  
Answer: D. Stalling precedes collapse in some situations but does not require it.

**B25.** Assertion: Ionizing radiation can create clustered lesions. Reason: A transition changes a purine to a purine or a pyrimidine to a pyrimidine.  
Answer: B. Both are true but the definition of a transition does not explain clustered energy deposition.

## 19. Part C — Analytical and quantitative questions (original)

All numerical datasets below are hypothetical teaching examples, not reported experimental values.

**C1.** A C:G pair becomes U:G. After copying of the U-containing strand with A insertion and copying of the A-containing daughter, which pair is fixed? Identify the mutation class.  
Answer: T:A; transition at the original C position. The undamaged G-containing parental strand can still yield C:G. It is incorrect to treat the first U:G intermediate as an already fixed mutation.

**C2.** Two guanine adducts direct insertion of A and T, respectively. Starting from G:C, predict the canonical substitutions after copying of the newly inserted nucleotide.  
Answer: A insertion directs T on subsequent copying, yielding T:A, a transversion. T insertion directs A, yielding A:T, a transition. This is why 8-oxoG and O6-methylG can have different mutation signatures despite both modifying G.

**C3.** A purified duplex retains full native length but yields a shorter labeled strand after alkali treatment. A specific assay detects AP sites before alkali. Which interpretation is best?  
Answer: Alkali can convert AP sites to breaks; the short band need not reflect a pre-existing SSB. Native length alone cannot exclude other lesions; use matched untreated and cleavage-specific controls.

**C4.** A plasmid sample initially contains compact supercoiled DNA. After treatment, an open circular band increases, but the linear band does not. What change is most consistent?  
Answer: Nicking of one strand relaxes covalently constrained circular DNA. This is not definitive proof of exclusively SSBs, but the unchanged linear band argues against extensive conversion to linear DSB products.

**C5.** Two populations contain 80 lesions/genome immediately after equal treatment. After two hours X has 20 and Y 60. If background is negligible, what is the most direct interpretation?  
Answer: X has faster net disappearance (75% versus 25%). Initial formation appears similar at the sampled time; repair, dilution and selection need to be distinguished before attributing the difference solely to repair.

**C6.** A 5 × 10⁶ bp complete duplex has 40% GC. If probability of oxidation per G is 10⁻⁶, calculate expected G lesions.  
Answer: 10⁷ nucleotide residues; 40% are G+C, half are G = 2 × 10⁶. Expected count = 2. Counting all GC residues would double the estimate.

**C7.** Independent blocking lesions occur with mean 0.8 per template. Estimate the proportion with no lesion and at least one.  
Answer: e^(−0.8) ≈ 0.4493; at least one ≈ 0.5507. The Poisson assumption fails for strongly clustered or heterogeneous events.

**C8.** A pulse increases lesion burden from a baseline of 10 to 170. After 3 h the total is 30. Estimate excess half-life under first-order kinetics.  
Answer: Initial excess 160, final excess 20, ratio 1/8 = three half-lives. Half-life 1 h. Using total values would incorrectly ignore background.

**C9.** Formation rate is 60 lesions/genome/hour and disappearance constant is 0.3 h⁻¹. A mutation halves the disappearance constant without changing formation. Determine the new steady-state burden.  
Answer: Initial 60/0.3 = 200; new 60/0.15 = 400 lesions/genome. Increased steady-state burden does not demonstrate increased formation.

**C10.** A lesion falls from 200 to 50 in 4 h. Estimate k and predict the amount after a total of 6 h.  
Answer: k = ln(4)/4 ≈ 0.347 h⁻¹; half-life 2 h, so after 6 h the amount is 25. Assumes no continuing formation and no dilution.

**C11.** A sample absorbs 0.012 J of ionizing radiation and has mass 0.004 kg. Compute dose. Can DSBs be counted from it alone?  
Answer: 3 Gy. No: a measured or assumed lesion yield is required, and energy deposition affects more than DNA.

**C12.** Compare photon energies at 300 nm and 400 nm.  
Answer: E₃₀₀/E₄₀₀ = 400/300 = 4/3. The 300 nm photon has about 33% more energy. This does not establish 33% more lesions because absorption and quantum yield differ.

**C13.** A population has 500 premutagenic lesions on average. 96% are removed before copying; 25% of the remaining lesions cause an error; 40% of errors become fixed. Calculate expected fixed substitutions.  
Answer: 500 × 0.04 × 0.25 × 0.40 = 2. Each fraction applies to the previous stage; adding fractions is invalid.

**C14.** Two strains show equal initial CPD burden. A photolyase-containing strain loses CPD signal in visible light, but a matched photolyase-deficient strain does not. Which additional group tests whether light alone accounts for the result?  
Answer: The enzyme-deficient irradiated light group is already an important control; add the competent irradiated dark group and unirradiated baseline. A light-dependent difference restricted to enzyme competence supports photoreactivation; it does not prove every UV lesion is a CPD.

**C15.** After equal exposure, one sample exhibits a higher comet tail than another. Addition of a crosslinking condition lowers the tail signal despite equal break-inducing treatment. Explain.  
Answer: Crosslinks can constrain DNA migration. Lower migration need not mean fewer initiating breaks. Compare the break-only group, crosslink-only group and combined group, with viability and matched preparation controls.

**C16.** A mutant accumulates uracil after damage, while the wild type transiently shows increased AP sites. What molecular step may differ?  
Answer: Removal of uracil by a glycosylase could be impaired in the mutant; the wild-type AP sites could be processing intermediates. The data do not prove repair completion; follow AP-site disappearance and mutation outcomes.

**C17.** A polymerase incorporates normally on unmodified DNA but stops at a bulky lesion. A second polymerase extends across the lesion without reducing its chemically measured abundance. Which distinction is demonstrated?  
Answer: Bypass/tolerance versus lesion removal. Extension across a damaged template does not chemically reverse the adduct. Sequencing of products is needed to assess accuracy.

**C18.** Four hypothetical lesions are either all far apart on one strand or grouped as two closely opposed pairs. Which arrangement is more likely to produce DSB intermediates during processing and why?  
Answer: The opposed pairs, because adjacent processing on both strands can compromise duplex continuity. Counts alone do not capture spatial complexity. Outcome still depends on separation, processing timing and chemistry.

**C19.** Match the primary molecular obstacle: I AP site; II interstrand crosslink; III SSB; IV O6-methylG. Options: a misleading pairing surface; b missing base; c covalent obstruction to strand separation; d interruption of one backbone.  
Answer: I–b, II–c, III–d, IV–a. This is classification by immediate structure, not by final mutation outcome.

**C20.** Select all correct statements: I a G:T mismatch can arise by deamination; II U:A necessarily changes sequence relative to T:A; III 8-oxoG can pair with C; IV N7-methylG can lead to an AP site.  
Answer: I, III, IV. II is false because U:A may arise from precursor incorporation opposite A and transmit the same pairing information as T:A.

**C21.** RNA output decreases from a gene containing a single lesion on its template strand; the DNA sequence of descendants is unchanged. Does this rule out an effect of DNA damage?  
Answer: No. A lesion can block RNA polymerase or alter transcription without ever being fixed as a DNA mutation. Compare lesion placement on transcribed and nontranscribed strands, and measure full-length transcripts.

**C22.** A checkpoint-defective cell continues division sooner after damage than wild type, but its long-term chromosome abnormalities increase. What inference is appropriate?  
Answer: The checkpoint can delay progression to protect genome integrity. Faster initial growth is not evidence of better repair. Compare initial damage, cell-cycle distributions and long-term viability.

**C23.** A chemical increases γ-H2AX foci but the number of foci does not match an independent physical break measurement. Why is that not necessarily contradictory?  
Answer: Foci reflect a signaling response over chromatin regions; they can overlap or occur during replication stress and have different temporal behavior from breaks. A one-to-one conversion requires validation.

**C24.** A mutT-deficient bacterium shows an increase in substitutions even when a test for one oxidized base in the starting parental DNA detects no difference. Give a plausible explanation.  
Answer: Oxidized dGTP in the soluble pool can be incorporated during synthesis. The precursor lesion would not necessarily be detected in the original template. Measure precursor oxidation or test the effect of restoring MutT.

**C25.** A treatment reduces surviving colonies by 90% but increases mutation frequency among survivors only slightly. Can you conclude that it produces few DNA lesions?  
Answer: No. Blocking or lethal lesions may prevent severely damaged cells from forming colonies; the surviving population is selected. Distinguish lesion burden, survival and mutation frequency among surviving lineages.

**C26.** A chemical adduct assay shows more N7-methylG than O6-methylG, yet G:C → A:T substitutions dominate. Explain why abundance alone is insufficient.  
Answer: O6-methylG can directly miscode with T and yield the observed transition; N7-methylG can instead destabilize the glycosidic bond. Relative persistence, bypass probability and selection also affect mutation spectra.

**C27.** After equal UV exposure, a 6–4PP-specific assay declines more rapidly than a CPD-specific assay. Which of the following follows: (A) CPDs cannot be removed; (B) initial CPD yield was necessarily larger; (C) the measured net disappearance rates differ; (D) the two assays measure every lesion.  
Answer: C. A and B are unjustified by rate differences, and D is false. Different lesion-specific recognition and processing can account for the observation.

**C28.** A DNA sample has 100 independent lesions spread over 50 equally accessible fragments. Under a Poisson model, estimate how many fragments are lesion free. Then explain why a clustered distribution could change the answer.  
Answer: Mean 2 per fragment; 50e^(−2) ≈ 6.77, or about 7 expected intact fragments. Clustering leaves more fragments without events while concentrating damage in others; independence and equal rates are assumptions.

**C29.** A polymerase stalls after a nick is encountered by the fork; a break marker increases only after cells enter S phase. What alternative explains delayed breaks without new exposure?  
Answer: Replication-associated conversion of a pre-existing single-strand discontinuity into a one-ended break. Compare nonreplicating and replicating cells, and establish initial nick burdens. A delayed marker can also reflect signaling kinetics.

**C30.** Two DNA samples have equal sequence mutation frequency after recovery, but one has twice the initial chemical lesion burden. Propose two nonexclusive explanations and a test.  
Answer: The higher-burden sample may repair more lesions before copying or lose more heavily damaged cells; alternatively its lesions may be less miscoding. Use early and later lesion-specific measurements, long-term survival, and matched mutation scoring. Equal final mutation frequencies cannot establish equal damage susceptibility.
