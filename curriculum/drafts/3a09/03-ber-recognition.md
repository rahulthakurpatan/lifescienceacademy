## 6. Base excision repair: recognizing a single altered base

A chemically modified base is not always a substrate for direct reversal. In many instances, changing it back would require a reaction for which the cell has no practical enzyme. Instead, the complement provides a reliable solution. Base excision repair starts by removing the base that has become inappropriate and then rebuilds the position using the opposing strand. The crucial advance over simply digesting damaged DNA is precision: the initial reaction removes a nitrogenous base rather than destroying an entire nucleotide chain. A class of enzymes called DNA glycosylases has evolved to perform this task.

The bond targeted by a DNA glycosylase is the N-glycosidic bond linking the anomeric carbon, C1′, of deoxyribose to a nitrogen atom of the base. In a purine nucleotide this is normally N9 of the purine; in a pyrimidine nucleotide it is N1. The backbone instead contains phosphodiester bonds joining the sugar of one residue to the next. Hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond and hydrolysis of a phosphodiester bond produce different intermediates and different chemical products. In a simple monofunctional glycosylase reaction, water participates in breaking the base–sugar linkage, releasing the base and leaving an abasic sugar. The phosphate connections on either side of the sugar initially remain intact. The resulting AP site is named for the absence of a purine or pyrimidine, not for an absence of phosphate.

The active site of a glycosylase must distinguish its substrate from an enormous excess of normal bases. This is possible because the enzyme does not interact only with the exposed edge of a base in a groove. Many glycosylases interrogate DNA by bending the duplex and promoting extrusion of a nucleotide from the stacked helix into an active-site pocket. Within that pocket, hydrogen bonds, steric constraints and electrostatic interactions reveal the chemical identity of the candidate base. Favorable contacts can stabilize the correct substrate and position the glycosidic bond for cleavage. Incorrect candidates may fail to bind tightly or fail to reach the reaction geometry. A residue of the enzyme may occupy the space created when the base is flipped out, stabilizing the local distorted DNA.

Base flipping is not a unique signature of repair. Other DNA enzymes, including methyltransferases, can also move a nucleotide out of the helix for chemical modification. Nor does every glycosylase inspect DNA by an identical sequence of physical movements. The relevant principle is that local DNA deformation and exposure of the candidate base improve the opportunity for chemical discrimination. The energy required to disrupt stacking can be balanced by favorable protein–DNA contacts. A lesion that is more readily extruded may be easier to interrogate, but the ease of extrusion alone is insufficient to predict catalytic specificity. A normal base that enters the pocket may still be rejected before cleavage.

Why does the cell employ multiple glycosylases instead of a single enzyme that removes any unusual base? The chemical diversity of lesions makes a universal active site difficult to reconcile with accuracy. An extra oxygen atom, loss of an amino group, ring opening and addition of a methyl group produce different patterns of shape and bonding. A recognition pocket designed to distinguish uracil from thymine may not be well suited to distinguish an oxidized purine from guanine. Furthermore, the pairing partner can matter as much as the damaged base itself. Several families of enzymes with partially overlapping substrate ranges provide broader coverage while preserving specificity for particular chemical and structural features. Redundancy also means that deletion of a single glycosylase may not eliminate all activity toward a given substrate.

Uracil-DNA glycosylase is a classical example. Uracil differs from thymine by the absence of a methyl group at the fifth carbon of the pyrimidine ring. It can arise in DNA through deamination of cytosine or misincorporation of dUMP during DNA synthesis. These origins create distinct pairing contexts. Deamination of a C:G pair produces U:G, whereas incorporation of dUMP opposite adenine produces U:A. Removal of uracil from a U:G pair followed by synthesis using guanine restores cytosine. Removal from U:A followed by synthesis using adenine restores thymine. The same initiating activity can therefore correct both a potentially mutagenic mismatch arising from chemical damage and the presence of an inappropriate but normally pairing base. The identity of the newly synthesized nucleotide is dictated by the remaining complement, not by the identity of the base that was removed.

Thymine itself is the usual partner of adenine in DNA. When 5-methylcytosine deaminates, however, the product is thymine paired with guanine. Because thymine is a normal DNA base, its recognition as inappropriate requires context. Thymine-DNA glycosylase and MBD4 provide examples of activities that can recognize certain G:T mismatches, particularly in relevant sequence contexts. Their role helps explain why methylated CpG sites can be mutation hotspots despite the availability of repair. The lesion is harder to distinguish from normal DNA than uracil because its deamination product is an ordinary DNA base. Repair is not necessarily absent at methylated sites; rather, recognition must discriminate an inappropriate pairing context, and a missed event can generate a stable transition.

A second source of base lesions is oxidation. Guanine has a relatively low oxidation potential compared with the other common DNA bases and is particularly susceptible to certain oxidative reactions. 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine, often abbreviated 8-oxoG, can pair with cytosine in one conformation and with adenine in another. Its ability to pair with adenine creates a path toward G:C to T:A transversions. A glycosylase directed against the oxidized base must act in the correct pairing context to protect genetic information. Removing oxidized guanine when adenine has already been inserted opposite it could cause synthesis of thymine opposite adenine, fixing the mutation. The cell therefore benefits from distinct recognition activities that repair the oxidized base itself and remove incorrectly inserted adenine.

In the bacterial GO system, MutM, also called Fpg, removes 8-oxoG and related substrates, with efficient repair of oxidized guanine paired with cytosine. MutY removes adenine from inappropriate pairings such as A opposite 8-oxoG. MutT performs a different task: it hydrolyzes an oxidized nucleotide triphosphate in the nucleotide pool, reducing the probability that an oxidized precursor will be incorporated into DNA. MutT does not excise a base from the chromosome. Human OGG1, MUTYH and MTH1 provide related examples of the complementary functions, although enzyme families and exact substrate preferences should not be treated as identical between organisms. These systems illustrate three different opportunities for maintaining sequence fidelity: prevent insertion of a damaged precursor, correct the damaged base in DNA, and correct the inappropriate partner that would otherwise stabilize a mutation.

Follow the information through an 8-oxoG:A pair. Removal of adenine leaves an abasic site on the strand that acquired the incorrect partner. After processing and gap filling, cytosine can be restored opposite the oxidized guanine. Removal of the oxidized guanine from the resulting pair creates an abasic position on the original strand; synthesis using cytosine now installs guanine. This two-stage reasoning shows why sequential correction can recover the original pair even after an incorrect nucleotide has been inserted. The first replacement does not by itself return the entire duplex to normal because the oxidized base remains. Nor is the process guaranteed: the polymerase filling the first repair gap must install the appropriate partner despite the lesion's ambiguous coding properties, and other events may compete. The important conclusion is the complementary logic of the glycosylases rather than a claim that all molecules undergo a fixed ordered sequence.

A mutation in a glycosylase gene can influence a mutation spectrum without abolishing replication itself. For example, if a cell lacks MutY, adenine opposite 8-oxoG may persist and later template thymine. The expected enrichment of G:C to T:A mutations reflects the chemical behavior of the lesion and the missing correction reaction. Yet the presence of this mutation class in a population does not prove that every mutation originated from 8-oxoG. Other damage and polymerase errors can generate the same final sequence. Defined substrates and genetic complementation provide more specific evidence than the final mutation spectrum alone.

## 7. The AP site and the chemistry of intermediate processing

Base removal is only the first stage of repair. An abasic position has lost the chemical group that ordinarily identifies its complementary partner. Its sugar can exist in different forms, including a ring-opened aldehyde form, and the site can be susceptible to additional chemical reactions. It may be generated spontaneously by hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond even without glycosylase action. This is why the downstream BER machinery is needed not only after deliberate base excision but also after spontaneous depurination or depyrimidination. If a cell accumulates AP sites, the problem is not necessarily excessive activity of a particular glycosylase; spontaneous loss of bases or defective downstream processing can contribute.

A major route for processing AP sites involves an AP endonuclease. In human cells the principal enzyme in this role is APE1. It hydrolyzes the phosphodiester bond on the 5′ side of the abasic sugar, yielding an upstream DNA terminus with a free 3′ hydroxyl and a downstream end carrying a 5′ deoxyribose phosphate residue, often called 5′-dRP. This reaction transforms a continuous strand containing an abasic sugar into a nicked intermediate suitable for further repair. The polymerase cannot simply ignore the residual sugar if the final goal is a normal continuous strand. It must be removed or displaced. The upstream 3′ hydroxyl is the starting point for nucleotide addition, whereas the downstream 5′-dRP must be processed to yield a ligatable end.

The distinction between glycosylase and AP endonuclease can be demonstrated with defined substrates. Suppose a double-stranded oligonucleotide contains one uracil residue. Incubation with uracil-DNA glycosylase may create an AP site but leave the covalent chain intact. A denaturing gel that detects chain lengths will not necessarily show a shorter labeled strand merely because the base has been removed. Subsequent treatment with an AP endonuclease creates a break at a defined location, producing a shorter labeled fragment when the strand is separated under denaturing conditions. An assay that detects free uracil and an assay that detects backbone cleavage report two different steps. Some AP sites can also break under harsh chemical or thermal conditions, so sample preparation must be chosen or interpreted with care.

Glycosylases can be divided mechanistically into monofunctional and bifunctional classes. A monofunctional glycosylase performs base removal without itself cleaving the backbone. A bifunctional glycosylase also has AP lyase activity that can act on the abasic intermediate. The lyase does not perform the same hydrolysis as APE1. Instead, cleavage can occur through elimination chemistry involving the abasic sugar. In beta elimination, a break on the 3′ side of the abasic site leaves an upstream terminus bearing a modified sugar group that blocks ordinary polymerase extension. Some glycosylase-associated lyases can also carry out delta elimination, producing a 3′ phosphate end. The exact end chemistry depends on the enzyme and reaction. These routes explain why a BER diagram that starts with a bifunctional glycosylase must not automatically show a clean 3′ hydroxyl immediately after backbone incision.

End-processing enzymes convert these intermediates into a chemically appropriate substrate. APE1 can help remove certain 3′ blocking residues produced by beta elimination. A polynucleotide kinase phosphatase such as PNKP can remove 3′ phosphate in relevant mammalian BER reactions, making the hydroxyl available for DNA synthesis. In other contexts PNKP can phosphorylate a 5′ hydroxyl to produce the phosphate needed for ligation. These activities solve different chemical problems at the two sides of a nick or gap. A 3′ phosphate must not be mistaken for a 5′ phosphate: the former blocks the hydroxyl required by polymerase, while the latter is part of the normal substrate required by ligase.

The need to process ends explains a common phenotype of defective BER. A cell can show efficient loss of the original base lesion yet accumulate nicked or gapped DNA. Such a cell has initiated repair successfully but cannot complete it at an adequate rate. Indeed, initiation can convert a lesion that was temporarily tolerated into a more disruptive strand intermediate. An increase in AP sites after exposure may indicate active glycosylases, decreased AP endonuclease activity, spontaneous base loss or some combination. The correct interpretation requires measuring the different pools over time. A single endpoint measurement of damage is often insufficient to identify the defective step.

Consider a bacterial example involving a uracil lesion. A monofunctional glycosylase such as Ung releases uracil. AP endonucleases such as exonuclease III and endonuclease IV provide routes for processing the AP site. Despite its name, exonuclease III has AP endonuclease activity; the protein name alone is not a complete description of its biochemical functions. DNA polymerase I participates in repair synthesis and processing, and DNA ligase seals the remaining nick. Bacterial BER shares the general sequence of chemical operations with eukaryotic BER, but the identity and relative contribution of the proteins are different. A question asking for the main human AP endonuclease should not be answered with a bacterial protein merely because the reactions are analogous.

The order of the reactions can be investigated through a set of selective defects. If glycosylase activity is absent, a defined base lesion remains but little AP-site product from that lesion is generated. If AP endonuclease activity is defective after successful monofunctional glycosylase action, AP sites can accumulate and fewer incised substrates enter synthesis. If gap-filling polymerase activity is impaired, loss of the base and incision may still occur, but incompletely synthesized repair intermediates accumulate. If ligation is blocked, synthesis can be substantially complete while a backbone nick persists. These predictions are strongest for a defined substrate and a pathway in which no alternate enzyme bypasses the missing step. In living cells, redundant activities and altered damage responses can complicate the phenotype. The sequential logic remains useful because it specifies which intermediate would be expected to rise immediately after a selective bottleneck.
