## 13. Evidence for NER, strand-specific repair and clinical relevance

The excision product is an especially informative molecular signature of NER. A defined duplex with a single bulky lesion can be used to compare the products formed by a bacterial repair system and a mammalian cell extract. If the label lies within the damage-containing region and the reaction releases an oligonucleotide of characteristic size, separation by denaturing electrophoresis can distinguish the excised fragment from the much longer parental strand. A bacterial system typically produces an oligomer near 12–13 nucleotides; a mammalian system typically produces one near 24–32 nucleotides. The difference does not imply that mammalian DNA contains a larger version of the original lesion. The same chemical adduct can be surrounded by different incision machinery, which determines the size of the removed segment.

In an experiment using this approach, the lesion-free duplex is an important specificity control. If short labeled fragments appear equally from damaged and undamaged substrates, nonspecific nuclease degradation is a plausible alternative to damage-directed excision. The position of the label also matters: an end label far outside the excised tract may detect an incision fragment but not the released lesion-containing oligomer. Product identification requires knowledge of which strand is labeled and where the label lies in relation to the lesion. A mutation preventing damage recognition should reduce specific excision products, whereas a defect in ligation could allow characteristic excision products to appear even as final DNA closure remains defective.

Unscheduled DNA synthesis tests another aspect of repair. After UV exposure of cells whose bulk S-phase replication is excluded or accounted for, incorporation of labeled nucleotide into DNA can indicate reconstruction of short excision patches. In a global-genome NER defect, UV-induced repair synthesis may be reduced substantially because many genomic lesions fail to enter the pathway. A selective transcription-coupled repair defect can produce a relatively modest change in total incorporation while markedly reducing recovery of RNA synthesis. Most DNA in a genome is not simultaneously being transcribed on the same strand, so a global measurement can obscure a large local defect in actively transcribed regions. These two assays measure different outcomes and are complementary, not mutually contradictory.

A strand-specific experiment illustrates transcription coupling more directly. Suppose equal initial lesion signals of 100 units are measured on the template and non-template strands of a transcribed gene. After an identical repair interval, 25 units remain on the template strand and 65 on the non-template strand. The fraction removed is 75 percent from the template and 35 percent from the non-template. If transcription is suppressed before damage and recovery under otherwise matched conditions, preferential template repair might diminish. If a mutant specifically lacks a transcription-coupling factor, the differential may likewise be reduced. These hypothetical observations would support transcription-associated repair. Alternative explanations include strand differences in chromatin accessibility, lesion detection efficiency or initial damage distribution. Measuring both initial burdens and repair kinetics is necessary to exclude the most obvious alternatives.

Xeroderma pigmentosum illustrates the physiological consequences of failed protection against UV damage. Many forms of the condition result from inherited defects in NER components, compromising removal of photoproducts and increasing susceptibility to UV-induced mutations and skin cancer. The classical discovery that affected cells show reduced UV-induced repair synthesis helped establish the biological relevance of NER. An important exception is the XP variant form, associated with defects in DNA polymerase eta, a damage-tolerance polymerase rather than a core NER enzyme. Such cells can retain excision repair yet handle remaining UV lesions poorly during replication. Therefore the disease name alone is not proof that a particular patient cell lacks excision activity. The distinction anticipates the next chapter's treatment of repair versus tolerance.

Cockayne syndrome provides a contrasting example because defects in transcription-associated repair factors such as CSA and CSB can strongly impair recovery of transcription after UV exposure. The clinical consequences are complex and involve functions beyond the simple pathway diagram. At examination level the useful distinction is that global genome repair and transcription-coupled repair are separable at their initiation stages and share core machinery. It is not appropriate to infer an entire clinical phenotype from the sole activity of one pathway. The same protein may have roles in additional processes, and genetic variants can have different effects on protein function. Similarly, TFIIH illustrates functional overlap between transcription and NER, so defects in its components can have consequences extending beyond lesion excision.

## 14. Quantitative comparison of repair capacity and the timing of genome restoration

Quantitative models become useful when their assumptions match the biology. If the removal of a defined lesion population is approximately proportional to the number remaining and repair capacity is not saturated, a first-order model may describe the early recovery period. Let L(t) be the number of lesions at time t and let k be a positive apparent removal constant with units of inverse time. The rate equation is dL/dt = −kL. Separating variables gives dL/L = −k dt. Integrating from the initial time to t yields ln[L(t)/L(0)] = −kt, or L(t) = L(0)e^(−kt). The half-time is the time at which L(t)/L(0) = 1/2, giving t½ = ln(2)/k. These equations describe disappearance of the measured lesion, not necessarily completion of every downstream repair intermediate.

For example, if a defined photoproduct signal falls from 800 to 200 units in 40 minutes, one quarter remains. Under a first-order assumption, two half-times have elapsed, so t½ is 20 minutes and k = ln(4)/40 = 0.0347 min⁻¹. At the same constant rate, another 20 minutes would reduce the signal from 200 to 100 units. The model assumes no new damage, constant effective removal capacity, homogeneous substrate accessibility and an assay whose signal is proportional to lesion number. These assumptions can fail during active stress, depletion of MGMT-type proteins, saturation of a recognition enzyme or a transition from accessible to less accessible chromatin regions. In such cases the calculated k is an empirical summary rather than an invariant molecular rate constant.

When two independent first-order routes act on the same lesion, a simplified model predicts that their rate constants add. If a photolyase-dependent route removes CPDs with an apparent constant kP and NER removes the same CPDs with constant kN, then dL/dt = −(kP + kN)L. For hypothetical values kP = 0.03 min⁻¹ and kN = 0.01 min⁻¹, the combined half-time is ln(2)/0.04 = 17.3 minutes, while the NER-only half-time is about 69.3 minutes. This calculation does not imply that photolyase and NER repair the same lesion twice. It models competing opportunities for either pathway to remove it. The model fails if pathway interaction changes recognition, if one route alters the accessibility of the other, or if the light exposure produces additional damage. Its value lies in specifying a testable expectation for a simple case.

Repair synthesis can also be considered as a nucleotide budget. Suppose a sample completes 100 short-patch BER events, 20 long-patch BER events averaging six new residues, and 30 NER events averaging 28 new residues. If each replaced residue represents one incorporated dNTP, the total incorporation is 100 × 1 + 20 × 6 + 30 × 28 = 1,060 nucleotides. Only 150 repair events occurred, yet the average patch size is 1,060/150 = 7.07 nucleotides per event. The 30 NER events account for 840 of the 1,060 incorporated residues, about 79.2 percent, despite representing only 20 percent of the events. A radiolabel distributed equally among nucleotide types would therefore weight the contribution of NER heavily. In a real experiment, base composition and the nucleotide species labeled can alter this relationship.

An idealized comparison of two cells further clarifies the problem. Cell A corrects 280 lesions by single-nucleotide BER. Cell B corrects ten lesions through NER with a 28-nucleotide patch each. Both incorporate 280 nucleotides in repair synthesis. They do not correct equal numbers of lesions, and they need not have equal survival outcomes because the lesions may differ greatly in their biological effects. Direct reversal would contribute no repair-patch incorporation despite correcting chemically important damage. A count of incorporated nucleotides becomes a lesion-count estimate only after appropriate information about patch length, pathway composition and detection efficiency is supplied.

The kinetics of DNA repair must ultimately be connected to the arrival of replication and transcription machinery. A lesion corrected just before a replication fork arrives can preserve the original sequence. The same repair reaction after a mispair has already been established may not fully recover that information. If two strands are damaged at nearby positions, the normally reliable complementary template may itself be compromised, and simultaneous processing can increase the risk of a more serious discontinuity. Chromatin organization can delay access to nuclear lesions; DNA-bound proteins and transcriptional activity can alter the relative probability of recognition. None of these observations invalidates the canonical repair mechanisms. They explain why actual repair outcomes depend on the timing, localization and coordination of enzymes as well as their catalytic capabilities.

The most reliable way to integrate the chapter is to reason from the structure of the substrate to the chemical reaction and finally to its product. A photolyase splits an abnormal covalent linkage without replacing the original bases. An alkyltransferase restores a base through transfer of a substituent. A glycosylase breaks a base–sugar bond, after which backbone processing and synthesis restore the affected position. NER brackets the lesion with two strand incisions and replaces a short nucleotide tract. All three pathways aim to preserve the original genetic message, but only excision repair requires a newly synthesized patch. The next chapter considers what happens when the backbone itself is seriously broken or when replication must continue despite a persistent lesion. Those situations require additional strategies and cannot always be solved by simply copying an intact opposing strand.
