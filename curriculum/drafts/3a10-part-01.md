# DNA Repair II: Strand Break Repair and DNA Damage Tolerance

## 1. The problem of a broken DNA backbone

A chromosome carries information in the order of its nucleotide bases, but that order must also be preserved in a physically continuous molecule. A cell can tolerate a temporary interruption in one backbone more readily than an interruption in both backbones at nearly the same position. In the first situation, the opposite strand can hold the pieces together by base pairing and supply the sequence needed for restoration. In the second situation, neither strand spans the damaged region. Two chromosome fragments may separate, become associated with incorrect partners, or fail to segregate properly when the cell divides. The distinction between these situations is the starting point for understanding strand break repair.

Imagine a short double-stranded DNA sequence containing a particular gene. If one phosphodiester bond is missing, the nitrogenous bases on either side of the interruption can remain correctly paired with the opposite strand. The encoded message need not have changed. Nevertheless, a physical defect remains in the molecule. If instead two nucleotides disappear from one strand, the complementary strand still specifies what must occupy the gap. In both examples, a repair mechanism can restore the original order without consulting another chromosome. But if both strands lose the same two base pairs, neither original strand contains the missing information. A cell that wishes to reconstruct the lost sequence must obtain that information from a separate homologous molecule or accept the possibility of a changed junction.

This difference can be understood without memorizing any enzyme names. The essential questions are whether the backbone is broken, whether genetic information is missing, and where a reliable template can be found. The answers determine which chemical reactions can solve the immediate problem. Ligation joins compatible existing termini but cannot manufacture a missing nucleotide. Polymerization adds nucleotides but normally requires a template and a primer terminus. Homologous repair supplies a template from another DNA molecule, whereas direct end joining may restore continuity without reproducing all the original bases.

A fourth problem occurs during replication. A template can remain covalently continuous yet contain a chemically modified base that prevents a replicative polymerase from advancing. Removing the modification is one solution, but the copying machinery may have to continue before removal is possible. Specialized polymerases or access to a different template can enable DNA synthesis across or around the obstruction. Such mechanisms are called DNA damage tolerance. Their immediate task is to complete DNA synthesis, not necessarily to remove the original chemical lesion. A restored replication product and a restored parental template are therefore distinct molecular achievements.

The preceding repair mechanisms, including base excision and nucleotide excision, are important because they prevent some of these more severe problems. Excision also deliberately generates short-lived strand interruptions as part of repair. A cell must coordinate the removal of an unwanted base or segment with DNA synthesis and ligation. Otherwise the repair intermediate may persist long enough to become a new source of genome damage. This chapter examines how the cell responds when the continuity of the backbone is itself threatened, or when synthesis encounters a lesion that has not yet been eliminated.

## 2. DNA ends: the chemistry that governs repair

The two strands of a DNA duplex are antiparallel. A phosphodiester linkage connects the 3′ oxygen of one deoxyribose to the phosphate attached to the 5′ carbon of the next nucleotide. DNA polymerase uses the terminal 3′ hydroxyl as the reactive group for forming a new bond with the incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate. The template is read in the opposite direction, and the newly synthesized chain grows from its 5′ end toward its 3′ end. This chemical direction is invariant among the ordinary DNA polymerases considered here. A diagram that places an extending polymerase at a free 5′ end without supplying another primer is chemically incorrect.

The terminal groups on a broken DNA molecule are not always those produced by a familiar restriction enzyme. A clean nick has a 3′ hydroxyl and an adjacent 5′ phosphate. A damaged nick may instead possess a 3′ phosphate, a 5′ hydroxyl, a sugar fragment or a protein residue covalently attached to a terminus. These groups obstruct the reactions required for restoration. A polymerase cannot extend a 3′ phosphate as if it were a 3′ hydroxyl, and a conventional DNA ligase cannot simply connect a 3′ hydroxyl to an adjacent 5′ hydroxyl. The chemistry must be corrected before the backbone can be rebuilt.

A nick is an interruption in a single strand with no missing nucleotide between its two ends. A gap is an interruption in which one or more nucleotides are absent. These terms describe structure rather than origin. A gap may be generated deliberately by excision repair or arise during DNA synthesis, while a nick may be created by a damaged sugar, an enzyme, or incomplete processing of a newly synthesized strand. A clean nick can in principle be closed immediately, whereas a gap first requires replacement synthesis or a mechanism that restores the appropriate strand segment.

DNA ligase acts by activating the 5′ phosphate so that the adjacent 3′ hydroxyl can form the final phosphodiester linkage. In ATP-dependent ligases, ATP provides an adenylyl group to the enzyme, generating an enzyme–AMP intermediate with release of pyrophosphate. Transfer of AMP to the DNA phosphate creates an activated DNA intermediate. The 3′ hydroxyl attacks this intermediate, displacing AMP and generating the intact backbone. An analogous sequence operates in NAD-dependent ligases, which obtain AMP from NAD rather than ATP. Many bacterial replicative ligases use NAD, whereas mammalian nuclear ligases use ATP. Neither statement should be interpreted as a universal rule for every organism or every bacterial ligase.

The use of an activated intermediate explains why ligation is not a spontaneous and indiscriminate welding process. Two termini must be correctly positioned relative to one another. In a nick within a duplex, complementary base pairing provides much of that alignment. For disconnected duplex fragments, additional proteins may be required to hold the appropriate ends together. Even when the chemical groups are compatible, the structural context affects the efficiency and accuracy of the reaction. The role of a ligase is fundamentally different from that of a nuclease, which cuts or removes DNA, and from that of a polymerase, which extends DNA using nucleotide substrates.

A clean end is not necessarily a safe end. Consider a chromosome cut by a nuclease that generates two perfectly ligatable termini. If those termini are rejoined to one another without alteration, the original chromosome may be restored. If an end is joined to a fragment from another chromosome, all the necessary chemical reactions may still occur successfully, but the resulting chromosome is rearranged. A repair pathway must therefore solve a problem of molecular identity in addition to a problem of chemical compatibility.

The two ends of a double-strand break may be blunt, meaning that the backbones terminate at approximately corresponding positions, or staggered, leaving a single-stranded extension. Staggered cuts may expose a 5′ overhang or a 3′ overhang. The polarity of the exposed strand is named by its terminal end, not by the direction in which a diagram happens to be drawn. Complementary overhangs can anneal and help align fragments before ligation. Noncomplementary overhangs may require trimming, filling or other processing. The amount of sequence that must be modified varies greatly with the original end structure.

In an examination, the chemical symbols at a break can be more informative than the name of the damaging agent. If a nick is marked 3′-P and 5′-OH, the first required reactions are removal of the obstructing 3′ phosphate and addition of a phosphate to the 5′ hydroxyl. If the same diagram shows a missing nucleotide, DNA synthesis is also necessary. If the termini are already suitable and the base sequence is complete, ligation alone may be sufficient. These deductions follow from chemical constraints and do not depend on remembering a rigid sequence of proteins.

## 3. How single-strand breaks arise and become consequential

Reactive oxygen species can damage the deoxyribose portion of DNA. The resulting chemical changes may destabilize the sugar–phosphate backbone and produce a strand interruption with unusual terminal groups. The same reactive species can modify bases, which may subsequently be excised and create a repair intermediate. Ionizing radiation can generate strand breaks both through direct energy deposition and through indirect chemical reactions involving water-derived radicals. The exact structure of each break varies, so a population of damaged cells contains substrates that need different processing reactions.

Not every single-strand break is caused by an accidental chemical event. During ordinary DNA metabolism, enzymes transiently interrupt the backbone. Type I topoisomerases, for example, create reversible single-strand cleavage intermediates to permit changes in DNA topology. In a productive catalytic cycle the original backbone is restored. A persistent covalent protein–DNA intermediate can arise if rejoining fails or if the cleavage complex encounters an obstruction. In that situation a normally useful intermediate becomes a lesion requiring additional repair. It is therefore important to distinguish a transient enzymatic intermediate from an unresolved pathological break.

During base excision repair a glycosylase removes a damaged base, leaving an abasic site. Cleavage of the backbone at or near that site creates an interruption that must be processed, filled when necessary and sealed. Nucleotide excision repair similarly produces an excision gap that should be filled and ligated. These pathways illustrate an apparent paradox: maintaining DNA sometimes requires deliberately breaking DNA. The cell resolves the paradox through coordination. Removing an abnormal chemical group is advantageous only when restoration reactions follow in a controlled manner.

A single-strand break can also interfere with transcription. RNA polymerase travels along a DNA template and depends on a stable template geometry. The consequences of a break depend on whether it lies in the transcribed strand, whether it changes the local structure and whether proteins accumulate at the damaged region. A lesion in a nondividing cell may therefore matter even though no replication fork is approaching it. Long-lived differentiated cells need repair mechanisms for continuous maintenance of the genome, not simply for successful chromosome duplication.

The encounter between a replication fork and a nick is especially important. Before replication, the intact complementary strand helps maintain the connection between the two pieces of the interrupted strand. At the fork, helicase separates the parental strands. If the interrupted strand is used as the template in an orientation that causes fork run-off at the discontinuity, a daughter duplex can acquire a broken end. This is often described as conversion of a single-strand break into a one-ended double-strand break during replication. Its topology differs from an ordinary two-ended break made by cutting an already completed duplex. A matching end may not exist nearby to be joined by simple end joining.

The distinction is easiest to see when considering what is actually continuous after strand separation. A nick divides one parental strand into separate covalent fragments; base pairing keeps them close until the fork unwinds the region. Once the duplex is opened, that support is lost. The replication apparatus cannot turn the discontinuous template into a normal continuous parental strand merely by extending its daughter strand. Repair or restart must recover the interrupted replication structure. An intact sister molecule can be a valuable source of information and structural continuity at this stage.

The biological consequences of unrepaired single-strand breaks therefore depend on their lifetime and on what the chromosome is doing. A nick repaired in seconds or minutes may leave no permanent mutation. A similar nick encountered during replication can become much more serious. In a nondividing cell the break may persist and interfere with gene expression or recruit repair activities that consume metabolic resources. This is why counting only newly formed breaks is insufficient for predicting cellular damage; the persistence of the lesions and the timing of cellular processes also matter.

## 4. The coordination of single-strand break repair

Mammalian nuclear single-strand break repair provides a useful representative model. A common early response is binding of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1, or PARP1, at an interruption. PARP1 recognizes abnormal DNA structure and is activated by DNA binding. It consumes NAD as substrate to synthesize chains of ADP-ribose on itself and other targets. The resulting modification changes the molecular environment around the break and promotes assembly of repair factors. PARP1 is a sensor and signaling enzyme, not the enzyme that synthesizes replacement DNA or completes the phosphodiester backbone.

The modification is reversible. Poly(ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase and related activities remove the ADP-ribose signal after it has served its purpose. This provides a means of turning off recruitment as the repair reaction progresses. Auto-modification of PARP1 can also reduce its retention at DNA and facilitate its release. A repair protein that never dissociates from a repaired or partially repaired site can itself obstruct access by the enzymes that must act next. Dynamic association is therefore part of productive repair, rather than an incidental property of the proteins.

XRCC1 is a central organizer in many mammalian single-strand break repair events. Its importance does not arise from a single bond-forming catalytic reaction. Rather, it interacts with multiple repair proteins, including DNA polymerase beta, DNA ligase III and enzymes that correct damaged termini. By associating with the site and recruiting appropriate partners, XRCC1 helps coordinate the order of reactions. A scaffold defect can impair several apparently distinct chemical activities at once even when all the individual enzymes remain catalytically competent in a purified system.

One useful end-processing example is polynucleotide kinase phosphatase, abbreviated PNKP. It can remove a blocking 3′ phosphate and phosphorylate a 5′ hydroxyl. These two activities move inappropriate DNA ends toward the configuration needed for extension and ligation. Not every single-strand break requires PNKP: a clean nick lacks these specific terminal defects. Consequently, the phenotype of PNKP deficiency should be most pronounced when substrates requiring its activities are abundant, rather than identical for every possible form of DNA damage.

Other enzymes resolve particular blocked termini. Tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 can help remove covalent residues associated with trapped topoisomerase I cleavage complexes, although additional processing may still be necessary after its action. Aprataxin can resolve a DNA adenylate intermediate left by abortive ligation. These examples establish a general principle: the cell needs enzymes that recognize distinct chemical obstacles, because a single general-purpose joining enzyme cannot accommodate every damaged end. At examination level it is more useful to understand the chemistry motivating these activities than to memorize a catalog of rare substrates.

After the ends become chemically suitable, a missing segment may be replaced. DNA polymerase beta commonly participates in short-patch repair in the mammalian nucleus, using the opposite intact DNA strand as a template. If no nucleotide is missing, polymerase action may not be needed. Depending on the substrate, additional synthesis and processing factors can participate. DNA ligase III associated with XRCC1 can seal the remaining nick; DNA ligase I can also contribute to nuclear repair in appropriate contexts. The proteins listed here represent common pathways rather than an obligatory universal sequence in every cell.

An experimentally distinguishable defect in gap filling should leave different substrates from a defect in final sealing. When polymerization is blocked, missing nucleotide positions persist. When synthesis is successful but ligation is blocked, a newly synthesized patch may be present even though the backbone remains discontinuous. Measuring labeled nucleotide incorporation alone cannot establish that repair is complete. A complementary assay for strand continuity or residual damage is required. This distinction connects molecular mechanism directly to experimental reasoning.
