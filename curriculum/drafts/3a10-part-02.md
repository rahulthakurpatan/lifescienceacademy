## 5. The special danger of a double-strand break

A double-strand break (DSB) interrupts both covalent backbones of a DNA duplex. Even if the ends remain nearby in a nucleus, the chromosome is no longer continuous. A cell with multiple such breaks faces a further problem: the ends from different break sites can become potential partners for joining. Restoration of a physically intact molecule is not sufficient if the fragments have been reassembled in the wrong order. A DSB is therefore a threat to both local nucleotide sequence and larger-scale chromosome organization.

Ionizing radiation can produce DSBs directly or through clustered damage. Two strand interruptions close to one another on opposite strands can disrupt the duplex even when the positions do not correspond exactly. Enzymes that cut both strands can create more precisely defined breaks. DNA replication through an interrupted template can create a one-ended break, as discussed in the previous section. These sources differ in the structure of their products, and the most appropriate repair response is partly determined by the substrate rather than merely the name of the original insult.

A two-ended break in a linear chromosome generates two fragments. One may contain the centromere and the other may not, depending on the position of the break. At mitosis, a centromere is needed to establish the kinetochore and permit directed chromosome movement. A fragment without a functional centromere has no ordinary mechanism for reliable segregation. Broken chromosomes can consequently produce chromosome fragments that are lost or excluded from daughter nuclei. In cells that continue through division despite unresolved damage, the resulting abnormal distribution of genetic material may be more consequential than the loss of a few individual bases.

Consider a chromosome with two breaks surrounding a segment. If the two outer ends are joined without the intervening segment, a deletion is produced. If that segment is reversed and its ends are joined to the remaining chromosome, an inversion can result. If breaks occur on two chromosomes and ends are exchanged, a reciprocal translocation is possible. These outcomes illustrate why the arrangement of ends must be considered. A single local sequencing assay may show that one junction has formed but cannot establish that the rest of the genome has been correctly reconstructed.

A circular bacterial chromosome presents a related but not identical topological problem. A DSB can convert a circular molecule into a linear one. The broken molecule can become susceptible to further degradation, and its ability to complete replication and segregation may be impaired. Some bacterial systems can use another homologous DNA molecule generated during replication to restore continuity. Their reliance on recombinational repair varies with species, growth state and the available pathways. A model built from a mammalian linear chromosome should not simply be transferred to bacteria without considering these differences.

The cell's two broad solutions to a two-ended DSB are to reconnect the ends or to reconstruct the broken region with information from a homologous DNA template. Classical non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) represents the first strategy. Homologous recombination-directed repair (HR) represents an important form of the second. They are not equivalent choices under all circumstances. If the original ends are compatible and close together, reconnecting them can be very efficient. If sequence information has been lost and a faithful donor is available, copying from that donor can restore the missing bases. If the break is one-ended, simply joining to any available broken end is particularly hazardous.

The distinction between repair strategies is often introduced as error-prone versus error-free repair. That shorthand is useful only when accompanied by its assumptions. End joining can perfectly restore clean compatible ends; homologous repair can copy variants from a donor or produce rearrangements if the wrong template is chosen. The deeper distinction concerns the source of information used to make the repair. A pathway that directly rejoins ends does not require an extended homologous donor, while template-directed repair normally depends on sequence correspondence with a separate DNA molecule. Error rates follow from substrate and pathway details rather than from the names alone.

## 6. How experimental observations distinguish repair routes

Early radiation genetics established that damage survival was not simply a fixed consequence of the amount of energy deposited. Different organisms and mutant strains could exhibit different sensitivities even under comparable exposure. This suggested that biological processes occurring after damage influenced whether cells retained the ability to grow. However, survival experiments alone could not reveal the molecular mechanism. A mutation that affects repair, cell division, stress signaling or metabolism can all change survival. To identify a repair route, investigators need to examine the damaged DNA and the products of its restoration.

One conceptual approach is to introduce a defined DSB into a DNA substrate whose sequence is known. In a cell-free system, adding protein fractions and measuring the appearance of joined molecules can reveal end-joining activity. In cells, a damaged reporter can regain function after a repair event. When the DNA sequence is recovered and examined, the nature of the repair product helps distinguish precise joining, local sequence changes and copying of information from a donor. These approaches complement one another: biochemical assays show which reactions are possible, whereas cellular genetics reveals which reactions matter in the living system.

Defined donor sequences are especially valuable. Suppose a target gene is interrupted by a break and an otherwise homologous donor carries a silent marker that is absent from the target. A repaired target that acquires this marker provides evidence that information from the donor was transferred during restoration. The marker is valuable because a perfectly restored target sequence alone might have arisen by precise end joining or by template-directed repair using a genetically identical copy. A donor-specific marker makes those outcomes distinguishable. The marker should be placed and interpreted so that its acquisition requires the hypothesized information transfer, not an unrelated random mutation.

Classical genetic reasoning compares the effects of losing specific repair genes. If disruption of a DNA end-binding protein greatly decreases end-joining reporter activity and expression of the normal protein restores it, the combined evidence supports a role for the protein in that pathway. Yet the evidence does not by itself establish whether the protein is a catalytic ligase, a scaffold or a regulator. A separate biochemical experiment can test whether purified protein binds DNA ends, stimulates a joining reaction or has catalytic activity of its own. Genetic and biochemical evidence answer different levels of the mechanistic question.

An informative historical connection emerged from studies of mammalian cells defective in both radiation resistance and immune receptor gene rearrangement. Lymphocytes create regulated DSB intermediates when assembling antigen receptor genes. If a cellular defect impairs repair of accidental radiation-induced breaks and prevents completion of these programmed rearrangements, the two processes may share a joining mechanism. The severe combined immunodeficiency phenotype in certain repair-defective mouse strains helped establish this connection. Identification of DNA-dependent protein kinase components and associated end-joining proteins later clarified the molecular basis of these observations. The clinical and immunological consequences are useful evidence for the general repair mechanism, not a substitute for its biochemical explanation.

A second classic line of evidence arises from the strong effects of defects in recombinational repair genes on recovery from damage. Recombination-deficient bacteria frequently show sensitivity to DNA insults that disrupt replication or chromosome continuity. In eukaryotic systems, the ability to restore a broken reporter by using a homologous donor depends on proteins required for homologous DNA transactions. These findings explain why the same machinery that can exchange genetic information during recombination also has an important role in preserving genome integrity during vegetative growth. Detailed strand-exchange chemistry will be considered in the following chapter.

The design of an experiment determines the strength of the conclusion. If a mutant forms fewer colonies after radiation, it is appropriate to say that it is more sensitive under the conditions tested. It is not appropriate to conclude that a particular enzyme has failed to catalyze ligation unless additional measurements establish that reaction. If a reporter detects only one class of repair products, a decline in reporter activity may mean that repair has shifted to a different pathway, not that all repair has disappeared. Evidence for a pathway must therefore be tied to what the assay actually detects.

## 7. Classical non-homologous end joining: recognizing and protecting the ends

Classical NHEJ in mammalian nuclei begins when exposed duplex DNA ends become available to repair proteins. The Ku complex, formed by Ku70 and Ku80, is a major early participant. Its structural organization allows it to encircle duplex DNA near a free end. Because a free double-stranded end is unusual within an intact chromosomal region, this binding property makes Ku well suited to detecting a break without requiring recognition of a particular nucleotide sequence. The system can therefore act at many different locations across the genome.

Ku binding also changes the availability of the DNA end for other reactions. An unprotected end can be attacked by nucleases or become involved in uncontrolled reactions. Occupation by Ku helps organize a repair complex and favors conservation of the end structure. This is important because every nucleotide unnecessarily removed from an otherwise repairable end increases the possibility of sequence alteration. A system that rapidly binds and protects ends can sometimes restore the original sequence without extensive synthesis or processing.

In vertebrate cells, Ku can recruit the catalytic subunit of DNA-dependent protein kinase, DNA-PKcs. Association of Ku, DNA-PKcs and DNA ends creates a signaling and structural assembly that participates in coordinating end joining. DNA-PKcs is a protein kinase, so its catalytic action involves transferring phosphate groups to protein substrates using ATP. It does not directly form the missing DNA phosphodiester linkage. Phosphorylation events, including autophosphorylation, contribute to rearrangements of the complex and regulate access to the DNA ends.

End tethering brings the two ends into proximity. A useful way to think about this step is to distinguish proximity from chemical readiness. End-binding and tethering proteins can hold an otherwise difficult substrate in a repair complex even when the terminal groups cannot yet be ligated. Conversely, a chemically ligatable end may not produce a useful repair product unless the correct partner is aligned. End joining requires coordination between spatial organization and terminal processing.

The details of the joining complex depend on the substrate. Some clean, compatible ends can be rejoined with relatively limited processing and fewer required activities than difficult ends. DNA-PKcs and end-processing enzymes are particularly significant in contexts where terminal structure must be modified. Therefore, a simple educational scheme showing Ku followed by DNA-PKcs, processing and ligation should be read as a representative sequence, not as a claim that every protein must act catalytically at every break. The pathway has flexibility because the substrates that it encounters are heterogeneous.

A repair defect can consequently be substrate dependent. A mutation in Ku can interfere with efficient recognition and organization across a wide range of breaks. A mutation in an enzyme needed to open a particular end structure might have a much smaller effect on simple compatible ends. A defect in the final ligase should interfere with production of covalently sealed products even when the recognition and alignment steps occur normally. Predicting these differences is more useful than memorizing a single linear list of components.

## 8. End processing and the chemistry of final joining

Some double-strand breaks expose complementary single-stranded extensions that can form base pairs with one another. If the ends carry appropriate terminal groups, annealing recreates a locally continuous duplex with nicks in its two backbones. Ligation can seal those nicks without changing the sequence. Blunt ends can also be joined, although their alignment is not stabilized by complementary overhang base pairing. These are important counterexamples to the assertion that NHEJ inevitably produces mutations.

More complicated breaks require processing. A nuclease can remove damaged terminal nucleotides or unpaired flaps, and a DNA polymerase can fill recessed regions or add nucleotides that make the termini more suitable for joining. In vertebrate NHEJ, Artemis is a representative nuclease associated with DNA-PK-dependent end processing. DNA polymerases mu and lambda can participate in end-associated synthesis. Their contributions depend on end geometry and available template information. Neither a nuclease nor a polymerase replaces the final need for backbone ligation.

A useful distinction is between trimming a nucleotide and filling a nucleotide. Trimming removes information from the broken molecule. Filling can restore complementary information when a suitable template is present, but filling does not guarantee restoration of the original sequence if the template has already been lost or if the polymerase uses an alternative arrangement. For example, an overhang that retains the original sequence may guide faithful fill-in of its complementary strand. If both strands of the original sequence have been removed, local fill-in cannot reconstruct that sequence without another source of information.

Short complementary regions at broken ends can help stabilize their alignment. Such local complementarity does not automatically make the reaction homologous recombination. Classical NHEJ can use short base-pairing opportunities while remaining independent of an extended homologous donor molecule. Alternative end joining pathways may make more conspicuous use of short internal sequence matches, often called microhomologies, after limited resection. Finding a few matching bases at a junction is therefore not sufficient to diagnose a particular pathway without additional evidence about the substrate and the proteins required.

In mammalian classical NHEJ, DNA ligase IV works in a complex with XRCC4, with XLF contributing to efficient assembly and joining in many settings. XRCC4 helps stabilize and organize ligase IV; XLF participates in end alignment and stimulation of joining. These accessory proteins should not be described as alternative ligases. The catalytic joining function is supplied by ligase IV, which uses ATP-dependent activation chemistry. The existence of distinct organizational and catalytic functions explains why deficiency of a noncatalytic component can be as consequential for repair as loss of an enzyme's active site.

Each duplex contains two covalent backbones, so a completed repair must restore both. The two sealing events need not take place at the exact same moment. An intermediate in which one strand has been joined but the other remains nicked is chemically possible. A laboratory measurement that detects association or pairing of two DNA fragments can therefore overestimate complete repair if it does not distinguish noncovalently associated or partially joined products from fully ligated molecules. Sequence analysis and measurements of covalent continuity address complementary questions.

Consider a hypothetical clean staggered break with complementary overhangs. If the cell anneals the original partners and seals both backbones without nucleotide addition or removal, the product has no sequence change. Consider instead a damaged break where three base pairs are lost before joining and no homologous donor is used. If no replacement information is provided, the final product contains a three-base-pair deletion. Both events may be classified as NHEJ, yet they have very different consequences for the encoded message. In a coding sequence, a deletion of three nucleotides preserves the downstream reading frame but can still alter protein function; a deletion of two generally changes the reading frame. Location matters as well: a small change in a regulatory motif can have a large effect even outside a protein-coding region.

End joining thus involves an important biological balance. Extensive processing can convert difficult ends into substrates that can be joined, but processing carries a risk of losing information. Rapid restoration of chromosome continuity can preserve viability even when some local sequence is altered. Protection, selective processing, alignment and ligation together make classical NHEJ a versatile response to DNA breaks rather than a single indiscriminate reaction.
