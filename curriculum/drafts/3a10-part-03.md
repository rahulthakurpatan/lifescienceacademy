## 9. Template-guided repair: recovering information rather than merely continuity

When a DSB has removed genetic information, reconnecting its ends cannot in itself replace that information. The cell must obtain a sequence from somewhere else. After DNA replication, each chromatid has a sister that normally carries nearly the same nucleotide sequence. The sister chromatid can serve as a donor during homologous recombination-directed repair. This is a central reason why template-guided repair becomes particularly useful during S and G2 phases of the eukaryotic cell cycle. It exploits the temporary presence of a newly produced copy of the chromosome.

A diploid cell also possesses a homologous chromosome inherited from the other parent. Its sequence is similar but not necessarily identical to that of the broken chromosome. If the homolog is used as a donor, allelic differences may be transferred during repair. This possibility distinguishes sequence recovery from restoration of precisely the previous allele. Sister chromatids are usually preferred for repair during ordinary somatic growth because they are nearby and generally provide a closely matching sequence. Homologous chromosomes have a central role in meiotic recombination, a context in which the biological objectives and regulation differ.

Before a broken end can use a homologous donor in the usual HR pathway, the DNA must be processed to expose a single-stranded region. The reason is structural: a strand that remains paired throughout its length with its original partner cannot readily test its complementarity to the bases of a second duplex. End resection removes DNA preferentially from the 5′-terminated strands near the break, leaving 3′ single-stranded extensions. The direction is important. The resulting 3′ terminus can serve as a primer for subsequent repair DNA synthesis after it finds a complementary donor strand.

The processing involves coordinated nucleases. In mammalian cells the MRE11–RAD50–NBS1 complex, usually abbreviated MRN, helps recognize and organize broken DNA ends, and CtIP contributes to initiation of resection in appropriate contexts. Further processing extends the single-stranded regions as required. The proteins that initiate and extend resection are not all functionally interchangeable. At the present level the important principle is that the regulated reaction creates a primer-compatible single-stranded end and changes the substrate from one favorable for direct end joining to one that can participate in homologous interactions.

Exposed single-stranded DNA is itself chemically and structurally vulnerable. It can form secondary structures, become degraded or engage in inappropriate interactions. Replication protein A, abbreviated RPA, binds single-stranded DNA and helps protect it. Protection alone is not equivalent to finding a donor. The strand must eventually participate in a homology search, a function involving the recombinase RAD51. Accessory factors including BRCA2 help assemble RAD51 on the single-stranded substrate, replacing RPA in the appropriate molecular context. BRCA1 has important roles in the regulatory and processing events that favor homologous repair. The different roles of these proteins should not be collapsed into the statement that they are all DNA polymerases.

RAD51-associated DNA can interact with a homologous duplex and identify a region in which complementary base pairing is possible. One of the strands of the donor duplex is displaced locally as the incoming strand pairs with its complementary donor strand. The resulting structure includes a displacement loop. The free 3′ end of the invading strand can now be extended by DNA polymerase using donor sequence information. The broken recipient acquires new DNA complementary to the intact donor, which explains how lost information can be restored despite the original damage to both backbones.

The chemical reason that a 3′ single-stranded extension is useful is now apparent. Resection alone does not restore sequence information; it prepares a substrate for the homology search. Homology recognition alone does not replace the missing nucleotides; it brings a primer and template into productive alignment. Polymerization then performs the copying reaction, after which the repair intermediates must be processed and connected to restore continuous duplex DNA. Each stage solves a distinct problem, and the failure of any one of them can interrupt the overall process.

In a common noncrossover route, newly synthesized DNA can be displaced from the donor and anneal to a complementary region at the other end of the broken molecule. Further synthesis, removal of inappropriate flaps and ligation restore the recipient. This conceptual route is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing. Other forms of homologous repair involve joint molecules whose processing may generate different recombinant products. The detailed Holliday junction mechanisms, branch migration and crossover analysis are the subject of the following chapter. For the present purpose, it is enough to recognize that homologous repair can transfer local sequence information without exchanging whole chromosome arms.

A particularly valuable feature of this mechanism is that the donor molecule need not be sacrificed. It acts as a template, and the genetic information can remain present in the donor after the recipient is restored. This differs from simply taking an existing physical DNA fragment from one chromosome and inserting it into another. Polymerization can reproduce donor sequence information in the recipient while leaving the donor's sequence intact. However, the outcome depends on the route used to process the intermediate; the statement should not be interpreted as a guarantee that all homologous DNA transactions preserve chromosome arrangement.

## 10. Selecting a donor and choosing between repair pathways

The availability of a suitable template does not depend merely on whether the cell contains DNA with a similar sequence. A donor must also be accessible at the appropriate time, in an appropriate chromosomal context. In G1, before the relevant chromosomal region has been replicated, a sister chromatid is absent. Classical NHEJ therefore has a particularly important role in repairing ordinary two-ended breaks in this phase. In S and G2, sister chromatids become available, and regulated resection and homologous repair can contribute much more substantially. NHEJ nevertheless remains active during these phases. A cell-cycle diagram that assigns NHEJ exclusively to G1 and HR exclusively to G2 misrepresents the overlapping operation of the pathways.

Cell-cycle regulation controls the reactions that make a break suitable for template-directed repair. Cyclin-dependent kinase activity changes as cells progress through the cycle, affecting factors required for resection. In mammalian cells, regulators associated with end protection oppose extensive processing in some contexts, whereas BRCA1-associated regulation can favor resection and homologous repair when appropriate. At examination level, the key causal connection is that processing determines substrate availability. Protecting the double-stranded ends favors joining, while producing extended single-stranded 3′ tails favors homologous interactions. Regulation of the processing step thus influences pathway choice even before the final repair synthesis or ligation takes place.

The decision is not a conscious inspection of a list of repair options. It emerges from competition among molecular activities, their abundance and regulation, chromatin environment, break structure and timing. If an end-binding complex occupies a newly exposed duplex end, it can limit access by nucleases. If resection begins and removes a substantial region of one strand, the original ends are no longer simple joining substrates. The biochemical state of the DNA has changed, making some subsequent reactions more likely and others less favorable. This makes pathway choice a dynamic process rather than a rigid decision tree.

A one-ended DSB arising at a replication fork has a special requirement. Direct joining is attractive only if there is an appropriate second end to join. An unrelated end elsewhere in the genome does not solve the original replication problem and may create a rearrangement. Template-directed repair can instead use an intact homologous molecule to initiate recovery of the broken replication structure. The exact mechanisms of restart differ between organisms and contexts, but the simple topological reasoning remains valid. A broken fork requires restoration of an arrangement that allows synthesis to continue, not indiscriminate closure of whatever termini happen to be available.

Repeats in the genome complicate donor selection. Suppose a broken sequence belongs to a family of repeated elements and several other locations contain similar DNA. Pairing to a sequence at the wrong chromosomal location could transfer inappropriate information or promote a structural rearrangement. A high degree of complementarity is necessary for standard homologous repair, but it does not identify the correct genomic location by itself. Spatial organization and the association between sister chromatids help make the most appropriate donor available. This explains why template-directed repair is highly accurate under favorable conditions without being universally free from genetic consequences.

In a heterozygous diploid, homologous repair using the other parental chromosome can replace one allele with the corresponding sequence from the donor. Imagine a locus containing alleles A and a, where a break damages the chromosome carrying A. If the region is restored by copying from the homolog carrying a, both repaired and donor copies can carry a in the repaired interval. The cell has not necessarily lost an entire chromosome, but it has lost heterozygosity at that locus. The example is intentionally simple; the size of a converted region and its genetic consequences depend on the repair event. It illustrates how a chemically accurate copying reaction can nevertheless change genetic identity.

Repair pathway selection also has implications for experimental interpretation. If a cell population contains different proportions of G1 and S/G2 cells, a comparison of homologous repair reporter activity between two populations may partly reflect cell-cycle distribution rather than a direct alteration of repair chemistry. A mutant that slows progression through G1 could show reduced HR reporter activity even when its HR enzymes are normal. Comparing populations matched for cell-cycle phase or measuring their DNA content helps distinguish these possibilities. The principle is a recurring theme in Part C problems: a change in a measured outcome can have more than one plausible cause.

## 11. Other outcomes at broken ends: alternative joining and annealing

The two major pathways do not exhaust the possible responses to a DSB. Cells possess other mechanisms that exploit limited sequence similarity or repeated sequences around a break. These routes are worth understanding at the level of principle because a question that presents an unusual deletion junction may otherwise be misclassified as either precise NHEJ or faithful sister-directed repair. The terminology varies with organism and experimental definition, so the structure of the intermediate and the final product should be examined first.

Alternative end joining can occur after limited resection exposes short complementary sequences near a break. The exposed regions can anneal, and incompatible flaps may be removed before remaining gaps are filled and the backbones sealed. If the matching sequences lie internal to the original termini, intervening nucleotides may be lost during processing. This creates a deletion whose junction may contain one copy of the matching sequence. The term microhomology-mediated end joining is often used for a class of such events. In mammalian systems, polymerase theta participates in an important alternative end-joining route, but not every observed microhomology-associated junction uniquely identifies polymerase theta activity.

Consider a simplified sequence arrangement on the same original strand: a short repeat M, a segment X, and another copy of repeat M. A break between the repeats followed by processing may expose complementary single-stranded regions that correspond to the two copies of M. If repair aligns those repeats and removes unpaired material, the product can contain a single copy of M and lack X together with the redundant repeat copy. Sequencing reveals a shortened junction. The existence of matching sequence at the junction helps explain how alignment occurred, but does not establish the complete enzyme pathway without genetic or biochemical evidence.

Single-strand annealing provides a related but distinct concept when more extensive direct repeats flank a DSB. Resection can expose complementary repeat sequences on opposite sides, which anneal. Nonpaired tails are processed, remaining gaps are filled as needed, and the DNA is ligated. The product characteristically loses the intervening sequence and one repeat copy. Because the pathway uses complementary sequences within the broken molecule, it is homology-dependent in a broad sense. It does not require the same RAD51-mediated invasion of an intact donor duplex that characterizes conventional sister-directed HR. Calling every homology-dependent process faithful homologous repair would therefore obscure an important mechanistic distinction.

For examination purposes the differences can be derived from the location of the template. In classical NHEJ, the original ends are brought together and processed as required without an extended homologous donor. In microhomology-associated alternative joining, short matching regions help connect processed ends. In single-strand annealing, flanking repeated sequences on the broken molecule provide an alignment opportunity, commonly at the cost of deleting DNA. In conventional HR, an intact homologous molecule supplies sequence information that may restore the missing interval. These strategies all solve a continuity problem, but the genetic products can be strikingly different.

An observed deletion can originate from more than one route. Classical NHEJ may generate deletions by terminal trimming; alternative end joining may produce deletions associated with internal matches; single-strand annealing may remove a large interval between repeats. Therefore, deletion size or the mere presence of short matching sequence should not be used as an absolute diagnostic criterion. A stronger conclusion combines junction sequences with pathway-specific genetic dependencies, appropriate reporter design, and controls for the structure of the starting break.

These alternatives also explain why disabling one pathway need not abolish every repair event. If classical NHEJ is impaired, some breaks may be repaired by homologous repair or alternative joining, depending on the cell-cycle phase and substrates. If HR is impaired, other routes may still restore continuity, sometimes with altered sequence outcomes. A survival assay may thus reveal incomplete sensitivity, while a sequencing assay reveals a substantial change in the distribution of repaired products. Both observations can be consistent with a significant defect in one particular pathway.
