## 12. Bacterial break repair and the conservation of template-guided recovery

The principles of strand-break repair apply across living organisms, but the available proteins and the architecture of the chromosomes vary. Escherichia coli is a useful model because its double-strand break repair machinery has been extensively studied and because many of its genetic features can be related directly to classical experiments. It is also a reminder that a bacterium does not possess mammalian G1, S and G2 phases. The ability of a bacterium to use homologous DNA depends on its replication state, chromosome copy number and other cellular conditions, rather than on the names of eukaryotic cell-cycle phases.

A major bacterial route for processing double-stranded DNA ends in E. coli involves the RecBCD complex. This protein assembly combines DNA-unwinding and nuclease-associated functions. As it engages a double-stranded DNA end, it processes the DNA and can alter its activity upon recognition of a Chi sequence in an appropriate orientation. The resulting substrate can support loading of RecA on a 3′ single-stranded region. A useful consequence of this organization is that detection of the end, processing of the substrate and initiation of homologous repair can be coordinated by a relatively integrated bacterial system. It would be misleading to identify RecBCD as a DNA ligase: the complex prepares a substrate for later steps rather than directly restoring a missing phosphodiester bond.

RecA is a bacterial recombinase whose DNA-associated state enables homologous pairing and strand exchange. It has a functional relationship to the eukaryotic recombinase RAD51, although the proteins are not identical and their accessory machinery differs. E. coli uses RecA-associated processes to recover DNA information from homologous templates and to support restoration of damaged replication structures. The detailed base-pairing transactions and recombinant products are addressed in the next chapter. Here, the relevant point is that break processing followed by access to homologous sequence provides a way to preserve the original information even when the damaged duplex cannot itself supply it.

The structure of the starting DNA determines which initial processing system can operate. A broken duplex end is not the same substrate as a single-stranded gap within an otherwise continuous daughter molecule. In E. coli, RecBCD is strongly associated with processing of duplex ends, whereas RecFOR-associated processes contribute to RecA loading at gaps and other single-stranded regions. This distinction is useful because both types of substrate can ultimately require homologous DNA interactions. Finding a RecA-dependent phenotype alone does not establish whether the initial lesion was a duplex break or a single-stranded daughter gap.

In rapidly growing bacterial cells, ongoing replication can generate multiple copies of some genomic regions even though the organism is conventionally described as haploid. Those copies may provide opportunities for homologous repair. In other circumstances, such as a cell with no accessible intact copy of a damaged region, the feasibility of the same pathway can be reduced. Ploidy and template availability must therefore be treated as separate ideas. Describing a bacterium as haploid concerns the normal genetic organization of the organism; it does not prove that every region of its genome is present in exactly one physical copy at all times.

Some bacteria have a form of non-homologous end joining involving a bacterial Ku protein and a multifunctional LigD protein or related components. This system can be valuable when homologous templates are limited, for example in certain nonreplicating bacterial cells. The bacterial system does not require the full mammalian Ku70/Ku80–DNA-PKcs–XRCC4–ligase IV assembly. The shared functional challenge is end recognition, processing and joining, but the molecular solutions differ. E. coli is not a standard example of the bacterial Ku–LigD pathway, so it is important not to generalize the existence of this pathway to all bacteria.

The evolutionary comparison reveals a distinction between conservation of a function and conservation of an entire pathway. The chemistry of phosphodiester bond formation and the need for an appropriately placed 3′ hydroxyl are broadly shared. Homologous pairing is also widespread. Nevertheless, the proteins that deliver these activities, their regulation and their relative importance can differ substantially. An examination question that uses an unfamiliar organism should be solved using the given pathway components and experimental evidence, rather than assuming that the complete mammalian model is universally applicable.

## 13. Replication arrest is not the same event as chemical damage removal

The high fidelity of normal replicative DNA polymerases depends partly on their ability to reject incorrect nucleotide–template geometries. A replicative polymerase selects substrates in a tightly organized catalytic site, and some replicative polymerases also proofread newly synthesized DNA. These properties minimize mutations on ordinary templates. However, the structural selectivity that confers accuracy also makes many chemical lesions difficult for the enzyme to traverse. A bulky adduct or an abnormal linkage between neighboring template bases may prevent the template from adopting the geometry required for normal synthesis.

A replication fork contains more than one molecular activity. Helicase opens the parental duplex, polymerases extend the daughter strands, and accessory proteins coordinate the movement of these components. When DNA synthesis encounters an obstacle, the response can involve changes in the coupling between unwinding and polymerization. Exposed single-stranded DNA can accumulate. This creates a substrate for single-stranded DNA-binding proteins and damage-response signaling. The fork may slow, undergo remodeling, or leave a gap that is filled subsequently. The precise architecture is context dependent, but the general distinction between stalling of polymerization and physical breakage of the DNA remains important.

A chemically modified template can be completely continuous before replication. If a polymerase cannot extend across the modified region, the daughter strand can remain incomplete even though the parental backbone is intact. The chemical lesion and the daughter-strand discontinuity are therefore separate features. One can measure the persistence of the lesion in the parental template independently of the length or completeness of the newly synthesized DNA. This separation is essential for recognizing DNA damage tolerance.

Damage removal directly changes the damaged substrate. Photolyase, where present and applicable, reverses a specific photochemical lesion. Excision repair removes a damaged base or segment and replaces DNA using an intact complement. In contrast, damage tolerance enables DNA synthesis to proceed despite the lesion. A template can retain its chemical modification after a successful tolerance event. The lesion may be repaired later, persist, or contribute to further problems when the DNA is used again. Bypass is an immediate solution to a synthesis problem, not a guarantee of eventual chemical restoration.

One benefit of tolerance is that it reduces the time for which replication remains obstructed. Persistent replication arrest can lead to incomplete chromosome duplication, activation of checkpoint responses and potentially more severe lesions. A cell that has a way to complete a daughter strand despite a damaged template can sometimes preserve chromosome integrity and survive until repair is possible. The cost is that synthesis across some lesions is less accurate than synthesis across undamaged DNA. When assessing these pathways, survival and mutation frequency must be considered separately: a pathway can increase one while also increasing the other.

The word tolerance has a precise meaning here and should not be confused with an assertion that the cell is unaffected by damage. A cell can have improved ability to continue replication without removing more chemical lesions per unit time. Conversely, a cell can remove lesions effectively yet have trouble replicating when even a small number remain. In an experiment where one population has normal lesion removal but defective post-damage DNA synthesis, a tolerance defect is a plausible explanation. To establish that conclusion, measurements must distinguish lesion persistence from newly synthesized DNA and from simple changes in cell viability.

A lesion that strongly distorts the template may have very different effects on different polymerases. The polymerase that excels at copying normal DNA need not be the polymerase that performs best on a particular altered substrate. Evolution has produced specialized polymerases with active-site properties that allow some otherwise difficult template geometries to be accommodated. This specialization illustrates a common trade-off in enzyme function: optimizing recognition of the ordinary substrate does not necessarily optimize activity on every unusual substrate. The cell can reduce the cost of this trade-off by restricting the access of specialized enzymes to situations where they are needed.

## 14. Translesion DNA synthesis: polymerase switching and lesion specificity

Translesion DNA synthesis, abbreviated TLS, refers to synthesis that uses a damaged DNA template directly and proceeds past a lesion that obstructs normal replication. A specialized polymerase gains access to a primer–template junction, incorporates a nucleotide opposite or near the lesion, and can extend the resulting primer far enough to permit subsequent synthesis. Depending on the lesion and the polymerase involved, insertion and extension may be accomplished by the same enzyme or by different specialized enzymes. Normal replicative polymerization can resume when an appropriate, relatively undistorted primer–template structure has been re-established.

TLS polymerases still synthesize DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. They require a suitable primer terminus and nucleotide substrates. They do not reverse the ordinary chemistry of DNA synthesis in order to cross a lesion. Instead, their catalytic sites and interactions with DNA differ in ways that permit use of some structurally altered templates. Many specialized Y-family polymerases lack intrinsic 3′ to 5′ proofreading activity and have lower fidelity than replicative polymerases on ordinary DNA. Their structural flexibility can be beneficial at a particular lesion but costly if they are allowed to copy extensive regions of undamaged DNA.

A well-established example is human DNA polymerase eta, a Y-family polymerase encoded by POLH. Ultraviolet radiation can produce a cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer that links neighboring pyrimidines within one strand. A common example involves adjacent thymine bases. The altered arrangement presents a challenge to ordinary replicative polymerases. Polymerase eta has structural features that allow it to accommodate a cis-syn cyclobutane thymine dimer and frequently insert the correct adenine bases opposite the two thymine positions. The original photoproduct remains in the template during this process. The accurate copying event therefore represents lesion bypass rather than reversal of the photochemical reaction.

This example is especially important because it corrects a widespread misconception about TLS. It is often summarized as error-prone synthesis, but accuracy depends on the particular polymerase–lesion combination. Polymerase eta is relatively well suited to certain UV-induced dimers and can reduce mutagenesis compared with less suitable alternative bypass activities. The same polymerase is not a high-fidelity replacement for a replicative polymerase on all undamaged DNA. A specialized enzyme may therefore protect the genome in one context and increase the risk of copying errors in another.

A related human genetic observation concerns xeroderma pigmentosum variant, abbreviated XP-V. Cells with defective POLH have difficulty carrying out the appropriate form of lesion bypass after UV exposure. Classical xeroderma pigmentosum groups associated with nucleotide excision repair defects have a different immediate molecular problem: impaired removal of photoproduct-containing DNA segments. Both defects can increase sensitivity to UV-related damage and the risk of mutagenesis, but they should not be assigned to the same biochemical step simply because the clinical names are related. Comparing the persistence of UV photoproducts and the completion of newly synthesized DNA helps distinguish the two mechanisms.

Other eukaryotic TLS components illustrate the division of labor between insertion and extension. Some polymerases are effective at inserting opposite a difficult base but less effective at extending from the resulting primer. Polymerase zeta can contribute to extension from certain distorted primer termini. REV1 can participate through both catalytic and organizational functions in appropriate settings. The exact combination of enzymes depends on the lesion and the organism. At NET level, a useful conclusion is that TLS may involve sequential polymerase exchange rather than the permanent substitution of one polymerase for another.

The return to a replicative polymerase after bypass is biologically significant. If the specialized enzyme were to continue copying large amounts of normal template, its lower fidelity on that substrate could impose unnecessary mutational costs. A brief change of polymerase activity at the obstructed site allows the cell to retain the accuracy and efficiency of normal replication over most of the genome. Regulation of access, retention and release is consequently as important as the catalytic capacity of the bypass enzyme itself.

TLS can also take place after an initial replication event leaves a daughter-strand gap. The specialized polymerase may act at that gap rather than traveling continuously with the main replication fork throughout the entire process. In either case, what defines TLS is use of the lesion-containing template at the bypass step, not the precise position of the polymerase relative to the moving fork. Examination questions sometimes present a gap behind a fork and ask whether DNA damage tolerance can occur there. The answer is yes, provided that the gap-filling reaction uses the appropriate tolerance mechanism.

A useful way to compare two polymerases experimentally is to measure extension on otherwise identical DNA substrates, one containing a defined lesion and one without it. A replicative polymerase may produce a longer product on the undamaged substrate but stop immediately before the lesion. A specialized polymerase may produce a longer product across the damaged region while being less accurate on the normal substrate. Product length reveals whether synthesis continued; sequence analysis reveals which bases were incorporated. The combination is necessary to distinguish successful bypass from accurate bypass.
