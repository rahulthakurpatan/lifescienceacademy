## 15. Template switching: bypass without copying the damaged base

An alternative way to complete a newly synthesized DNA strand is to obtain information from a template that does not contain the obstructing lesion. During semiconservative replication, the two parental strands are separated and each serves as a template for a newly synthesized complementary strand. If one parental strand contains a lesion while the corresponding region of the other parental strand is undamaged, copying of the undamaged strand can produce a new DNA segment that contains useful sequence information. The blocked daughter strand may temporarily exploit that information rather than attempt to copy the damaged parental nucleotide directly. This is the conceptual basis of template switching during DNA damage tolerance.

The term template switching can be understood by keeping track of strand polarity. Suppose a parental strand contains a damaged thymine position. On the opposite parental strand, adenine occupies the corresponding position. Copying this undamaged adenine produces a thymine in the newly synthesized sister DNA. The blocked daughter strand needs an adenine at its own corresponding position. If it gains access to the newly synthesized sister strand containing thymine, it can obtain the appropriate adenine without using the damaged original thymine as the immediate template. The alternative template is not simply any nearby DNA strand; it must have the sequence orientation and complementarity required for correct extension.

Several related mechanisms can facilitate access to a sister-derived sequence during replication stress. One conceptual model involves remodeling of a replication fork so that newly synthesized strands can interact, allowing a blocked primer end to use a sister-derived template. Other models emphasize repair of daughter-strand gaps through homologous DNA transactions behind the fork. The detailed intermediates depend on the organism and the context. It would therefore be misleading to state that every template-switch event requires one particular fork shape. The shared feature is that synthesis obtains information from a relatively undamaged DNA template instead of forcing a polymerase to accommodate the original lesion.

Template switching is sometimes described as an error-free branch of damage tolerance. The description expresses the advantage of obtaining nucleotide information from an undamaged and appropriately homologous template. It does not mean that the pathway can never create a genetic alteration. Incorrect alignment within repeated sequences or abnormal processing of DNA intermediates may have genetic consequences. A faithful sequence-copying event can also fail to eliminate the original lesion. A distinction must be maintained between preservation of sequence accuracy in the newly synthesized molecule and removal of the chemical abnormality in the parental molecule.

TLS and template switching have different immediate substrates at the critical copying step. TLS works directly on a damaged template. Template switching avoids copying the lesion by obtaining the corresponding information elsewhere. Both may produce a completed daughter strand, and both can reduce the consequences of replication arrest. A measurement of daughter-strand completion alone may therefore fail to distinguish them. If a researcher can introduce a donor-specific marker or selectively alter the availability of an alternative template, the results can become more informative about how the new strand was produced.

Template switching should also be distinguished from homologous repair of a DSB. Both processes may employ homologous DNA interactions and transfer information from an alternative template. However, their immediate structural problems differ. In a typical tolerance event, a lesion blocks synthesis while parental DNA continuity can remain intact; the objective is to complete the daughter strand. In conventional DSB repair, both original backbones are interrupted and the objective is to restore chromosome continuity and sequence. An event may progress from one problem to the other if a stalled fork collapses. The classification should reflect the substrate at the stage under discussion, not merely the presence of a recombination protein.

## 16. Regulation of damage tolerance in eukaryotic cells

The proliferation cell nuclear antigen, usually called PCNA, is a sliding clamp that interacts with DNA and multiple DNA-metabolizing proteins. During ordinary replication it helps maintain productive association between polymerase and primer–template DNA. Its importance extends beyond processivity because it also provides an interaction platform for proteins that act when the replication machinery encounters difficulties. A change in PCNA-associated proteins can change what reaction is favored at the DNA without requiring the cell to dismantle every component of the replication apparatus.

The attachment of ubiquitin to PCNA provides a standard example of regulation by post-translational modification. In well-studied eukaryotic systems, the RAD6–RAD18 ubiquitination machinery promotes addition of a single ubiquitin to a conserved lysine in PCNA, commonly designated Lys164. This modification can facilitate recruitment or retention of specialized TLS polymerases that recognize PCNA-associated signals. Ubiquitination in this setting is not equivalent to marking PCNA for immediate destruction by the proteasome. Ubiquitin is a versatile regulatory signal, and the consequence depends on the substrate, the type of modification and the proteins that recognize it.

Extension of the ubiquitin modification into certain chains, classically associated with Lys63-linked ubiquitin chains in the budding yeast model, is associated with promotion of template-switching responses. The two forms of modification provide a useful introductory framework for understanding how protein modifications influence the choice of damage tolerance routes. Mammalian pathway regulation is more complex, and the relationship is not an absolute binary switch in every context. A student should understand the association of monoubiquitination with TLS and certain chain extensions with template-switching pathways without assuming that a single modification uniquely determines every event.

Why should the cell regulate specialized polymerases through access rather than synthesize every polymerase anew whenever replication stalls? Rapid control at the DNA can alter enzyme selection without waiting for a large change in transcription and translation. The specialized enzyme need not replace all normal replicative polymerases throughout the nucleus. It can be favored at particular replication-associated structures. This arrangement helps limit the potential mutational cost of low-fidelity synthesis on ordinary DNA while retaining access to bypass activities at damaged sites.

A mutation that prevents a particular regulatory modification can have a phenotype even when the specialized polymerase retains normal activity in a purified biochemical assay. The enzyme's catalytic capacity is not the same as its probability of reaching the appropriate substrate in a cell. Conversely, overexpression of a specialized polymerase might partially compensate for a recruitment defect under some conditions without repairing the regulatory mechanism itself. Such compensation should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that the regulator and polymerase perform identical reactions.

A hypothetical experiment could compare wild-type cells, a cell line with a PCNA modification-site mutation, and a line defective in POLH. Each population could be examined for normal replication, synthesis after UV exposure and persistence of photoproducts. If the two mutants both show defective synthesis after UV while displaying similar photoproduct removal to wild type, the data would be consistent with defects affecting tolerance. Yet the mechanisms could differ: one may impair regulation of several tolerance pathways, whereas the other impairs a particular lesion-bypass activity. The three measurements together provide more information than a single survival value.

This example links damage tolerance to concepts studied elsewhere in the course. Protein modifications can act as signals that alter interactions, rather than merely increasing or decreasing enzyme abundance. Enzymes with different substrate preferences can cooperate through temporary association with a common molecular platform. Cellular regulation can therefore determine pathway output even when the underlying catalytic reactions are unchanged. The same principles recur in signal transduction, cell-cycle regulation and metabolic control, although the specific molecular components differ.

## 17. The bacterial SOS response: evidence and regulatory logic

The bacterial SOS response is a well-studied example of inducible regulation during DNA damage. In E. coli, this response coordinates expression of genes involved in DNA repair, replication-associated recovery, cell-division control and damage tolerance. It is not a single DNA repair enzyme or a single lesion-removal reaction. It is a regulatory program that changes the cellular availability of multiple activities in response to signals associated with DNA damage.

An important historical observation was that DNA damage could induce cellular changes extending beyond the immediate chemical alteration of DNA. Studies of bacterial responses to ultraviolet radiation, mutagenesis and prophage induction helped establish the idea that damaged DNA could trigger a regulated response. Genetic studies involving recA and lexA were particularly informative because alterations of these genes changed whether inducible functions were expressed. The modern understanding distinguishes RecA's role in DNA transactions from its role in stimulating the regulatory event that relieves repression of SOS genes.

Under ordinary growth conditions, LexA binds operator regions associated with numerous SOS-regulated genes and limits their transcription. Repression does not necessarily mean that expression is zero; basal levels vary among genes. When DNA synthesis encounters damage or other circumstances generate persistent single-stranded DNA, RecA can assemble on the exposed strand in an ATP-associated active state. This RecA-containing nucleoprotein structure stimulates the self-cleavage reaction of LexA. Cleavage reduces the concentration of functional repressor available to occupy its operators, allowing increased transcription of the regulated genes.

It is important to describe the cleavage reaction accurately. RecA is commonly referred to as a coprotease in this regulatory context because it promotes the self-cleavage of LexA. LexA supplies the catalytic machinery for its own cleavage. RecA does not simply act as an ordinary protease that indiscriminately cuts unrelated proteins. The dependence on DNA-associated RecA helps couple regulatory changes to the presence of an appropriate DNA signal. Without the signal, LexA can remain functional and maintain repression of its target genes.

The regulated genes do not all have identical functions. Some contribute to the removal of damaged DNA, some participate in recombinational recovery, and some support tolerance of remaining lesions. In E. coli, SulA can inhibit cell division by interfering with FtsZ assembly. This can give the cell additional time before attempting division with incompletely replicated or damaged chromosomes. SulA does not seal DNA breaks, remove photoproducts or synthesize replacement DNA. Its protective effect concerns coordination of cell division with genome status.

The temporal pattern of SOS induction is also informative. Different LexA-regulated operators and additional regulatory mechanisms can produce differences in the timing and extent of gene expression. Many repair-associated functions can be induced before strong expression or activation of some mutagenic bypass activities. A cell can therefore first attempt to reduce damage through repair and later increase access to tolerance routes if the problem persists. The exact time course depends on the damage, growth conditions and particular genes, so fixed universal induction times should not be memorized.

As recovery proceeds and persistent single-stranded DNA declines, the signal that favors RecA's regulatory activity diminishes. Newly synthesized LexA can again accumulate in a functional form and restore repression. This creates a reversible response with negative feedback rather than a permanent change in the genetic constitution of the bacterium. The distinction between regulated expression and mutation is important: an SOS-induced cell can transiently alter protein abundance without acquiring a permanent mutation, although some of the induced pathways can increase the probability of mutation.

A noncleavable LexA mutant illustrates the logic of the system. If the mutant repressor still binds its operators but cannot undergo the normal self-cleavage reaction, damage-associated RecA activity will not efficiently relieve repression. The genes controlled by LexA will fail to display the usual induced expression pattern, even though some constitutive repair activities remain. A different mutant with impaired RecA function may also fail to induce the response, but in addition may have defects in homologous recombination. These mutants can show similar regulatory phenotypes for distinct mechanistic reasons.

A constitutively derepressed system provides a complementary comparison. If functional LexA repression is lost, many SOS-regulated genes may be expressed even in the absence of the normal damage signal. This does not mean that every induced enzyme is automatically active, because some activities require additional molecular conditions. Nor does it mean that the DNA must be damaged at the time of measurement. A reporter under the control of a LexA-regulated promoter is a measure of regulatory state, not a direct count of DNA breaks or damaged bases. This distinction is essential when interpreting genetic reporter experiments.
