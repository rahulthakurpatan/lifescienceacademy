## Why cells exchange DNA rather than merely copy it

DNA replication preserves information by making another copy of an existing sequence. Homologous recombination uses the same basic language of complementary bases, but it changes the relationship between DNA molecules. A strand from one molecule can pair with a complementary strand in another molecule, displacing its previous partner. Once such a pairing has been established, the second molecule can serve as a template for synthesis, or the connected molecules can undergo a rearrangement of their strands. The result can be restoration of missing sequence, a new combination of inherited markers, or both.

Consider a chromosome broken through both strands at the same position. A single-strand lesion can often be repaired by reading the other strand at that position. A double-strand break is different: at the gap between the broken ends there may no longer be a continuous template on either strand. If an intact sister chromatid contains the same sequence, the broken chromosome can use information stored in that physically separate molecule. The first challenge is to find it. The second is to establish pairing without indiscriminately melting all the available DNA. The third is to copy or exchange information and then separate the two molecules safely. Homologous recombination addresses each of these challenges in a coordinated series of molecular reactions.

The word homologous in this context means that the DNA regions share corresponding sequence sufficiently similar to support the reaction. It does not require that two entire chromosomes be identical. Maternal and paternal copies of a chromosome share the same general sequence organization, but often differ at particular bases. Sister chromatids usually carry nearly identical sequences because they originate from the same replication event. A donor plasmid can also carry a region that corresponds to part of a chromosome. These situations provide different biological substrates for the same fundamental type of DNA recognition.

The repair outcome should not be confused with a genetic crossover. A break repaired using a sister chromatid may yield precisely the original sequence, so an ordinary genetic analysis might reveal no novel allele combination. Nevertheless, strand exchange and DNA synthesis have occurred. Conversely, a crossover between two nonsister chromatids can alter the linkage of markers without changing the number of copies of either marker in the complete collection of meiotic products. A molecular reaction must therefore be distinguished from the genetic signature by which it is detected.

Another distinction is between a reciprocal exchange of chromosome arms and a local replacement of sequence information. In a reciprocal crossover the distal DNA regions of two chromatids become associated with different proximal regions. In gene conversion a recipient acquires sequence corresponding to a donor without a necessarily detectable reciprocal replacement at that donor. One can observe gene conversion within a crossover product or within a noncrossover product. These are two different descriptions of genetic outcomes, not mutually exclusive labels.

Recombination has to be controlled because the same process that can rescue a broken chromosome can connect repeated sequences at incorrect genomic positions. Accurate pairing with a sister chromatid tends to preserve the organization of the genome. Pairing with a dispersed repeat can instead produce a deletion, inversion or exchange between chromosomes. The important biological problem is not simply to maximize strand exchange, but to accomplish an appropriate exchange and complete it in a way that preserves functional chromosomes. This balance between flexibility and stability will explain many of the phenotypes considered later.

## DNA substrates, homology and strand identity

The double helix is made of two antiparallel polynucleotide chains. At each ordinary base-paired position, one strand contains a base complementary to the base on the other. Adenine ordinarily pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine. The two backbone directions are opposite: if one chain is followed from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end, its partner runs three-prime to five-prime in the same spatial direction. Keeping track of this geometry is indispensable when predicting recombination products.

Suppose that the top strand of a donor duplex reads 5′-AGTCCGTA-3′ from left to right. The lower strand reads 3′-TCAGGCAT-5′ in that same drawing. A free strand identical to the top strand cannot form an ordinary Watson–Crick duplex with the donor top strand, but can pair with the donor lower strand. If the free strand enters the double helix, the original top strand becomes locally displaced. The invading strand and displaced strand have corresponding sequences and polarity, while the invading strand and its new pairing partner have complementary sequences and opposite polarity. A student who understands these distinctions can identify which of the strands a polymerase will use as template later.

A short stretch of identity between DNA molecules is not equivalent to a complete biological recognition signal. In homologous recombination the productive pairing generally extends across a region of sequence correspondence. Stable strand exchange is influenced by the length of compatible sequence, the number and arrangement of differences, the structure and accessibility of the substrates, and the proteins present. There is no single universally applicable minimum length that describes every RecA-family reaction and every organism. A simplified exam problem may specify a threshold; such a threshold should be used as a condition of that problem rather than memorized as a universal law.

Distinguish the physical strands from the sequence information they carry. During the reaction a parental strand can remain covalently intact while it changes its base-pairing partner. The new duplex can contain one strand originating from each parent. If the two parents differ at a particular nucleotide, the new duplex can contain a mismatch at that position. This mixed-origin base-paired region is called heteroduplex DNA. Even where the sequences match perfectly, a heteroduplex can exist in the physical sense: the two strands have different molecular origins. A visible mismatch is a possible consequence, not a requirement for formation of a heteroduplex.

A nick is a discontinuity in the phosphodiester backbone of one strand, without necessarily losing a nucleotide or breaking the opposing strand. A single-stranded gap is a region in which nucleotides are missing from one strand, leaving its complement exposed. A double-strand break interrupts both backbones. These are chemically different structures, and they are not interchangeable labels in diagrams. The original Holliday model begins with a pair of nicks at corresponding positions in homologous duplexes. A commonly used contemporary framework begins instead with a double-strand break in one molecule, processed to expose single-stranded tails. Both models explain strand exchange but do not prescribe the same initial lesion.

A free three-prime hydroxyl group is useful for a separate chemical reason. DNA polymerase adds an incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate to this group. When the three-prime end of an invading strand is appropriately paired to a donor template, the end can prime DNA synthesis. A five-prime single-stranded tail may have sequence similarity and may participate in some DNA interactions, but it cannot simply substitute for a three-prime primer terminus in the standard extension reaction. The distinction is about direction of covalent synthesis, not just which end is drawn on the right.

The words homologous chromosome and homologous DNA also need to be separated. In a diploid organism homologous chromosomes are the corresponding members of a chromosome pair. Homologous DNA regions may occur on sister chromatids, on homologous chromosomes, or at duplicated positions that are not allelic. A repeated segment on a different chromosome can be homologous as a DNA sequence without making those chromosomes a normal homologous pair. This fact underlies the risk of nonallelic homologous recombination. A question about a crossover must specify whether the substrates are sisters, homologs or repeated regions, because their exchange can have different genetic consequences.

## The genetic observations that demanded a molecular explanation

The classical picture of recombination was developed before the enzymes responsible for strand exchange had been identified. Genetic crosses demonstrated that markers located on the same chromosome could be inherited in new combinations. A reciprocal exchange between homologous chromatids accounted for many such observations. However, studies of fungi revealed segregation patterns that could not be accounted for merely by exchanging the chromosome segments on either side of a locus.

Fungal meiosis is particularly useful for this question because the products of a single meiotic event can often be recovered together. Instead of averaging alleles across thousands of unrelated offspring, the investigator can ask whether the products of an individual meiosis retain the expected allele balance. For a diploid heterozygous for A and a, ordinary replication followed by meiosis produces four chromatids in which two carry A and two carry a, provided that no conversion or mutation changes the allele state. A reciprocal crossover changes associations between neighboring markers but does not by itself change this two-to-two inventory at the locus.

Occasionally the products contain three copies of one allele and one of the other. This observation is called non-Mendelian segregation in the context of a single meiotic tetrad. It does not require violation of the chromosomal separation mechanism. If one chromatid has acquired the alternative sequence during recombination, the chromatids can separate normally yet carry a changed inventory of alleles. The scientific challenge was to connect a local, nonreciprocal genetic event to a molecular process that could also explain reciprocal exchange.

In 1964 Robin Holliday proposed a mechanism for gene conversion in fungi. The proposal connected the genetic data to the double-helical structure of DNA. A strand from each homolog could exchange its pairing relationship with the other duplex, generating a joint intermediate. As the exchanged region extended, strands from different parental DNA molecules could pair across an allelic difference. Correction of a mismatch in such a region could restore the previous allele or convert the allele state. A second choice, involving separation of the joint DNA structure, could determine whether distal markers were exchanged. Thus one model connected the local sequence event to the larger chromosome-level event.

The model was valuable because it offered a structural explanation of gene conversion without requiring an entire new round of chromosomal replication. It was also experimentally useful: a DNA junction with four duplex arms was a prediction that could be investigated using model substrates, structural analysis and proteins acting on branched DNA. The later discovery that enzymes can recognize, migrate and cleave these junctions provided a biochemical basis for the kind of intermediate that the genetic model required. The history illustrates how a genetic observation can motivate a molecular structure, which can in turn be tested.

The original model is not the only path to a homologous recombinant. In 1983 Szostak, Orr-Weaver, Rothstein and Stahl proposed a double-strand-break repair model of recombination. In that framework a break in one chromatid is followed by processing of its ends and invasion of an intact homologous duplex. The intact donor provides information for repair synthesis. The resulting intermediates can include two Holliday junctions rather than the single junction of the classical model. This framework explains why missing DNA can be restored through recombination and also connects recombination more directly to DNA damage repair.

Later genetic and molecular observations showed that many noncrossovers are generated without resolution of a stable double Holliday junction. A newly extended invading strand can be displaced and anneal to the other broken end. This pathway is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing. The lesson is not that the 1964 model has become irrelevant. Rather, the four-way junction and heteroduplex concepts remain foundational, while the initiating structures and routes to final products must be distinguished. The appropriate model is selected according to the experimental evidence and the biological context.

The two models make separable predictions. If a system requires resection of a double-strand break, a defect in end processing may reduce repair even if a junction-cleaving enzyme remains functional. If a single junction is supplied directly as a synthetic substrate, end processing is unnecessary for testing whether that particular junction can be resolved. A well-designed experiment separates the step being tested from upstream events. This is why the existence of a Holliday junction in a biochemical reaction does not establish that every recombination reaction starts with symmetrical nicks.

## Creating a recombination-competent DNA end

A fresh double-strand break can have many different end structures. Two strands may end opposite one another, or one strand may protrude. Some broken ends carry chemical modifications that must be removed before they can participate in ordinary DNA synthesis or ligation. The recombination pathway commonly converts these heterogeneous starting structures into a simpler functional substrate: single-stranded DNA with an available three-prime end. This preparation is known as end resection.

In a standard double-strand-break repair model, the strands that terminate in five-prime ends at the break are preferentially removed. The corresponding three-prime-ended strands become exposed. Consider a horizontal DNA duplex with the upper strand running five-prime to three-prime from left to right. At the break, the left duplex has an upper three-prime end and a lower five-prime end. The right duplex has an upper five-prime end and a lower three-prime end. Removal of the lower strand from the left end and the upper strand from the right end therefore exposes two tails of opposite spatial orientation, but both tails terminate in three-prime ends at the original break region. The two ends are chemically equivalent in the capacity to prime synthesis.

Resection is often described as net five-prime to three-prime degradation of the DNA strand that must be removed. This is a description of the net result and not a claim that every individual enzyme involved has the same catalytic polarity. For example, in eukaryotic cells several nuclease and helicase activities cooperate to produce the final three-prime single-stranded substrate. An endonucleolytic incision away from a blocked end can be followed by processing in different directions. At the examination level the important result is which strand remains available and what information that strand can recover.

In Escherichia coli the RecBCD complex is a major route for processing double-stranded DNA ends. It combines DNA unwinding and nuclease functions, and its activity is regulated by encounter with an appropriately oriented Chi sequence. The complex alters its processing behavior and promotes RecA loading onto the recombination-competent single-stranded product. The Chi response is important because it connects a bacterial DNA-end processing enzyme to the downstream homology-search reaction. Chi is not a universally conserved origin of homologous recombination or a substitute for the homologous region in the donor. Other bacteria can use other end-processing systems.

Single-stranded gaps can also be recombination substrates. In E. coli, RecFOR pathway proteins promote productive RecA assembly at appropriate single-stranded gaps, in the presence of single-strand binding protein. A gap does not necessarily need to be converted to a two-ended break before homology-dependent repair can occur. A useful exam comparison is that RecBCD is associated with double-strand end processing, whereas RecFOR is associated especially with handling gaps and facilitating RecA loading. Both routes converge on a recombinase filament, rather than each catalyzing an entirely unrelated form of strand exchange.

In eukaryotes, the MRN complex in mammals, corresponding to MRX in budding yeast, contributes to initiation of resection with CtIP or its yeast counterpart Sae2. Further processing involves nucleases and helicase-associated activities, including EXO1 and DNA2-containing pathways in suitable systems. These names are less useful than understanding their order: end recognition and processing first, then protection of the resulting single-stranded DNA, then assembly of a strand-exchange filament. The recombinase does not by itself perform all of the preparatory processing.

An exposed single-stranded region is vulnerable to nucleases and inappropriate base pairing with itself. Bacterial SSB and eukaryotic RPA bind single-stranded DNA, reduce secondary structure and protect the substrate. However, because these proteins occupy the DNA, the recombinase has to be loaded in a regulated fashion. In vertebrates BRCA2 facilitates assembly of RAD51 on suitable single-stranded DNA. Without the appropriate mediator, abundant RAD51 protein may not be sufficient to restore an efficient strand-exchange reaction. The quantity of an enzyme and its productive localization are separate experimental variables.

End processing also influences competition between repair pathways. Conventional end joining works with broken ends that can be brought together and processed for ligation. Extensive resection changes the substrate into one better suited to homology-dependent reactions. In many eukaryotic somatic cells, cell-cycle-dependent regulation makes extensive resection more favorable after replication has begun, when a sister chromatid becomes available. This does not mean that every break in S phase undergoes homologous recombination or that every G1 break must use the same alternative pathway. The timing of damage, end structure, local context and availability of a donor all influence the result.

Predict the phenotype of an end-processing mutant by asking which substrate it fails to produce. A cell may detect a break but accumulate poorly resected ends. If the recombinase is functional, supplying it with an already prepared single-stranded substrate in a biochemical assay could reveal normal strand pairing. By contrast, an inactive recombinase would fail to pair even a properly processed substrate. This distinction prevents the common inference that every recombination-deficient mutation directly destroys strand-exchange catalysis.

## RecA and RAD51: building a filament for a homology search

A DNA molecule in a cell is surrounded by vast amounts of unrelated DNA. A free single strand cannot efficiently inspect this material by simply waiting for every potential donor helix to melt. RecA in bacteria and RAD51 in eukaryotes solve this problem by organizing the invading DNA into a nucleoprotein filament. The DNA is associated with many recombinase subunits in an extended arrangement that supports the recognition of compatible sequence in a duplex. The protein–DNA complex is the active search unit.

ATP binding favors the functional DNA-binding state of RecA-family recombinases. RecA and RAD51 are ATPases, meaning that they can catalyze hydrolysis of ATP. Their nucleotide states influence filament stability, assembly, turnover and the dynamics of the reaction. It is misleading to assert that hydrolysis supplies a fixed amount of energy for each complementary base pair formed. The contribution of nucleotide binding and hydrolysis varies among particular recombinases and reactions. In simplified biochemical systems, early pairing or limited exchange can occur with conditions that restrict hydrolysis, while turnover and extended processing depend strongly on nucleotide cycling.

The first major stage is called presynapsis. It includes production of single-stranded DNA, its binding by protective proteins, and assembly of the recombinase filament. Synapsis is the productive encounter of the filament with a donor duplex and establishment of matching base-pairing relationships. Postsynapsis includes the reactions after that pairing, such as repair synthesis, exchange extension, processing of joint molecules and removal of the recombinase. These stages are functional categories. Their boundaries may overlap in the actual molecular system, but they help assign the defect of a mutant to a specific part of the pathway.

During the homology search, the nucleoprotein filament makes transient encounters with duplex DNA. A nonmatching encounter is unlikely to progress to a stable extended pairing. A sufficiently compatible region allows the invading strand to form new base pairs with the complementary donor strand. As the interaction extends, the original donor partner is displaced from that region. The process differs from recognition of a short enzyme binding motif because the identity of the target depends on the sequence of the invading DNA itself. If the invading sequence changes, the preferred homologous target may change even though the recombinase protein is unchanged.

An ATP-binding-defective mutant can fail because it does not establish the active filament architecture. A mutant that binds nucleotide and DNA but hydrolyzes ATP inefficiently can instead form unusually persistent complexes. Such a mutant need not exhibit precisely the same phenotype as an ATP-binding mutant. A static observation of DNA-associated protein is therefore insufficient to establish efficient exchange. One must also examine the progression from binding to pairing and the production of completed recombinant molecules.

The eukaryotic recombinase DMC1 is a related protein used in meiosis in many organisms, where it helps promote interactions between homologous chromosomes. RAD51 has central roles in mitotic repair and also participates in meiotic recombination, with the relative contributions depending on organism and meiotic context. Do not equate RecA with DMC1 in every biological setting, or infer that RAD51 exists only in mitosis. What is conserved is the general principle of recombinase-mediated DNA pairing and exchange, while the control of donor choice and participation of associated proteins differs.

Classic purified-protein experiments helped demonstrate that RecA directly participates in homologous pairing. Shibata and colleagues reported pairing of single-stranded DNA with homologous superhelical duplex DNA using purified E. coli RecA in 1979. Reconstitution was particularly informative because it reduced the number of cellular components needed for the measured activity. The observation supported a direct biochemical role rather than a merely indirect regulatory association. It did not imply that purified RecA alone completes all chromosome repair in a living cell.

When designing an interpretation of such an experiment, compare homologous and unrelated donor sequences under otherwise matching conditions, include a reaction without recombinase and distinguish DNA association from true base-paired product formation. The topology of donor DNA can influence the ease of invasion, so equivalent substrates should be used when possible. Measuring a stable joint molecule rather than total protein-bound DNA makes the readout more informative. A mutation that eliminates protein–DNA binding would affect both measurements, whereas one that binds DNA but fails to recognize homology could preserve the first and eliminate the second.
