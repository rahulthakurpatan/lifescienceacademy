## Strand invasion and the displacement loop

The term strand invasion describes a change in the identity of the strands paired within a donor DNA molecule. Consider a single-stranded tail protruding from a broken recipient duplex. The tail carries sequence that also occurs in the intact donor. Its recombinase filament interacts with the donor duplex, and the complementary strand of the donor pairs locally with the recipient tail. The other donor strand must become unpaired from its original partner over the invaded interval. That displaced strand is not necessarily cut. It can remain covalently continuous with the rest of the donor chromosome.

The resulting three-stranded structure is often called a displacement loop, or D-loop. It is important to interpret three-stranded correctly: the invading strand is not forming three conventional Watson–Crick bonds at each position. In the established heteroduplex portion two strands are paired, and the third is displaced. There can be additional transient contacts during recombinase-mediated recognition, but the stable pairing product is understood in terms of an ordinary complementary duplex and a locally unpaired strand. The invading strand often extends from a recipient double-stranded region, and the remainder of the donor molecule is still double-stranded on both sides.

The geometry can be reconstructed using a short sequence. Suppose a recipient tail ends in 5′-GACTTA-3′, with its three-prime end available for extension. A donor contains the corresponding top-strand sequence 5′-GACTTAGC-3′ and its bottom-strand complement 3′-CTGAATCG-5′. If the recipient invades the donor, its last adenine pairs with the thymine on the donor bottom strand. The next template base in the direction of synthesis is cytosine, so the first newly incorporated nucleotide will be guanine. The following template base is guanine, so the next incorporated nucleotide will be cytosine. The recipient strand has gained the sequence GC by copying the donor, without requiring transfer of a donor strand segment into the final recipient DNA.

This distinction between copying information and transferring a covalent segment is essential. A diagram of newly synthesized DNA should use a separate symbol for new nucleotides instead of implying that every donor-colored tract consists of original donor molecules. Newly synthesized DNA has the sequence dictated by the donor template, but its nucleotides come from the cellular nucleotide pool. A genetic assay does not generally distinguish those molecular origins. A label attached to the original donor strands, by contrast, could in principle provide additional information about physical strand inheritance.

The topological state of the donor can affect strand invasion. Negative supercoiling tends to favor local unwinding in many duplex contexts, because a portion of the torsional strain can be relieved by opening the DNA. A relaxed or positively supercoiled molecule may respond differently to the same reaction. This is one reason classical in vitro pairing experiments often use a defined circular donor. However, homologous recombination in cells is not restricted to negatively supercoiled naked DNA circles. Chromatin organization, associated proteins and topological enzymes influence access to actual cellular substrates.

A D-loop is a joint molecule, but it is not automatically a classical four-way Holliday junction. A three-stranded invasion intermediate can be extended by polymerase, can be reversed, or can participate in additional strand interactions that eventually produce a four-arm junction. The distinction matters in a question that supplies an electron micrograph or a drawing of connected DNA molecules. The number of lines drawn across one another on the page is not sufficient to determine whether a genuine covalent junction is present. One needs to know how strands continue through the central region and which are paired.

Following invasion, repair synthesis requires an extendable three-prime end and a functional polymerase. If the invading end carries a terminal mismatch, extension may be impaired even though an upstream section of the tail is successfully paired. If it lacks the necessary hydroxyl group, the usual polymerase reaction cannot proceed until the end is repaired. Thus a positive strand-pairing measurement need not predict successful DNA restoration. A mechanistic assay can detect the D-loop independently of nucleotide incorporation and thereby distinguish the defect.

After extension, the new strand may remain associated with the donor long enough to capture the other broken end, or it may dissociate and pair with a complementary strand from the recipient. Different choices can lead to different intermediate structures even when the same donor and the same initial break are used. Strand invasion is therefore a shared starting operation for several repair outcomes, not a guarantee that a crossover will occur.

## Reconstructing the original single Holliday junction model

The original Holliday model is best learned by tracing individual backbones rather than memorizing a drawing. Begin with two homologous double helices aligned in the same sequence orientation. Label their top strands P and Q, and their complementary bottom strands p and q. Both upper strands run in the same spatial direction from five-prime to three-prime, while both lower strands run in the opposite direction. The labels record molecular origin; they do not mean that the four strands carry entirely different sequences.

The historical scheme begins when equivalent strands of both duplexes acquire nicks at corresponding positions. For example, each upper strand can be nicked at the same aligned coordinate. Each nick provides a discontinuity between a three-prime hydroxyl and a five-prime end, under the simple chemical assumptions of the model. The uncut lower strands remain continuous. The corresponding upper strand segments can exchange pairing partners, such that a segment originating in one parent is now paired with the complementary strand of the other parent. At the crossover position the relevant ends can be covalently joined.

DNA ligation closes the nick after the exchange of partners. The two molecules are then connected through a structure in which four duplex arms converge at a central strand crossover. The unbroken strands still record their original continuity, while the newly rejoined strands traverse the junction from one parental region to another. Because of these connections, the molecules cannot be interpreted as simply two independent duplexes touching one another. The junction is a real DNA intermediate.

This model does not imply that two DNA helices can freely pass through one another without changing their chemical connections. The strand exchange is made possible by cleavage and joining in the proposed mechanism. A line that crosses another in a textbook illustration could otherwise represent one strand passing over another in three-dimensional space without any covalent connection. To avoid the ambiguity, diagrams should label strands and use explicit joins or gaps where bonds are formed or cut.

The classical model accounts for heteroduplex formation because exchanged strands can form new duplexes over an interval. If corresponding alleles are identical across that interval, the new base pairs are normal even though their strands have different origins. If an A:T pair in one parental duplex corresponds to a G:C pair in the other, mixed pairing can yield A:C and G:T mismatches depending on which strands have exchanged. Such mismatches can subsequently be corrected. In this way the single structural intermediate offers an explanation for nonreciprocal sequence changes.

Branch migration is a movement of the junction along homologous DNA. As the crossover position moves, some base pairs in the original duplex arrangements are replaced by base pairs between strands of different parental origins. The length and location of the heteroduplex region can therefore change. This migration does not require cutting the phosphodiester backbone at every nucleotide position. Instead, it primarily involves breaking and reforming base-pairing interactions as the junction moves, with the strands maintaining the continuity established by the earlier exchange.

A junction can be represented in an open square arrangement, with four duplex arms extending from its center. Under some conditions, DNA arms stack to produce a more compact crossed arrangement. Different drawings can correspond to the same covalent structure in different conformations. Rotating one part of a model may change which lines look horizontal or vertical without changing the molecular outcome of a particular cleavage. This is why an instruction such as cut horizontally to make a crossover is unsafe unless it is tied to a particular labelled diagram.

Two distinct pairs of strands can be cleaved in the simple single-junction model. The cleavage positions and the manner in which the resulting nicks are joined determine which parental left and right regions are connected in the final duplexes. One arrangement restores the original flanking-marker association, producing a noncrossover. The other exchanges the association of the flanks, producing a crossover. Both products can retain a local heteroduplex region. The two cleavage choices are geometric possibilities, not evidence that a real enzyme chooses each with equal probability.

The symmetric-nick model is historically important because it unifies crossover and conversion in a simple structural scheme. It is not a universal description of how recombination begins in cells. A modern diagram of repair following a double-strand break can produce a single joint structure or a double Holliday junction by a different sequence of operations. Answering an exam question correctly requires recognizing which model the question has specified. If the question states that the DNA begins with paired single-strand nicks, use the classical scheme; if it begins with a break and resection, follow the donor-template repair model.

## Branch migration: movement of a junction without movement of a gene

Branch migration changes the location at which strands pass between the two duplexes. Imagine that two homologous duplexes have a junction at coordinate zero, and that they share matching sequence for fifty bases to the right. If the junction moves ten bases to the right, the region over which the parental strands are paired with new partners changes accordingly. No gene has physically traveled ten base pairs along its covalent backbone. The backbone sequence remains ordered; the boundaries between different pairing relationships have moved.

The distinction is similar to changing the contact surface between two complementary strips rather than moving the printed characters on either strip. This analogy is useful only for the base-pairing aspect. A real DNA junction has covalent connections, helical twist and constraints on its three-dimensional motion that a flat strip does not reproduce. The actual branch point can move in either direction depending on the substrate and the forces acting on it.

A perfectly homologous tract can permit spontaneous branch migration through thermal fluctuations. At a particular step, existing base pairs open and new ones form. When both alternative pairing states have comparable stability, there may be little free-energy bias for movement in one direction. The position can therefore fluctuate rather than travel steadily. This explains why a motor protein can be biologically valuable even though the underlying base-pair rearrangement is chemically possible without a continuous energy input.

Heterology, such as a nonmatching nucleotide or a larger insertion, can impede migration by making an alternative paired state less favorable or geometrically difficult. A mismatch is not always an absolute barrier. The size and nature of the difference, surrounding sequence, DNA structure and motor activity determine whether migration can proceed. At the level of a simplified model a block may be treated as an impenetrable barrier if explicitly stated. In a biological interpretation it is preferable to predict a reduced probability or altered distribution of migration rather than a universal stop at every mismatch.

In E. coli RuvA and RuvB provide a well-characterized system for active branch migration. RuvA recognizes the four-way junction and helps organize the DNA arms into a suitable configuration. RuvB is an ATP-dependent motor that associates with the DNA and drives translocation through the complex. As DNA passes through the organized junction, old pairing partners are replaced by new ones. RuvB functions in oligomeric rings in the characterized bacterial system. The directionality and rate depend on the way the motor complex is assembled and on the substrate.

RuvA is often described as a junction-recognition protein and RuvB as a branch migration motor. These assignments are more informative than treating both as generic helicases. RuvA does not serve as the principal ATP-driven translocase in this system, and RuvB does not provide the canonical resolving endonuclease activity. A defect in RuvA can prevent efficient targeting or organization of the motor even if RuvB retains ATPase activity in isolation. A defect in RuvB can preserve recognition of a junction while preventing efficient ATP-driven movement.

The RecG protein in E. coli is another DNA translocase involved in processing branched DNA structures. Its existence illustrates that RuvAB is not the only route by which branched intermediates can be remodeled. The functions of these systems overlap in some contexts but are not identical for every substrate. For a standard examination comparison, RuvAB is the model junction-migration system, RuvC is the model junction resolvase, and RecA is the principal strand-exchange recombinase. Other organisms need not use the same full set of bacterial proteins.

Consider two assays using junction substrates. In one assay a branch point is initially positioned at an engineered location and a readout reports whether migration reaches a downstream position. In another assay the branch point is fixed by sequence differences and cleavage products are measured. A protein deficient in ATP-dependent migration could show reduced activity in the first assay yet permit normal cleavage in the second, provided the resolvase is functional and the substrate is suitable. A cleavage-defective resolvase would have a different pattern. Such orthogonal assays reveal the order of molecular steps more clearly than survival alone.

The biological function of branch migration is not limited to extending the heteroduplex to its maximum possible length. Movement can reposition the junction relative to available cleavage sites, remodel intermediate structures or help particular routes of intermediate removal. Excessive or misdirected processing could encounter sequence differences or influence which markers lie in the final heteroduplex tract. A crossover position measured genetically therefore does not necessarily reveal the initial position of strand invasion or the exact original break site.

A quantitative analogy makes the difference between thermal and motor-driven movement clearer. In an idealized one-dimensional random walk, each step moves a junction one base pair left or right with equal probability and the steps are independent. After n steps, the mean signed displacement is zero, but the root mean squared displacement is the square root of n base pairs. After one hundred steps that characteristic displacement is ten base pairs, not one hundred. A hypothetical motor moving consistently in one direction at a defined step rate would show a much larger net displacement in the same number of steps. Real branch migration has sequence-dependent energies, boundaries and protein interactions, so the random-walk relationship is an educational model rather than a measured universal law.

## Resolving a four-way junction and predicting the products

Formation of a joint molecule is not sufficient to restore two separate chromosomes. The covalent connectivity of the intermediate has to be changed or removed. In the classical single-junction scheme this is accomplished by resolution: a structure-specific nuclease introduces a pair of appropriately positioned cuts, and the resulting nicked duplex products can be sealed. A conventional resolvase does not merely dissociate proteins from DNA. It changes the continuity of selected DNA strands.

The bacterial RuvC protein is a model Holliday junction resolvase. It recognizes the junction structure and introduces related incisions in two strands. The geometry of the cuts allows separation into two duplex molecules containing ligatable nicks, assuming the usual end chemistry. DNA ligase subsequently restores backbone continuity. RuvC can exhibit preferences for sequences at which cleavage occurs, which illustrates that recognition of the DNA structure and selection of a particular cleavage position are distinct features. RuvA and RuvB can cooperate with RuvC in processing the intermediate.

An important experimental advance was the use of synthetic four-way junctions as substrates. Because the four strands and their sequences could be specified in advance, investigators could distinguish a nuclease that recognized the branch structure from one that simply degraded duplex DNA. Appropriate comparisons include an ordinary duplex, a junction substrate, a reaction without the candidate enzyme and products analyzed under conditions that reveal the lengths of cut strands. A preference for branched substrates together with paired cuts at related positions supports the interpretation of a junction-resolving activity. It does not by itself establish how frequently that route is used in living cells.

To predict products without depending on the orientation of a drawing, assign the left flanks of the two parental duplexes the labels L1 and L2, and their right flanks R1 and R2. The initial duplexes are L1 joined to R1 and L2 joined to R2. A noncrossover product pair retains those left-right associations. A reciprocal crossover product pair consists instead of L1 joined to R2 and L2 joined to R1. A heteroduplex near the original pairing region can occur in either case. The flanking labels should be far enough from the locally exchanged tract that mismatch correction at those markers is excluded in the question.

For a concrete genetic example, let one parental chromatid carry markers A and B, and the other a and b, with a recombination region between the markers. Before exchange the two combinations are AB and ab. A noncrossover preserves AB and ab, apart from any changes within the intervening tract. A reciprocal crossover produces Ab and aB. In a normal meiosis involving four chromatids, a single crossover between two nonsister chromatids affects only the participating two; the other two can retain the parental combinations. Counting the products requires specifying whether the denominator is all four chromatids or just the two participating ones.

A local conversion is different. If an internal marker C becomes c through repair of a mismatch, the flanking labels could still remain A and B in the converted chromatid. Alternatively, the same local conversion could accompany a crossover that places A next to b. The occurrence of a local change cannot establish the linkage of distal markers. In experimental genetics, placing distinguishable markers on both sides of the recombination region enables the two outcomes to be scored separately.

Consider a hypothetical culture in which one hundred independent two-ended repair events are scored with informative flanking markers. If seventy are noncrossovers and thirty are crossovers, the crossover fraction among the scored events is 0.30. It does not follow that thirty percent of all chromatids in a population are recombinant, because not all chromatids need have undergone a scored event. Nor does it imply that a particular resolvase always cuts in one orientation thirty percent of the time: some events may have followed a route that never created a junction. The observation is a distribution of final outcomes, not a direct measurement of one enzyme's microscopic cleavage preference.

Failure to resolve a junction can impair chromosome segregation even if much of the original damage has been repaired. Two physically connected DNA molecules cannot always separate freely during cell division. Unresolved joint molecules may therefore accumulate in cells defective in late-stage processing. Such accumulation is conceptually different from a failure of strand invasion, which may cause broken or resected DNA to persist without forming the same junction population. Time-resolved measurements of DNA structures can discriminate these phenotypes.

Eukaryotes possess several pathways for processing junction-like intermediates. Structure-selective nucleases can generate crossover or noncrossover products depending on the intermediate and cleavage geometry. In contrast, the dissolution of a double Holliday junction by coordinated migration and topological unlinking gives a noncrossover. These processes should not be collapsed into a generic final step simply called ligation. The important distinction is whether strands are cut and rejoined to separate the molecules or whether the joint topology is eliminated without classical paired junction cleavage.

A common diagrammatic trap arises when the same four-way junction is redrawn after rotation. A pair of strands that appeared vertical can now appear diagonal or horizontal. The resulting crossover classification must not change because a drawing was rotated. Trace the covalent path from L1 through the rejoined central region and identify whether it reaches R1 or R2. Then trace L2 independently. If one final duplex connects L1 to R2, the reciprocal partner in a simple balanced two-product model connects L2 to R1. This method is more reliable than memorizing the direction in which a pair of scissors is drawn.
