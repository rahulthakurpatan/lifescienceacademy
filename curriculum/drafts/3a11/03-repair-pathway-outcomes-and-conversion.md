## Double-strand-break repair: from one damaged chromatid to two junctions

The original Holliday scheme begins by modifying both participating duplexes at corresponding positions. A double-strand-break repair scheme begins with an asymmetric problem. One chromatid has lost continuity while the other remains intact. The intact chromatid contains information the broken molecule needs. In the standard two-ended repair model, the first task is to create single-stranded three-prime tails at the break; the second is to establish a donor interaction that permits copying; the third is to reconnect the two broken ends in the correct order.

Label the damaged molecule X and the intact homologous donor Y. After resection, one tail of X can invade Y and pair with the complementary strand. The remaining tail of X is not yet paired to Y. Polymerase extends the invading three-prime end using Y as a template. As the extension grows, a segment of the displaced Y strand becomes available to pair with the complementary sequence on the second tail of X. This interaction is described as second-end capture. It helps connect both sides of the original break to the same donor-associated structure.

The polarity of the two broken ends explains why second-end capture is possible. Each original end carries a three-prime terminus, but the tails belong to opposite strands of the broken chromosome. If the first invading strand copies a region of the donor, the newly synthesized tract includes sequence complementary to the corresponding strand on the other recipient end. The displacement and extension processes provide an opportunity to establish the second pairing. The result depends on compatible sequence and correct orientation; merely bringing both broken ends near a donor does not ensure the original order can be recovered.

Additional synthesis and ligation can establish a double Holliday junction intermediate in the classical double-strand-break repair model. The two junctions define a connected region between X and Y. The two molecules are joined at two distinct branch points rather than just one. Their shared structure can include heteroduplex DNA and stretches of newly synthesized DNA. The exact arrangement of mixed-origin base pairs depends on the path by which the intermediate developed and how the junctions moved.

A useful mental distinction is between repair of the gap and removal of the joint molecule. A polymerase can fill in the missing sequence while the two duplexes remain connected. Completion of synthesis therefore does not prove that the final chromosomes are independent. Conversely, removing a joint molecule before sufficient synthesis has occurred may leave a partially restored recipient that still requires processing. An experimental readout that detects recovery of an internal marker is different from a readout that detects physical separation of full-length products.

The 1983 double-strand-break model proposed that broken DNA could be enlarged into a double-stranded gap and that repair of such a gap using homologous information could account for gene conversion. A gap physically lacks some recipient sequence. If the corresponding donor carries an alternative allele, synthesis of new DNA across the missing region may directly reproduce the donor allele. This is a route to conversion that does not depend exclusively on correction of a mismatch. Heteroduplex formation around the gap can provide additional opportunities for mismatch correction.

The standard textbook image of two junctions is a model of one possible intermediate, not a promise that each break follows this route. A cell can use multiple pathways after the initial invasion. If a question states that a stable double Holliday junction was isolated, it is appropriate to analyze resolution or dissolution of that junction. If it only states that a damaged gene was repaired using a homologous donor, the presence of such an intermediate cannot be assumed. Likewise, detection of a D-loop does not establish that the second end has been captured.

The contrast between the original model and the double-strand-break model can be tested by labeling the recipient and donor separately. Suppose the recipient contains a missing segment and the donor is intact. A system that merely exchanges preexisting strand partners without any synthesis cannot restore the total number of nucleotides in the recipient unless it transfers sequence material or uses another source. A polymerase-dependent restoration of the missing region is therefore evidence for a copying step. To distinguish copying from redistribution of donor DNA, one can track the integrity of the donor as well as incorporation of new nucleotides. A conclusion about the initial lesion must also rely on a direct observation or defined starting substrate.

The biological importance of the two-ended repair model is that it explains how a chromosome can recover information from a physically separate molecule while retaining its original flanking organization. It also explains why repair can sometimes produce a crossover. The next issue is to understand which operations determine whether the two molecules emerge with their original distal associations or exchanged associations.

## Alternative routes after invasion: SDSA, resolution and dissolution

One possible outcome after repair synthesis is that the extended recipient strand leaves the donor duplex. The donor's two original strands can pair with one another again. The newly extended recipient strand then pairs with complementary sequence on the other resected end of the broken chromosome. Further synthesis where needed and ligation complete restoration of the damaged molecule. This route is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing, abbreviated SDSA.

The logic of SDSA can be understood by considering what is carried away from the donor. The extended strand contains sequence copied during its temporary association with the donor. It need not carry a distal donor chromosome arm. When it anneals to the other broken end of the recipient, the left and right recipient flanks are brought together through the recovered sequence. Consequently, the standard SDSA route yields a noncrossover with respect to distal markers. Local gene conversion remains possible because the copied interval may contain donor-specific alleles.

A noncrossover should not be mistaken for a reaction with no strand invasion. SDSA requires productive homologous pairing and synthesis before the new strand is returned to the recipient. It lacks the stable double-junction stage of the classical alternative, not the earlier strand-exchange operation. A mutation that impairs RAD51-mediated invasion can reduce SDSA just as it can reduce double-junction repair. Conversely, a factor required for a specific junction-processing pathway may leave some SDSA events relatively unaffected.

The name annealing in SDSA refers to pairing of complementary single-stranded sequences during reunion of the two sides of the broken molecule. This differs from initial strand invasion, which requires accessing a complementary strand already paired within a donor duplex. Simple annealing of two exposed single strands does not face precisely the same structural challenge as invasion of an intact double helix. The two operations can therefore use different proteins or be affected differently by experimental conditions.

Single-strand annealing, abbreviated SSA, is a different homology-dependent repair route. If a double-strand break lies between two direct repeats, resection can expose complementary repeat sequences on opposite sides of the break. Annealing of those repeats, removal of unpaired tails and restoration of continuity can eliminate the intervening segment and one repeat copy. This mechanism need not involve RAD51-mediated invasion of an intact donor duplex. Although both SSA and SDSA contain the word annealing, their substrates, dependence on donor-directed synthesis and typical genetic consequences differ. SSA is introduced here only to prevent confusion with the strand-exchange mechanisms of the node.

In the double-junction route, the two junctions can be processed by nucleases. The pattern of resolution across both junctions determines how the distal regions of the two DNA molecules are connected. A simple labelled model may represent each junction as one of two possible switching operations. If the two operations together restore original distal associations, the final molecules are noncrossovers. If they leave an odd number of net switches, the final molecules are crossovers. This is a bookkeeping model for a specified strand connectivity, not a claim that the molecular cuts are always independent or equally likely.

A second route for eliminating a double Holliday junction is dissolution. In a well-studied eukaryotic model, a RecQ-family helicase such as BLM in mammals promotes convergent migration of the two junctions, while topoisomerase III alpha and associated proteins help remove the resulting DNA interlinks. The key principle is topological unlinking without the classical paired endonucleolytic cuts at both junctions. Dissolution yields noncrossover products. This provides an important way for somatic cells to repair DNA while avoiding potentially harmful exchange of distal chromosome arms.

It is useful to understand the role of the topoisomerase by recalling that two DNA molecules can become linked even if their complementary base-pairing relationships have been rearranged. A helicase can alter the structure of a joint molecule without necessarily removing every topological interlink. A topoisomerase transiently changes covalent strand continuity in a controlled manner and allows strand passage followed by restoration of continuity. In the dissolution reaction the helicase and topoisomerase activities address different aspects of the same physical problem. Calling dissolution a simple melting reaction would miss the requirement to remove the remaining linkage.

Resolution and dissolution should therefore be distinguished by mechanism and outcome. Resolution involves nuclease-mediated processing of junctions and can yield different flanking-marker associations. Dissolution removes double-junction topology in a way that retains noncrossover distal associations. SDSA avoids formation of a stable double-junction substrate by removing the extended invading strand and pairing it with the other recipient end. All three routes can restore a damaged sequence using homologous information, but their late intermediates and outcome tendencies are different.

Repair of a one-ended break is another context in which the two-ended scheme cannot be transferred without modification. A collapsed replication fork can leave only one accessible broken DNA end in a particular local structure. If that end invades homologous DNA, synthesis may have to extend much farther to restore a functional chromosome. Break-induced replication is a standard conceptual example of such a route. The reaction does not rely on immediate capture of a nearby second end in the same way as ordinary two-ended repair. A detailed molecular treatment of this pathway would go beyond the scope of this chapter, but the distinction explains why the number and arrangement of DNA ends matter.

A question about final products may provide an observed increase in crossovers after loss of a dissolution-promoting protein. It is reasonable to propose that intermediates that would otherwise dissolve can now be processed through crossover-producing routes. It is not reasonable to conclude solely from that observation that the mutation increases the initial rate of strand invasion. An increase in one final outcome can reflect altered partitioning between late pathways rather than increased initiation. This distinction between flux into the repair network and distribution among branches is a recurring theme in molecular reasoning.

## Gene conversion: why local inheritance can be nonreciprocal

Gene conversion is recognized genetically when one DNA sequence becomes more like another related sequence in a manner that does not require a reciprocal replacement at the donor. The name does not imply conversion of an entire gene into an unrelated gene or a change in the fundamental properties of a chromosome. The affected tract may be smaller than a gene, encompass a whole gene, or extend beyond a gene depending on the recombination event. At the examination level the most useful unit is an informative marker located within a region affected by pairing or repair synthesis.

Consider two homologous duplexes containing different alleles at one aligned base pair. One duplex contains A paired with T and the other contains G paired with C. If a strand from the first duplex pairs with a complementary-origin strand from the second, a mismatch can appear because the bases at that position no longer represent the original matched pair. For example, the A from one parent may become paired opposite C from the other. Recognition and repair of that mismatch can replace one base according to the information on the opposing strand.

If the A-containing strand is corrected using the C-containing strand as template, the appropriate replacement is G. If the C-containing strand is corrected using the A-containing strand as template, the appropriate replacement is T. The chemical restoration of ordinary base pairing is successful in both cases, but the genetic outcomes differ. One product now carries G:C, the other A:T. The direction of mismatch correction determines whether a local marker has been restored to its previous state or converted to the alternative state, relative to the recipient being followed.

Mismatch repair in recombination should not be reduced to the familiar rule that replication errors are corrected by removing the newly synthesized strand. The substrate here may be a heteroduplex made from two preexisting parental strands or a mixture containing newly synthesized DNA. Strand-discrimination signals and repair biases depend on the biological system and the particular intermediate. In an exam problem one may be told which strand was excised, or which strand provides the template. That information should be used explicitly rather than assuming that the donor allele always wins.

A gap-repair event can produce the same observed allele conversion without a mismatched base being the immediate cause. Suppose a damaged chromatid originally carried the allele a but a double-strand gap removes the entire distinguishing sequence. If repair synthesis uses a donor carrying A, the newly synthesized recipient tract may carry A. The original a information has been lost at the gap. In this case the sequence transfer follows directly from copying the donor. The final genetic observation of conversion alone does not prove which of these molecular routes was used.

The relationship to ordinary segregation becomes clear in a tetrad. Begin with two A-bearing chromatids and two a-bearing chromatids following replication of a heterozygote. Suppose one of the a chromatids undergoes repair and becomes A, while the other three chromatids retain their marker states. The tetrad now contains three A chromatids and one a chromatid. The donor can remain A; no new a allele has to be created at that donor. The reciprocal balance characteristic of ordinary crossover is therefore absent at the converted locus.

In some fungi the products of meiosis undergo an additional mitotic division before spore formation. If all four meiotic products contain fully paired DNA at the marker, their allele states are duplicated during this division. A three-to-one tetrad then yields a six-to-two octad, under the simplifying assumption that all products are recovered and no additional change occurs. A two-to-two tetrad ordinarily yields four-to-four. If a heteroduplex mismatch persists through meiosis and is segregated during the subsequent DNA replication and mitosis, the two descendants of a single meiotic product can carry different alleles. This phenomenon is called postmeiotic segregation.

For example, three meiotic products with fully resolved marker states could contribute four A spores and two a spores after the additional division, while the fourth product contains an unrepaired A/a heteroduplex and contributes one A and one a descendant. The octad then contains five A and three a. An octad of five-to-three therefore provides a particularly useful conceptual example of persistent heteroduplex information being separated in a later division. The precise ordering of spores can provide more information, but ordered-ascus linkage analysis is reserved for the inheritance unit.

Notice that conversion is defined relative to the parental alleles and the segregation pattern, not by whether the converted allele is dominant. If A and a encode two different molecular sequences, either can in principle be transferred in a suitable context. Dominance is a relationship between genotype and phenotype, whereas conversion is a DNA sequence event. A dominant phenotype can mask a particular marker in some assays, so a genotype-sensitive readout is preferable when the purpose is to measure conversion itself.

Gene conversion can also occur between related sequences within a genome, such as duplicated genes. Over evolutionary time, repeated transfer of sequence between copies can contribute to similarity within a family. This helps explain why sequence comparisons between paralogous genes need not reflect only independent accumulation of mutations after duplication. The outcome depends on donor choice, conversion direction and selection. It does not follow that all duplicated genes are constantly homogenized or that every region of similarity was produced by conversion.

An important distinction is between allele balance at one site and linkage of markers on either side. A tetrad can show a three-to-one ratio at an internal locus while the external markers retain the parental combinations. That is evidence for a conversion associated with a noncrossover at the scored flanks. A different tetrad can show the same internal three-to-one ratio and recombinant external markers. That is conversion associated with a crossover. Conversely, a reciprocal crossover between markers can occur while the internal locus retains an ordinary two-to-two ratio. The classification must specify which observation is being described.

The absence of conversion at a marker does not prove that a heteroduplex never formed there. The original allele could have been restored, both parents might have carried the same base pair, or a mismatch could have been handled in a way that leaves no detectable departure in the particular assay. A tract may also lie outside the few marker positions chosen by the investigator. Genetic reporters measure informative differences, not every molecular exchange. A conversion tract inferred from marker positions is therefore a region bounded by observations, not necessarily the exact physical boundary of the heteroduplex.

## Template choice in mitotic repair, meiosis and bacterial recombination

The same elementary pairing mechanism serves different biological objectives. In many mitotically dividing eukaryotic cells, homologous repair is most beneficial when it restores the original chromosome without creating new associations of distant regions. A sister chromatid is usually an attractive donor because its sequence is nearly identical to that of the damaged molecule and it is held near the original chromosome after replication. Repair can then recover sequence with minimal genetic change. Mechanisms favoring noncrossover outcomes are particularly compatible with this objective.

A homologous chromosome can also serve as a donor when suitable sequence is available. However, it may contain different alleles. Local copying can alter the genotype of one chromatid. A crossover between homologous nonsister chromatids in a mitotic cell can, depending on subsequent chromatid segregation, produce daughter cells that have lost heterozygosity over distal regions. Loss of heterozygosity means that a locus previously represented by two distinct alleles becomes represented by the same allele state in both chromosomal copies of a descendant cell. It need not be accompanied by a reduction in chromosome copy number.

For a simplified example, assume a diploid cell carries homologs with distal markers B and b. After replication there are two B chromatids and two b chromatids. A reciprocal exchange between one B chromatid and one b chromatid can alter which distal segment is connected to a particular centromere. If the daughter cell inherits chromatids that now both carry B distally, it can become B/B at that distal marker even though it retains two chromosomes. Another segregation arrangement can retain B/b heterozygosity. The crossover creates the possibility of loss of heterozygosity; it does not establish that every daughter will exhibit it.

Meiotic recombination places different demands on the same general molecular process. Programmed double-strand breaks initiate recombination in many meiotic systems, with Spo11 providing the canonical initiating activity. Donor-choice mechanisms favor interactions between homologous chromosomes in many organisms, and selected recombination events mature into crossovers. These crossovers contribute to the physical associations between homologs that support their orderly segregation during the first meiotic division. Other repair events become noncrossovers. The chromosome movements, meiotic stage descriptions and genetic mapping relationships belong to the earlier cell-division and later inheritance chapters.

The distinction between intersister and interhomolog exchange is particularly important when interpreting a meiotic phenotype. A recombinase may be active yet fail to support the normal pattern of interhomolog interactions if donor choice is altered. Counting total repair products is then insufficient to demonstrate normal meiotic function. An assay that distinguishes sister and homolog templates provides more specific information. Similarly, counting only crossovers cannot determine the total number of initiated breaks, because many breaks are repaired without distal exchange.

In bacteria there is no ordinary eukaryotic meiotic division, but homologous recombination is crucial for chromosome repair and can also help integrate incoming genetic material. If a DNA fragment acquired by transformation or another transfer process contains a region corresponding to the bacterial chromosome, it can participate in RecA-mediated homologous pairing. The process of DNA entry and the process of establishing it within a chromosome are distinct. DNA transfer can occur without stable chromosomal integration, and a recombination defect can reduce integration even if entry remains intact. A plasmid with a functional replication origin may be maintained independently rather than recombining into the chromosome.

Bacterial cells can also contain replicated chromosome regions that serve as repair templates. Their donor availability depends on chromosome copy number, replication status and species-specific physiology rather than the eukaryotic G1, S and G2 framework. It would therefore be wrong to conclude that bacteria cannot use template-guided repair because they lack eukaryotic sister-chromatid terminology or a conventional mitotic cell cycle. The underlying requirement is access to a suitable DNA template, not possession of a particular cell-cycle naming scheme.

In archaea, RadA is a related strand-exchange recombinase. Its existence, together with bacterial RecA and eukaryotic RAD51 and DMC1, illustrates an evolutionarily conserved solution to the challenge of homologous pairing. The conservation of a recombinase family does not imply conservation of every accessory factor or identical reaction regulation. For example, one should not transfer the E. coli RuvABC pathway unchanged into a diagram of mammalian chromosome repair. Comparative questions often test precisely this distinction between conserved principles and organism-specific molecular machinery.
