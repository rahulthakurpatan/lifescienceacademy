# Fundamentals of Transcription: From DNA Template to RNA

## 1. Why cells require an RNA copy of genetic information

A cell faces two different demands that cannot be satisfied simply by making more DNA. It must retain genetic information with sufficient stability for transmission to daughter cells, and it must use particular parts of that information whenever their products are needed. A chromosome contains instructions for many activities, but a growing cell does not require all its possible products in equal amounts at every moment. Some genes must be expressed continuously, others must respond to a change in nutrient availability, and still others must remain almost silent. Selective synthesis of RNA provides a molecular solution to this difference between storing information and using it. Transcription is the production of an RNA molecule by reading a nucleic acid template; in the ordinary cellular process studied in this chapter, the template is DNA.

A DNA molecule does not have to be consumed to produce an RNA molecule. Its sequence is used to select the order of incoming ribonucleotides, which are connected into a new covalent chain. Once the chain is released, the DNA remains available for a subsequent round of transcription. It is therefore possible for one DNA region to give rise to many RNA molecules over time. An increase in RNA output does not require a corresponding increase in chromosome number. This property is central to the regulation of cellular physiology. A cell can change the use of its genome without first changing the sequence of that genome.

Imagine a bacterium whose chromosome contains the instructions for enzymes involved in utilization of two different nutrients. When only one of the nutrients is available, it would be wasteful to produce large amounts of both groups of enzymes. A suitable change in transcription can alter the amount of messenger RNA for one group relative to the other. The effect of this alteration depends on subsequent translation and protein lifetime, but it begins with the selective use of DNA. In a multicellular organism, a similar principle operates on a larger scale. Two differentiated cell types can possess essentially the same genomic sequences and nevertheless accumulate very different collections of RNAs and proteins. Differences in gene expression, rather than differences in the DNA sequence of every expressed gene, help explain their distinct functions.

RNA is not always an intermediate that will be translated into protein. Ribosomal RNA becomes a major structural and catalytic component of ribosomes. Transfer RNA participates in the selection and delivery of amino acids during translation. Other RNA products assist processing or regulation of nucleic acids. In these cases, the RNA molecule itself is an important functional product of gene expression. The general definition of transcription must therefore refer to RNA synthesis rather than to messenger RNA synthesis alone. The basic chemical reaction applies whether the completed RNA eventually serves as a messenger, adaptor, structural component or regulatory molecule.

In the usual diagram of genetic information transfer, replication is represented as DNA to DNA, transcription as DNA to RNA, and translation as RNA to protein. These arrows indicate the direction in which sequence information is used. They do not mean that DNA is chemically transformed into RNA or that RNA is transformed into protein. In each reaction, a template helps determine the order of a new chain assembled from its own substrates. Distinguishing information transfer from material conversion is particularly useful when interpreting radioactive labeling experiments. Label incorporated into newly synthesized RNA originates in the RNA precursor pool; it does not have to come from a labeled DNA backbone.

The following sections develop the fundamental consequences of this arrangement. First, the evidence for a messenger and a DNA-directed polymerizing enzyme establishes why the mechanism was proposed. Next, chemical structure explains how a nucleotide becomes attached to a growing RNA. Finally, the polarity of nucleic acid strands and the orientation of a promoter explain which DNA sequence is transcribed, where the transcript begins, and why a polymerase moves in a particular direction.

## 2. Discovery of the RNA intermediate

Once the structure of DNA was established, a major biological problem was to explain how the order of its bases could influence the order of amino acids in a protein. In eukaryotes, the bulk of the genetic material is in the nucleus, whereas much protein synthesis occurs in the cytoplasm. Even in bacteria, which lack a nuclear envelope, the DNA chromosome and the ribosomal machinery are functionally distinct systems. A direct association of every actively translated protein sequence with the chromosome seemed insufficient to explain how many copies of a protein could be produced. The existence of an intermediate carrying sequence information provided an attractive explanation, but an attractive explanation is not yet evidence.

One historical possibility was that each gene produced a particular ribosome specialized for synthesizing one protein. Under that model, a new kind of protein might require the assembly of a new kind of ribosome. Another possibility was that ribosomes were reusable machinery and obtained their specific instructions from a more temporary RNA molecule. An experiment capable of separating old ribosomes from newly synthesized RNA could discriminate between these alternatives. The crucial issue was not whether RNA was present in a ribosome fraction, since ribosomes themselves contain RNA. The issue was whether a newly supplied genetic program could direct protein synthesis using previously existing ribosomes.

In 1961, Sydney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson published a study of RNA synthesized after bacteriophage infection of Escherichia coli. A bacteriophage introduces genetic information that leads to a change in the types of protein produced by the infected bacterium. By using isotopic labeling and density-based separation, the investigators could distinguish pre-existing bacterial ribosomes from newly synthesized material. The study supported the interpretation that a newly made, relatively unstable RNA associated with ribosomes that had existed before infection. A new set of specialized ribosomes was not required for every newly expressed protein. The important result was an association between new RNA information and old translational machinery, rather than a simple demonstration that bacteria contain RNA.

A student analyzing such an experiment should ask what each label identifies. A stable isotope changes the mass of a molecule without changing the identity of its chemical element. A radioactive isotope can be used to detect newly incorporated material. If the components of a system are labeled at different times, their association can be examined without assuming that all material in a separated fraction has been produced at the same time. A density gradient separates particles according to their physical properties under the chosen conditions; detection of radioactivity in a fraction supplies a second type of evidence. Appropriate controls are necessary to establish the positions of heavy and light ribosomes and to distinguish new RNA from background labeling.

The findings do not imply that every RNA molecule is short-lived. Ribosomal and transfer RNAs can persist much longer than many messenger RNAs under comparable conditions. Nor did the experiment by itself establish the detailed chemistry of nucleotide incorporation or identify all the factors required for promoter recognition. It answered a question about the relationship of an unstable RNA intermediate to an existing translational apparatus. Later experiments examining synthesis by isolated enzyme systems and the relationship between DNA and RNA sequences addressed the molecular copying mechanism. These are distinct levels of evidence.

The historical lesson remains useful for modern examination questions. When an observation is compatible with two competing models, look for a prediction that differs between them. A radioactive RNA appearing near ribosomes would be insufficient if both models predicted such an appearance. Its association with particles that were demonstrably made before the change in genetic program is more informative. Likewise, a change in RNA abundance after infection might indicate altered RNA synthesis, altered degradation, or both. An experimental conclusion should match the particular variable that the experiment actually measured.

## 3. Establishing DNA-directed RNA polymerization

The existence of messenger RNA creates a second question: how is its sequence produced? A plausible answer is that an enzyme uses DNA to order ribonucleotides by complementary base pairing. However, a chemical system containing DNA and RNA does not necessarily carry out such a reaction. RNA might be present as a contaminant, or an enzyme might add nucleotides without copying a template. Evidence for DNA-directed synthesis requires showing that the product is newly formed and that its formation or sequence depends on the DNA supplied.

An informative biochemical experiment uses a preparation containing RNA polymerase and a defined DNA template, together with ribonucleoside triphosphates and appropriate reaction conditions. Formation of RNA can be followed by incorporation of labeled nucleotide into material that is separated from unincorporated small molecules. At this level, the experimental aim is to distinguish polymerized nucleotide from nucleotide that merely remains in solution. The appearance of a labeled macromolecular fraction is evidence of incorporation, provided that the procedure actually separates free label and that background controls are satisfactory.

The comparison becomes stronger when separate reactions omit the enzyme, the DNA template or an essential substrate. Without catalytically active polymerase, template-directed chain formation should be severely reduced or absent in an otherwise defined system. Removing the DNA should prevent DNA-directed synthesis, although residual activity in a crude preparation may reveal contaminating templates or other enzymes. Omitting one nucleotide species will limit extension through positions where that nucleotide is required. These controls address the causal contribution of each component. A single positive tube cannot establish which component was responsible for a detected product.

An RNase-sensitive labeled product is consistent with RNA formation, but RNase sensitivity alone does not identify the sequence of the product. To determine whether information has been copied from DNA, the investigator can compare the product with known template sequences or test specific hybridization between the RNA and complementary DNA. For example, if a defined template is changed at one position and the corresponding RNA base changes in the predicted manner, the evidence directly connects template sequence and product sequence. This reasoning is stronger than merely showing that total synthesis increases when DNA is added.

A further distinction concerns the enzyme's ability to initiate a chain. An enzyme that only extends a supplied oligonucleotide might still produce template-dependent RNA, yet it would not establish that transcription can initiate without a pre-existing primer. A suitable experiment provides an initiation-competent template, appropriate transcription machinery and the necessary ribonucleotides, but no added primer. Controls are required to exclude contaminating short nucleic acid primers if that is a plausible concern. Detection of products with newly formed 5′ ends under these conditions supports de novo initiation. The precise factors required for initiation differ across organisms and polymerases; the experimental principle does not depend on treating all RNA polymerases as identical.

These examples illustrate a hierarchy of conclusions. Incorporation of nucleotide label establishes that a chemical product is being made only if the detection procedure is valid. Product identity requires evidence that it is RNA. Template dependence requires comparison with reactions lacking or altering DNA. Copying fidelity requires a comparison between the known template and the RNA sequence. De novo initiation requires evidence that the first nucleotide does not come from an existing primer. In examinations, a distractor often jumps from one level of evidence to another. A researcher who measures total nucleotide incorporation has not necessarily measured the number of completed transcripts or established their sequences.

## 4. DNA and RNA as chemically related information carriers

The ability to copy DNA into RNA depends on two properties of nucleic acids. Their chains have a chemically defined direction, and their bases can recognize complementary partners. The first property constrains the direction of enzyme-catalyzed chain extension. The second makes the sequence of a template informative. Neither property depends on a base being a part of a protein-coding region. A nucleotide in a noncoding region can be copied using precisely the same chemical logic as a nucleotide in a coding region.

Each nucleic acid nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and phosphate. Adenine and guanine are purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. A nucleoside consists of the base and sugar without phosphate. In a nucleotide, one or more phosphates are attached. DNA normally contains deoxyribose and thymine, whereas RNA contains ribose and ordinarily uracil. The structural difference between ribose and deoxyribose at the 2′ position is especially important for the chemical behavior of their polymers. RNA has a hydroxyl group at that position; DNA has hydrogen instead.

The 2′ hydroxyl contributes to the greater susceptibility of RNA to backbone cleavage under alkaline conditions. Under appropriate conditions, a deprotonated 2′ oxygen can attack the neighboring phosphate within the RNA chain, producing cleavage products through an intramolecular reaction. DNA lacks the corresponding 2′ hydroxyl and cannot follow the same pathway. This difference helps explain why RNA and DNA have different chemical stability profiles. It does not mean every RNA molecule degrades immediately in the cell. RNA folding, binding proteins and the regulation of nucleases all influence biological lifetime. Conversely, a DNA molecule can be damaged or degraded by other reactions despite lacking the 2′ hydroxyl.

The sugar carbons are numbered with primes to distinguish them from positions in the base. The 1′ carbon is attached to the base, the 3′ position provides a hydroxyl group for chain extension, and phosphate associated with the 5′ position is central to formation of the backbone. In a conventional polynucleotide, a phosphodiester linkage joins the 3′ oxygen of one sugar to the 5′ oxygen of the next sugar through a phosphate. A chain with such linkages is chemically asymmetric even if its sequence of bases happens to read the same in either direction. A 5′ end is not interchangeable with a 3′ end.

Complementary base pairing arises from the arrangement of chemical groups that can donate or accept hydrogen bonds and from the geometry of the bases. In standard RNA-DNA pairing, RNA adenine pairs with DNA thymine, RNA uracil with DNA adenine, and RNA guanine with DNA cytosine. RNA cytosine pairs with DNA guanine. These relationships are the foundation for sequence conversion problems, but it is the enzyme that helps select and position the correct substrate in the context of a template. Base pairing by itself does not form the phosphodiester bonds required to create a covalent RNA chain.

A double-stranded DNA molecule has two antiparallel strands. If the upper strand is drawn with its 5′ end at the left and its 3′ end at the right, the lower complementary strand runs from 3′ at the left to 5′ at the right. This arrangement allows both strands to serve as potential sources of template information, but in opposite directions. A gene is not required to use the upper or lower strand by convention. The orientation of the transcription unit establishes the relevant template. The role of a particular strand must always be defined relative to the gene under consideration.

## 5. The actual substrates: ribonucleoside triphosphates

RNA polymerase normally uses ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP as substrates for DNA-directed RNA synthesis. These are adenosine, guanosine, cytidine and uridine triphosphates. Each supplies a ribonucleoside monophosphate residue to the growing RNA after the appropriate bond-forming reaction. The triphosphate form has a chemical role beyond carrying the base: it makes the incoming nucleotide an activated substrate. A ribonucleoside alone lacks the phosphates needed for the usual polymerization chemistry.

The three phosphates of an incoming nucleoside triphosphate are designated alpha, beta and gamma, beginning with the phosphate closest to the sugar. During ordinary chain extension, the alpha phosphate becomes the new backbone phosphate. The beta and gamma phosphates depart together as pyrophosphate, often represented as PPi. This provides a useful way to remember what becomes part of the RNA. A triphosphate does not remain attached at every internal position. If it did, the backbone would contain three phosphates between successive sugars rather than the usual single phosphodiester-linked phosphate.

The initiating nucleotide is a special case. In typical de novo initiation by a cellular RNA polymerase using standard NTPs, the first nucleotide retains its 5′ triphosphate in the immediate primary product. Its 3′ hydroxyl then participates in making the first internucleotide linkage. For each later nucleotide, the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphate is retained in the backbone while the other two phosphates are released. This difference matters for labeling problems. A gamma-phosphate label can be retained at the original 5′ end if the appropriately labeled nucleotide is used to initiate transcription and that end remains unmodified; it is not normally retained in the backbone during elongation.

The same ATP molecule that serves as a building block for RNA is also used in many other cellular energy-transfer reactions. Its role depends on the reaction in question. When ATP is selected opposite DNA thymine in the polymerase active site, its adenine base becomes part of the RNA sequence. It is not simply hydrolyzed by a separate motor to cause the incorporation of some unrelated nucleotide. GTP, CTP and UTP likewise contribute their own residues and activated phosphate groups. Accessory proteins and other transcription-associated processes can consume nucleoside triphosphates separately, but an extra ATP hydrolysis is not an obligatory universal step for each nucleotide added by RNA polymerase.

Consider an RNA sequence written as 5′-AGCUA-3′. The sequence requires two adenine residues and one each of guanine, cytosine and uracil. If the RNA starts with ATP by de novo initiation and is not subsequently modified, the first ATP contributes the 5′ triphosphate as well as the first adenine-containing residue. The second through fifth nucleotides each cause the release of one pyrophosphate during formation of four linkages. This example illustrates why a count of RNA residues is not identical to a count of internucleotide bonds. It also illustrates why the chemistry of initiation must be stated when performing phosphate-label calculations.

Nucleotide concentrations can influence transcription, because a substrate must be available at the moment its complementary template position is encountered. However, it would be misleading to assume that a high concentration of only one NTP can generally replace a shortage of another. High concentrations do not change the standard correspondence between template bases and product bases. At a position requiring UTP, excess ATP cannot normally produce an accurate extension. A shortage of one substrate can lead to slower elongation, accumulation of paused complexes or premature products, depending on the system and conditions. Such effects can be interpreted only with knowledge of the template sequence and the experiment's measured output.

## 6. Formation of the phosphodiester bond and energetic coupling

The elementary elongation reaction can be represented as RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi, where RNA(n) is the growing chain containing n residues. The terminal 3′ hydroxyl is the reactive group that participates in formation of the next linkage. The incoming NTP is positioned by complementary interaction with the DNA template and by the enzyme's active site. The resulting product possesses a new terminal 3′ hydroxyl, so the chemical arrangement can be used again during the next step. This repetition explains how a polymerase can assemble a chain hundreds or thousands of residues long without changing the fundamental linkage chemistry.

Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium ions, are essential to the catalytic mechanism of standard cellular RNA polymerases. A simplified two-metal-ion picture helps explain how the enzyme facilitates nucleophilic attack at the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphorus and stabilizes charge during the reaction. The metal ions help organize reactive groups and lower the activation barrier. They are not consumed as a component of every RNA phosphodiester bond. A reaction may fail when magnesium is removed or chelated even when DNA and nucleotides are present, because the catalytic environment has been disrupted.

Catalysis and thermodynamic driving force are different concepts. An enzyme accelerates a reaction by providing a pathway with a lower activation barrier; it does not change the equilibrium constant for a specified reaction. The chemical potential of the nucleotide triphosphate and the concentration of products contribute to the driving force of chain formation. Hydrolysis of released pyrophosphate to two orthophosphate molecules can favor net polymerization by lowering the amount of PPi available for the reverse reaction. This subsequent hydrolysis is chemically distinct from formation of the RNA linkage. Describing both processes as a single ATP hydrolysis obscures what actually happens.

It is possible under appropriate conditions for a polymerase to carry out the reverse of a nucleotide-addition reaction in the presence of pyrophosphate. This reaction, called pyrophosphorolysis, illustrates that the chemical direction of the elementary reaction is not enforced by a rule that forbids reversal. Cellular conditions, substrate supply, product removal and the organization of the transcription complex promote net extension. The biological direction of RNA synthesis refers to how successive nucleotides are added to the growing chain, not to an absolute claim that every individual catalytic step is chemically irreversible.

The difference between the number of residues and the number of phosphodiester bonds can be derived from simple chain construction. A single nucleotide has no internucleotide linkage. Adding a second nucleotide creates one linkage. Adding a third creates one more, giving two linkages. Each subsequent addition increases both the residue count and the linkage count by one, preserving a difference of one. A linear, unbranched RNA with L residues therefore has L − 1 internucleotide phosphodiester bonds. Under the stated standard de novo mechanism, construction of that RNA releases L − 1 molecules of pyrophosphate during incorporation of the second through last nucleotide. Subsequent processing and nonstandard initiation chemistry can alter the ends or number of bonds in a final RNA product, so the relationship must be applied to the molecular form specified in the question.

For an unprocessed 800-residue primary RNA made by standard initiation, the number of incorporated ribonucleotides is 800 and the number of backbone linkages is 799. If all released PPi is subsequently hydrolyzed, 1598 orthophosphate molecules arise from that pool of PPi. No assumption about the composition of the sequence is needed for this calculation. In contrast, calculation of the amount of UTP incorporated requires information about the number of DNA adenines in the transcribed template segment. Chemistry determines the total bond count, whereas sequence determines the distribution among four substrate types.
