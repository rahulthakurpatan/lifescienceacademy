## 7. Why RNA grows from 5′ to 3′

The direction of RNA synthesis is a consequence of the chemical arrangement of the growing chain and the substrates available to the enzyme. During elongation, the existing chain supplies its free terminal 3′ hydroxyl and the incoming nucleotide supplies an activated 5′ phosphate group. After the linkage forms, the incoming sugar becomes the new terminal residue and presents a new 3′ hydroxyl. The previous 3′ end is no longer the end of the chain. It has become an internal position connected to the next nucleotide. Repeated use of this arrangement makes the RNA progressively longer at its 3′ end. The chain is said to grow in the 5′ to 3′ direction even though the growing chemical bond is being formed at the 3′ end.

Students sometimes assume that a 5′ to 3′ polymerase must move along a DNA strand whose sequence is also written 5′ to 3′ in the same direction. This confuses product polarity with template polarity. The RNA and the DNA strand that it copies form antiparallel base pairs. If the RNA is growing toward the right in a diagram, its 5′ end lies to the left of its 3′ end. The template under that RNA must have its 3′ orientation to the left and its 5′ orientation to the right. The polymerase therefore reads successive template positions in the 3′ to 5′ direction while producing the RNA from 5′ to 3′. Both descriptions refer to the same molecular movement, not to two opposite polymerase motions.

Consider a very short template written 3′-TACG-5′. Suppose the first transcribed template position is the thymine at the left. RNA polymerase selects ATP, then UTP, then GTP, then CTP in the simplified sequence shown. The RNA product is 5′-AUGC-3′. The first base incorporated is adenine, and the last is cytosine. If a student reverses the RNA sequence because the two strands are antiparallel, the answer becomes incorrect: the direction has already been accounted for by placing the RNA 5′ end opposite the template 3′ end. The bases should be complemented from left to right when the template is already written 3′ to 5′.

The situation changes when the template is presented 5′ to 3′. For example, the same template segment above can be written 5′-GCAT-3′. The physical molecule has not changed; only the convention of writing it has changed. A polymerase reading from its 3′ end now moves from the rightmost symbol toward the leftmost symbol. To report the RNA in the conventional 5′ to 3′ form, first read the template in reverse order and then substitute complementary RNA bases. The answer remains 5′-AUGC-3′. The reversal is necessary because the template was originally displayed in the opposite direction from the order in which it is read.

Directionality must not be confused with the absolute orientation of a drawing or chromosome. A polymerase can move leftward on a particular map if the template for that transcription unit has its 3′ to 5′ polarity toward the left. The fact that the printed arrow points left does not indicate 3′ to 5′ RNA synthesis. It indicates the position and direction of transcription relative to the page. The new RNA still extends at its 3′ end. A useful habit is to label both ends of the DNA strands before attempting any question involving an arrow, inversion or neighboring gene.

This chemical explanation also helps identify a genuine distinction between RNA polymerase and a nuclease. A nuclease hydrolyzing a chain can remove residues in a direction defined by the end from which it begins; a polymerase adds residues to a growing chain. Saying that an enzyme acts in a 3′ to 5′ direction is incomplete unless the context specifies whether the enzyme is reading a template, extending a product or removing nucleotides. During transcription, the standard polymerization direction of the product is 5′ to 3′, and the complementary template is read 3′ to 5′.

## 8. Complementarity, the coding strand and the template strand

For a particular transcription unit in a double-stranded DNA molecule, the strand whose bases are read by the polymerase is the template strand, sometimes called the antisense strand in relation to that transcript. The complementary DNA strand is the non-template strand. For a protein-coding transcription unit, this strand is conventionally called the coding or sense strand because its sequence, read in the same 5′ to 3′ order as the primary RNA, matches the RNA sequence apart from the substitution of uracil for thymine and possible later alterations to the RNA. This terminology does not mean that the non-template strand is directly read during synthesis of that RNA.

For example, consider the DNA segment with coding strand 5′-ATGCCATTA-3′ and template strand 3′-TACGGTAAT-5′. If the entire displayed segment is transcribed in the direction from left to right, the primary RNA segment is 5′-AUGCCAUUA-3′. The coding DNA sequence contains thymine wherever the RNA contains uracil. The template has complementary bases at every position. These three sequences describe one copying relationship. They are not three independent information sources or three distinct transcription events.

The term coding strand is also used by convention in discussions of noncoding transcription units, although non-template is clearer when there is no protein-coding message. An RNA that will function without translation still resembles the non-template DNA sequence in the same local copying relationship. The word coding in coding strand should not be interpreted as evidence that the entire strand or every part of a transcript encodes amino acids. Even a protein-coding gene can contain transcribed regions that are not translated. Those regions follow the same base-pairing rules as the region that eventually specifies a polypeptide.

A sequence problem is easiest when the question supplies the coding strand in its conventional direction. Under the assumption that the displayed region is transcribed and the product is an unmodified primary sequence, replace T with U and preserve base order. If the question supplies the template strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction, complement each base and preserve order. If the template is supplied in the 5′ to 3′ direction, reverse the order and complement. A coding strand displayed 3′ to 5′ requires reversal without complementation before T is replaced with U. These are not four different biological processes. They are four ways of describing the same pair of antiparallel molecules.

To illustrate the fourth case, suppose a coding DNA segment is written 3′-TTACG-5′. Reversing the order produces 5′-GCATT-3′. Replacing thymine with uracil yields 5′-GCAUU-3′. Complementing the originally displayed sequence instead would generate a sequence associated with the opposite strand rather than the specified transcript. The end labels and the identity of the strand must both be considered. Memorizing a rule such as replace T with U without checking those two conditions will fail whenever the question changes the representation.

In practice, a double-stranded DNA sequence without a promoter or other directional information does not define a unique transcript. It defines two potential complementary sequence orientations. To infer which is used, the question must identify the template, the coding strand, a transcription arrow, a promoter orientation or experimentally determined transcript sequence. A DNA region can in principle contain multiple transcription units, some using one strand and some the other. In overlapping transcription, two distinct RNAs can even share a portion of the DNA coordinate interval while having different sequences because they are copied from opposite strands.

There is an additional qualification concerning the relationship between the RNA and the coding strand. The simple equality applies to the templated portion of the initial transcript, assuming accurate copying. Processing may remove internal sequences, alter bases or add nucleotides at an end. Thus a mature eukaryotic RNA may not be an uninterrupted sequence identical to one continuous region of coding DNA after T-to-U replacement. It is not necessary to learn the detailed processing mechanisms at this point; the important distinction is that the copying rule predicts a primary templated sequence, while the molecular history of the final RNA may include other reactions.

## 9. Sequence construction from incomplete and inverted representations

One valuable way to check a proposed transcript is to reconstruct the DNA duplex from it. Suppose an unmodified primary RNA segment is 5′-UAGGCU-3′. The corresponding non-template DNA segment, written 5′ to 3′, is 5′-TAGGCT-3′. The template can be written antiparallel beneath it as 3′-ATCCGA-5′. If a question requests the template in the usual 5′ to 3′ form, it becomes 5′-AGCCTA-3′. Checking the answer by aligning the RNA and template in opposite orientations avoids many mistakes that result from performing a mental reverse complement without tracking strand ends.

The same method can be used when some positions of a duplex have been omitted. If an RNA product contains guanine at its fourth position, the corresponding template position should contain cytosine, assuming faithful standard transcription. The non-template position contains guanine. This relationship allows a student to fill missing bases without having to reconstruct the entire sequence. However, if the supplied RNA is edited or if an error has occurred during transcription, the prediction may not hold. Questions should state or imply the standard unmodified case before a simple one-to-one relation is assumed.

Inversions are another source of confusion. Imagine that a promoter and the DNA region it transcribes are inverted together as an intact block. Relative to a fixed chromosome coordinate system, the transcription direction reverses. If the same promoter remains capable of recruiting its transcription machinery and the internal relationships between promoter and gene remain unchanged, the intrinsic RNA sequence may be unchanged. This is because the same information-bearing duplex is now located in the opposite orientation, rather than the gene being arbitrarily assigned to a different template. In contrast, reversing only a promoter while leaving the downstream sequence fixed may direct transcription into a different DNA region. The outcome depends on the actual orientation and location of all relevant elements.

Consider a defined educational map. A plus-reference DNA strand has the sequence 5′-ATGCCGTA-3′ across an interval. A rightward transcript that covers this entire interval has the sequence 5′-AUGCCGUA-3′. A leftward transcript covering the same interval instead uses the plus-reference strand as its template, read from the right. Its RNA sequence is 5′-UACGGCAU-3′. These RNAs are reverse complements of each other in the standard case. They are not identical simply because their coordinates overlap. The sequence of a transcript is determined by both the interval and the strand.

At the exam level, it is useful to distinguish three operations on a sequence. Reversal changes the order of symbols but not their chemical identities. Complementation changes every base to its pairing partner but does not by itself change the order in which symbols are written. A reverse complement performs both operations and is necessary when comparing two antiparallel strands written in the same conventional direction. A student should be able to explain which operation represents a change in notation and which represents a change in molecular identity. Reversal alone is a change in presentation, whereas the complementary strand is a different physical chain.

A sequence can be self-reverse-complementary over a particular interval. For example, the DNA sequence 5′-GAATTC-3′ has a complementary strand that reads the same in the conventional 5′ to 3′ notation. This is a property of that short sequence, not a general characteristic of DNA or a reason that strand orientation may be ignored. If a transcribed interval were exactly symmetric in that sense, the two hypothetical primary RNA sequences could coincidentally match. The transcription events would still have distinct directions and templates. Sequence identity in a special case is not proof that the same DNA strand was read.

When interpreting gene diagrams, it is often more reliable to annotate the transcription direction first and only then examine sequence details. Begin at the location of +1, follow the arrow to the end of the relevant interval, identify the strand that runs 3′ to 5′ along that path, and write its complementary RNA. This procedure unites spatial reasoning and chemistry. A diagram can be rotated, the reference strand can be exchanged, and the sequence notation can be reversed without changing the underlying molecular rule.

## 10. The transcription unit as a spatial and functional entity

A transcription unit is a region of DNA associated with the production of an RNA from a defined initiation region through a downstream transcribed region to the region where transcription ends. In a simplified introductory diagram, its principal features are a promoter, a transcription start site, the DNA region copied into RNA, and a termination region. The promoter provides information used by the transcription machinery to initiate at the appropriate position and orientation. The start site identifies the first nucleotide position that is actually transcribed. The copied region provides the sequence information of the transcript. Termination enables the synthesis event to end and the machinery to be released or reorganized for further use.

These elements are not necessarily separate pieces with visible boundaries in an actual chromosome. They are functional designations assigned on the basis of DNA sequence, molecular interactions and experimental evidence. Some promoter elements lie upstream of the start site; in some systems important promoter elements can overlap the start site or extend downstream. A gene diagram showing a promoter as a rectangular block entirely upstream is a useful simplification, but it is not a universal statement about promoter architecture. The exact organization of bacterial and eukaryotic promoter elements belongs to later chapters. Here the essential principle is that a promoter directs initiation rather than specifying an amino acid sequence.

A transcription unit may contain one protein-coding region, several coding regions or no protein-coding region at all. In bacteria, an RNA transcribed from one promoter can contain the information for several polypeptides. Such a transcript is commonly described as polycistronic. The existence of several protein-coding regions on one RNA does not require several separate transcription events, although an actual locus can contain additional internal promoters or other arrangements. Conversely, a gene giving rise to a functional noncoding RNA does not require a translation start codon in order for transcription to occur.

It is therefore important to distinguish the boundaries of a transcription unit from the boundaries of a protein-coding region. The first transcribed nucleotide can precede the translation start by a considerable number of bases. The RNA may also continue beyond the translation stop codon. In a simple mature messenger RNA these untranslated segments are called the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions. The existence of these regions demonstrates that RNA polymerase does not identify its starting and stopping positions by applying the rules of codon recognition. It reads DNA in response to transcription-related signals. The ribosome subsequently interprets appropriate RNA features in the context of translation.

The term terminator also needs careful handling. In many simplified bacterial examples a defined DNA sequence, or the RNA sequence generated from it, participates in signaling the end of transcription. A primary transcript can contain bases derived from portions of a termination region. In a eukaryotic protein-coding gene, processing at the 3′ end of the RNA and cessation of RNA polymerase II transcription can occur at different positions. Consequently, a drawn terminator boundary cannot always be assumed to equal the end of the mature mRNA. The general idea of ending a transcription event is shared, but the molecular methods and relationships between ends differ.

A transcription unit is also distinct from the complete set of regulatory regions that influence its output. DNA sequences outside the copied interval may alter the probability of initiation. A regulatory element can therefore affect an RNA without being part of the RNA's sequence. The simplest examples involve promoter mutations that reduce initiation frequency without changing the sequence of the transcripts that still form. This is a powerful conceptual distinction in mutation experiments: the effect of a change in DNA can be informational, regulatory or both. Its location relative to the actual start site and the transcription machinery helps identify which possibility is most likely.

## 11. The +1 convention, upstream and downstream

The transcription start site is designated +1 in the conventional gene-relative coordinate system. It is the first nucleotide of the newly transcribed RNA, corresponding to a particular base pair in the DNA. The position immediately upstream is −1, and there is no zero position in this notation. Positions downstream of the start site are +2, +3 and so on. The negative positions denote locations before the start in the direction in which transcription proceeds. They do not denote physical positions below a reference plane or bases that are chemically negative. They are a coordinate convention chosen to make the start site a common point of reference.

The absence of zero has a small but important consequence for length calculations. Suppose a regulatory segment extends from −20 through −1, inclusive. It contains 20 base pairs. The interval from +1 through +30 contains 30 base pairs. Together the contiguous interval from −20 through +30 contains 50 base pairs, not 51. The ordinary formula endpoint minus start plus one applies directly when both positions are on the same uninterrupted side of the coordinate system. When an interval crosses from negative to positive gene-relative positions, the omitted zero must be accounted for. This is an example of a mathematical rule being valid only for a particular convention of labeling positions.

If transcription begins at +1 and proceeds continuously to a last incorporated base designated +400, the unprocessed transcript contains 400 residues, assuming that every position is copied once and no insertions or deletions occur. A promoter element occupying positions −35 through −30 is not included in that RNA merely because it is close to the start. A downstream promoter element at positive coordinates, however, could lie in a region that is copied into the transcript. Thus the proper criterion for inclusion in a primary RNA is whether the DNA position is within the actual transcribed interval, not whether the position is called a promoter element.

The words upstream and downstream are relative to the direction of transcription. Consider a chromosome map drawn with increasing reference coordinates from left to right. For a rightward gene, an upstream site lies to the left of +1 on that map. For a leftward gene, an upstream site lies to the right of +1. The same numerical chromosome coordinate can therefore be downstream of one gene and upstream of another. A statement that all upstream positions have lower chromosome coordinate numbers is incorrect whenever a gene is oriented toward decreasing reference coordinates.

Suppose gene A has a transcription start site at genomic coordinate 1000 and is transcribed toward increasing coordinates. Its position +20 is at coordinate 1019, and −20 is at coordinate 980. Gene B has its start site at coordinate 2000 and is transcribed toward decreasing coordinates. Its +20 lies at 1981, whereas its −20 lies at 2020. In both cases, moving from +1 to +20 requires movement by 19 positions. Moving from +1 to −20 requires movement by 20 positions because there is no zero. Writing down the direction before performing the arithmetic protects against off-by-one errors.

Some questions use absolute genomic coordinates rather than gene-relative notation. If an inclusive interval runs from coordinate 500 through coordinate 699, it contains 200 bases regardless of whether transcription moves from 500 to 699 or from 699 to 500. What changes is which endpoint corresponds to +1. For a rightward transcription unit starting at 500, +1 is coordinate 500. For a leftward unit starting at 699, +1 is coordinate 699. Under the same simplified assumptions, the two hypothetical primary RNAs would have equal lengths but potentially very different sequences. Orientation determines strand and order; the number of positions determines length.

Actual genes can use alternative initiation sites. A transcript population may therefore contain molecules starting at different nearby or distant positions rather than a single universal +1. In a teaching diagram, +1 refers to the particular start site under discussion. An experiment that maps several 5′ ends must establish whether they reflect multiple transcription starts, RNA processing or degradation. The coordinate convention remains useful, but it does not require every transcript from a locus to share one end.

## 12. Promoter, transcription start, translation start and termination signals

A promoter and a transcription start site answer different questions. The promoter answers where and in which orientation the transcription apparatus is recruited and organized to initiate. The start site answers which DNA position supplies the first nucleotide of the RNA under the conditions being considered. A change in promoter sequence may affect the probability of initiation, the selection of a start site or both. A mutation at the first transcribed nucleotide may change the sequence of the resulting RNA and can sometimes influence initiation itself. These effects should be evaluated from the experiment rather than assigned solely on the basis of a word such as promoter.

A translation start codon is an RNA sequence interpreted by the translational machinery in an appropriate context. In the familiar example, AUG can serve as the start codon and specify methionine during initiation, with the bacterial initiating amino acid often supplied as formylmethionine. However, not every AUG is a translation start, and transcription does not require AUG at its first three positions. RNA polymerase reads individual template positions according to nucleotide complementarity, whereas translation uses a decoding system that considers groups of three bases in a reading frame. The chemical operation and the recognition problem are different.

For an illustrative transcription unit, assume +1 corresponds to the first base of an RNA leader, and the first base of the selected translation start codon is +31. The leader contains 30 residues. If the first base of a stop codon is +331, there are 300 residues from +31 through +330, corresponding to 100 sense codons under the assumption of a continuous reading frame without interruptions. The stop codon occupies positions +331 through +333 and does not encode an additional ordinary amino acid. If the final nucleotide of the RNA in this simplified model is +400, another 67 residues follow that stop codon. Nothing in the arithmetic implies that RNA polymerase ceased synthesis at +333.

The distinction between translation termination and transcription termination is particularly important. Stop codons are recognized in the context of ribosomal decoding. A terminator is involved in cessation of transcription. A mutation changing a sense codon to a stop codon can produce a shorter polypeptide without necessarily causing RNA polymerase to stop at the mutated site. Secondary effects can occur in some biological systems, including effects of translation on downstream expression in bacteria, but the primary event must not be confused with direct recognition of a stop codon by RNA polymerase.

An analogous distinction applies to the start. Deletion of an appropriate promoter can strongly reduce the formation of RNA even if the coding sequence and its AUG remain intact. Mutating a translation start can strongly reduce production of the protein while leaving transcription initiation intact. If both mutations lead to the same low protein measurement, the protein measurement alone cannot locate the defect. Measuring RNA abundance, mapping its ends and assessing protein output relative to RNA can help distinguish the two mechanisms. A promoter that remains functional can generate an RNA even when the RNA is incapable of directing normal translation.

A further complication is that promoter activity cannot always be inferred from sequence resemblance to a known motif. A candidate promoter might resemble a common recognition sequence but fail to support initiation in its particular context. Conversely, a functional promoter may differ from a textbook consensus sequence. At the introductory level, the promoter is best defined by its role in initiation. Later chapters will explain how different transcription systems recognize promoter elements. A functional test that measures initiation from a candidate region is more informative than a purely visual judgment that a sequence looks like a promoter.

The four terms can now be organized into a causal order. A promoter helps organize initiation at a transcription start site. RNA polymerase synthesizes the primary transcript. A protein-coding RNA can subsequently be used by ribosomes, which select a translation start and eventually encounter a stop codon in the reading frame. Meanwhile, transcription itself ends according to mechanisms specific to its transcription system. In bacteria, transcription and translation of a given RNA can overlap in time, but this temporal coupling does not make the two sets of signals identical. Understanding these distinctions provides the basis for interpreting more detailed molecular diagrams in the next nodes.
