## 13. Initiation as the creation of a new RNA chain

The first covalent bond of a new RNA presents a different problem from the bonds made during subsequent elongation. An elongating polymerase already has a chain whose terminal sugar supplies the necessary 3′ hydroxyl. At the beginning of a transcription event there is no such polymeric RNA end. Cellular DNA-dependent RNA polymerases solve this problem through de novo initiation. In the usual mechanism, an initiating ribonucleoside triphosphate is positioned opposite the first template base, and a second ribonucleoside triphosphate is positioned opposite the next template base. The 3′ hydroxyl of the first nucleotide participates in forming the bond to the second nucleotide. A new RNA chain now exists with a defined 5′ end and a free 3′ hydroxyl.

This explanation clarifies why the statement that RNA polymerase needs no primer does not contradict the requirement for a hydroxyl group in the polymerization reaction. A primer is a pre-existing nucleic acid chain that provides a suitable end for extension. The initial NTP in de novo transcription supplies a hydroxyl group as a substrate within the initiating complex rather than as a preformed oligonucleotide. Once the first linkage is made, the growing RNA itself supplies the terminal hydroxyl during elongation. The chemical requirement remains, but the source of the hydroxyl changes as the process proceeds.

DNA polymerases used in cellular genome replication generally require an existing primer-template junction with an accessible 3′ hydroxyl. During cellular DNA replication, primase or a primase-associated system generates the starting segment, after which a replicative DNA polymerase extends an appropriate end. The difference is therefore not that DNA synthesis lacks suitable nucleotides capable of forming phosphodiester bonds. It is that the usual replicative DNA polymerase requires a different substrate arrangement for productive catalysis. An RNA polymerase is equipped to organize the initial nucleotides without a pre-existing chain. Exceptions involving specialized polymerases, unusual priming strategies and viral enzymes do not invalidate the comparison of ordinary cellular transcription with ordinary chromosomal DNA replication.

A reaction containing DNA, all four NTPs and an RNA polymerase preparation does not automatically initiate at the intended site. The relevant polymerase must be capable of using the template under the supplied conditions; accessory factors may be necessary for proper promoter recognition or start site selection. Primer independence must not be confused with independence from all other molecular requirements. An RNA polymerase cannot usually begin accurate gene-specific transcription merely because a random piece of double-stranded DNA is present. The initiation apparatus must organize an appropriate template region and permit access to the bases that will be copied.

It is helpful to separate three events in an introductory representation of initiation: association of the transcription apparatus with a suitable promoter, local opening of the DNA duplex, and synthesis of the first short RNA. These are functional steps, not a claim that each system proceeds through an identical list of named complexes. A bound polymerase may not yet have a template strand correctly positioned for catalysis. An opened DNA region may not yet contain an RNA product. An initial RNA may be formed without progressing into a stable elongation event. Different experimental measurements can detect these distinctions, so it is inappropriate to infer completion of initiation from promoter occupancy alone.

The immediate RNA end formed through standard initiation is frequently triphosphorylated at the 5′ position. Its subsequent fate depends on the organism and transcript type. Eukaryotic messenger RNA precursors commonly undergo cap formation, and other RNA classes can undergo distinct end-processing reactions. Noncanonical initiating substrates and primer-dependent initiation occur in particular biological contexts, so the simplest description of the 5′ triphosphate should be qualified as the usual de novo reaction with standard NTPs. A primary transcript is defined by its origin in transcription, not by an unchanging chemical feature that all cellular RNAs must retain throughout their lifetime.

## 14. Selecting a promoter and gaining access to DNA bases

The bases of a double-stranded DNA molecule are normally engaged in complementary pairing within the duplex. For a polymerase to select an incoming ribonucleotide by the appropriate template base, that base must become accessible. Transcription therefore involves local separation of the two DNA strands. It does not require the entire chromosome to unwind or the two DNA strands to separate permanently. A short region is opened and maintained in association with the transcription machinery, while much of the DNA ahead of and behind the active region remains double-stranded. The local nature of this rearrangement helps preserve chromosome integrity during selective gene expression.

Promoter recognition provides information about where to establish this opening and how to orient the machinery. A sequence-specific interaction can distinguish one region of a DNA molecule from many other regions composed of the same four types of nucleotide. The spatial arrangement of recognized bases and associated DNA properties can orient the polymerase complex. An asymmetrically arranged promoter therefore provides a directional signal. The exact components that establish specificity differ between bacterial, archaeal and eukaryotic transcription systems, and their molecular identities will be treated in the relevant subsequent chapters.

DNA strand separation requires overcoming the interactions that stabilize the duplex. Hydrogen bonding contributes to complementary pairing, and base stacking contributes substantially to helix stability. Local opening also depends on the shape of the DNA and on the interactions between the DNA and the initiation complex. A simple count of hydrogen bonds alone is not sufficient to predict the melting behavior of an arbitrary promoter sequence. Sequence context, salt concentration, temperature and DNA topology can all affect the energetic balance. In the cell, proteins and other components of the transcription machinery can favor an opened state through their binding interactions and, in certain systems, through energy-dependent processes.

An intuitive but inaccurate model imagines that polymerase simply finds the first available exposed base and starts to copy indefinitely. This would produce many inappropriate RNAs and would not reliably connect transcript boundaries with cellular functions. The promoter helps solve the positioning problem, while start site selection determines the first incorporated base in the particular initiation event. Sequence features at or near the start and the geometry of the assembled machinery can influence the position selected. The consequences of a promoter mutation may therefore include a reduced number of transcripts, a shift in their starting positions or altered responsiveness to regulatory factors.

Consider an educational comparison of two DNA fragments identical throughout their intended transcribed regions but differing in a short upstream sequence. If one fragment produces much more correctly initiated RNA than the other under matched conditions, the upstream difference is evidence of an effect on the process leading to productive transcription. It does not show that the upstream sequence was incorporated into the RNA. To identify the precise defective step, it would be useful to examine whether the polymerase-containing complex binds, whether the DNA opens and whether short initiated products progress to longer ones. An effect on total product yield is compatible with defects at more than one stage.

The promoter is not consumed in a transcription cycle. After a polymerase progresses away from the initiation region or otherwise leaves it available, another productive initiation can occur. The DNA can therefore support repeated rounds of transcription. The actual interval between rounds is determined by factors such as availability of the machinery, promoter occupancy and regulation, rather than by a fixed stoichiometric relation between one DNA copy and one RNA copy. This is a central difference from the usual aim of chromosomal replication, in which the entire genetic complement must be duplicated in a coordinated manner.

## 15. The elongating transcription complex

Once a productive transcription complex has formed, it must maintain the relationship between the DNA template and the growing RNA while moving to successive positions. The active region of an elongating complex contains a locally opened segment of DNA, an RNA-DNA hybrid involving part of the newly synthesized RNA and its template, and a route through which the older part of the RNA separates from the DNA and emerges. Ahead of the active center, DNA bases that will be read must become accessible. Behind it, the two DNA strands can pair again as the RNA is displaced from the template.

The entire growing RNA does not remain paired to the DNA for the duration of transcription. If a polymerase has synthesized a thousand nucleotides, the hybrid within a typical elongation complex comprises only a short portion near the active center. The bulk of the RNA made earlier has already emerged from this region. This arrangement allows the product to become available for folding and interactions with other molecules while synthesis is still under way. The dimensions of the hybrid and the open region depend on the system and state of the complex; they should not be treated as invariant numbers applicable to all polymerases.

A useful picture of elongation is a repeating cycle. The appropriate NTP becomes available and is selected at the current template position. Its reactive groups and the growing RNA end become arranged for catalysis. Formation of a new linkage extends the product, and pyrophosphate is released. The polymerase and its nucleic acid components change their relative positions so that the next template base can participate in another round of incorporation. Actual polymerases explore different conformational and translocation states, and the individual microscopic events need not be captured by a rigid one-step mechanical model. At the examination level, the repeating chemical and positional changes explain the net progress of the enzyme.

The movement of the open region along the DNA creates a distinction between transcription and a stable RNA-DNA hybrid produced in an experimental hybridization assay. In an elongating complex, local hybrid formation and disruption are normal components of productive synthesis. A persistent RNA-DNA hybrid outside the usual moving active region can arise in certain biological circumstances and can affect DNA organization or stability, but it is not necessary for every transcript to remain attached to the chromosome after synthesis. The ordinary endpoint of a productive transcription event is release of the RNA from its transcription complex.

RNA chain extension also has consequences for DNA topology. As a polymerase moves along a helical DNA template, relative rotation of the DNA and the complex may be required. If that rotation is constrained, torsional changes can accumulate, often represented by increased positive supercoiling ahead and negative supercoiling behind the moving complex in a simplified model. Enzymes that manage DNA topology can influence the ability of transcription to proceed. This connection is especially useful after studying DNA structure and replication, because it demonstrates that transcription is not purely an abstract operation on a sequence of letters. It occurs on a three-dimensional molecule subject to physical constraints.

The term processivity describes the ability of a polymerizing enzyme to continue synthesis through multiple additions without dissociating from its substrate complex. It is not identical to the speed of nucleotide incorporation. A highly processive enzyme may extend a chain relatively slowly yet remain associated until a long product is formed. A fast but poorly processive enzyme may add several residues rapidly and then dissociate before reaching the normal endpoint. Experiments measuring RNA length distributions and experiments measuring incorporation rates can distinguish these properties when their designs permit. Processivity helps explain how a polymerase can produce an RNA much longer than the short DNA-RNA hybrid present at any one moment.

## 16. Transcription fidelity and the consequences of incorrect incorporation

The ability of RNA polymerase to choose the appropriate nucleotide is essential because an incorrect base can alter the biological activity of the RNA. A substitution in messenger RNA may alter a codon and, in some circumstances, the amino acid incorporated into the resulting protein. A substitution in a noncoding RNA may alter its folding or its recognition of a binding partner. Not every error has a detectable functional effect. Its significance depends on its position, the identity of the substituted nucleotide, the purpose of the RNA and the extent to which other cellular processes compensate.

Fidelity originates in several aspects of the transcription complex. Complementary pairing favors appropriate substrates, but pairing is not the sole determinant. The active center also constrains how nucleotide geometry, the template and the terminal RNA end are arranged for catalysis. Incorrect substrates may be less likely to reach a productive state. Some errors that nevertheless occur can be corrected when an elongation complex changes position and cleaves part of the newly formed RNA to restore an end suitable for extension. Detailed accessory factors and organism-specific mechanisms will be introduced in later treatments of transcription elongation. At this point it is sufficient to understand that transcription fidelity reflects selection and possible correction rather than an infallible chemical rule.

A transcription error is different from a mutation in the DNA template. If a DNA substitution is present at a given genomic position, every accurate transcript made from the affected template under comparable conditions can carry the corresponding altered base. If the DNA is unchanged and one polymerase makes a copying error, other transcripts produced from the same region need not contain that error. A transient RNA error does not automatically become a stable heritable change in the chromosome. This difference helps explain why cellular genome duplication and RNA production have distinct biological consequences even though both rely on templated nucleotide selection.

The distinction should not be overstated as a claim that transcriptional errors are biologically harmless. A single RNA may be used repeatedly by translational machinery. Consequently, an error in a messenger molecule can affect more than one protein molecule. Errors in an important functional RNA may also have physiological consequences. The key distinction is the normal route by which the error propagates. A chromosomal mutation can be transmitted through DNA replication to descendant cells, whereas an error in an ordinary cellular RNA usually affects products derived from that RNA and disappears when the molecule and its consequences are removed.

Consider a simplified model in which the probability of an incorrect incorporation is a constant p at each of L independent positions, with no subsequent correction. The probability of an entirely correct sequence is (1 − p) raised to the power L. The probability that at least one error occurs is one minus that expression. For small p and a sufficiently small product Lp, the latter probability is approximately Lp. The approximation follows from expansion of the product: the first-order term for one event is Lp, while terms involving multiple events are of higher order. The formula is useful only under the model's assumptions; different sequence positions may have different error probabilities and errors can influence subsequent elongation or be corrected.

For a hypothetical per-position error probability of one in one hundred thousand and a transcript length of one thousand positions, Lp is 0.01. The exact independent-position probability of at least one error is approximately 0.00995, or 0.995 percent. This numerical example is not a universal measured RNA polymerase error rate. It is an illustration of how a low probability per event can produce a higher probability for a long sequence containing many events. A transcript twice as long would have approximately twice the error probability only while the first-order approximation remained valid. The expected number of errors, Lp, is not generally identical to the probability of at least one error, since one transcript can contain several errors.

To distinguish a DNA mutation from a recurrent RNA sequence difference experimentally, both DNA and RNA information are relevant. A mismatch seen in a single RNA read can result from biological misincorporation, RNA modification, an error in conversion or sequencing, or other sources. A reproducible RNA difference absent from genomic DNA warrants further investigation but does not by itself establish a particular editing mechanism. The beginner's sequence-complementarity rule is a prediction about faithful primary synthesis, and the experimental scientist must consider whether the molecules being measured actually satisfy that assumption.

## 17. Pauses, productive progression and the meaning of transcription rate

A polymerase does not necessarily advance at the same speed through every DNA sequence. The probability of an addition and the time spent in different transcription complex states can vary with nucleotide availability, DNA sequence and interactions with other molecules. A pause is a temporary interruption or reduction in the net progress of an elongating complex. A paused complex may resume synthesis. A stalled complex may remain arrested for a longer period under the conditions studied. Neither term automatically implies that the RNA has been released or that the transcription event has ended.

It is important to distinguish a pause from termination because the two can produce superficially similar measurements. Imagine a short experiment in which many RNA molecules have a length of about 150 residues. This could indicate that the polymerases have temporarily accumulated at the corresponding template position. It could also indicate that the RNA products have been released at that position and cannot continue. A length measurement at a single time point does not decide between these alternatives. Evidence that the short chains subsequently become longer in a suitable continuation experiment would support pausing or arrest followed by recovery. Evidence that the RNAs were released and cannot be extended within the same complex would support a different interpretation.

A mutation can change the average time required to make a transcript without changing its final length. Suppose an RNA polymerase adds residues at an idealized rate of 40 nucleotides per second during active extension and produces a 1200-residue RNA. Ignoring the distinct timing of initiation, the active extension time estimated from length divided by speed is 30 seconds. If the polymerase also spends ten seconds in a pause, the overall synthesis time in this simple example becomes 40 seconds. The average effective rate across the entire interval is 30 nucleotides per second, even though the active extension rate remains 40 nucleotides per second. A lower observed average rate does not establish that the chemistry of every addition became slower.

The exact length convention deserves attention in very short examples. If a chain of n residues has already formed, an L-residue completed chain requires L − n additional incorporations. For an elongating 120-residue RNA that must reach 600 residues, there are 480 additions remaining. At a uniform model rate of 30 additions per second, these would take 16 seconds. Using 600 divided by 30 would overestimate the remaining time because it would count residues already present. For long transcripts initiated from nearly zero length, L divided by an average rate is often a useful approximate completion time. The assumptions should be explicit.

The speed at which one elongation complex advances must also be distinguished from the number of RNA molecules produced per unit time by a gene or cell. Several RNA polymerases can occupy different positions on one DNA region when initiation is sufficiently frequent and the physical arrangement allows it. In a simple steady pipeline, one complex may take a long time to complete a transcript while new complexes begin at shorter intervals. Once the pipeline is filled, completed transcripts can emerge at intervals closer to the initiation interval than to the transit time of a single polymerase. Slower elongation may increase the number of simultaneously occupied complexes if initiation remains unchanged and crowding is negligible, but it need not increase completed transcript output.

This distinction is crucial for interpreting a measurement of polymerase density. High density can result from frequent initiation, slow movement or long residence at a pause. A gene with many associated polymerases is not necessarily producing more full-length RNA per unit time than one with fewer polymerases. To estimate output, one must know whether the polymerases are productively elongating and completing transcripts, and how rapidly they enter and leave the transcription unit. An occupancy measurement is a snapshot of how many complexes are present; a rate measurement describes how many events occur during an interval. The two quantities are related only through additional assumptions about the dynamics of the system.

## 18. Termination as a distinct stage of RNA synthesis

A transcription event must eventually end. If a polymerase were to continue through a chromosome without appropriate limits, many unwanted sequences could be included in the RNA and the polymerase would remain engaged longer than necessary. Termination is the stage at which a productive transcription complex ceases further RNA synthesis as part of the normal transcription cycle. Release of the RNA and dissociation or reorganization of the polymerase complex allow the molecules to participate in subsequent cellular activities. This general description applies across systems, but their specific termination signals and pathways differ.

The physical problem of termination is distinct from simply running out of substrate. When an essential NTP is absent, a polymerase can stop at a position that requires that nucleotide. If the missing substrate is restored, a suitably retained complex may resume synthesis. A biological termination pathway, in contrast, promotes ending of the transcription event in response to relevant sequence or molecular signals. An experimentally truncated RNA can arise from either mechanism or from RNA cleavage after synthesis, which is why the presence of a short product does not by itself identify normal termination.

Bacterial and eukaryotic systems illustrate different ways of implementing this general stage. In familiar bacterial examples, particular sequences can generate RNA features or support the activity of factors that cause transcription to end. In nuclear eukaryotic transcription, termination of RNA polymerase II can be linked to events involved in forming the RNA's 3′ end, and the position where the polymerase stops need not coincide with the position where the mature messenger RNA ends. The details of intrinsic and factor-dependent bacterial termination, and of the various eukaryotic polymerase systems, are intentionally separated into the following nodes. Their shared conceptual point is that a signal changes the behavior of an elongation complex rather than merely instructing a ribosome to stop translation.

A failure of termination can cause a polymerase to continue beyond an expected endpoint, producing a readthrough RNA. Suppose two otherwise identical DNA fragments contain the same initiation region and transcribed sequence but differ at a candidate termination region. If one fragment yields many short RNAs of the expected length and the other yields a larger fraction of RNAs extending into downstream DNA, the difference is consistent with altered termination efficiency. To strengthen this conclusion, the investigator must consider the possibility that short RNA species were generated by cleavage or that the long products arise from an additional initiation site. Mapping transcript ends and analyzing whether the downstream RNA is physically continuous with the upstream transcript can help resolve these possibilities.

Termination efficiency is often expressed as a fraction of relevant transcription events that end at a specified region, provided that the experimental quantities correspond to events rather than merely to steady-state molecule counts. In a hypothetical assay, 180 out of 200 transcripts reaching a candidate terminator end there and 20 read through. The apparent termination efficiency is 180 divided by 200, or 90 percent. The readthrough fraction is 10 percent under the assumption that those two outcomes exhaust the events. If some complexes stall indefinitely or produce undetected products, the simple two-outcome calculation will no longer represent all complexes that reached the region.

A promoter and terminator can consequently influence different aspects of output. A weakened promoter may reduce the number of events entering a transcription unit, while a weakened terminator may alter the distribution of transcript endpoints. In a system with a downstream readthrough reporter, changing the initiation frequency can also change the reporter signal. It is therefore helpful to normalize readthrough to the amount of transcription entering the candidate region or compare upstream and downstream measurements under matched conditions. This reasoning illustrates the general need to identify the denominator in a molecular experiment before interpreting a percentage as a mechanistic property.
