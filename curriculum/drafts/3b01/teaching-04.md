## 19. Comparing transcription with DNA replication

Transcription and replication share a foundational principle: the order of nucleotides in an existing nucleic acid strand guides the order of nucleotides in a newly synthesized chain. Both processes normally rely on complementary base recognition, activated nucleoside triphosphates, a polymerizing enzyme and extension at a 3′ end. These similarities explain why the concepts of template polarity and phosphodiester linkage formation recur in both chapters. They also provide a basis for distinguishing the biological purpose and organization of the two processes.

During chromosomal replication, the cell must duplicate the DNA genetic complement so that daughter DNA molecules contain the necessary genetic information. In the ordinary semiconservative mechanism, each resulting duplex contains a parental strand paired with a newly synthesized DNA strand. Both parental strands serve as templates, even though the antiparallel orientation of the strands creates distinct leading- and lagging-strand arrangements at a replication fork. Transcription has a different purpose. It produces an RNA from a particular transcription unit, and only one DNA strand serves as the template for that RNA at any given position. The other strand remains part of the parental DNA duplex and is not converted into a second RNA simply as a consequence of copying the first.

This difference explains why the terms leading strand and lagging strand are not appropriate labels for the two DNA strands of an ordinary transcription unit. They describe the relationship of continuous and discontinuous DNA synthesis to the direction of movement of a replication fork. In transcription, one RNA polymerase synthesizes one RNA chain along the selected template. It does not need to produce a complementary RNA on the non-template strand at the same time, so the requirement that leads to a lagging-strand solution at a replication fork is absent. Other transcription units may use either strand, but that is a consequence of their own directional organization rather than a requirement of each transcription event.

The substrate pools also differ. Replicative DNA polymerases normally incorporate deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, while RNA polymerases normally incorporate ribonucleoside triphosphates. Both types of synthesis involve formation of phosphodiester bonds and release of pyrophosphate during chain extension. DNA replication generally uses a primer-generating system, whereas normal cellular transcription can begin de novo. The enzymes that solve these distinct molecular problems need not have the same active-site architecture or associated factors even though the basic chemical transformations are related.

An experimental perturbation that prevents formation of a new DNA replication primer need not directly prevent an ordinary RNA polymerase from initiating a transcript. Nevertheless, a perturbation of DNA metabolism may alter transcription indirectly through changes in nucleotide pools, cell viability or DNA integrity. In a whole-cell experiment, the specificity of an observed effect must therefore be distinguished from the specificity of its direct molecular target. A purified enzyme experiment and a cellular phenotype answer different questions. The former can isolate the immediate reaction, while the latter reveals the consequences of that reaction in a connected system.

The different outcomes of replication and transcription also help explain why the processes are regulated in different ways. A typical chromosome must be duplicated in a coordinated manner during a cell division cycle. A particular RNA may be made repeatedly whenever its function is needed. If an investigator doubles the initiation frequency of a promoter without changing the chromosome copy number, the RNA production rate can rise. If the number of chromosome copies increases but the transcription machinery becomes limiting, output may not rise in direct proportion to copy number. Neither a DNA concentration measurement nor an RNA concentration measurement alone determines the efficiency of the other process.

Finally, the chemical contrast between an RNA product and a DNA product permits useful analytical discrimination. Under suitable conditions an RNA-containing sample can be treated with an RNase, whereas DNase acts on susceptible DNA. The susceptibility depends on the enzyme, substrate structure and reaction conditions. Such treatments can help identify which nucleic acid carries a measured label, but appropriate controls are required. A loss of a signal after DNase treatment before transcription could indicate removal of the template; a loss after RNase treatment of the product could indicate that the measured material contains RNA. The order of treatment matters because removing a template and degrading a product are distinct interventions.

## 20. From synthesis to measured RNA abundance

A cell's RNA content is not determined by transcription alone. RNA molecules are formed, can undergo processing, and are eventually removed. A sample taken at a single moment reveals the amount that remains under the conditions examined, not directly how many molecules were made during a particular interval. This distinction is important because many biological experiments report RNA abundance and then discuss a change in gene expression. A change in expression may reflect altered transcription, altered RNA stability, or a combination of mechanisms. A foundational understanding of transcription should include the mathematical distinction between synthesis rate and RNA amount.

Let R be the number of molecules of a specified RNA per cell in a simplified model, and let s be its constant rate of production in molecules per cell per unit time. Suppose removal is proportional to the number of molecules present, with proportionality constant k having units of inverse time. Then the net change in RNA amount can be written dR/dt = s − kR. The first term counts formation of the species being measured, and the second counts removal. For a primary transcription product, s may reflect productive transcription into that measurable species. For a mature RNA, processing and maturation may contribute to the effective formation rate as well. The equation is a simplified mass balance, not a universal complete kinetic model.

At steady state, the amount does not change with time, so dR/dt is zero and the two opposing rates are equal. Rearranging gives R = s/k. It follows that doubling synthesis at unchanged k doubles the steady-state amount, but halving k at unchanged synthesis has the same effect. A measurement of the final amount alone cannot distinguish the two. The rate of removal at steady state is not zero: molecules can be continuously formed and removed even when the total number remains constant. This distinction between dynamic steady state and absence of activity is important throughout biology.

For a hypothetical RNA synthesized at ten molecules per cell per minute and removed with k = 0.2 per minute, the predicted steady-state abundance is fifty molecules per cell. If an intervention doubles synthesis to twenty molecules per minute, the new steady-state prediction is one hundred molecules per cell. If a different intervention instead reduces k to 0.1 per minute while synthesis remains ten, the same abundance is predicted. Measuring nascent synthesis or decay after a validated interruption of production can distinguish these possibilities. A simple comparison of two RNA abundance measurements cannot do so.

If production ceases at time zero and first-order removal continues with constant k, the equation becomes dR/dt = −kR. Separating variables and integrating gives R(t) = R(0)e^(−kt). The half-life is found by setting R(t) equal to one-half of the initial amount, so t½ = ln(2)/k. In the first hypothetical example, a k of 0.2 per minute predicts a half-life of approximately 3.47 minutes. Reducing k to 0.1 per minute increases the predicted half-life to about 6.93 minutes. This model assumes a homogeneous measurable RNA population and a constant removal probability per unit time; multiple molecular species, cell division, changing conditions or complicated maturation can violate those assumptions.

The synthesis and removal distinction also helps interpret transient experiments. Immediately after a promoter becomes more active, the RNA pool may still be small because time is required for new molecules to accumulate. If transcription ceases, a stable RNA may remain detectable for a considerable period. A cell can therefore retain a molecular record of earlier activity in its RNA population. Observing an RNA after a stimulus has disappeared does not prove that the promoter is still active. Measuring newly formed material over a defined interval provides more direct evidence of ongoing synthesis than simply measuring all existing RNA.

The same caution applies to estimates of protein output. A change in RNA production may precede a change in protein abundance, and the relationship depends on translation efficiency and protein turnover. A researcher studying a transcription mechanism should ideally measure a variable close to the proposed step, such as initiation, elongation or newly synthesized RNA, rather than assuming that every downstream phenotype is a direct measure of polymerase activity.

## 21. Experimental reasoning: mapping where RNA begins and ends

One of the first tasks in understanding a transcription unit is to identify the positions that contribute to the RNA. A DNA sequence can suggest where a promoter or coding region might be located, but the existence of a transcript and the position of its ends are experimentally testable questions. If an RNA's 5′ end is identified and aligned to a known genomic sequence, the alignment establishes a candidate location for its beginning. Whether that location is the original transcription start site requires attention to processing, degradation and the type of molecule measured. A mature RNA end can be different from the initial end created by RNA polymerase.

In a conceptual primer-extension experiment, a short oligonucleotide complementary to a known RNA region is annealed to an RNA sample. Reverse transcriptase extends the primer using RNA as a template, producing complementary DNA. If the RNA has a defined 5′ end and extension reaches that end, the length of the resulting DNA can reveal the distance between the known priming location and the RNA end. This technique is useful because a known internal sequence provides an anchor from which the position of the end can be inferred. The experiment uses reverse transcription as an analytical method, not as the normal mechanism by which the cell made the original RNA.

Consider an educational example in which the primer spans positions +51 through +70 on an RNA of known orientation. Its 3′ end is aligned opposite position +51, so extension adds complementary DNA corresponding to positions +50, +49 and so on through +1 if the original RNA begins at +1. Fifty new DNA residues are added to the twenty-residue primer, giving an idealized seventy-residue product. If the RNA instead begins at position +11 of the original coordinate system, forty new residues are added and the final DNA product contains sixty residues. This calculation assumes complete extension to the end and correct primer binding. Premature arrest of reverse transcriptase can generate a shorter product without demonstrating a different RNA start.

A complementary type of evidence is obtained by hybridizing an RNA sample with a labeled nucleic acid probe that recognizes a particular region. A probe targeting an upstream portion of a transcription unit can detect RNA containing that region; a probe targeting downstream DNA-derived sequence can detect RNAs that include that downstream region. The fact that two probes both detect RNA in a sample does not, by itself, prove that both regions are on the same physical molecule. If the question concerns readthrough from one promoter, continuity of the RNA or appropriate size information is needed to distinguish a long transcript from two separate transcripts.

The Northern blot provides an introductory example of how size and sequence-specific detection can be combined. RNA species are separated by size under suitable conditions and detected by hybridization with a complementary probe. A shorter or longer band can suggest a difference in transcript length, while band intensity under appropriately controlled conditions can give a relative indication of abundance. A shift in band size need not be due to a transcription termination defect; it might result from a different start site, altered processing or degradation. A single probe identifies molecules containing a complementary region but does not define every nucleotide of those molecules.

Orientation-specific detection requires careful probe design. Suppose a defined rightward transcript matches the plus-reference strand except for the substitution of uracil for thymine. A probe designed to hybridize directly to that RNA must have the complementary sequence in the proper antiparallel orientation. A single-stranded probe identical to the RNA is generally not the probe that detects it by ordinary complementary hybridization. If transcription occurs in both directions across the same DNA interval, orientation-specific probes or equivalent directional methods can help distinguish the products. The exact experimental conditions must ensure that the detected signal reflects the intended nucleic acid species rather than contaminating DNA or nonspecific binding.

These methods illustrate how several measurements can complement one another. A start mapping assay gives positional information about an RNA end, a hybridization assay gives evidence for particular sequences, and a size measurement gives information about the distribution of product lengths. None of these is identical to directly observing promoter recognition by a polymerase. Together, appropriate measurements can constrain a model of the transcription unit. The ability to connect an experimental observable to the relevant step is more important at this stage than memorizing a list of laboratory techniques.

## 22. Integrating the transcription cycle with cellular organization

The same fundamental copying logic operates in bacterial, archaeal and eukaryotic cells, despite major differences in their transcription machinery and cellular organization. In bacteria, transcription occurs in a cellular environment without a membrane-bounded nucleus. Ribosomes may begin translating suitable messenger RNAs while transcription is still in progress. This makes the emerging RNA a site at which transcription and translation can become functionally connected. Nevertheless, the polymerase produces the nucleotide sequence by reading DNA, while the ribosome reads codons in RNA. Their spatial proximity does not merge their reaction mechanisms.

In eukaryotes, transcription of most nuclear genes occurs within the nucleus, whereas translation of ordinary nuclear-encoded messenger RNAs occurs in the cytoplasm. RNA processing and transport therefore form important links between initial RNA synthesis and its use. A newly synthesized nuclear RNA may already begin to undergo processing before transcription finishes. It would be misleading to imagine that every transcript is first released as an entirely untouched chain and that every processing event begins only afterward. The distinction between primary sequence synthesis and subsequent chemical processing remains useful even when the events overlap in time.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts also contain genetic information and have systems for producing RNA. Their existence shows that eukaryotic RNA synthesis is not confined to the nucleus. The detailed transcription machinery varies among organisms and organelles, but ordinary DNA-directed RNA polymerization still requires an appropriate template, substrates and catalytic machinery. Archaeal transcription provides another useful comparison: the absence of a nucleus does not imply that the transcription apparatus must be identical to the familiar bacterial model. Cellular location and molecular ancestry are different criteria for classification.

There are biological cases in which RNA serves as a template for synthesis of RNA rather than DNA serving as the template. Some RNA viruses use RNA-dependent RNA polymerases, and cellular systems in some organisms also employ RNA-dependent RNA synthesis. Reverse transcription instead produces DNA using an RNA template. These reactions are not exceptions to the direction of information copying in the ordinary DNA-directed transcription mechanism defined in this chapter. They are related but distinct biochemical pathways. The exam question must be read carefully to identify the template and product rather than assuming that any polymerase called an RNA polymerase necessarily uses DNA.

The fundamental copying rules also explain the logic of a common biotechnology application: producing an RNA of a chosen sequence from a suitable DNA template. The desired sequence must be placed in the appropriate orientation relative to an initiation region recognized by the selected transcription system. A promoter that directs transcription into the opposite orientation can yield an unintended complementary RNA or fail to produce the intended product. Similarly, changes in the end of the transcribed region can alter the length or composition of the resulting RNA. Such applications rely on the same strand and coordinate reasoning used throughout this chapter, not on a separate set of base-pairing laws.

A meaningful final model of transcription should allow predictions at several levels. Changing a base within the copied interval changes the corresponding primary RNA position if faithful synthesis continues. Changing a promoter may alter the number of initiation events or their positions. Changing the concentration of a required substrate can alter productive extension under suitable conditions. Altering termination can change the distribution of RNA endpoints. Altering RNA removal can change RNA abundance without necessarily altering transcription. These predictions connect chemistry, sequence, kinetics and experimental interpretation in a coherent framework. They also prepare the student for the next chapter, where the bacterial RNA polymerase and promoter recognition machinery explain how the general initiation problem is solved in a specific cellular system.
