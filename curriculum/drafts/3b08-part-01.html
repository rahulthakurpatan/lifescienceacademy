1. Why an RNA transcript needs splicing

Imagine a cell that must manufacture a protein whose amino acid sequence is already specified in its chromosome. RNA polymerase II can copy the relevant region into RNA, but copying is not necessarily the last operation needed to produce a usable message. In many eukaryotic genes, the nucleotide sequences that will be adjacent in the finished messenger RNA are separated from one another in the chromosome. Transcription copies intervening sequences as well as the sequences ultimately retained. A second molecular operation must recognize the correct boundaries, remove particular RNA segments and join the retained pieces in the correct order. This operation is RNA splicing. For most introns in nuclear protein-coding genes, it is performed by a ribonucleoprotein machine called the spliceosome.

Splicing is therefore an operation on the RNA copy of genetic information. It does not ordinarily excise DNA from the chromosome or change the inherited sequence of the gene. The same DNA can be transcribed again, and the next RNA molecule can undergo a similar or different processing decision. This distinction between a permanent change to the genome and a choice made during RNA processing underlies both the classical experiments on interrupted genes and many examination questions about tissue-specific expression. A chromosome may remain completely unchanged while its corresponding messenger RNAs differ in length, composition and capacity to encode a protein.

The term precursor messenger RNA, or pre-mRNA, refers to an RNA that still requires maturation before it can serve as the appropriate messenger. In a typical animal cell, RNA polymerase II produces the precursor, a cap is attached to its emerging 5′ end, introns are removed, and the 3′ end is generated through cleavage and, for many transcripts, polyadenylation. These operations can overlap in time. An RNA molecule need not remain untouched until the polymerase reaches the end of the entire gene. Splice-site recognition may begin while later portions of the precursor are still being synthesized. The earlier chapters on transcription and end processing provide the context; this chapter concentrates on the removal and joining of internal RNA regions.

The distinction between exon and intron describes the fate of a segment in a particular RNA processing pathway. In the usual description of a protein-coding gene, exons are the segments retained in the mature transcript and introns are the intervening segments excised during splicing. Exons do not necessarily consist entirely of protein-coding information. The portion of an exon preceding the translation initiation codon can be part of the 5′ untranslated region, and the portion beyond the termination codon can be part of the 3′ untranslated region. A whole exon may be untranslated. Conversely, an intron can contain sequences important for the regulation of its own removal or the choice of neighboring exons. The statement that an intron is removed is not equivalent to the claim that it has no biological function.

Consider a gene whose transcribed region contains three exons separated by two introns. For a particular fully spliced product, the polymerase copies exon 1, intron 1, exon 2, intron 2 and exon 3. If all three exons are retained, the mature RNA contains exon 1 immediately next to exon 2, followed immediately by exon 3. The junction between exons 1 and 2 is a new adjacency in the RNA, although the sequence of each exon itself need not have changed. The transcript still has a 5′ to 3′ polarity. Splicing neither reverses the exons nor randomly shuffles their order. Ordinary cis-splicing connects regions of the same precursor in their existing orientation. A distinct phenomenon called trans-splicing joins regions derived from different RNA molecules and is not the general rule for mammalian nuclear pre-mRNAs.

To appreciate the demand for precision, imagine that the first exon ends with the first nucleotide of a codon. Its partner exon can begin with the remaining two nucleotides. After ligation the ribosome reads a complete codon across the exon junction. A shift of even one nucleotide at either splice boundary can change the grouping of all subsequent nucleotides into codons. Depending on the location, the resulting protein may contain a different amino acid sequence and terminate prematurely. Nevertheless, not every splicing error creates a frameshift, and not every change in the reading frame is equally consequential. The location of the affected segment, whether it lies in the coding region, its length and the position of the new stop codon all matter.

We must also distinguish the introns removed from conventional nuclear pre-mRNA from intervening sequences found in other biological settings. Some ribosomal or organellar RNA introns can splice through catalytic RNA structures; certain transfer RNA introns are removed by a different enzymatic pathway. Bacterial transcripts generally do not require the nuclear spliceosome, although particular bacteria and their mobile genetic elements can contain self-splicing introns. An organism that lacks the conventional spliceosomal apparatus is therefore not necessarily incapable of any RNA splicing. Mechanism rather than the word intron alone determines which processing pathway is involved.

For an examination, a useful first task is to identify the molecular stage at which a proposed event occurs. If a change is detected in genomic DNA, transcription and subsequent processing may both transmit its consequences. If the DNA remains unchanged but mature RNAs differ in their exon junctions, a processing choice becomes a plausible explanation. If RNAs have identical junctions yet differ in individual nucleotides, RNA editing may instead be relevant. If proteins differ despite apparently identical messenger RNA sequences, the explanation may lie at translation or post-translational modification. These are distinct possibilities, and experiments must distinguish them rather than simply assign every difference in gene output to splicing.

2. How interrupted genes were discovered

Before the discovery of split genes, much molecular reasoning about gene expression had developed from bacteria and their viruses. For many familiar examples, the DNA region specifying a protein corresponded continuously to the RNA message. That correspondence provided a reasonable starting hypothesis for eukaryotic genes as well. A mature transcript was expected to align continuously with the DNA region that encoded it. The discovery that this alignment was interrupted was important precisely because it challenged a straightforward prediction of the earlier view rather than merely supplying an additional detail about RNA chemistry.

Adenovirus offered a productive experimental system because its genome is relatively compact, its RNA products can be studied in infected cells, and its DNA can be mapped. In 1977, Susan Berget, Claire Moore and Phillip Sharp examined the relationship between adenovirus type 2 late messenger RNA and its DNA template. A parallel line of work associated with Richard Roberts and colleagues also established the discontinuous nature of adenovirus messenger RNA sequences. The observations became central to the discovery of split genes, for which Sharp and Roberts received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The scientific point is more important than memorizing a pair of names: sequence correspondence between the mature RNA and its genomic template can directly test a hypothesis about how an RNA was formed.

In a DNA–RNA hybridization experiment, complementary sequences in the two molecules can form a hybrid double helix. If a mature RNA contains a continuous copy of one uninterrupted genomic segment, its complementary DNA strand can pair over the corresponding continuous region. If instead the mature RNA contains two adjacent segments whose corresponding DNA regions are separated by an additional sequence, the DNA lying between those regions has no partner in that mature RNA. Under conditions allowing the relevant strands to hybridize, the unpaired region can form a loop. Electron microscopy can reveal the resulting architecture. The exact appearance depends on the DNA preparation and hybridization arrangement, so a student should not interpret every loop in every nucleic acid preparation as proof of splicing.

In the adenovirus work, the position of sequences complementary to the late hexon messenger RNA was not consistent with a single uninterrupted genomic stretch. Parts of the RNA near its 5′ end corresponded to separate regions upstream of the main coding portion. A model in which a longer nuclear transcript was processed to join distant segments could account for this pattern. The paper by Berget, Moore and Sharp reports that the leader segments mapped to multiple upstream locations. Their interpretation explains why the final RNA contains sequence neighbors that are not immediate neighbors in the chromosome. The structural evidence made it necessary to rethink how the primary transcription product becomes a mature message.

It is useful to reconstruct the reasoning as a hypothetical experiment. Suppose a DNA fragment contains region A, a 900-nucleotide intervening region and region B. A purified RNA contains sequences complementary to A and B but lacks the intervening region. The hybridization model predicts that the DNA corresponding to the 900 nucleotides cannot participate in the same RNA–DNA duplex and will be displaced from it. If the same DNA is hybridized with an unprocessed precursor that includes the intervening region, a larger extent of continuous pairing becomes possible. Comparing the two preparations helps distinguish a genuine RNA composition difference from an artifact caused by one particular DNA structure. Appropriate controls include known uninterrupted RNA–DNA pairs, verification of the RNA and DNA identities, and attention to partial degradation or incomplete hybridization.

Hybridization alone establishes a relationship between sequences; it does not directly identify the chemical bond that was broken during maturation, the enzyme that performed the reaction or the order in which the exons were joined. Those are mechanistic questions that require additional evidence. For instance, mapping the ends of the mature RNA could establish the precision of the junctions, whereas detecting a longer precursor and shorter product in a time-dependent assay could support a precursor–product relationship. Isolating a reaction intermediate could disclose the topology of the excised intron. Each experiment addresses a different link in the explanation. A strong answer separates the observation of discontinuous sequence correspondence from the later discovery of the detailed spliceosomal mechanism.

Modern sequence-based approaches make the same logical distinction easier to see. Complementary DNA synthesized from mature RNA can be compared with the genomic sequence. If a single cDNA molecule contains an exon–exon junction that is absent from the continuous genomic sequence, the intervening genomic region is a candidate intron. Sequencing a precursor containing both boundaries strengthens the interpretation that the mature junction arose during processing. A genomic DNA contamination control matters in amplification experiments because genomic DNA naturally contains the intervening region. A no-reverse-transcriptase control can reveal whether DNA contamination is contributing to what is supposed to be a measurement of RNA. The method does not require the examiner to accept that every computationally detected junction is genuine; independent validation can remain important.

The discovery of interrupted genes changed the relationship between genes and gene products. A gene could now be understood as a source of a precursor that is interpreted by an additional molecular apparatus. The apparatus must recognize the right sites even when many superficially similar sequences occur elsewhere in the transcript. It also creates an opportunity for regulated variation, because different subsets of a precursor's segments may be retained. To explain either fidelity or diversity, we first need to identify the information in the precursor that makes a boundary recognizable.

3. Reading the signals at an intron boundary

An intron cannot be recognized simply by measuring its length or by identifying a stretch that fails to encode a protein. Lengths vary greatly, and a retained exon need not be translated at all. The splicing machinery must instead use molecular signals, meaning particular sequence and structural features that interact with recognition factors. For a typical major-spliceosome intron in a mammalian precursor, the principal signals comprise the 5′ splice site, the branch-point region, a pyrimidine-rich tract near the 3′ end and the 3′ splice site. Their organization provides information about the direction, approximate location and chemical readiness of the sites that will participate in splicing.

The 5′ splice site is the boundary at the beginning of an intron when the RNA is read from 5′ to 3′. It is often called the donor site. In many major-class introns, the first two intronic nucleotides are GU. The 3′ splice site is the boundary at the end of that intron, often called the acceptor site. The final two intronic nucleotides are usually AG. A sequence diagram conventionally written as exon 1 | GU … AG | exon 2 places both GU and AG on the intron side of their respective boundaries. Neither is retained merely because it marks a splice site. The phosphodiester bonds at the two vertical bars, rather than the bonds between G and U or between A and G, are the ones replaced during the standard reaction.

Recognition extends beyond the two terminal nucleotides. One familiar representation of a mammalian major-class 5′ site is MAG|GURAGU, where M means A or C and R means A or G. This is a consensus pattern, not an obligatory exact sequence. The nucleotides in the neighboring exon and the following intronic region contribute to recognition; the importance of an individual base depends on its position and the surrounding interactions. At the acceptor end, a typical arrangement contains a branch-point adenosine followed downstream by a region enriched in U and C and then an AG near the exon boundary. The branch point is commonly located tens of nucleotides upstream of the acceptor in mammalian introns, but its precise distance and sequence vary. It is not correct to impose one universal number of intervening bases on all organisms or introns.

A consensus is a statistical description of sequences that tend to be favored at comparable positions. It does not guarantee that every instance of a short pattern is a functional splice site. The GU dinucleotide occurs many times in a long RNA molecule just by chance. Under a simplified model in which all four nucleotides occur independently at equal frequency, the probability of GU at a particular adjacent pair is one sixteenth. A sequence of 1,601 nucleotides contains 1,600 adjacent pairs, giving an expected count of 100 GU occurrences by linearity of expectation. The overlapping pairs need not be independent for this expected count to be valid. This calculation is illustrative rather than a model of an actual gene, because real base composition and sequence dependence vary. It nevertheless demonstrates why the first two nucleotides of the intron alone cannot locate a unique donor among many candidates.

The branch-point adenosine supplies a functional chemical group rather than merely serving as an address label. RNA nucleotides contain ribose with a hydroxyl group at the 2′ carbon. In the branch-point reaction, the 2′ oxygen of an internal adenosine acts as the attacking nucleophile. The adenosine is already part of the normal RNA backbone, so its existing 3′ and 5′ connections remain while an additional linkage forms at its 2′ oxygen. This produces a branched molecule. The ability to position an otherwise internal hydroxyl group at a distant phosphodiester bond is a central achievement of the spliceosome. Adenosine is strongly preferred in standard examples, but branch-point selection and unusual branch nucleotides should be considered in the context of the particular system rather than treated as chemically impossible exceptions.

The polypyrimidine tract contributes to recognition of the downstream region, especially in the mammalian system. Its interaction with protein factors helps coordinate the branch point and acceptor. Replacing several pyrimidines with purines can impair recognition even when the AG dinucleotide is completely unchanged. Conversely, strengthening one recognition interaction may sometimes compensate for a weak neighboring signal. This is why mutation experiments are often interpreted in pairs: changing a single site reveals a defect, and a carefully chosen second change can test whether the defect arose from a lost molecular interaction. The branch region, tract and terminal AG must be considered in their positional context, not as three unrelated words in a memorized list.

Although GU–AG describes the dominant canonical arrangement, there are genuine noncanonical terminal combinations. GC–AG introns can be processed by the major machinery, and some introns of the minor spliceosome possess AU–AC ends. However, the minority U12-type pathway also processes introns with GU–AG termini. Consequently, terminal dinucleotides do not provide a reliable classification of all introns into major or minor pathways. The wider splice-site and branch-point consensus, and the specific recognition apparatus required, distinguish the classes. These variants are particularly useful in examination statements that misuse words such as all, always or exclusively.

The labels 5′ and 3′ do not identify the two ends of the entire gene. A multi-intron transcript contains repeated pairs of 5′ and 3′ splice sites. The intron's 5′ end is downstream of its preceding exon, and its 3′ end is upstream of its following exon. On a DNA sequence printed in the opposite orientation, the apparent order of motifs may be reversed and complemented. A student should first convert the sequence to the RNA orientation used by the machinery. The next task is to understand how small nuclear RNAs and proteins interpret these distributed recognition signals and bring them into one catalytic center.
