4. The components of the spliceosome

The spliceosome is unusual among cellular enzymes because it is assembled from several pre-existing RNA–protein particles and many additional proteins onto its substrate. Its components can be reused, but the active arrangement is not a rigid structure waiting to cut every intron in an identical manner. Different interactions form, disappear and are replaced as the precursor advances toward the first and second chemical reactions. This dynamic organization solves a difficult problem: the initial recognition of a splice site requires one kind of RNA–RNA pairing, whereas catalysis requires a different arrangement in which reactive groups on the precursor face one another. A system in which all components remained fixed in their initial positions would have difficulty accomplishing both functions.

The small nuclear RNAs involved in the major pathway are conventionally named U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6. The letter U reflects their historical identification as uridine-rich small nuclear RNAs, rather than indicating that every nucleotide is uridine. Each RNA acts in association with proteins, forming a small nuclear ribonucleoprotein, abbreviated snRNP. Pronunciation of this abbreviation as snurp is common, but the important distinction is structural: an snRNA is the RNA molecule, whereas an snRNP includes the RNA and its associated proteins. The spliceosome includes several snRNPs plus factors that are not permanent constituents of a single snRNP. It is consequently inaccurate to describe five naked RNAs acting independently in solution as the complete spliceosome.

U1 is an early reader of the donor site. A sequence near the 5′ end of U1 snRNA forms complementary interactions with the region surrounding the 5′ splice boundary. The pairing is antiparallel, as are the complementary strands of nucleic acid helices. If a donor region becomes less complementary to U1, the initial interaction may weaken or be lost. Proteins in and around the particle stabilize recognition and cooperate with other factors, so a simple count of complementary bases does not completely determine the fate of a splice site. The interaction nevertheless provides a particularly clear example of how a short RNA can guide a biological machine to a sequence. The guide sequence and the guided reaction site are not the same molecule: U1 is supplied in trans, whereas the site being recognized lies within the precursor.

U2 recognizes the branch region at a later stage of assembly. Its sequence pairs with portions of the precursor around the branch-point adenosine in a way that leaves the reactive nucleotide protruding from the local helix. This unpaired or bulged adenosine can present its 2′ hydroxyl group to the catalytic environment. A perfectly uninterrupted helix at this position would not necessarily be desirable. In this mechanism, correct recognition involves arranging a controlled imperfection in base pairing, not maximizing pairing everywhere. The difference helps answer a common reasoning problem: a mutation that creates more complementary bases could impair rather than improve splicing if the mutation causes the essential branch nucleotide to be sequestered in an unsuitable arrangement.

U4 and U6 initially form a paired RNA system within a U4/U6 di-snRNP. Their association masks regions of U6 that will later take part in the catalytic RNA architecture. The inactive arrangement is compatible with transport and assembly of the spliceosomal particles without premature formation of the catalytic core. During activation, remodeling separates U4 from U6, and U4 leaves the assembling complex. U6 can then form the interactions required for the active state. This change explains why the absence of U4 from an active catalytic spliceosome is not evidence that U4 has no role in splicing. A component needed to construct or control an enzyme can depart before its chemical reaction begins.

U5 has a different task. It helps hold and position exon sequences so that the appropriate termini remain aligned during catalysis. At the first reaction, the upstream exon is separated from the intron, yet it must not diffuse away or be lost before the second reaction. Interactions involving U5 and associated proteins help maintain the useful relationship between the exons and the catalytic site. A defect in exon positioning can therefore affect the ligation step even if initial splice-site recognition and the first cleavage have occurred. Because the same apparatus participates in both stages, actual mutations may disturb several steps, and the stage of failure must be inferred experimentally.

U6 contributes centrally to the active RNA structure. During activation, the donor site is transferred from its initial U1-associated environment to interactions involving U6. U6 also interacts with U2, forming a configuration that positions catalytic metal ions and aligns the substrate. U6 is not simply a replacement copy of U1: U1 primarily assists in early recognition, whereas U6 is a central part of the activated catalytic organization. A useful way of remembering the difference is to ask which RNA identifies the site initially and which RNA participates in arranging the site during chemical conversion. The answer changes with the assembly stage, even though the substrate's original nucleotide sequence remains the same.

Proteins are essential to the overall reaction. Some stabilize the small nuclear RNAs, others recognize precursor sequences, some hold the complex together, and others use nucleotide hydrolysis to drive changes in interactions. The Sm proteins associated with several spliceosomal snRNPs contribute to particle organization; U6 is instead associated with a different related set of proteins. Large scaffold proteins such as Prp8 help organize the active environment and contact components of the reaction machinery. The distinction between an RNA-based catalytic center and a protein-containing enzyme is therefore not contradictory. Both descriptions can be correct when they refer to different levels of the system. The biological machine is a ribonucleoprotein, and the chemistry at its active site is supported by a specific RNA architecture.

Additional recognition proteins help interpret the 3′ portion of the intron. In a standard mammalian model, SF1 can associate with the branch region during early assembly, and U2AF helps recognize the polypyrimidine tract and the neighboring acceptor region. The larger U2AF subunit is commonly described as interacting with the pyrimidine tract, and the smaller subunit as contributing to recognition near the AG. These names are helpful only if attached to a mechanism. Without the recognition of downstream features, a donor site could be coupled to an unsuitable acceptor farther along the RNA. Coordinating signals from both ends reduces the number of acceptable combinations. Yeast and other organisms use related machinery but differ in the relative contributions of these features and factors.

The inventory of snRNPs should be interpreted dynamically rather than as a list of obligatory simultaneous active-site residents. If one isolates an early complex, U1 may be present and the U4/U6 pair may not yet have joined. If one isolates an activated complex, U1 and U4 may already have departed. Both results are consistent with the five major snRNAs being required for the normal cycle. In an experimental question, therefore, the answer to whether a component is present depends on the assembly state being investigated. This is the conceptual preparation needed to follow the progression from an uncommitted precursor to a catalytically competent machine.

5. Assembly: from splice-site selection to an active complex

Assembly begins with the unprocessed precursor, but the precursor is not simply a straight strand displayed on a page. It folds locally and is associated with RNA-binding proteins. Sites become available as the polymerase synthesizes the RNA, and the accessibility of a potential donor or branch region can affect recognition. An exam-level mammalian model divides assembly into early recognition, stable recruitment of U2, addition of the remaining major particles and activation. These stages are often denoted E, A, B and activated B complexes, followed by catalytic and post-catalytic states. The names are useful for ordering observations, not for implying that every intron in every organism traverses exactly the same set of experimentally isolated complexes.

At the early stage, U1 recognizes the 5′ splice site, while factors near the other end help identify the branch region, pyrimidine tract and acceptor. These interactions establish a preliminary relationship between regions that can be separated by hundreds or thousands of nucleotides. In the simplest isolated intron, this preliminary relationship is easy to draw as an arc connecting its boundaries. In a real multi-exon RNA, several possible interactions may coexist or compete. Early splice-site recognition is a commitment-building process rather than the moment of covalent cleavage. A mutation that prevents formation of this early assembly is predicted to reduce the abundance of all later intermediates that depend on it.

Stable association of U2 with the branch region marks the classical A complex. Remodeling of earlier interactions permits U2 to establish its branch-site pairing, replacing the initial branch recognition arrangement. The branch adenosine is left positioned for its later chemical function. ATP-dependent events contribute to this transition, even though no covalent RNA cleavage occurs simply because ATP has been hydrolyzed. This distinction is useful when interpreting experiments in which a complex accumulates in the absence of a nucleotide energy source. If the system cannot make a necessary conformational transition, recognition may take place without progressing to the eventual product.

The U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP joins the assembling complex. This particle brings the U4–U6 paired system and U5 together with their associated proteins. At this stage, all five major snRNA species may be represented in a pre-catalytic arrangement. Yet the catalytic configuration has not been fully established. It would be chemically risky to allow an active RNA catalytic center to act before the intended substrate positions were selected. Assembly establishes the necessary contacts first; remodeling subsequently permits the reactive groups to approach in a productive geometry. In a sequence of gel-separated or purified complexes, addition of these components can be monitored without assuming that the appearance of all five RNAs is equivalent to product formation.

Activation requires major changes in RNA–RNA interactions. The initial U1–donor association is disrupted and the 5′ splice site becomes associated with the U6-containing catalytic environment. The U4–U6 interaction is dismantled, permitting U6 to form new intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, including pairing with U2. U4 departs. U5 helps position the exon ends, and proteins organize the newly formed active architecture. In this progression, recognition and catalysis are linked by an ordered handoff rather than by the permanent binding of one guide particle. An RNA helicase or related ATP-dependent remodeling factor can facilitate the exchange of pairing partners, showing how energy input helps direct a chemical system through different conformational states.

The distinction between helicase-like remodeling and chemistry is important. ATP-dependent RNA remodeling factors alter RNA–RNA or RNA–protein contacts. In many cases they do not behave like simple helicases moving along an unlimited nucleic acid substrate; instead, their activity can rearrange a local ribonucleoprotein structure. For NET-level reasoning, it is sufficient to understand that ATP hydrolysis can help release an interaction, change the relative positions of components or drive progression through a checkpoint. The exact named remodeling factor is not needed for every problem unless it is specified. If a question describes a mutation that prevents U4 release, the expected direct consequence is an activation defect, not failure to recognize the original 5′ site by U1.

An activated complex must place the first scissile phosphodiester bond near the branch-point nucleophile. This requires bringing together parts of one RNA that were previously far apart in the linear sequence. The chemical reaction depends on spatial geometry, so a site can be recognized correctly yet remain catalytically unavailable if the complex cannot arrange it. Conversely, a molecule that is capable of phosphotransfer chemistry is not necessarily an efficient splicing catalyst if it lacks the means to recognize and align the appropriate substrate. This separation of recognition and reaction provides a powerful way to interpret mutant phenotypes. Instead of asking only whether splicing decreased, one asks whether substrate binding, assembly, activation or one of the actual chemistry steps was disrupted.

After the first transesterification, the resulting complex contains two RNA products in an intermediate relationship: an upstream exon with a newly exposed 3′ hydroxyl and a branched intron still attached to the downstream exon. Remodeling helps establish the second-step arrangement. The upstream exon is brought into a productive relationship with the acceptor bond. After exon ligation, the mature exon-containing RNA must be released, the excised intron must be removed, and the snRNP components must be returned to a usable state. Splicing is a cycle in the sense that the machinery can be recycled, not in the sense that an excised intron is normally reinserted into the same messenger RNA.

A useful experiment can distinguish between failure of initial assembly and failure of catalytic activation. Suppose a researcher supplies a normal precursor to a nuclear extract under conditions that allow the spliceosome to function. Protein or RNA association assays indicate that U1 and U2 bind, and an assay for the tri-snRNP indicates that it is also recruited. The unprocessed RNA accumulates, however, with no detectable first-step intermediates. A mutation affecting an activation factor is consistent with the observations. A defect in the initial donor signal would be less consistent because normal recruitment of U1 was observed. Nevertheless, failure of the chemical first step after successful activation would also be possible. A further measurement of U4 association or of the active configuration would help discriminate between those alternatives.

Consider another case in which a normally processed transcript contains two introns and a mutation disrupts the first donor site. One should not automatically conclude that the second intron will remain unspliced. Assembly for different introns can be at least partly independent; it is possible to recover a precursor that retains the first intron but has lost the second. The processing of one intron may also influence recognition of its neighbor, especially when splice sites cooperate across an exon. The result depends on transcript architecture and assembly timing. A diagram that always removes introns in strict order from the 5′ end is an oversimplification unless the question explicitly establishes that order.

6. Splicing in a transcribing nucleus

The nuclear environment brings transcription and RNA processing into proximity. The carboxy-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II consists of repeated sequences that undergo changes in phosphorylation state during transcription. Proteins associated with RNA processing can interact, directly or indirectly, with the transcription machinery or nascent RNA. This organization makes it possible for a newly synthesized donor region to acquire recognition factors before the polymerase has completed the downstream portion of the gene. Once the branch and acceptor regions become available, additional assembly events can occur. Splicing can also be completed after transcription, so co-transcriptional processing is a common organizational principle rather than an absolute rule for every intron.

The direction of polymerase travel introduces an important constraint. The upstream portion of a precursor becomes available first. A downstream acceptor cannot be used before it has been synthesized and is accessible. Yet a long intron may require considerable time to transcribe, and a weak early site may compete with a stronger alternative after further RNA emerges. The rate of transcription can thus influence which choices become available at different times. In some contexts a slower polymerase gives additional time to recognize a weak site before a competing downstream site appears; in other contexts it has a different effect because regulatory binding, RNA folding or local sequence organization changes the competition. No universal equation relates slower elongation to increased exon inclusion in all transcripts.

In organisms with long introns and relatively short exons, recognition may initially occur across an exon. Protein contacts and RNA recognition on its upstream and downstream sides can cooperate to define it as a segment to retain. This mode is called exon definition. The precursor ultimately undergoes chemical reactions across introns, so the initial cross-exon recognition must be reconciled with productive cross-intron assembly. In organisms whose introns tend to be short and whose exons are not the convenient small units, recognition across an intron can be particularly important. This distinction explains why weakening a donor immediately downstream of an exon can sometimes lead to skipping of that exon, rather than only to retention of the downstream intron.

For example, suppose exon 2 is flanked by two long introns and requires recognition of its upstream acceptor and downstream donor to be efficiently defined. A mutation in its downstream donor reduces recruitment of U1. If cooperative exon recognition fails, the upstream exon may be joined directly to exon 3, bypassing exon 2. The same mutation in a different architecture might instead cause accumulation of a transcript retaining the downstream intron or activation of a nearby cryptic donor. All three outcomes are mechanistically possible. A prediction in an examination should take account of the information given about the available competing sites and the method used to identify the product.

Splicing therefore cannot be understood merely as the conversion of a completed string of RNA letters into a shorter string. It is a spatially and temporally organized process involving a changing substrate, multiple recognition partners and a catalytic machine assembled in stages. This framework explains why the next question, how the chemical bonds are actually exchanged, can be addressed independently from how the sites were first identified. The chemical mechanism has an invariant logic even though the upstream selection of a particular site is subject to variation.
