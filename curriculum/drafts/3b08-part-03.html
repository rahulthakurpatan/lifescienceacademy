7. The chemical foundation of the two reactions

The chemistry of an RNA backbone provides the basis for understanding both splicing reactions. Consecutive nucleotides within an ordinary RNA chain are connected through a phosphate group that forms ester bonds with two ribose sugars. One connection involves the 3′ oxygen of an upstream nucleotide, and the other involves the 5′ oxygen of the downstream nucleotide. The phosphate lies between them, and the conventional description is a 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. During splicing, two specific phosphodiester linkages are replaced. Instead of cleaving every bond within the unwanted sequence and reconstructing the exons from separate nucleotides, the machinery exchanges the oxygen attached to each target phosphate. Most bonds within the exons and intron remain untouched.

A transesterification is a reaction in which an ester linkage is exchanged for another ester linkage. In the RNA context, a suitably positioned hydroxyl-derived oxygen attacks a phosphorus center and displaces an oxygen previously attached to that phosphorus. A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor, whereas the phosphorus center is electrophilic. This description should not be mistaken for the claim that the free hydroxyl group reacts rapidly with the backbone under ordinary cellular conditions. The molecular environment must permit the reaction to occur at a useful rate, and the correct attacking oxygen, target phosphate and departing oxygen must be arranged appropriately. Catalysis improves the accessibility and chemical suitability of this transition.

A good mechanistic representation follows the identity of the atoms rather than memorizing two arrows without knowing their meaning. Suppose the RNA contains upstream exon E1, intron I and downstream exon E2. The phosphate at the E1–I junction is the first target. It joins the 3′ oxygen of the last nucleotide of E1 to the 5′ oxygen of the first nucleotide of I. The phosphate at the I–E2 junction is a different target and joins the 3′ oxygen of the last intronic nucleotide to the 5′ oxygen of the first nucleotide of E2. After both reactions, the phosphate originally present at the second junction becomes the phosphate connecting E1 to E2. The phosphate originally present at the first junction becomes part of the branch linkage inside the excised intron.

These reactions do not require the introduction of an external nucleotide triphosphate to make each new phosphodiester connection. At the level of the net chemical transformations, one ester bond is replaced by another, so the reaction differs fundamentally from nucleotide polymerization. In transcription, an existing 3′ hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphate of an incoming nucleotide triphosphate and pyrophosphate is released. In pre-mRNA splicing, the reacting phosphorus centers are already part of the precursor backbone. The spliceosome nevertheless uses ATP during its assembly, activation, quality control, rearrangements and disassembly. The distinction is between energetic expenditure by the complete catalytic cycle and the direct chemical requirements of each of its two bond exchanges.

Metal ions play an important role in phosphoryl transfer. The active architecture organizes divalent metal ions, normally magnesium in the biological context, in a way that helps position reacting groups, stabilize charges and facilitate the departure of leaving groups. The splicing reactions are consistent with a two-metal-ion catalytic framework. The fact that the active center is an RNA-based structure does not imply that free RNA in solution will automatically reproduce the entire reaction. RNA folding, ion binding and stabilization by proteins are indispensable features of the intact machine. It is also not valid to infer that magnesium only serves as a nonspecific salt: changing ion identity or the coordinating groups can have effects on the chemistry that are distinct from changes in gross ionic strength.

When considering a chemical reaction at a charged phosphate, think of the activation barrier as the energetic difficulty of reaching a reactive arrangement of atoms. Catalytic interactions can lower the effective barrier without being consumed in the final product. Energy input to remodel the enzyme can change how frequently the proper arrangement is reached, while the catalytic groups make the bond exchange itself possible. These are cooperating processes, not mutually exclusive explanations. This distinction helps resolve a common apparent paradox: a spliceosome can be an ATP-consuming machine even though its fundamental phosphoryl-transfer reactions are not driven by direct ATP cleavage at each junction.

8. First transesterification: creating the lariat intermediate

The activated spliceosome arranges the 5′ splice site and the branch-point adenosine near the catalytic center. The branch adenosine is internal to the intron and already joined to its neighboring nucleotides through the normal RNA backbone. Its 2′ hydroxyl is not the same as a 3′ hydroxyl at the end of an RNA molecule. This internal 2′ hydroxyl provides the nucleophile for the first step. Its oxygen attacks the phosphorus at the donor boundary, the phosphate connecting the upstream exon with the first nucleotide of the intron. Productive alignment brings together these two distant positions in the folded and protein-associated precursor.

As the reaction proceeds, the bond between that phosphate and the upstream exon's 3′ oxygen is replaced by a bond between the phosphate and the branch adenosine's 2′ oxygen. The upstream exon is released from its original covalent connection to the intron and acquires an available 3′ hydroxyl at its end. It remains associated with the spliceosome, because it will supply the attacking group in the next reaction. The intron's first nucleotide is now connected through its 5′ phosphate to the 2′ oxygen of the internal branch adenosine. Since the ordinary backbone between the intron's first nucleotide and the branch point remains intact, this additional connection closes a loop.

The branched RNA has the general shape of a lasso, or lariat. The loop extends from the beginning of the intron along the ordinary backbone to the branch region. The remaining downstream part of the intron forms a tail that continues into the downstream exon until the second reaction takes place. In a conventional diagram, the branch adenosine lies at the point where the new 2′–5′ linkage meets the normal 3′–5′ RNA backbone. It therefore has three backbone connections rather than the two normally encountered at an internal nucleotide. A lariat is not simply a covalently closed circular RNA. It has a branch and a tail, and the chemistry at its branch is distinct from an ordinary 3′–5′ backbone junction.

The first reaction generates two covalently separate RNA molecules, even though both can remain within one ribonucleoprotein complex. One consists of the upstream exon, or the already processed exon-containing portion of the RNA upstream of the cut. The other consists of the lariat-form intron still covalently attached to the downstream exon-containing portion. A question that asks what is released at this step must distinguish release from a covalent bond from release into solution. The exon has been separated from the intron chemically, but the spliceosome retains it for the second reaction. A question asking whether the intron has been fully excised should be answered in the negative: the 3′ junction has not yet been replaced.

It is possible to design a hypothetical precursor in which the normal branch adenosine has its 2′ hydroxyl replaced by a hydrogen atom, leaving much of the surrounding recognition sequence unchanged. If this is the only usable branch site, the first chemical reaction should be strongly impaired because the intended attacking oxygen is missing. This prediction follows from the chemical mechanism and does not depend on a memorized association between adenine and splicing. It also illustrates why an adenosine base alone is not sufficient; the ribose group is part of the functional nucleophile. If alternative branch sites are available, however, they might support residual splicing, so the absence of a particular intermediate cannot be interpreted without considering substrate design.

Replacing the donor region with a sequence that cannot be recognized is a different kind of perturbation. It may also block the appearance of the lariat intermediate, but it does so before the active arrangement has been formed. If the two mutations have identical effects on the final mature RNA concentration, a simple endpoint measurement will not distinguish their causes. Observing assembly intermediates or testing a compensatory change in a recognition RNA would provide additional evidence. This principle is common in biochemistry: a change in final product can originate from more than one step, and identification of the limiting step requires intermediate or binding measurements.

The topology of the intermediate itself provides mechanistic evidence. Branched molecules often migrate anomalously on denaturing electrophoresis relative to ordinary linear molecules of equal nucleotide count. That property can help identify a candidate lariat intermediate, but the mobility of a band alone is not an unambiguous structural identification. A branch-specific cleavage or debranching reaction and mapping of the branch junction provide stronger evidence. It is also useful to consider which exon-associated sequences are present in the branched intermediate. Detection of an intron together with the downstream exon but not the upstream exon matches the predicted result of the first reaction much more closely than an observation of a short intron-only RNA.

9. Second transesterification: precise ligation of the exons

The first reaction generates a new reactive terminus on the upstream exon. During the next stage the spliceosome rearranges its substrate and catalytic contacts so that this 3′ hydroxyl can approach the phosphate at the acceptor boundary. This is the phosphate linking the final intronic nucleotide to the first nucleotide of the downstream exon. Unlike the first step, which uses an internal 2′ hydroxyl and produces an unusual linkage, the second step uses a terminal 3′ hydroxyl and restores the familiar arrangement of the RNA backbone in the mature message.

The 3′ oxygen of the upstream exon attacks the acceptor phosphate. The bond between the phosphate and the 3′ oxygen of the intron's last nucleotide is replaced by a bond between the phosphate and the upstream exon's 3′ oxygen. The downstream exon remains connected to its original 5′ phosphate, but that phosphate is now connected to the upstream exon rather than to the intron. The new exon–exon junction is therefore a normal 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. The branch within the intron survives this reaction. The excised intron retains its unusual internal linkage and now has a free 3′ end where it was formerly attached to the downstream exon.

Notice that the newly produced exon junction does not require a DNA template to direct its ligation. The relevant RNA sequences are already present, and the machinery holds their ends in the correct spatial arrangement. Nor does the normal spliceosomal mechanism require a separate ATP-dependent RNA ligase to attach the exons after an independent hydrolytic intron cleavage. Both removal and joining are accomplished by the coordinated phosphotransfer reactions. The two chemical events preserve the identity and orientation of the retained nucleotides, while changing which nucleotide neighbors lie next to one another.

Consider a lesion that selectively prevents the upstream exon's new 3′ hydroxyl from participating in the second reaction after the first has completed. A normal mature product cannot form at that junction under the stated assumptions. The upstream exon and the branched intron–downstream-exon intermediate are expected to accumulate if they are stable and other processing pathways do not remove them. This is fundamentally different from the result of a complete first-step block, which would prevent both intermediate species from appearing. In practice, a mutation at the 3′ splice region can influence earlier assembly as well as the second reaction, so an observed mutation cannot be assigned a second-step-specific effect merely because it is located near the acceptor.

Another possible perturbation is a change in the exon-positioning activity of U5. If this change specifically compromises the productive alignment of the two exon ends, the second reaction can be inefficient despite initial recognition and first-step chemistry. The prediction is conditional: U5 interacts with the substrate through multiple stages, so a severe disruption can have additional consequences. A researcher would compare the amounts of intact precursor, first-step products and mature RNA, preferably over time, while checking whether overall levels of the splicing apparatus remain comparable. A defect in final product accompanied by substantial accumulation of first-step products is more informative than a decrease in mature RNA alone.

In both reactions, the phosphate that remains attached to the downstream side of the original bond is transferred to a new upstream oxygen. The attacking oxygen in the first reaction belongs to an internal intronic nucleotide, whereas in the second it belongs to the end of the first exon. This parallelism helps students derive the lariat structure without memorizing a complicated image. Draw the starting chain, mark the two boundary phosphates, identify the attacking hydroxyl for step one, preserve all other connections, and then identify the free exon hydroxyl for step two. If the resulting drawing contains a newly created 2′–5′ bond in the mature exon–exon junction, the construction is wrong. The exceptional linkage belongs to the removed intron.

The completed spliceosomal reaction is followed by product release and machinery recycling. The removed lariat is often debranched by an enzyme that hydrolyzes the special 2′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. Once linearized, the intron can be degraded by cellular RNA-processing and degradation pathways. Certain introns can contribute to stable functional RNAs or other processing products, but those outcomes should not be generalized to all excised introns. The reason debranching is relevant is structural: the RNA topology created by the first reaction poses a distinctive problem for ordinary linear RNA turnover. Debranching is not required to create the normal exon–exon linkage itself; it acts on a product after the two core reactions.

An instructive conservation exercise tracks nucleotide counts. Suppose a precursor consists of a 140-nucleotide upstream exon, a 260-nucleotide intron and a 190-nucleotide downstream exon. Before splicing there are 590 nucleotides in one RNA chain, ignoring terminal modifications. After the first step, the free upstream exon contains 140 nucleotides and the branched intron–exon intermediate contains 450. After the second step, the ligated exons contain 330 nucleotides and the excised intron contains 260. The total is still 590 immediately after the reactions, before degradation. The chemistry rearranges connectivity; it does not destroy 260 nucleotides as individual monomers at the moment of splicing. This distinction is useful when interpreting mass conservation in a cell-free reaction.

10. What makes the spliceosome a catalytic RNA system?

Historically, discovering small nuclear RNAs associated with the splicing machinery suggested that they might do more than guide substrate recognition. Base pairing explains how some regions of the precursor are selected, but it does not establish which molecular groups lower the barrier to the actual reaction. Distinguishing a structural or guide RNA from a catalytic RNA requires evidence that the RNA contributes directly to the active chemistry. The fact that removal of an snRNA abolishes splicing is not sufficient by itself, because the RNA might be required only to assemble the catalytic protein or position the substrate.

Several lines of evidence support the central role of the spliceosomal RNAs. U2 and U6 form an RNA architecture with features reminiscent of catalytic group II introns. The U6-containing active site is closely associated with the catalytic metal ions. A 2013 study by Fica and colleagues investigated metal-ion coordination during nuclear pre-mRNA splicing and provided direct chemical evidence supporting catalytic roles of U6 RNA. The important inference was not merely that an RNA could bind a metal, but that particular RNA groups contribute to positioning metals involved in the reaction. Structural and biochemical evidence together therefore support a ribonucleoprotein enzyme whose catalytic center is RNA-based. A full discussion of RNA catalysis belongs to the later node on ribozymes.

One approach to testing metal-dependent chemistry uses a change from an oxygen-containing ligand to a sulfur-containing group. The sulfur substitution alters preferences for metal-ion interactions. If a suitably chosen metal restores activity in the modified system relative to an appropriate control, this can support a direct interaction between the metal and the substituted site. Such reasoning requires controls because a metal ion might also influence assembly, folding or other features of the reaction. The examination-level lesson is how a chemical perturbation, together with a specific rescue pattern, can discriminate direct participation from a merely correlative observation. It does not require mastering the detailed experimental construction of the modified substrates.

Modern structural observations are complementary to such perturbation experiments. A structural model may place the relevant RNA groups, substrate phosphates and metal ions near one another in an active complex. That arrangement is consistent with a proposed reaction mechanism, but a static structure cannot by itself reveal every transient interaction or prove that each nearby group contributes to catalysis. Functional changes and chemical rescue help make that connection. Conversely, a biochemical experiment may establish the importance of a group without fully specifying the three-dimensional arrangement. Integrating the two forms of evidence yields a stronger mechanistic explanation than treating either alone as a complete proof.

The central catalytic role of RNA also has an evolutionary implication. Group II introns can carry out related chemistry using folded RNA, and their active architecture has meaningful similarities to parts of the spliceosome. These correspondences support models relating the evolution of spliceosomal splicing to ancient self-splicing introns. They do not constitute direct observations of the historical evolutionary transition, and the detailed route remains an evolutionary reconstruction. In exam reasoning, distinguish the experimentally supported similarity of chemical mechanisms and active-site features from a hypothesis about how one ancestral system may have given rise to another. This prepares us to examine how the same major spliceosomal chemistry can produce alternative mature messages.
