11. Constitutive splicing and alternative splice-site choices

The reactions we have studied explain how two selected RNA regions can be connected. They do not yet explain why those regions are chosen. When all relevant introns are removed according to the usual arrangement and the same set of exons is regularly joined, the process is described as constitutive splicing. When a precursor can be processed using different acceptable combinations of sites, the resulting variation is called alternative splicing. These terms describe the choices made by the processing system; the underlying spliceosomal chemistry can remain the same. The spliceosome does not need a completely new catalytic mechanism for each possible transcript, because its ordinary catalytic center acts on whatever donor and acceptor are properly selected and brought together.

Consider a gene whose RNA contains four exons, E1, E2, E3 and E4. Suppose E1 and E4 are always retained and E2 can be included or omitted. If E3 is also always retained, two products are possible under this simple rule: E1–E2–E3–E4 and E1–E3–E4. In the first RNA, there is a junction between E1 and E2 and a separate junction between E2 and E3. In the second, a new E1–E3 junction directly joins the flanking regions. Exon 2 is a cassette exon in this example. Its absence from one mature product does not mean that it was absent from the gene or that transcription skipped the genomic region. The excluded region was transcribed but was not retained in that particular product.

A cassette exon is sometimes described as being removed together with its neighboring introns. This is a convenient description of the final connectivity, but it must not be interpreted as a separate enzyme simply deleting a completed exon from a previously finished messenger RNA. If the spliceosome chooses the donor after E1 and the acceptor before E3, the intervening region comprising the intron after E1, E2 and the intron before E3 can behave as one excised region for that processing event. In the inclusion pathway, separate connections are made across the two flanking introns. The different products arise because different boundaries are paired, and a drawing should identify those boundaries rather than merely erase a rectangular block.

Not all alternative splicing involves the inclusion or omission of a complete predesignated exon. If a region contains two usable donor sites associated with the same downstream acceptor, the choice determines how far the upstream exon extends. Selecting a donor farther toward the 3′ end can retain additional nucleotides in that exon compared with choosing an upstream donor. Similarly, two usable acceptor sites paired with the same donor can alter where the downstream exon begins. Selecting an acceptor farther downstream removes a longer region and shortens the retained downstream segment. These relationships follow directly from the direction in which RNA is read. They are more reliable than attempting to memorize which end of an exon changes without drawing the relevant boundary.

Mutually exclusive exons illustrate another type of constraint. Imagine two neighboring candidate exons, X and Y, between constitutive exons A and B. A regulated pathway might produce A–X–B or A–Y–B, while avoiding products that contain both candidates or neither candidate. Such a constraint may arise through competition, incompatible local processing arrangements or coordinated regulation. The name mutually exclusive describes the resulting pattern, not one universal molecular explanation. When counting products, the restriction changes the calculation: two mutually exclusive exons contribute two choices, whereas two independently optional exons could contribute four combinations, including both and neither.

Intron retention occurs when a particular intervening region remains in an RNA product that would otherwise be expected to lose it. It must be interpreted carefully. A nuclear preparation containing incompletely processed RNA can appear to show abundant intron retention even when those RNAs are transient precursors rather than intentionally retained mature products. To distinguish regulated retention from a processing delay, one may examine the cellular compartment, the processing status of other introns, the transcript's ends and the persistence of the retained form. A retained intron can introduce an early termination codon, change the coding sequence, alter the untranslated region or prevent an RNA from reaching the cytoplasmic translation machinery. No single outcome follows from the label alone.

Changes in transcript ends can also contribute to the diversity of mRNAs derived from a locus. An alternative promoter may change which first exon is present, and alternative cleavage and polyadenylation may change the final RNA region. These events can interact with splicing, for instance when selection of a last exon is coupled to selection of a cleavage site. Nevertheless, the underlying operations must be kept distinct. A change in 3′ end formation alone is not proof of a change in internal splice-site choice. Likewise, two RNAs beginning at different transcription start sites do not necessarily establish alternative splicing. Sequence comparison and knowledge of the primary transcripts are needed to assign the mechanism.

Alternative splicing challenges an overly simple version of the relationship between gene number and protein diversity. A single gene can give rise to several mature RNA isoforms, and some of these RNAs may encode different proteins. Other differences may change RNA stability, localization or translation without changing the encoded amino acid sequence. Some isoforms may not give rise to stable proteins at all. Thus the number of observed RNA isoforms is not automatically the number of protein isoforms, and the number of theoretically possible splice combinations is not necessarily the number found in a living cell. Biological constraints and selective processing determine which potential products are actually made and maintained.

12. How RNA sequence and regulatory factors determine splice-site choice

The presence of consensus splice-site signals does not guarantee identical use of those sites in every transcript or every cell. Initial recognition is influenced by the affinity and accessibility of the RNA sequences, the concentrations and activities of RNA-binding proteins, and cooperation or competition among nearby sites. A strong donor and acceptor may support efficient assembly without extensive additional assistance. A weaker site may depend more strongly on auxiliary recognition. This dependence allows sequence features outside the core splice-site motifs to influence the final processing decision. The regulated result need not involve a change in the catalytic center; a change in which substrate arrangement reaches that center can be enough.

Auxiliary regulatory sequences are commonly classified by their positions and effects. Exonic splicing enhancers are sequences within an exon that promote its recognition in a particular context. Exonic splicing silencers have the opposite effect. Related enhancing and silencing sequences can occur in introns. These labels refer to observed functional effects rather than an immutable chemical property of a short sequence. A motif that enhances exon inclusion when placed at one position might not produce the same response in a different transcript. The regulatory proteins present, the neighboring recognition sites and the structure adopted by the RNA all influence the outcome.

Members of the serine/arginine-rich, or SR, protein family often promote recognition through interactions with RNA elements and other splicing factors. For example, a protein bound within a weak exon may help stabilize a recognition factor at a neighboring site, thereby supporting exon definition. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins, commonly referred to as hnRNP proteins, can act as regulators and frequently repress particular splicing events in appropriate contexts. It would be incorrect to turn these common tendencies into an absolute rule that every SR protein always enhances all splicing and every hnRNP protein always suppresses it. Effects depend on the protein, sequence, position and competing interactions. For the current node, the useful principle is that local RNA-binding proteins influence site choice by changing the probability of productive assembly.

A regulatory sequence can be distinguished from a trans-acting factor using genetic reasoning. A mutation in an enhancer sequence lies within the same precursor whose processing it influences; it is a cis-acting change. A mutation in the gene encoding an RNA-binding protein may affect many transcripts on different chromosomes because the protein can act on multiple precursor molecules. This is a trans-acting change. If two differently marked reporter transcripts are introduced into the same cell and only the reporter carrying a mutated enhancer changes its splicing pattern, that result is consistent with a local cis effect. If both reporters change when a regulatory protein is depleted, a shared trans influence is more plausible. Indirect effects remain possible, so rescue experiments and controls improve the inference.

RNA folding adds another layer of interpretation. A donor sequence may possess favorable primary-sequence complementarity to a recognition RNA but become partly hidden within an intramolecular helix. Changing a nearby sequence could expose the donor or conceal it without altering the donor itself. In a hypothetical compensatory mutation experiment, two sequence changes that individually disrupt a proposed intramolecular stem but restore pairing when combined could test the structural explanation. If both single mutants expose a site and the double mutant restores its concealment, the pattern supports a role for the structure rather than an effect of one individual nucleotide. Alternative explanations such as overlapping protein-binding motifs must still be considered.

Tissue-specific splicing can result from the same precursor encountering different regulatory environments. A factor abundant in one tissue may promote use of a weak site, while another factor present elsewhere may favor the competing choice. The underlying genomic sequence need not differ. Developmental changes in factor expression or activity can likewise change the isoform composition over time. Splicing variation is therefore a means of interpreting a gene in a cellular context. A useful biological comparison considers both the shared gene and the differing processing environment, rather than treating tissue-specific proteins as necessarily encoded by different genes.

Competition between sites is especially important when interpreting variants. Suppose a donor normally supports strong inclusion of an exon and a nearby cryptic donor has little use. A mutation weakening the normal donor may allow the cryptic site to be selected more frequently. A cryptic site is a potential site that is normally unused or used at a low level but can become more prominent under altered conditions. It need not have been created by the mutation itself. Conversely, a mutation can create a new candidate recognition motif in a region that previously lacked one. These are two distinct routes to abnormal junctions: revealing a pre-existing competitor and generating a new competitor. Sequencing the resulting RNA junction helps identify which boundary was actually used.

It is useful to distinguish a reduction in splicing efficiency from a redistribution among products. If a perturbation reduces all mature isoforms and increases unprocessed RNA, an impairment of a shared processing step is plausible. If total mature RNA remains approximately constant but the fraction containing a particular exon changes, altered choice is more plausible. Both patterns can occur together, and measured abundance also depends on RNA degradation. A well-designed comparison should therefore measure multiple species and avoid treating a single band or a single quantitative signal as sufficient evidence of the complete pathway.

13. What a splice choice means for the encoded product

The biological effect of a splice choice is determined not only by the RNA sequence removed or retained but also by its relationship to translation. In a protein-coding transcript, the ribosome interprets codons in a reading frame established at translation initiation. A change in the number of coding nucleotides before a downstream region can preserve that reading frame or alter it. When an internal coding segment of length divisible by three is removed, and the same initiation site and downstream coding region are used, the downstream reading frame is preserved. If the removed length is not divisible by three, the reading frame generally changes unless another event compensates for the difference. These are arithmetic consequences of triplet decoding rather than special rules invented for exon boundaries.

Suppose a cassette exon contributes exactly ninety coding nucleotides and lies entirely between the initiation and termination codons. Omitting it changes the length of the coding region by thirty triplets. The downstream frame remains the same. The resulting protein need not be functionally normal: removing thirty amino acid positions can eliminate a structural element, binding region or regulatory sequence. At the novel exon junction, a codon may also be reconstructed from nucleotides that were formerly separated by the skipped region, so one should not assert that the protein always differs only by a simple uninterrupted thirty-residue deletion with no junctional change. The frame calculation predicts the continuity of downstream decoding, not every detail of the new amino acid sequence.

If instead the omitted exon contributes ninety-one coding nucleotides, the difference leaves a remainder of one when divided by three. Under the same assumptions, decoding downstream of the new junction occurs in a changed frame. A premature stop may arise, but its exact location depends on the nucleotide sequence. It is not possible to infer the number of amino acids in the altered protein from the exon length alone. One must identify the initiation codon and translate the actual reconstructed coding sequence until the relevant stop codon. When an exon includes both untranslated and coding sequence, it is especially important not to apply the divisibility rule to its total length without checking which part contributes to the translated region.

Intron phase offers a compact way to reason about the relationship between splice boundaries and codons. A phase-zero intron separates two complete codons. A phase-one intron interrupts a codon after its first nucleotide, and a phase-two intron interrupts after its second nucleotide. These are properties of the position of an intron relative to a specified coding frame. For an internal coding exon bounded by two introns of the same phase, the contribution of that exon to the coding sequence has length divisible by three. To see why, represent positions of boundaries by their remainders upon division by three. The difference between two positions with equal remainder is a multiple of three. Consequently, matching flanking phases can favor a frame-preserving exclusion of a whole internal coding exon under the stated assumptions. The phase rule does not independently establish that the exclusion occurs or that the resulting protein is functional.

Some splice variants primarily alter untranslated regions. A changed 5′ untranslated region can influence translation initiation without changing the protein's coding sequence, and a changed 3′ untranslated region can alter binding of regulatory factors or affect the lifetime and location of the RNA. In other cases, altered processing generates a premature termination codon and the RNA becomes susceptible to surveillance pathways such as nonsense-mediated decay. The precise response depends on transcript architecture and cellular context. RNA export and detailed quality control are assigned to a later node, but this connection explains why an isoform's abundance is not determined by splice-site choice alone. Its lifetime after processing can change how frequently it is detected.

A meaningful application of the same principle is the comparison between genomic DNA and complementary DNA for expression of a eukaryotic protein in bacteria. If a genomic fragment contains introns that require a nuclear spliceosome, a bacterial expression system generally cannot perform the appropriate processing. A cDNA representing the mature, correctly spliced RNA can supply a continuous coding region instead, provided the other requirements of the expression system are satisfied. This is not a claim that every cDNA automatically produces a functional protein in bacteria, since initiation signals, folding and post-translational requirements can still limit expression. It isolates the contribution of splicing to one practical difference between the genomic sequence and the sequence used for expression.
