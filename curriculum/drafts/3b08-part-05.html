14. Quantitative reasoning about transcript diversity

Counting possible splice products requires a description of the allowed biological decisions before the calculation begins. The simplest counting problem contains constitutive terminal exons and a specified number of independent cassette exons between them. For each cassette exon there are two states, included and excluded. If all states are compatible with every other state and the order of the exons is fixed, the number of different exon-selection patterns for n independent cassette exons is 2 raised to the power n. The exponent counts decisions, not the total number of exons. The result follows from the multiplication principle: every choice for the first cassette can be combined with either choice for the second, and the same multiplication continues for each additional independent cassette.

For instance, take a precursor with six exons. Let the first and last be compulsory and the four internal exons independently optional. The maximum number of products under these assumptions is 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. There is exactly one product with no optional exons, and exactly one with all four. To count products containing exactly two of the four optional exons, choose two positions from four: the binomial coefficient 4 choose 2 is 4! divided by 2!2!, equal to 6. One does not multiply this value by the number of arrangements of the selected exons, because their order remains the genomic order. Counting all allowed inclusion numbers gives 1 + 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 16, an independent way of checking the original calculation.

More complex rules change the count. If a gene has three independently optional cassette exons, followed by a cluster from which precisely one of five exons must be retained, there are 2³ × 5 = 40 products, provided all those choices can coexist. If the cluster can also be omitted entirely, the factor is six instead of five and the count becomes 48. If the first optional exon must be present whenever the fifth member of the cluster is selected, the unrestricted calculation overcounts. Four of the eight cassette patterns exclude that first exon. Each of those four is incompatible with the fifth member. The resulting number under the new restriction is 40 − 4 = 36. This example demonstrates why the biological assumptions must be stated before an arithmetic shortcut is used.

One well-known biological illustration is the Drosophila melanogaster Dscam1 gene. Its variable exon clusters are conventionally described by 12, 48, 33 and 2 alternatives, respectively. The processing pattern selects one member from each cluster, while the constant exons supply the remainder of the transcript. If the four cluster choices are combined independently, the potential diversity is 12 × 48 × 33 × 2 = 38,016 combinations. The number is a combinatorial repertoire, not a claim that every fly cell simultaneously produces all 38,016 transcripts or that all products have equal abundance. Expression patterns and biological constraints can restrict actual usage. The example illustrates how structured, mutually exclusive choices can generate a large potential repertoire without requiring an equally large number of separate genes.

The biological consequence in Dscam1 is more than a large number. Different exon choices can specify variants of extracellular recognition regions or of a membrane-associated segment. The resulting family of related cell-surface proteins is important in neuronal recognition and wiring. This is a useful example of the difference between combinatorial potential and a set of proteins with a shared structural framework. It also contrasts with a cassette exon whose inclusion disrupts a reading frame and causes its transcript to be degraded. In the Dscam1 illustration, compatible alternatives are arranged in a way that can preserve a broad protein-domain organization, showing how exon architecture and splice regulation can cooperate.

Another quantity commonly used in interpreting alternative splicing is the fraction of measured molecules that include a selected cassette exon. For an ideal experiment that counts individual mature isoforms without bias, let I denote the number of molecules containing the exon and S the number lacking it. The inclusion fraction is I divided by the sum I + S. Multiplying by 100 expresses the value as a percentage, often termed percent spliced in or PSI. The two categories must be mutually exclusive and together exhaust the population being analyzed. If other isoforms cannot be unambiguously assigned, the denominator must be defined explicitly rather than treating every RNA measurement as belonging to one of the two classes.

In a hypothetical experiment, the control sample contains 180 inclusion molecules and 60 skipping molecules, while a treated sample contains 90 inclusion and 150 skipping molecules. Total measured molecules are 240 in both samples. The control inclusion percentage is 180 divided by 240 times 100, or 75%. The treated value is 37.5%. The difference is a decrease of 37.5 percentage points, while the relative decrease in inclusion fraction is 50% of the control value. The two quantities answer different questions. A decrease of 37.5 percentage points should not be described as a 37.5% relative reduction. Since the total mature count is unchanged in this idealized dataset, redistribution of isoform proportions is especially apparent, although the measurements do not alone establish a direct action on the spliceosome.

When junction reads rather than intact molecules are counted, the calculation needs care. Inclusion of a cassette exon creates two flanking junctions, whereas skipping creates one junction between the constitutive exons. Simply adding both inclusion-junction counts and comparing the sum with the skipping-junction count can give the inclusion isoform two opportunities to contribute. In an idealized dataset with equal sampling of junctions, a common simple approach is to average the two inclusion-junction counts before comparing with skipping. Suppose counts for the two inclusion junctions are 100 and 120 and the skipping junction count is 90. Their average is 110, and the estimated inclusion fraction is 110 divided by 200, or 55%. This calculation assumes comparable junction detectability; real sequencing analyses may account for effective lengths and other biases. If the two inclusion junction counts differ substantially, complex isoforms or measurement artifacts may require investigation.

Steady-state RNA abundance also depends on degradation. For a simple model with a production flux f in molecules per minute and first-order loss constant d in inverse minutes, the number N of molecules changes at rate f − dN. At steady state, production equals loss, so N = f/d. If two isoforms are produced at equal rates of 20 molecules per minute but one has a loss constant of 0.1 per minute and the other 0.2 per minute, their expected steady-state abundances are 200 and 100 molecules. A measurement of those abundances would suggest an inclusion fraction of two thirds if the first isoform were the inclusion form. Yet the production fractions were exactly equal. A steady-state abundance measurement cannot be equated with a direct measurement of processing flux unless degradation differences are excluded or accounted for. The model assumes a constant source, first-order losses and enough time to reach steady state.

The rate of splicing of a defined precursor can be treated with a simple kinetic model when the assumptions are suitable. In a hypothetical pulse experiment, a population of labeled precursor RNA is followed after labeling stops, and its only significant loss is a first-order conversion to product. If k is a constant effective conversion rate per minute, then the precursor fraction remaining after time t is exp(−kt). For a precursor fraction of 0.25 after 10 minutes, rearranging gives k = −ln(0.25)/10 = 0.1386 per minute. Its effective half-time is ln(2)/k, approximately five minutes. This value is an empirical description of the observed population under the model, not the rate constant of every individual assembly or catalytic transition. A multistep pathway, parallel degradation or a mixture of fast and slow precursors can invalidate a single-exponential interpretation.

15. Experiments that distinguish recognition, catalysis and splice-site choice

A mechanistic experiment is strongest when it is designed to distinguish two plausible explanations. A decrease in the abundance of a mature RNA could reflect slower transcription, slower splicing, faster degradation or defective export followed by turnover. To attribute the observation specifically to splicing, the investigator must look for the processing intermediates or junction changes predicted by that hypothesis. Experiments often compare the normal transcript with a defined altered transcript, or compare the same transcript in a normal and altered processing environment. A useful control should make an alternative explanation less likely, not simply add another sample to an experiment.

Compensatory base-pair changes provide a classical way to test direct recognition by U1. The prediction is that a mutation in the donor sequence which disrupts its pairing with U1 should reduce use of that donor, and a complementary mutation in U1 could restore the interaction. Zhuang and Weiner examined this principle in adenovirus E1A alternative donor sites in 1986. Their findings included strong suppression of one donor mutation but much weaker suppression of another. This difference was informative: direct base pairing is important, but restoring complementarity alone does not necessarily repair every feature of recognition. A mutation may disrupt other contacts or later interactions that are not restored by modifying U1. The experiment is a useful example of evidence for a specific interaction accompanied by evidence against an overly exclusive model of that interaction.

In a simplified hypothetical reporter experiment, wild-type precursor with normal U1 yields 90% correctly spliced RNA. Mutant precursor with normal U1 yields 15%, mutant precursor with a complementary U1 variant yields 72%, and mutant precursor with an unrelated U1 variant yields 17%. These figures are educational data, not results from the historical paper. The contrast between the two U1 variants supports sequence-specific rescue rather than a general effect of supplying additional U1. However, restoration is incomplete compared with the wild type. Measuring U1 variant abundance and testing whether it is assembled into functional particles would help rule out differences in expression. Testing the normal precursor with the complementary variant would additionally assess whether the changed U1 has a broad nonspecific effect on processing.

Cell-free splicing assays can reveal the order of the chemical transformations by following labeled precursor and products over time. A normal reaction should initially contain mostly precursor, then develop intermediate species, and eventually accumulate ligated exons and released intron. If a short-lived intermediate is rapidly converted, its abundance can remain low even if every precursor molecule passes through that state. Therefore, absence of a prominent band in a final-time sample does not demonstrate that the intermediate never existed. Early time points, appropriate labeling and conditions that selectively slow a later step can reveal a species that is otherwise difficult to detect. This logic is transferable to many enzyme pathways in which intermediate abundance depends on both formation and consumption rates.

Imagine a precursor with two exons of 100 and 150 nucleotides separated by an intron of 200 nucleotides. The unmodified transcript has 450 nucleotides; first-step products contain 100 and 350 nucleotides; second-step products contain 250 and 200 nucleotides. A label confined to the first exon detects the precursor, free first exon and mature RNA, but not the intron-containing intermediate that lacks the first exon. A label confined to the intron detects the precursor, branched intermediate and excised intron, but not the mature exon product. In a real gel the branched species may not migrate according to their nucleotide counts. Combining differently located labels with appropriate structural tests is consequently more informative than assigning every band exclusively on the basis of its apparent length.

A minigene reporter provides a convenient model for studying alternative exon usage. A cloned segment containing a cassette exon, its flanking constitutive exons and sufficient neighboring intronic sequences can be transcribed in a suitable cell. Investigators can change candidate regulatory motifs and compare the junctions formed. If deleting an enhancer reduces the proportion of inclusion RNA while leaving the relevant donor and acceptor intact, the enhancer is implicated in exon recognition. Nevertheless, a deletion may change spacing or RNA folding as well as remove a binding motif. Smaller targeted substitutions, preservation of length where feasible, restoration of the original sequence and comparisons in the same cell context help refine the conclusion. A minigene also lacks some features of its native chromosomal setting, so its behavior need not perfectly reproduce all regulation of the full endogenous gene.

Reverse-transcription PCR is particularly useful for interpreting simple cassette-exon reporters. Primers placed in constitutive exons flanking the variable exon can produce products of two lengths. Suppose the flanking portions included in the amplicon total 160 nucleotides and the alternative exon contributes 84 additional nucleotides. The skipping product is 160 base pairs and the inclusion product is 244 base pairs. A product of intermediate length could reflect use of an alternative internal site rather than mere partial inclusion in a population of molecules. Sequencing the product across its junction identifies the actual boundaries used. Endpoint PCR band brightness may not reliably equal molecule number, because shorter fragments can amplify differently and reactions can approach saturation. Quantitative claims require an appropriately calibrated measurement or a method that counts isoforms more directly.

Northern analysis using probes complementary to selected RNA regions can also help identify intermediates or isoforms. A probe against a constitutive exon may detect multiple products that share that exon. A probe against a variable exon detects only transcripts containing that region, subject to appropriate sequence specificity. An intronic probe may detect a retained-intron transcript, an unprocessed precursor or a released intron. Thus two signals at similar positions on a gel do not by themselves establish that the corresponding sequences occur in the same molecule. A targeted junction assay, or another method that determines connectivity directly, can answer that question. The distinction between detecting a sequence and demonstrating its neighbors is central to interpreting RNA processing evidence.

When testing an inhibitor or depletion of a spliceosomal component, a further difficulty is that the affected machinery may act on a large number of transcripts. A delayed cellular response could reflect secondary effects of altered gene expression rather than the immediate molecular consequence. An acute, carefully controlled cell-free experiment on a defined substrate can isolate a processing step more directly, while an intact-cell experiment can show the physiological importance of the perturbation. Neither replaces the other. In examination problems, note the timescale of an observed response, the specificity of the assay and whether normal transcription and RNA integrity have been checked. These observations help distinguish direct processing defects from indirect changes in RNA abundance.

16. Comparative splicing pathways and the connection to catalytic RNA

The major spliceosome accounts for the conventional processing mechanism described throughout this chapter, but it is not the only means of removing RNA introns. A minor spliceosome acts on a distinct class of introns in organisms that retain the corresponding machinery. It uses several different small nuclear RNAs, U11, U12, U4atac and U6atac, while sharing U5 with the major system. U11 and U12 have recognition roles analogous in broad outline to those of U1 and U2. The minor system illustrates how related functional tasks can be accomplished by distinct sets of recognition and catalytic components. Some of its substrates have AU–AC boundaries, but others have GU–AG termini, so the sequence context is essential to identifying the pathway.

Group I and group II introns offer a different comparison. Certain representatives can catalyze their own excision in suitable conditions because the intron RNA itself folds into a structure capable of arranging the reacting groups. In a conventional group I example, a free guanosine species supplies the first attacking 3′ hydroxyl, and the guanosine becomes attached to the intron's 5′ end. The released upstream exon then supplies its 3′ hydroxyl for exon ligation. The primary excised group I intron product is linear, although subsequent reactions can produce other forms. In a conventional branching pathway of group II introns, an internal adenosine supplies a 2′ hydroxyl for the initial reaction, forming a lariat analogous to that seen in the spliceosome. Some group II introns can use alternative hydrolytic pathways, so lariat formation should be understood as the characteristic branching mechanism rather than an unconditional property of every possible reaction.

Self-splicing introns and spliceosomal introns differ in how the catalytic structure is supplied. A self-splicing intron's own sequence can encode much of the architecture required for its excision. The spliceosome instead assembles a catalytic arrangement from reusable small nuclear RNAs, proteins and the substrate. Many group I and II introns require or benefit from proteins in living cells to reach an active fold efficiently, despite the ability of certain representatives to carry out chemistry in protein-free laboratory systems. The term self-splicing therefore refers to an intrinsic catalytic capacity of the RNA under appropriate conditions, not an assertion that all naturally occurring introns in these classes function entirely without proteins. This comparison is a bridge to the later discussion of ribozymes and should not be confused with the unrelated cleavage and ligation pathways used to process many transfer RNA introns.

Differences between organisms provide context for the biological role of splicing. In many bacterial genes, a continuous coding region can be transcribed and translated without removal of conventional spliceosomal introns. In eukaryotic nuclei, RNA processing creates a separate level at which a gene's output can be modified before the transcript reaches the translation machinery. Introns and alternative processing are distributed unevenly among eukaryotes, however, and their relative frequency or importance cannot be inferred solely from organismal complexity. Many yeast genes lack introns, whereas numerous animal and plant genes contain them. Comparing the mechanisms rather than assigning a simple ranking of biological sophistication provides the more scientifically useful conclusion.

Splicing also contributes to the interpretation of genetic variants. A change within an intron may be far from a protein-coding triplet yet alter the amount or identity of functional messenger RNA. A change within an exon that appears synonymous when translated in isolation may disrupt a regulatory motif or splice-site recognition. Thus a sequence variant can affect gene expression through processing even when its direct codon-level effect is small or absent. A prediction of this kind should be tested by observing the actual RNA products rather than inferred solely from the variant's location. The principle connects molecular processing to inheritance and explains why knowledge of genomic sequence is necessary but sometimes insufficient to predict a gene's functional output.

Finally, place the entire process back into the flow of genetic information. A cell transcribes its DNA, selects processing sites through interactions among RNA and proteins, rearranges the precursor's connectivity, and releases products whose future depends on their composition and cellular handling. Splicing is simultaneously a problem of sequence recognition, enzyme assembly, chemical catalysis and selective information retention. Its two fundamental chemical reactions explain how a chosen intron is removed; regulated site selection explains how the same precursor can yield distinct products. The next processing mechanisms, RNA editing and maturation of ribosomal and transfer RNAs, alter RNA in different ways and must be distinguished from this exon-joining mechanism.
