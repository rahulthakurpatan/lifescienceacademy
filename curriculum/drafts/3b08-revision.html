Formula and assumption sheet

  • One intron: Lprecursor = LE1 + LI + LE2. Immediately after step one: LE1 and LI + LE2; after step two: LE1 + LE2 and LI. Ignore end modifications and degradation.
  • Independent optional exons: 2n products; exactly k included: n!/[k!(n−k)!]. Fixed order; all counted combinations must be allowed.
  • Independent mandatory exclusive clusters: multiply their numbers of choices. Subtract incompatible combinations when choices are conditional.
  • PSI = 100I/(I+S), where I and S are inclusion and skipping molecule counts. Use a justified count model for junction reads.
  • Coding-frame preservation for an internal coding deletion: removed nucleotide count ≡ 0 (mod 3), assuming unchanged start and downstream context.
  • Simple first-order conversion: P(t)/P(0)=e−kt; t1/2=ln(2)/k. Applies to constant effective rate, no competing losses.
  • Steady-state abundance N=f/d for constant production flux f and first-order loss constant d; equal flux does not imply equal abundance.

Twelve misconceptions to eliminate

  1. Exon means protein coding. Exons can contain untranslated regions and noncoding RNA sequences. Retention, not translation, defines their role.
  2. Splicing removes DNA. Ordinary pre-mRNA splicing rearranges RNA connectivity, leaving the genomic sequence intact.
  3. GU and AG are sufficient recognition signals. Short motifs recur by chance; broader signals and cooperative factors distinguish productive sites.
  4. All introns start GU and end AG. Noncanonical terminal patterns exist; minor-class recognition cannot be inferred from terminal dinucleotides alone.
  5. U1 is the central chemical catalyst. U1 helps early recognition; U6 and U2 participate in the active RNA architecture.
  6. All five major snRNAs remain together at catalysis. U1 and U4 depart during activation; their earlier functions remain essential.
  7. Branch A attacks through its 3′ hydroxyl. Its internal 2′ hydroxyl attacks in the first canonical reaction.
  8. Step one releases the complete intron. The intron is still attached to the downstream exon until step two.
  9. ATP supplies the exon-junction phosphate. The junction phosphate originates in the precursor; ATP-dependent remodeling assists the overall cycle.
  10. Every skipped exon frameshifts the protein. Coding length, position and reading frame determine the effect; untranslated exons may not change amino acid sequence.
  11. Every predicted splice combination is expressed. Combinatorial counts depend on assumptions; biological regulation and stability restrict observed products.
  12. A lariat band migrates like a linear molecule of equal length. Branch topology alters mobility; identification requires appropriate controls or structural evidence.

Active recall

  1. Draw a three-exon precursor and two excision reactions without changing exon order.
  2. Label the donor, branch A, polypyrimidine tract and acceptor in their 5′→3′ order.
  3. Explain why GU alone is insufficient to select a donor.
  4. Describe the roles of U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6 at different stages.
  5. Track both boundary phosphates into the two final products.
  6. Explain how ATP-dependent remodeling differs from the chemistry of phosphotransfer.
  7. Predict the species that accumulate during a selective second-step block.
  8. Draw exon skipping, alternative donor choice and mutually exclusive exons.
  9. Derive the count of exactly three included exons among six independent cassette exons.
  10. Explain why equal total transcript abundance does not establish identical splicing.
  11. Design a control for a compensatory donor/U1 experiment.
  12. Compare group I and group II initiation without equating self-splicing with universally protein-free processing.

Mastery checklist

Before moving to RNA editing and other RNA maturation pathways, make sure you can independently reconstruct the two chemical steps, predict the effect of losing a specific recognition interaction, read a labeled-intermediate experiment, distinguish RNA abundance from processing flux, count constrained splice choices, predict reading-frame consequences and explain the main differences between spliceosomal and self-splicing pathways.
