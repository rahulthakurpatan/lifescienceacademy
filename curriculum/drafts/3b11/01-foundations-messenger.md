## 1. One chemical language, many cellular jobs

A cell must use its hereditary information without losing the ability to pass that information to its descendants. DNA provides a comparatively stable record, but the record alone cannot carry out most of the work of the cell. A sequence specifying an enzyme must be made available to a molecular machine that assembles amino acids. That machine must distinguish the meaning of one triplet from another, bring the appropriate amino acids into a common reaction chamber and form peptide bonds. Before the machine itself can function, several of its components have to be synthesized, folded and assembled. RNA participates in each of these tasks. Some RNAs act as readable copies of instructions. Other RNAs are permanent or reusable parts of a machine, and still others recognize particular molecular targets so that those targets can be processed. The major RNA classes represent different solutions to these problems, rather than different versions of the same chemical substance.

Suppose that a bacterial cell produces two proteins in response to a nutritional change. It does not need to construct two new varieties of ribosome, one dedicated to each protein. It can make two different messages and use its existing ribosomes to interpret them. The messages are messenger RNAs, or mRNAs. A ribosome has its own RNAs, known as ribosomal RNAs or rRNAs. An rRNA does not normally carry the instructions for making the protein being synthesized. Its nucleotide sequence specifies the shape and chemical properties of a working part of the ribosome. Transfer RNA, abbreviated tRNA, provides the physical connection between the message and an amino acid. When the cell stops producing one message and starts producing another, the rRNAs and tRNAs can continue to serve. This separation between replaceable information and reusable machinery is a central organizational advantage of gene expression.

A similar distinction operates in the eukaryotic nucleus. A newly transcribed precursor mRNA may contain introns that must be removed. Small nuclear RNAs, or snRNAs, help identify the relevant portions of the precursor and support the reactions that join exons. Their sequences are not being decoded into a chain of amino acids during that operation. They act through their own structures and their ability to pair with another RNA. In the nucleolus, small nucleolar RNAs, or snoRNAs, guide chemical changes to selected nucleotides of ribosomal RNA and help with aspects of its maturation. Here again the information in an RNA sequence acts directly, without first being converted into a polypeptide. This makes it important to distinguish expression of an RNA gene from translation of an RNA molecule. Both a protein-coding gene and a tRNA gene can be expressed through transcription, but only the former has a protein product as its usual endpoint.

The same nucleotide alphabet underlies all these molecules. An RNA molecule contains ribose sugars, phosphate groups and nitrogenous bases, most commonly adenine, guanine, cytosine and uracil. There is no chemical rule that an RNA containing these bases must become a message. Instead, the order of nucleotides can determine a set of codons, a stable fold, a pattern recognized by a protein or a surface able to contact another nucleic acid. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. An mRNA can contain structured regions that influence its use, and a tRNA uses a short sequence to recognize the message. The distinction between classes depends primarily on what the whole molecule does in its biological setting.

One useful first question when encountering an unfamiliar RNA is therefore what happens to it after it is made. A transcript that serves as a template for a ribosome and specifies a polypeptide is an mRNA. A folded RNA retained in a ribosomal subunit is an rRNA. A small molecule bearing an anticodon and a terminal amino acid attachment site is a tRNA. An RNA whose recognition sequence aligns an intron or guides a modification is a functional RNA acting at the level of RNA processing. These criteria are much more reliable than a rule based only on length or abundance. Although many tRNAs are short and many mRNAs are long, size alone cannot determine the biological role of an RNA. A short mRNA and a longer noncoding RNA are perfectly possible.

## 2. Why RNA can fold, recognize and transmit information

Every nucleotide contains a base connected to the first carbon of a sugar. Ribose has hydroxyl groups at both the second and third carbons. DNA uses deoxyribose, which lacks the hydroxyl group at the second carbon. An RNA chain connects successive sugars through phosphodiester bonds involving their third and fifth carbons. This connectivity gives the chain a chemical direction, conventionally described from its 5′ end to its 3′ end. These terms refer to sugar positions and not to the number of bases in a segment. The orientation remains meaningful when the RNA folds back on itself, when two RNAs pair or when a protein attaches to one end. In a diagram, therefore, the ends should be labelled before reasoning about sequence recognition.

The ribose 2′ hydroxyl group helps explain both the vulnerability and the versatility of RNA. Under alkaline conditions it can be deprotonated and attack the adjacent phosphate within the same chain. Cleavage may generate a cyclic phosphate intermediate and a free hydroxyl terminus. This internal reaction is unavailable to ordinary DNA because its corresponding sugar position lacks that group. Consequently, the two nucleic acids can behave very differently in chemical stability tests even though their phosphate backbones appear similar. RNA is not necessarily short-lived in a living cell, however. A tightly folded RNA protected by proteins can persist far longer than an unprotected message. Chemical susceptibility and biological lifetime are related but are not interchangeable properties.

The bases make it possible for RNA to recognize nucleic acid sequences. Adenine generally pairs with uracil, and guanine with cytosine, through complementary hydrogen-bonding arrangements. Guanine and uracil also form a common noncanonical pair in RNA. Such a pair has a different geometry from the usual Watson–Crick pairs, but it can still be accommodated in many helical contexts. Adjacent bases interact through stacking, and these interactions contribute substantially to helix stability. It is not sufficient to count hydrogen bonds and assume that the RNA with the greatest total must always form the most stable structure. The sequence context, stacking, ionic conditions and structural constraints all contribute to the free energy.

When complementary stretches are present in one chain, the molecule can form a stem by pairing those stretches in opposite directions. A connecting segment at the end of a stem can remain unpaired, creating a hairpin loop. A nucleotide or short region protruding from one side of a helix forms a bulge. If both sides contain unpaired regions within a helix, the feature is called an internal loop. Multiple helices can meet at a junction. These apparently irregular features are not simply defects in a perfect helix. Their exposed bases can participate in sequence recognition, and their geometry can orient distant portions of the RNA. A loop in a tRNA, for example, exposes a triplet that must interact with a message, whereas loops in an rRNA help create surfaces for contacts inside a ribosome.

RNA secondary structure describes patterns of local and longer-range base pairing, without requiring a complete three-dimensional arrangement. Tertiary structure describes how those paired and unpaired regions occupy space relative to one another. A set of secondary-structure elements may assemble into a compact structure through stacking between stems, contacts between distant loops and interactions involving sugars and phosphates. A tRNA cloverleaf is a useful secondary-structure map, but the molecule in a ribosome has a different three-dimensional outline. A drawing of four arms in a plane cannot tell us which surfaces face a ribosomal protein or where the amino acid attachment point lies relative to the anticodon. These questions require the tertiary fold.

The negative charge of the phosphate backbone creates a physical obstacle to folding. Bringing several RNA segments into close proximity increases unfavorable electrostatic interactions unless the charges are screened or otherwise stabilized. Monovalent cations and divalent cations, including magnesium, can reduce this penalty. Magnesium can also occupy particular positions in structured RNAs and contribute to reaction chemistry in certain RNA-containing enzymes. The effects of a salt change are not identical for all RNAs. Some stems remain stable under conditions in which the more delicate tertiary contacts are lost. Accordingly, an experiment may reveal that base pairing has persisted even though a biological function dependent on the complete fold is impaired.

Consider the illustrative sequence 5′-GCGAAACGC-3′. The first three nucleotides can pair antiparallel with the last three, leaving the central AAA stretch in a loop. Position 1 pairs with position 9, position 2 with position 8, and position 3 with position 7. The sequence does not have to join its ends by a covalent bond to make a hairpin. If the guanine at position 1 is replaced by adenine, the resulting A–C mismatch may destabilize the outer pair. If position 9 is additionally changed from cytosine to uracil, an A–U pair becomes possible again. This is a compensatory mutation: the second sequence change repairs the potential interaction even though the initial sequence is not restored. Restoration of the fold can provide evidence that a specific paired contact matters. It does not guarantee the original thermodynamic stability because the identity and stacking of the terminal pair have changed.

Such reasoning is especially useful when interpreting a conserved RNA alignment. Suppose that corresponding paired positions are G–C in one species, A–U in another and C–G in a third. The conservation of pairing despite sequence variation supports a structural requirement. In contrast, if the same sequence is strongly conserved regardless of its possible pairing partner, it may be necessary for a sequence-specific contact. Comparative evidence must be interpreted with awareness of shared ancestry and the small number of independent changes in a limited alignment. A single paired substitution is suggestive, not proof that two nucleotides directly contact one another. A functional mutation test and structural measurement can complement the comparative inference.

## 3. From a gene to a working RNA or ribonucleoprotein

The production of an RNA starts with a polymerase, but the identity of the polymerase does not by itself establish the RNA's eventual function. In a typical eukaryotic nucleus, RNA polymerase II produces precursor mRNAs and many noncoding transcripts, including several spliceosomal snRNAs. RNA polymerase I produces the large ribosomal precursor from which the principal large and small subunit rRNAs are formed. RNA polymerase III transcribes tRNA genes, 5S rRNA genes, U6 snRNA genes and various other small RNA genes. There are taxon-specific exceptions and additional organellar or plant polymerases. The usual assignments are useful because they connect the upstream synthesis machinery to the type of RNA that becomes available. They must not be turned into a rule that polymerase II always makes protein-coding messages.

In bacteria, a single principal multisubunit RNA polymerase transcribes genes for messages and functional RNAs, with initiation specificity influenced by promoter recognition. A bacterial ribosomal RNA operon may yield a precursor that includes multiple rRNA sequences and, in some organisms, tRNA sequences. Processing separates the individual RNA products. This differs from a polycistronic mRNA even though both start with multiple functional elements in a single transcription unit. In a polycistronic message, distinct coding regions usually serve as templates for separate polypeptides. In an rRNA precursor, distinct regions become working RNA molecules. Calling every multigene transcript polycistronic without asking about the fate of its products can therefore obscure the underlying biology.

A nascent RNA can begin to acquire its three-dimensional structure while additional nucleotides are being synthesized. The sequence at the 5′ end is available before the sequence at the 3′ end, so early contacts may form before their eventual partners emerge. Protein binding and processing can stabilize or alter these contacts. Some arrangements of an RNA therefore reflect the route through which the molecule was formed as well as the eventual balance of free energies. A drawing that shows a mature fold does not establish that the molecule reached that fold in one step. For an examination question about a mutant, it is important to distinguish failure of the final structure from failure of an intermediate necessary for reaching that structure.

Many cellular RNAs function as ribonucleoproteins, or RNPs. An RNP contains RNA associated with protein through noncovalent interactions, although individual components can have covalent modifications of their own. RNA-binding proteins may contact a particular sequence, a double-stranded region, a loop or the shape and charge pattern of an entire fold. Their binding can protect an RNA, help it fold, position a target or recruit a processing activity. The RNA can make an equally essential contribution to the complex. In a guide RNP the protein may provide an enzymatic activity while the RNA determines which target sequence is recognized. In other complexes, RNA is itself responsible for an important part of the reaction chemistry. The ribosome illustrates this latter case. It is an RNP, yet it cannot be understood as a protein enzyme with an inert RNA support.

It is useful to think of RNP formation as the acquisition of a biological state rather than an inevitable consequence of transcription. Imagine a gene for an RNA guide that is transcribed normally but has a mutation in a sequence required for binding its protein partner. The RNA may be detectable by a complementary probe. Nevertheless, the functional guide complex could be absent because the transcript has failed to assemble. Conversely, a mutation in an assembly protein may reduce the abundance of mature RNA because an unprotected intermediate is degraded. Reduced RNA abundance would not by itself show that transcription had slowed. When evaluating the result of a mutation, one must separate synthesis, folding, assembly, stability and function instead of treating them as a single event.

## 4. Evidence for a replaceable messenger

The discovery of messenger RNA resolved a basic uncertainty about the relationship between genes and ribosomes. If the ribosome were specific for an individual protein, switching the type of protein synthesized would require a corresponding change in the population of ribosomes. A different possibility was that ribosomes were comparatively stable machines that could receive new instructions. This latter model predicted that a cell could redirect protein synthesis through a newly made RNA even while most ribosomes remained old. Bacteriophage infection provided a valuable situation for distinguishing the models because the host begins making products specified by the infecting viral genome.

In 1961 Sydney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson published evidence for a transient RNA intermediate. The strategy used bacterial ribosomes distinguished by isotopic density and radioactive labelling of newly synthesized RNA after phage infection. Newly made RNA became associated with ribosomes formed before the change in gene expression. The experimental logic depended on being able to distinguish the age of the ribosome from that of the RNA associated with it. Had the new RNA been confined to newly synthesized, specially programmed ribosomes, the pattern of association would have been different. The observed association supported the existence of a replaceable message carried to an already existing protein synthesis apparatus.

The two kinds of labels in this reasoning answer different questions. An isotope-dependent density difference identifies a population of ribosomal particles under suitable centrifugation conditions. A pulse of radioactive precursor allows newly formed RNA to be detected. Merely finding radioactivity in a ribosome-containing fraction would be ambiguous if all ribosomes were formed during the pulse. The old-ribosome distinction provides the crucial context. One must also avoid interpreting the newly associated RNA as a permanent structural component of the old ribosome simply because the materials sediment together. The biological result is informative because the change in expression, the labelling history and the association of new RNA with existing particles agree with the messenger model.

A complementary question is whether an RNA sequence can actually direct the formation of a particular amino acid sequence. Cell-free translation experiments made it possible to examine this with defined RNA substrates. In the classic 1961 poly(U) experiment by Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei, a synthetic RNA made of uracil residues directed incorporation of phenylalanine into a polypeptide in a cell-free system. This revealed a correspondence between the UUU triplet and phenylalanine. The significance for RNA classification is that a message has an experimentally recognizable template function. The experiment does not imply that natural messages generally consist of repeated identical codons, nor does it establish the whole genetic code on its own. It shows how changing the nucleotide instructions can change the identity of the translated product without constructing a new kind of ribosome.

These discoveries also suggest a powerful general design for Part C questions. Hold one component of a molecular system constant while varying another. If the ribosomal machinery is unchanged and replacing the supplied RNA changes the product sequence, the supplied RNA carries information relevant to the product. If the RNA message is unchanged but changing a transfer RNA changes the amino acid delivered, the effect lies at the adaptor stage. If neither message nor adaptor can be used after a defect in a ribosomal structural component, the lesion affects the working machine. A sequence of such comparisons can separate the different roles even if all three molecules are chemically recognizable as RNA.

## 5. Architecture of a protein-coding message

A typical mature eukaryotic nuclear mRNA has a 5′ untranslated region, a coding region and a 3′ untranslated region. Many such transcripts also carry a 5′ cap and a 3′ poly(A) tail. The untranslated regions are transcribed portions of the message that lie outside its main translated coding region. They can contain recognition sites for RNA-binding proteins and structural features that affect the efficiency or location of protein synthesis. Thus untranslated does not mean functionless. A nucleotide substitution in a region outside the main coding sequence can change the quantity of protein produced while leaving the amino acid sequence of that protein unchanged. This distinguishes effects on expression from changes to the protein's primary structure.

The coding region of an ordinary message is read in successive groups of three nucleotides, known as codons. A start codon establishes the main reading frame in context; a stop codon provides a termination signal and normally does not contribute an amino acid to the chain. The first nucleotide of the mRNA is not necessarily the first nucleotide of the start codon. For example, if transcription begins eighty nucleotides before the start codon, those eighty nucleotides can form part of the 5′ untranslated region of the mature message, subject to the transcript's actual processing pattern. A gene map using +1 to label transcription initiation therefore describes a different event from a translation diagram marking the start of the coding sequence.

The cap is chemically distinct from the first transcribed nucleotide. A modified guanosine is attached in an unusual 5′ to 5′ triphosphate arrangement to the RNA end. This creates a recognizable terminal structure and influences protection and interactions with binding proteins. The cap is not a codon and it does not become an amino acid. Likewise the usual eukaryotic poly(A) tail is an added run of adenosines at the processed 3′ end rather than an extension of the translated coding sequence. The detailed enzymes that install these features belong to RNA end processing. Here the key structural principle is that a message's physical length includes regions and terminal additions that are not translated into the main protein.

Consider a mature RNA with a 120-nucleotide 5′ untranslated region, a 903-nucleotide open reading frame from start codon through stop codon inclusive, a 250-nucleotide 3′ untranslated region and a poly(A) tail of 90 nucleotides. The count of ordinary residues is 120 + 903 + 250 + 90 = 1,363, excluding the separate cap guanosine. The open reading frame contains 903 / 3 = 301 triplets. Under the standard assumptions of one terminal stop codon, no recoding and no premature termination, the chain initially contains 300 amino acid residues. Counting the cap or the tail as part of the coding sequence would produce the wrong answer. Removing an initiator methionine or other residues during protein maturation could also change the final mature protein length, but that is a later process.

There are important natural departures from this common architecture. Replication-dependent histone mRNAs in many animals are notable examples of nuclear messages that terminate in a specialized stem-loop-containing region rather than the conventional long poly(A) tail. Bacterial messages generally lack the typical eukaryotic mRNA cap and long stabilizing poly(A) tail, although bacteria can make other terminal modifications and add short tails in different biological contexts. Some eukaryotic and viral mRNAs use alternative initiation strategies. Consequently, a cap or a poly(A) tail can be useful evidence about a particular transcript, but neither is a universal defining property of all mRNAs. The defining property remains that the molecule functions as a template for protein synthesis.

An mRNA may be actively translated by several ribosomes at the same time. The ribosomes are attached at different locations and each reads the same nucleotide sequence along its own trajectory. A group of ribosomes associated with one message is called a polysome or polyribosome. This arrangement makes it possible for a single transcript to support production of multiple protein molecules without making one transcript for each product. A polysome is not an arrangement of multiple separate mRNAs around one ribosome. In the common model, the mRNA is the shared thread and the ribosomes are the repeated machines. The polysome concept also illustrates the distinction between template copy number and protein output. A change in the rate at which ribosomes initiate can alter the output from the same number of messages.
