## 18. Additional functional RNA species and the limits of simple classification

The five RNA classes considered so far are essential landmarks, but they do not exhaust cellular RNA diversity. A particularly instructive example of a guide RNA outside the ordinary spliceosomal and nucleolar systems is found in the mitochondria of certain kinetoplastid protists. Some of their primary transcripts must undergo insertion or deletion of uridines to produce appropriate mature RNA sequences. Guide RNAs provide sequence information that helps define where these changes occur. The guide and the target transcript are distinct molecules; their complementary regions assist in organizing the editing process. The resulting mRNA can then perform its template function. It is the editing machinery, rather than a ribosome reading the guide as a conventional protein-coding message, that uses the information supplied by the guide.

Comparison with snoRNA-mediated modification is useful. In the mitochondrial editing example, the order or number of nucleotide residues in the edited transcript changes. In a canonical C/D snoRNA-directed methylation, a chemical group is added to the ribose at a selected residue without changing the basic nucleotide sequence. Both pathways may use complementary recognition and associated proteins, yet their outputs differ. A comparison of DNA sequence with mature RNA sequence could reveal the insertion or deletion of uridines, whereas methylation may require a measurement sensitive to the altered chemistry. The method needed to detect an RNA-dependent change follows from the nature of that change.

The signal recognition particle, or SRP, provides an example in which RNA participates in the localization of another molecular complex. In a typical mammalian cytoplasmic system, 7SL RNA associates with proteins in SRP. The particle can recognize an emerging signal sequence of an appropriate nascent protein and help direct the ribosome-associated complex toward the endoplasmic reticulum. Bacteria also possess related SRP systems containing RNA, with differences in composition. The RNA here helps organize a targeting complex. It does not carry the instructions for the nascent protein being recognized; those instructions are supplied by the separate mRNA. This relationship distinguishes an RNA involved in the localization of protein synthesis from an RNA that is itself the message for that protein.

The bacterial transfer-messenger RNA, known as tmRNA, shows that biological categories need not be perfectly exclusive. If a ribosome reaches the end of a truncated message without an appropriate stop signal, it may become trapped in an incomplete translation event. In many bacteria, tmRNA participates in rescuing such ribosomes with the help of associated proteins. A part of tmRNA resembles tRNA and can accept an amino acid, while another part supplies a short translated reading frame. The switch permits completion of a tagged product and release of the machinery. The tag can contribute to recognition of the incomplete protein for degradation. Experiments reported by Keiler and colleagues in 1996 helped establish this connection between a special RNA, protein tagging and surveillance of defective messages. The example is included to illustrate multifunctionality, not to replace the later treatment of translation and degradation.

Some additional functional RNAs act chiefly in regulating gene expression rather than constituting the principal message, adaptor or ribosomal core. MicroRNAs and small interfering RNAs can guide protein complexes toward particular RNA targets. Longer noncoding RNAs can participate in varied interactions and cellular processes. These classes are taken up separately because their diversity and regulatory consequences deserve their own treatment. For present purposes, one should recognize that an RNA may supply information directly through complementarity or through a binding surface. Calling the RNA noncoding does not imply that it lacks an encoded sequence or biological information. It means that the relevant established role is not primarily to serve as a template for a protein product.

## 19. Identifying RNA classes from experimental readouts

The chemical similarity of cellular RNAs means that an investigator must choose observations that distinguish their functions. A denaturing RNA gel can separate molecules according to their electrophoretic behavior, which under suitable conditions is related to length. It can reveal a prominent rRNA population or smaller RNA bands. However, different classes can overlap in size. A short band cannot be identified uniquely as a tRNA simply by comparing it with a length marker. A native gel can reveal differences in folding or association, but these differences can alter migration in ways that are not directly proportional to nucleotide number. A good interpretation separates the question of length from the question of structural or molecular state.

A Northern blot adds sequence specificity by detecting an RNA with a complementary probe. If a probe corresponds to a region retained in a mature RNA and also present in its precursor, both can be detected when the region is accessible under the assay conditions. A second probe against a removed spacer or intron can help identify an intermediate. A mature rRNA band and a larger precursor band may therefore represent successive forms of the same sequence rather than two independently transcribed genes. Similarly, a probe against a particular exon can detect several RNA isoforms, while a probe spanning a particular exon junction can provide information about a particular processed form.

A fractionation experiment asks a different question. Molecules can be separated according to the compartments or molecular particles with which they are associated. If an RNA is found primarily with isolated ribosomal subunits and its sequence corresponds to a known ribosomal RNA, the combination supports its classification. If an RNA is detected with translating ribosomes, however, it might instead be the message or a participating adaptor. An association with ribosomes alone does not prove that the RNA is a structural rRNA. The persistence of the association, the identity of the isolated particle, the sequence and the functional behavior provide additional evidence.

In a hypothetical experiment, one RNA of approximately 80 nucleotides associates transiently with translating particles and can carry an amino acid. Another RNA of about 1,500 nucleotides remains associated with an isolated bacterial small subunit after the removal of ordinary translating messages. A third RNA of several hundred nucleotides varies in abundance with nutrient conditions and directs the formation of a defined polypeptide in an appropriate translation system. The combination of observations identifies the first RNA as a transfer RNA, the second as a plausible 16S ribosomal RNA and the third as a messenger RNA. The classification uses functional evidence along with size rather than treating size as sufficient.

A more complex comparison involves changes in protein output. Suppose four independently perturbed cell populations each produce less of a reporter protein. In the first, the abundance of mature reporter mRNA falls, while its translation efficiency is unchanged. In the second, the message is present normally but the charged pool of a relevant tRNA is reduced. In the third, mature ribosomal subunits are scarce and many different messages are translated poorly. In the fourth, a precursor reporter RNA with an unremoved intron accumulates while correctly joined reporter RNA decreases. These observations indicate different points of failure along the information-processing system. The fourth result suggests a processing defect; the other data implicate, respectively, availability of message, availability of an amino acid-bearing adaptor and availability of the decoding machine. Further measurements would be needed to identify the precise molecular lesions in each case.

## 20. A connected view of RNA function

The major RNA classes can now be located in one coherent causal model. Nuclear polymerase II creates many pre-mRNAs that are processed with the help of spliceosomal RNAs. Many mature messages are transported into the cytoplasm in association with proteins. Ribosomal precursor RNAs are synthesized and processed with the aid of nucleolar guide complexes, while tRNA precursors acquire the structures and chemical properties required for amino acid transfer. The mature components converge during cytoplasmic protein synthesis. The message supplies the nucleotide instructions, the adaptors connect those instructions to amino acids and the ribosomal RNAs help create the machinery that decodes and joins the substrates.

The relationship is not entirely linear. Functional ribosomes are needed to produce many of the proteins that assist the maturation of new RNA molecules. Proteins required for assembling spliceosomal or nucleolar particles themselves originate from mRNAs whose maturation may depend on existing RNA processing complexes. A living cell inherits machinery and continuously renews it. This mutual dependence is a feature of an already functioning cellular system rather than a contradiction that requires one of its essential components to arise spontaneously in each generation. Studying the components separately helps identify their roles, while the integrated view explains why a lesion in one component can influence many downstream outputs.

This also explains why the three main questions about an RNA must be kept distinct. Where and how is it made? What structure and molecular partners allow it to work? What changes when its specific function is lost? An RNA can be transcribed in one compartment and function in another; its primary sequence may remain unchanged while maturation alters its chemistry; and its absence can affect a protein that the RNA does not encode. With these distinctions established, the wider noncoding RNA classes can be studied as molecules whose biological activities arise from their own interactions, before turning to catalytic and ligand-responsive RNAs in the next parts of the curriculum.
