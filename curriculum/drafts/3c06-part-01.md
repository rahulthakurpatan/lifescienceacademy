# Translation Elongation, Translocation and Decoding Accuracy

## 1. The biological problem: converting a sequence into a moving reaction

The amino acid sequence of a protein is specified indirectly. The ribosome does not copy the chemical structure of an RNA molecule into another polymer. Instead, it interprets a succession of three-nucleotide symbols and joins a chemically different succession of molecules. The two polymers are related by a code, not by complementary pairing between their backbones. This distinction is important because two separate recognition events are necessary: an amino acid must be assigned to its transfer RNA, and the transfer RNA must be assigned to the proper position on a messenger RNA. An error in either operation can alter the protein, even while the other operation remains entirely accurate.

Imagine that a completed initiation complex has already identified the start codon. The initial methionine, or formylmethionine in a typical bacterium, is attached to the initiator tRNA. That tRNA is in the peptidyl site, called the P site. The first residue of a protein therefore exists before any peptide bond has been formed. There is no need to deliver a second initiator tRNA to begin elongation. What remains is to find the amino acid specified by the following codon, link it to the first residue, and advance the reading apparatus by precisely one codon. The product of this operation must be a ribosome capable of performing the same operation again.

This repeated operation solves three linked problems. First, it must choose a suitable aminoacyl-tRNA from a mixture of many similar molecules. Second, it must form an amide linkage between a selected amino acid and the growing chain without losing the connection between the chain and the ribosome. Third, it must present the next message symbol to the decoding center without shifting the reading frame. The physical arrangement of the ribosome makes these problems interdependent. A chain transferred onto a tRNA in the wrong position will not be ready for the next round. A ribosome advanced before decoding is finished could skip the very codon whose amino acid should be incorporated. Elongation is consequently a coordinated cycle rather than a sequence of independent enzymes acting on freely diffusing products.

The cost of a translation error depends on the resulting protein. Replacing a surface residue by a chemically similar residue may have little effect. A substitution in an enzyme's active site can prevent catalysis, and a substitution in a region that stabilizes a folded core can change the fraction of protein molecules that fold correctly. The position and nature of the error matter. This explains why cellular investment in accurate protein synthesis is beneficial even though some errors are tolerated. Unlike a mutation in genomic DNA, a typical isolated translational mistake is not copied into the DNA of subsequent generations. Nevertheless, when many proteins are synthesized, a modest increase in the frequency of such mistakes can produce a substantial burden of defective molecules.

A useful conceptual distinction is between processivity and fidelity. Processivity concerns how effectively a ribosome remains engaged with a message while making successive residues; fidelity concerns whether the resulting sequence matches the prescribed sequence. A ribosome may synthesize a full-length but inaccurate product, or it may make a short product whose existing residues are all correct. Full-length product measurements alone do not separate these outcomes. An experiment designed to investigate accuracy must therefore include information about the identities of the incorporated amino acids, not simply about the mass of protein produced.

## 2. Orientation of the message, chain and adaptors

RNA has distinguishable chemical ends because its nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds between the three-prime and five-prime positions of neighboring ribose sugars. The convention for writing an mRNA sequence is five-prime to three-prime. A translating ribosome progresses in that direction relative to the message. If the first translated nucleotide is position 1, the next codons occupy positions 4–6, 7–9 and so forth. Moving the relative register by one nucleotide would replace the succeeding triplets by another set. Therefore a movement of three nucleotides is not an arbitrary property of the machine; it is required by the triplet organization of the code.

Proteins have another form of polarity. A chain possesses an amino terminus, or N terminus, and a carboxyl terminus, or C terminus. During ordinary ribosomal synthesis the first residue ultimately contributes the N-terminal end, and subsequent residues are incorporated at the C-terminal end. The N-terminal portion of a sufficiently long growing chain can emerge from the exit tunnel even while the rest of the protein has not been made. The C-terminal end remains attached to a tRNA until release. These facts connect translation to later folding and processing: the entire protein sequence does not become available simultaneously.

The direction in which a tRNA anticodon pairs with a codon is antiparallel. For example, the mRNA codon 5′-GCU-3′ may be recognized by an anticodon written 3′-CGA-5′, which is 5′-AGC-3′ when written in the conventional orientation. A student who writes an anticodon without indicating its direction can easily mistake the correct complementary sequence. In actual cells, modified nucleotides and permissible wobble pairing expand the set of codons read by certain tRNAs. A wobble pair accepted by the code should not be treated as an error simply because it is not a canonical Watson–Crick pair.

A tRNA links two distant functional regions. Its anticodon provides a sequence-recognition surface at one end of the folded molecule, while its acceptor end carries an amino acid or a growing peptide. The terminal adenosine of the CCA end supplies the ribose hydroxyl group involved in ester attachment. The architecture is effective because recognition at the small ribosomal subunit can position a substrate in the catalytic center of the large subunit through the shape of one adaptor molecule. The two reactions occur at physically separated regions, yet the same tRNA coordinates them.

The amino acid is not recognized by base pairing. An anticodon that normally specifies alanine will still base-pair appropriately if its tRNA has been artificially loaded with another amino acid. The ability of the ribosome to recognize the codon does not automatically guarantee the correct amino acid identity. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases normally establish that assignment upstream of elongation. In a decoding experiment, it is therefore essential to specify whether the tRNA was correctly charged. A mischarged cognate tRNA and a correctly charged near-cognate tRNA can yield the same substituted amino acid in a protein through different molecular causes.

## 3. The three ribosomal sites as changing functional states

The labels A, P and E describe binding sites, not three permanently distinct types of tRNA. A typical elongator tRNA enters through the aminoacyl site, becomes the carrier of the new peptide, moves toward the peptidyl site and eventually occupies the exit site after losing its acyl group. Its amino acid identity and anticodon have not changed during this journey. What has changed is its location and chemical cargo. This is why one should identify the chemical state of a tRNA independently of its site when solving diagrams.

At the beginning of a canonical elongation cycle, the P site contains the peptidyl-tRNA. For the first round the cargo is a single amino acid rather than a chain containing multiple residues, but the same description of the site is useful. The next sense codon is available at the A site. An E-site tRNA may remain transiently from the previous round; E-site occupancy is not a universal requirement for the chemical reaction. Once an incoming aminoacyl-tRNA is selected and fully accommodated, the ribosome contains a donor and an acceptor positioned appropriately for peptide transfer.

The small subunit binds the mRNA and interacts with the anticodon regions of the tRNAs. The large subunit interacts with their acceptor ends, contains the peptidyl-transferase center and provides the route through which the growing chain emerges. A tRNA therefore spans the interface between two subunits. When a textbook labels one tRNA as occupying the A site, it usually represents a classical state in which the anticodon end and acceptor end occupy corresponding regions of the two subunits. The distinction becomes important after peptide transfer, when the ends can move at different times.

In bacteria, the two principal ribosomal subunits are designated 30S and 50S, while the associated ribosome is designated 70S. For cytosolic translation in a typical eukaryotic cell the corresponding labels are 40S, 60S and 80S. These sedimentation coefficients are not additive masses. A sedimentation coefficient depends on molecular mass, buoyancy and frictional resistance, which changes when the subunits associate. The chemistry of the elongation cycle is highly conserved despite differences in composition, size and the proteins that participate.

The designation of a site also does not imply a rigid hole through which tRNA must pass. A ribosome is flexible, and tRNA conformations change during selection and movement. If all components were locked in place, a tRNA could not be transferred from the decoding configuration to the fully accommodated configuration or from the pre-translocation configuration to the next reading position. A useful mental model is a changing arrangement of contacts among ribosomal RNA, ribosomal proteins, tRNA, mRNA and the elongation factors. Site labels summarize the principal functions of these contacts and make a complicated molecular process tractable at examination level.

## 4. The repeating elongation cycle

The cycle begins when an appropriate aminoacyl-tRNA arrives at a ribosome with a sense codon in its A site. Delivery is assisted in bacteria by EF-Tu in its GTP-bound form, and in eukaryotic cytosol by the related factor eEF1A. The nucleotide state of the factor affects its interaction with tRNA and with the ribosome. At the first encounter, the anticodon can sample the codon, although the aminoacyl end is not yet in the final position needed for reaction. This arrangement allows sequence discrimination before the cell commits the substrate to peptide formation.

After productive selection, the delivery factor hydrolyzes GTP and undergoes changes that allow its release from the tRNA. The aminoacyl end of an accepted tRNA can then settle into the large-subunit catalytic site. This movement is called accommodation. It provides an additional opportunity to discriminate among substrates, because successful initial pairing and GTP hydrolysis do not guarantee that the tRNA will complete accommodation. Near-cognate substrates can be rejected after the GTP-consuming step. The distinction between initial recognition and final accommodation is central to understanding ribosomal proofreading.

With both reactants correctly positioned, the amino group of the A-site amino acid reacts with the ester linkage attaching the peptide to the P-site tRNA. The peptide is transferred onto the amino group of the incoming residue. The newly elongated peptide remains connected to the tRNA that originally entered the A site, whereas the tRNA that had carried the growing chain becomes deacylated. Neither tRNA disappears from the ribosome at the instant of the chemical reaction. The immediate product is a pre-translocation complex with different chemical states but initially the same codon positions.

The next operation is translocation. In bacteria EF-G facilitates the movement of tRNAs and mRNA, and eukaryotic eEF2 performs the analogous function. A GTP-dependent cycle of binding and conformational transitions promotes a one-codon advance. The peptidyl-tRNA takes the P-site position; the old deacylated tRNA moves toward the E site and subsequently leaves. The next codon becomes available at the A site. If that codon is another sense codon, the entire cycle can begin again.

If the next codon is a stop codon, the same ribosome has reached a different decision point. Ordinary elongator tRNAs do not normally decode standard stop codons as amino acids. Release factors mediate termination instead. Thus the stop codon is not part of a conventional amino-acid addition cycle. It follows the last sense codon, which must itself have been incorporated before termination begins. This timing explains why translation of a message encoding a protein of length n includes n minus one ordinary elongation cycles after initiation, assuming a standard uninterrupted reading frame.

The cycle is best learned as three mechanistically distinct operations with three distinct observables. Selection changes tRNA occupancy and initiates factor turnover. Peptide transfer changes the chemical identity of the tRNA cargo. Translocation changes the reading register of the ribosome. A mutation can disturb one operation and leave the others functional in a prepared experimental system. Accordingly, one should not conclude that any reduction in overall peptide synthesis identifies a defect in peptide-bond catalysis.

## 5. A complete first round traced through an example

Consider the artificial sequence 5′-AUG GCU UUU GGA UAA-3′. Under the standard code and ordinary bacterial initiation, the first residue is formylmethionine; GCU encodes alanine, UUU encodes phenylalanine, GGA encodes glycine, and UAA is a stop. Ignore subsequent chemical removal of the formyl group for the moment. The chain that is synthesized has four residues. The initial P-site adaptor is paired with AUG and carries formylmethionine. At the A site, GCU presents the recognition problem for the first elongation round.

An appropriately charged alanine tRNA can be delivered by EF-Tu. After selection and accommodation, its amino group attacks the carbonyl associated with the ester-linked formylmethionine in the P site. The resulting dipeptide is formylmethionylalanine. The alanine tRNA now carries the dipeptide; the initiator tRNA is no longer attached to the first residue. This is true even before the ribosome moves along the message. The identity of the first residue has not changed merely because its original tRNA has lost the cargo.

After translocation, GCU occupies the P-site register and UUU occupies the A-site register. Phenylalanine tRNA is selected, the dipeptide is transferred, and the resulting tripeptide is attached to phenylalanine tRNA. In the third cycle GGA supplies glycine. The tetrapeptide remains attached to the glycine tRNA after translocation into the P site. The A site now presents UAA. The ribosome contains the complete sequence but has not yet released the chain into solution. A measurement that detects only free tetrapeptide may therefore underestimate synthesis if termination is selectively blocked.

There are exactly three peptide bonds connecting four residues in a linear tetrapeptide. Each was formed by the transfer of the entire previously made portion of the chain onto a new residue. Nothing in this example requires the old residue-specific tRNAs to stay attached to their corresponding amino acids in the final peptide. The protein is a continuous polymer, not a collection of separate amino acid–tRNA pairs. The number of successful standard elongation rounds is three, not four and not five. The distinction among residue count, bond count, codon count and factor turnovers is the basis of quantitative translation questions.

For a cytosolic eukaryotic ribosome translating the same coding sequence, the initial residue is ordinarily unformylated methionine, and the factors differ by name. The orientation of the template, the order of residue addition and the number of peptide bonds remain the same. In comparing bacteria and eukaryotes, it is therefore important to distinguish universal logical constraints from system-specific initiation details. The same mechanistic reasoning can be applied to most standard examination sequences as long as the assumed genetic code and start position are clear.
