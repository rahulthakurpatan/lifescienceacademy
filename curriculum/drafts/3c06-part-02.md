## 6. The delivery factor: recognition through a GTP-dependent cycle

A cell contains a pool of aminoacyl-tRNAs, many of which differ only modestly in their anticodon sequence or nucleotide modifications. Direct movement of all these flexible, reactive molecules into the catalytic center would provide little opportunity to coordinate recognition with chemistry. EF-Tu supplies a controlled route of entry for most bacterial elongator aminoacyl-tRNAs. Its GTP-bound form binds the charged adaptor and produces a three-component complex composed of EF-Tu, GTP and aminoacyl-tRNA. This is commonly called a ternary complex. The aminoacyl end is held by the factor rather than being immediately available for peptide transfer.

The name ternary complex must be interpreted in context. An initiation factor can also participate in a complex of three components, but that does not make it the same molecular assembly. In elongation the incoming tRNA usually carries the next amino acid, not the special initiator role. EF-Tu discriminates against the bacterial initiator tRNA under normal conditions. The first residue has already reached the P site using initiation machinery. The specialization of these pathways ensures that ordinary delivery does not replace the unique event of selecting a start site.

GTP binding stabilizes a conformation of EF-Tu that has high affinity for aminoacyl-tRNA. The factor binds the adaptor in a way that leaves the anticodon region accessible for interaction with the decoding center. The association is not based exclusively on which amino acid the tRNA carries. Contacts with the tRNA body and the aminoacyl moiety together allow a diverse set of correctly charged elongator tRNAs to be delivered. Natural tRNAs have evolved appropriate structural properties for this common route, so one factor can handle substrates associated with many different codons.

At an available A-site codon, the ternary complex initially samples the ribosome. The small subunit can test the potential codon–anticodon interaction before the amino acid enters the peptidyl-transferase center. This early arrangement is often represented as an A/T configuration, where the anticodon region is associated with the A site and the acceptor region is still associated with the delivery factor. The label is a description of the contact pattern, not an additional conventional ribosomal site equivalent to A, P or E.

A favorable codon–anticodon geometry promotes structural rearrangements that couple the decoding center to activation of the factor's GTPase activity. GTP hydrolysis produces GDP and inorganic phosphate. Changes in the nucleotide-dependent conformation and release of phosphate contribute to weakening the interactions that held the aminoacyl-tRNA in the delivery configuration. The factor can disengage, leaving the substrate to complete accommodation or be rejected. Hydrolysis should therefore not be pictured as a small molecule directly pushing the amino acid into a new peptide bond. It changes the direction and timing of a network of molecular interactions.

The delivery factor does not permanently become inactive after one round. EF-Tu in its GDP-bound form must exchange GDP for a fresh GTP molecule before it efficiently delivers another substrate. GDP dissociation can be accelerated by the exchange factor EF-Ts. This factor changes the interaction of EF-Tu with its bound guanine nucleotide, facilitating the replacement step. EF-Ts is not the source of the chemical energy stored in GTP and it does not phosphorylate GDP to synthesize GTP. The cell's nucleotide metabolism maintains the available pool of GTP. EF-Ts assists nucleotide exchange; it is a catalyst for resetting the delivery factor.

This distinction predicts two different phenotypes. If EF-Tu cannot hydrolyze GTP normally, its tRNA complex may bind but fail to progress efficiently into accommodation. If EF-Ts function is lost, pre-existing EF-Tu–GTP can support some successful rounds, but the population of delivery-competent factor can decline as GDP-bound molecules accumulate. A time course may distinguish a failure immediately after substrate recognition from a failure to replenish active factor. In either case the overall synthesis of protein may be reduced, so an endpoint assay of total protein alone cannot determine which step is defective.

In eukaryotic cytosolic translation eEF1A performs the corresponding delivery function and eEF1B provides nucleotide exchange activity through its catalytic component or components. The bacterial and eukaryotic names are important, but their functional correspondence is more useful than a memorized list. Both systems use the nucleotide state of a delivery GTPase to coordinate access of most elongator aminoacyl-tRNAs to the decoding and catalytic centers. Both also require restoration of the GTP-bound population between rounds. A description in which EF-Ts carries amino acids to the bacterial A site, or eEF2 normally delivers charged tRNAs in a eukaryote, exchanges distinct mechanistic roles.

## 7. Initial selection: from random encounter to codon recognition

A codon is a short sequence with only three nucleotides. Many incorrect anticodons share two of the three expected bases. The decoding apparatus must distinguish these near matches from a much larger pool of unrelated anticodons, and it must do so while using relatively weak, reversible molecular interactions. It would be misleading to assume that a correct tRNA is the only molecule that can ever bind the ribosome. Noncognate and near-cognate tRNAs can encounter the complex and sometimes form temporary contacts. What matters is the probability that a given encounter progresses to the irreversible or effectively committed production of a peptide.

For a specified codon, a cognate tRNA is a tRNA that recognizes it according to the normal decoding rules of the organism. A near-cognate tRNA typically makes a close but incorrect match, for example with a mismatch at one position. A noncognate tRNA has a substantially less appropriate pairing pattern. These categories describe the relationship between a codon and an anticodon in a particular decoding system. A tRNA is not intrinsically cognate to every codon simply because its amino acid has been correctly attached. It can be cognate at one location and a poor competitor at another.

The small subunit does more than allow two strands of RNA to base-pair in solution. Conserved elements of its decoding center make interactions that favor the geometry of appropriate pairing, particularly at the first two positions of the codon. The third position is subject to different constraints, accommodating allowed wobble interactions. This explains the coexistence of accurate discrimination and degeneracy in the genetic code. The code does not require a different rigid decoding geometry for every synonymous codon. Conversely, the existence of wobble does not imply that arbitrary mismatches at the third position are harmless. The structural context of the pair and the particular tRNA are important.

A correct substrate can influence the structure of the binding site itself. This idea is usually called induced fit. It means that the binding event and the conformational response cannot always be separated into a simple model in which a completely rigid receptor accepts or rejects a ligand. Productive codon recognition accelerates subsequent rearrangements that favor GTPase activation and onward progress. Near-cognate interactions may be less stable or may fail to stimulate these transitions efficiently. Thus specificity can arise from both how long a substrate stays bound and how rapidly it enters the productive pathway while it remains bound.

The distinction can be illustrated with a hypothetical encounter that has two possible outcomes: dissociation or forward reaction. A tRNA that dissociates rapidly can still be accepted if its forward reaction is even faster. A tRNA that remains attached for a relatively long time can nonetheless be rejected if productive rearrangement is extremely slow. Consequently affinity alone does not define the final error rate. A measurement of the concentration needed for half-maximal binding is not equivalent to a measurement of the probability that the tRNA makes a peptide bond.

Translation takes place in a crowded environment where the concentrations of potential competitors are unequal. Some tRNA species are more abundant than others, and the fraction that is aminoacylated can change with amino acid availability. The arrival of an incorrect competitor and its conditional probability of being incorporated both influence the frequency of erroneous products. A highly selective ribosome can still be challenged by an altered substrate pool. Equally, a large pool of correctly charged cognate tRNA may support accurate and efficient decoding without any change in the molecular structure of the ribosome.

It is useful to distinguish rejection before GTP hydrolysis from rejection after hydrolysis. Before hydrolysis, an unsuccessful encounter can generally leave with no expenditure of GTP by the ribosome in that delivery attempt. After hydrolysis, rejection is more costly because a guanine nucleotide has already been consumed. The stages do not provide identical information: the first is coupled to codon sampling and activation of the factor, while the later stage tests whether the substrate can continue along the accommodation pathway. The system therefore spends energy on an additional discriminatory step rather than merely on accelerating a single binding reaction.

## 8. Accommodation and the commitment to peptide transfer

When the aminoacyl-tRNA first participates in codon recognition, its aminoacyl end is physically restrained by the delivery factor. It is not in the same position as a fully accommodated A-site substrate. The transition to accommodation involves a substantial change in the adaptor's contacts. The anticodon region remains associated with the decoding site while the acceptor end moves into the large-subunit catalytic center. In the final state the amino acid is appropriately oriented toward the ester-linked peptide in the P site.

This arrangement separates reading from reacting. If the aminoacyl end of every initially associated tRNA were already positioned to receive the peptide, even a short-lived incorrect pairing could produce a substitution. Delaying access to the catalytic center makes rejection possible while the chain remains safely attached to its preceding adaptor. The movement of the tRNA is not simply a passive drop into an empty cavity. The changing interactions must be compatible with both the decoding complex and the large-subunit environment. Rejected substrates fail to complete this transition and leave before they acquire the chain.

After hydrolysis of GTP, an incorrect tRNA can in principle be released without making a peptide bond. This provides evidence that GTP hydrolysis and chemical incorporation are not equivalent observables. A ribosome may hydrolyze more molecules of GTP through its delivery factors than the number of residues successfully added. In an experiment, the amount of phosphate released can therefore exceed the amount predicted from the simple ideal cycle, even when initiation and translocation have been excluded from the measurement. The excess does not imply that some residues contain multiple peptide bonds to their neighbors; it reflects unsuccessful attempts or other GTPase activity.

A tRNA's acyl group is essential for the standard forward reaction. Deacylated tRNA lacks the amino acid nucleophile required for chain extension. A lack of charged cognate tRNA can slow the arrival of a productive substrate even if the total tRNA concentration has not changed. Thus measuring total tRNA abundance does not necessarily reveal the supply relevant for elongation. Distinguishing charged and uncharged fractions is especially useful when nutrient availability changes. The total quantity of a particular tRNA could remain stable while the effective concentration of its ternary complex decreases markedly.

Accommodation should not be confused with aminoacylation. Aminoacylation is the upstream attachment of an amino acid to tRNA by a synthetase; accommodation is the placement of a charged adaptor into a ribosomal reaction-ready state. A student can separate these processes by asking two questions. Is the tRNA chemically loaded with an amino acid? Is that loaded molecule now correctly positioned for peptide transfer on a ribosome? An affirmative answer to the first question is not sufficient for the second. The separation of these events provides distinct experimental targets and distinct explanations of translational failure.

In a prepared elongation complex, one can distinguish the completion of accommodation from peptide-bond formation by detecting the positions of the reactants or the formation of the chemical product. A conformational probe might report that an adaptor has moved toward the large subunit; peptide analysis is needed to establish that the new covalent bond has formed. In a mechanistic model, these observables correspond to different states. The physical proximity of reactants is necessary for efficient catalysis but does not alone prove that the reaction proceeded. This distinction recurs throughout enzymology and is particularly important for Part C experiments.

## 9. Why a second selection step improves decoding

A single equilibrium binding event has an intrinsic limitation. Suppose a receptor interacts more favorably with the correct substrate than with an incorrect substrate by a small free-energy difference. At thermal equilibrium the ratio of their occupancies depends on this difference and on their respective concentrations. For closely related substrates, the difference may not yield the specificity needed for highly accurate biosynthesis. Simply allowing the system to equilibrate for a longer time does not create additional discrimination if the final decision still depends on the same equilibrium populations.

The kinetic proofreading concept, developed independently by John Hopfield in 1974 and Jacques Ninio in 1975, addressed this problem. A system can distinguish substrates more effectively by separating recognition into multiple stages and using an energy-consuming transition to keep the process from behaving as one reversible equilibrium. An incorrect molecule that passes the first check can still be eliminated at a later check. If the later decision provides additional discrimination, the combined probability of incorrect incorporation may be much smaller than the probability of surviving either check individually.

The ribosome uses this principle in a chemically specific context. Its initial recognition of the ternary complex can favor the correct codon–anticodon arrangement. GTP hydrolysis separates this recognition stage from further discrimination during accommodation. A mismatch that survives long enough for hydrolysis is not thereby assured of incorporation. After factor release it must still successfully complete the physical rearrangement that places the aminoacyl end in the catalytic center. The extra discriminatory opportunity is maintained by the chemical energy of the nucleotide cycle, not by proofreading the already formed protein sequence backward residue by residue.

The common analogy of a two-door security entrance is useful only if the doors are not imagined as identical and completely independent machines. The second checkpoint may respond to the history of the first and to the conformational state of the same tRNA. In real kinetic schemes different substrates have different association rates, forward rates and rejection rates. The idealized mathematical product of independent survival factors can illustrate the benefit of repeated selection, but it is not a universal formula for all biochemical situations. A proper model must state how the probabilities are defined and whether they are conditional on earlier steps.

The system gains accuracy at a cost. Some correct substrates may also be rejected, so increasing the stringency of selection can slow productive incorporation. A second check that rejects all molecules would have no translation errors among its products because it would make no products at all. Such a system would be biologically useless. The relevant question is how a cell achieves a useful rate of protein synthesis with an acceptable frequency of errors and expenditure of resources. Speed, accuracy and energetic cost are distinct properties that can respond differently to changes in the molecular machinery.

Proofreading is not the same as error correction after product formation. In the normal elongation cycle, the most important opportunity for avoiding a wrong amino acid is before the peptide bond is made. Unlike a DNA polymerase with a proofreading exonuclease, the ribosome does not routinely reverse each last peptide bond and remove an incorrectly incorporated amino acid. Downstream protein quality control can recognize some defective products, but degradation of a whole polypeptide is not the same molecular process as ribosomal selection of aminoacyl-tRNA. Distinguishing prevention, correction and disposal prevents a common misconception about the term proofreading.
