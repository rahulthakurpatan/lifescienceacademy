## 10. Quantitative interpretation of substrate discrimination

The idea of repeated selection can be expressed without advanced mathematics. Suppose a newly bound tRNA can either proceed at a rate k_f or dissociate at a rate k_r. Both rate constants have units of inverse time when they describe competing first-order reactions from the same state. Assume that these two processes are the only exits from that state and that their rates remain constant throughout an encounter. The probability density for the molecule to remain in the state until time t and then undergo the forward reaction is k_f multiplied by exp[−(k_f+k_r)t]. Integrating from zero to infinity gives the forward probability k_f/(k_f+k_r). The probability of rejection is k_r/(k_f+k_r). Their sum is one, as expected for two mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes. The average waiting time to either exit is 1/(k_f+k_r).

For example, imagine a cognate substrate whose first-stage forward rate is 90 per second and rejection rate is 10 per second. The probability of passing is 0.90. A near-cognate substrate with a forward rate of 10 per second and a rejection rate of 90 per second passes with probability 0.10. These are hypothetical values, not measured rate constants for a particular ribosome. If the numbers of initial encounters are equal, the ratio of wrong to correct substrates passing this first checkpoint is 0.10/0.90, or one ninth. It is not one tenth because the denominator is the number of correct passes, not the total number of original encounters.

Now assume that, conditional on passing the first checkpoint, a cognate tRNA has a 0.95 probability of completing accommodation while a near-cognate tRNA has a 0.05 probability. For the cognate species the total probability of passing both stages is 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.855. For the near-cognate species it is 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005. The wrong-to-correct product ratio for equal encounter frequencies is therefore 0.005/0.855, approximately 0.00585, or one wrong product for every 171 correct products. The fraction of all products that are wrong is instead 0.005/(0.855+0.005), approximately 0.00581. The two quantities are similar at low error frequencies, but they are not algebraically identical.

The conditional probability language is essential. Let C represent cognate substrate and W represent wrong substrate. Define p_1,C as the chance that an initial encounter of C passes step one, and p_2,C as the chance that an encounter of C that has already passed step one also passes step two. The overall chance is p_1,C p_2,C by the ordinary product rule for conditional probabilities. No assumption of statistical independence between the two checks is required because the second probability has already been defined conditionally. The same notation applies to W. If the second probability were defined over all initial encounters instead, multiplying it by the first would count some rejection events twice.

An actual ribosome may encounter many varieties of wrong substrate, not only one. Let J_i denote the encounter frequency of substrate i per unit time at an available A site, and let p_i denote its conditional probability of eventual incorporation from one such encounter. In a simplified renewal model with a fixed substrate pool and no persistent memory of failed encounters, its contribution to successful events is proportional to J_i p_i. The fraction of successful events involving wrong substrates is the sum of J_i p_i over the wrong species divided by the corresponding sum over all candidate species. If two incorrect tRNAs have different arrival rates and success probabilities, their contributions must be added as rates, not averaged as percentages without weighting.

Consider a cognate tRNA with an encounter frequency of 50 arbitrary events per unit time and a success probability of 0.8. The effective weight for its correct product is 40. A wrong species encounters at a frequency of 100 and succeeds in 0.002 of encounters, so its effective weight is 0.2. A second wrong species encounters at 10 and succeeds with probability 0.01, contributing 0.1. The wrong-product fraction is 0.3/40.3, or about 0.744 percent. Although the second wrong species has five times the success probability of the first, its smaller encounter frequency makes its total contribution lower. This reasoning is important whenever an examination problem changes both a kinetic discrimination parameter and the abundance of a tRNA.

The use of effective encounter frequencies in this example is a simplification. An A site occupied by a slowly rejected tRNA is temporarily unavailable to others. If some encounters are much longer than others, the overall throughput depends on the durations of unsuccessful attempts as well as on the probabilities of successful attempts. The product composition under a simple independent renewal process can still be computed from event probabilities, but converting the weights into absolute product flux requires a time accounting. This is why a preparation that rejects most wrong substrates can nevertheless show slow elongation in a mixture containing a large amount of a slowly dissociating competitor.

Thermodynamic reasoning gives another view. In a simple equilibrium model with equal substrate concentrations, a difference in standard binding free energy of ΔΔG = G_bind,wrong − G_bind,correct produces an equilibrium wrong-to-correct occupancy ratio exp(−ΔΔG/RT), where R is the molar gas constant and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, RT is about 2.48 kilojoules per mole. A favorable difference of 5.7 kilojoules per mole would give a ratio close to 0.10. A tenfold preference can be useful, but not sufficient by itself for very high fidelity. The energy-consuming sequence of selection transitions enables a system to do more than simply select once from this equilibrium mixture.

It is tempting to claim that kinetic proofreading always squares the equilibrium discrimination factor. That is only a property of certain idealized models and limiting conditions. Real substrates can differ in induced-fit kinetics, rates of accommodation, association frequencies and rejection pathways. The mathematical examples in this chapter illustrate mechanisms and make predictions under specified assumptions; they are not universal quantitative measurements of translation error.

## 11. Peptide transfer: the chemical reaction and its substrates

A polypeptide is a sequence of amino acid residues connected through amide linkages. During synthesis the relevant carbonyl is at the carboxyl-derived end of the preceding residue, and the relevant attacking group is the amino group of the incoming amino acid. The ribosome must therefore bring two chemically distinct reactive groups close together in a controlled orientation. It must also ensure that the product stays attached to an adaptor so that the next amino acid can be added to the same growing chain.

Consider the ordinary reaction using an incoming amino acid whose α-amino group is represented by H2N–CHR–. Its carboxyl group is linked as an ester to its own tRNA. The donor peptide has its terminal carboxyl group esterified to the P-site tRNA. In simplified chemical notation the donor is peptide–C(=O)–O–tRNA_P and the acceptor is H2N–CHR–C(=O)–O–tRNA_A. The amino group of the acceptor attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the donor ester. The reaction yields peptide–C(=O)–NH–CHR–C(=O)–O–tRNA_A and releases the P-site tRNA with a free hydroxyl group. The new amide bond is the connection between the old chain and the incoming residue.

At the first moment after reaction, the growing chain is therefore attached to the A-site tRNA. This fact is independent of the names or amino acid identities of the substrates. It follows directly from the chemistry: the carboxyl group of the incoming residue was already attached to its own tRNA before the reaction, and that linkage is not the one attacked during peptide transfer. The nucleophile is on the incoming amino acid, and the bond that breaks is the donor peptide's ester linkage to its old tRNA. The product retains the incoming residue's ester linkage, making that residue the next terminal donor when the cycle repeats.

The standard schematic often describes peptide-bond formation as a condensation between two free amino acids with the loss of a water molecule. That reaction is useful for representing the composition of an isolated dipeptide, but it is not the actual substrate-level mechanism of translation. On a ribosome the donor carboxyl group is already activated as an ester, and the chemical reaction is an acyl transfer from oxygen to nitrogen. The leaving group is associated with the deacylated tRNA, not a molecule of water eliminated from two free amino acids. This difference explains why aminoacylation upstream of the ribosome is indispensable for standard translation.

The ribosome does not form a peptide bond by adding a residue to the N terminus of the old chain. If the chain initially reads N–Met–Ala–Phe–C, its last residue Phe has the esterified carboxyl group attached to the current tRNA. An incoming glycine presents its amino group to that carbonyl. The new sequence is N–Met–Ala–Phe–Gly–C. The orientation follows from the chemical arrangement and does not require a separate directional motor to decide which protein end grows. The mRNA-reading direction is enforced by the order of codon exposure, while the chain-growth direction is enforced by the acyl-transfer chemistry.

The reaction is a nucleophilic acyl substitution. Electron redistribution around the carbonyl during attack leads through a tetrahedral configuration along the reaction pathway, and subsequent rearrangement enables departure of the tRNA-linked oxygen group. Appropriate proton transfers accompany the reaction so that the final amide and hydroxyl products have the expected bonding patterns. At examination level the important features are the identities of the nucleophile, electrophile, leaving group and final acyl carrier. One need not assign a single universal detailed proton-transfer route to every ribosome and substrate in order to explain these features.

A special case illustrates why precise terminology matters. Proline has a secondary α-amino group rather than the primary amino group found in many other proteinogenic amino acids. Its incorporation produces a peptide nitrogen with a different substitution pattern, but the fundamental direction of attack and transfer of the growing chain are unchanged. Certain proline-containing sequences are difficult substrates for rapid peptide transfer, which is one reason auxiliary elongation factors can improve synthesis of particular motifs. This exception concerns the rate and substrate geometry of the conserved reaction, not a separate genetic code for proline.

## 12. The peptidyl-transferase center is a catalytic RNA environment

The ribosome is often introduced as a ribonucleoprotein because it contains both RNA and proteins. That compositional description does not by itself identify which class of molecule forms the catalytic center for peptide synthesis. Structural and biochemical studies established that conserved large-subunit ribosomal RNA is central to the peptidyl-transferase reaction. In bacteria the relevant large rRNA is 23S rRNA; eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes contain the homologous catalytic region within their large-subunit rRNA, called 28S in mammals. The precise nomenclature differs among eukaryotes, but the conserved organization of the catalytic region is the main biological point.

In 2000, structural work by Nissen, Hansen, Ban, Moore and Steitz examined the large ribosomal subunit of an archaeon with substrate analogs. The position of those analogs placed the reaction center among conserved rRNA components rather than a conventional protein enzyme active site. This provided compelling structural evidence for the ribosome as an RNA catalyst. An important distinction is that the study was not an experiment in which all proteins were removed from a functioning modern ribosome and translation proceeded normally. Instead, it located substrate-like molecules in the organized environment of an assembled large subunit and helped identify the chemical nature of the active site.

Catalysis can arise from several contributions. Reactants are brought into a favorable relative orientation; conformations that would be unproductive in solution are constrained; an appropriate environment supports the reaction and its associated proton movements. The ribosome also positions the tRNA acceptor ends so that the product remains covalently attached to the correct carrier. The catalytic environment includes important contributions from the substrates themselves, particularly the terminal ribose of the P-site tRNA. It would be an oversimplification to attribute all catalysis to one specific rRNA base acting exactly like a classical protein general-base catalyst.

A reaction can be accelerated by a catalyst without the catalyst being a source of the chemical free energy driving the reaction. The activated ester at the donor tRNA is a crucial energetic feature of peptide transfer. The ribosome helps convert an existing activated bond into a peptide bond and the released tRNA; it does not hydrolyze GTP at the peptidyl-transferase center as a direct chemical reagent for forming each amide linkage. GTP is consumed at other stages of the standard elongation cycle, supporting substrate selection and directional movement. Separating the sources of chemical activation from the costs of coordinating the machine is essential for correct ATP and GTP bookkeeping.

The classification of the ribosome as a ribozyme has evolutionary significance because it demonstrates that RNA can have both informational and catalytic roles in contemporary cells. The messenger RNA provides sequence information, the transfer RNAs provide adaptors and the ribosomal RNA contributes to a catalytic molecular machine. This combination is consistent with ideas about an early evolutionary role for RNA, but it does not directly prove the exact history of the first biological translation system. A distinction must be maintained between structural evidence about an existing ribosome and a historical inference about ancient biology.

The catalytic role of RNA also does not mean that ribosomal proteins are unimportant. Proteins help assemble and stabilize ribosome structure, influence conformational transitions and provide interaction surfaces within the translation machinery. A mutation in a protein component can alter the behavior of the RNA-based catalytic center indirectly. Therefore, finding a protein mutation that reduces peptide formation does not refute the conclusion that rRNA constitutes the core catalytic environment. The functional phenotype of an assembled complex reflects the contributions of its components to both local chemistry and global organization.
