## 13. Chemical activation and the energetic logic of the peptide bond

The peptide-bond reaction cannot be understood by counting nucleotide hydrolyses alone. An unactivated carboxyl group is not a convenient acyl donor for a rapid controlled reaction with another free amino acid in the aqueous environment of a cell. Before translation, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases use ATP to form an aminoacyl adenylate and transfer the aminoacyl group onto tRNA. The resulting ester is an activated derivative of the carboxyl group. When the residue eventually participates in peptide transfer as the terminal donor, this ester linkage provides a chemically suitable substrate for acyl substitution.

The two steps of charging can be represented separately. Amino acid plus ATP yields aminoacyl-AMP and pyrophosphate. The activated aminoacyl group is transferred to a tRNA hydroxyl, releasing AMP. Pyrophosphate can then be hydrolyzed to two inorganic phosphates. These reactions help sustain directionality in the formation of charged tRNA. The net result is not that ATP remains bound to the tRNA until it arrives at the ribosome; rather, the chemical products of the nucleotide reaction have separated from the charged adaptor. The activation is stored in the acyl linkage itself.

A chain that contains many residues has consumed many such charging reactions, although the ribosome never carries the entire set of original tRNAs at once. The tRNA attached to the newest residue supplies the linkage that persists between successive rounds of elongation. In the next cycle, that linkage becomes the donor ester. Accordingly, the chemical activation associated with an incoming amino acid does not vanish immediately when its α-amino group receives the chain. The incoming amino acid's carboxyl linkage is retained in the product; it will serve as the donor connection in a later round or be hydrolyzed at termination.

This observation also clarifies a subtlety in energy accounting. A protein consisting of n residues contains n−1 peptide bonds but requires n correctly charged aminoacyl-tRNAs if all charging is counted from free amino acids, including the initiator. It is not sufficient to count charging only for residues that arrived at the A site after initiation when the question asks for synthesis from free amino acids. If the initial P-site tRNA and all subsequent aminoacyl-tRNAs are provided as already charged substrates, by contrast, their charging energy has already been spent before the defined experiment begins. Which processes are included in the accounting is part of the problem statement.

The standard energy cost assigned to an ATP-to-AMP conversion is two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents when pyrophosphate hydrolysis and restoration of ATP from AMP are taken into account. This does not mean that two ATP molecules were consumed by the synthetase itself. One ATP molecule enters each ordinary activation event and is converted to AMP rather than ADP. Restoration of the original nucleotide energy state requires two phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. If a problem asks for ATP molecules, count one per activation. If it asks for high-energy phosphate equivalents, count two per activation under the standard convention.

GTP-dependent factor reactions differ in that each ordinary GTP-to-GDP conversion is counted as one equivalent. A successful elongation round normally includes one GTP hydrolysis associated with aminoacyl-tRNA delivery and one associated with EF-G or eEF2. Neither of these nucleotide molecules supplies a phosphate group that is incorporated into the protein chain. Their turnover organizes binding, selection and movement. In a biological system, additional events, including rejected substrates, maintenance of nucleotide pools and other protein-processing reactions, may increase expenditure beyond the idealized cost.

The energetic contributions thus form a relay of different types. ATP-dependent chemical activation establishes a useful acyl substrate; GTP-dependent conformational cycles help deliver that substrate selectively and reset the ribosome's reading register. The peptidyl-transferase center uses the properly positioned activated substrate for bond formation. These operations are coupled through the state of the translation complex, even though their chemical reactions occur at different sites and times. The student should not assign the entire energetic cost of protein synthesis to the formation of the peptide bond itself.

## 14. The pre-translocation state and hybrid tRNA configurations

Immediately after peptide transfer, two facts have changed: the old P-site tRNA is deacylated, and the old A-site tRNA now carries the elongated peptide. The message has not necessarily advanced by a complete codon at that instant. The apparent contradiction in describing a peptidyl-tRNA in the aminoacyl site is resolved when the site names are recognized as locations rather than strict chemical definitions. The A site can transiently contain a peptidyl-tRNA, even though an aminoacyl-tRNA normally enters through it.

A tRNA spans two subunits, and its two ends need not move simultaneously. The standard classical-site notation writes two letters separated by a slash to describe the locations of the anticodon end on the small subunit and the acceptor end on the large subunit, respectively. A classical A/A tRNA occupies corresponding A regions on both subunits. A P/P tRNA similarly occupies P regions on both. After peptide transfer, an A/P configuration describes the new peptidyl-tRNA whose anticodon remains in the A register on the small subunit while its acceptor end occupies the P region on the large subunit. The old deacylated tRNA can adopt a P/E configuration. These hybrid states provide a useful intermediate between the chemical step and completed translocation.

The evidence for intermediate configurations was strengthened by work reported by Dmitri Moazed and Harry Noller in 1989. They used chemical probing of ribosomal RNA to investigate contacts made by tRNAs in different elongation states. The patterns of protection showed that after peptide formation, changes associated with the large subunit could occur before the complete shift relative to the small subunit. This supported a staged model of movement. The experiment did not consist of directly watching an entire movie of each atom in a translating ribosome. Rather, differences in chemical accessibility provided evidence about the positions of molecular contacts.

The logic of a protection experiment is that a nucleotide which becomes less accessible to a chemical reagent upon tRNA binding can reveal the region occupied or influenced by that tRNA. Comparisons across known complexes can help assign contact changes to a particular stage. However, a protected nucleotide is not always a direct contact with the tRNA, because a conformational rearrangement can also alter accessibility. Multiple probes, reference complexes and other structural information help interpret protection patterns. The power of this method is in comparing controlled states rather than treating every single protection signal as an independently exact position measurement.

The two principal subunits also undergo relative movements, commonly described as rotation or ratcheting, during the elongation cycle. The head of the small subunit can adopt different orientations as movement progresses. These are not separate ATP-powered machines attached to each subunit. They are conformational transitions of the ribosome itself, coordinated with tRNA interactions and elongation-factor binding. Thermal motion enables fluctuations among accessible states; chemical changes and nucleotide-dependent factor interactions favor particular transitions. A ribosome can therefore use its own flexibility without sacrificing the overall directionality required to preserve the reading frame.

A common examination diagram shows two tRNAs moving by a full site together immediately after peptide transfer. Such a diagram is a useful summary of the net outcome but does not depict all intermediate states. If a question explicitly distinguishes acceptor-end movement from anticodon-end movement, the hybrid-site description is necessary. If the question asks only for final P- and E-site occupancy after a completed translocation event, the simple classical-site model is adequate. One should not apply an unnecessarily complicated intermediate model to a simple endpoint question, nor use an endpoint cartoon to deny the existence of experimentally supported intermediates.

## 15. EF-G and eEF2: resetting the ribosome for the next codon

In bacteria the major factor promoting translocation is EF-G, a GTP-binding protein distinct from EF-Tu. It interacts with the ribosome after peptide formation and helps coordinate the rearrangements by which tRNAs and mRNA change their positions relative to the decoding center. Its GTPase cycle is coupled to movement, conformational transitions and efficient progression toward a post-translocation state. In eukaryotic cytosol the corresponding factor is eEF2. Although the factors are not identical in their complete structures, their central elongation functions are analogous.

The role of EF-G is sometimes illustrated as pushing the tRNAs physically from one site to the next. This mechanical analogy is helpful for remembering direction, but it can create an incorrect picture of a rigid motor acting on static objects. The ribosome has already acquired a different chemical and conformational state after peptide transfer. The movement of the acceptor ends can begin without a full EF-G-driven advance of the message. Factor binding and its nucleotide-dependent conformational cycle help bias the transitions and promote productive movement of the mRNA–tRNA complex. Completion and rapid factor turnover are part of the overall GTP-dependent process.

The peptidyl-tRNA must end in the P-site register because its attached terminal residue will be the donor for the next peptide bond. The deacylated tRNA must leave the P position because it can no longer provide a peptide donor. The A-site register must become available to the following sense codon. A successful translocation event accomplishes all three requirements through coordinated movement. If the ribosome were advanced without accompanying the relevant tRNA–mRNA interactions, the connection between the chain and the message's reading register could be lost.

The old tRNA does not need to exit at exactly the same instant that the next codon becomes available. E-site occupancy is a dynamic feature of a translating complex, and the precise timing of dissociation can vary. The E site facilitates release of a spent adaptor as the cycle proceeds, allowing it to return to the soluble tRNA pool for recharging. An empty E site is not itself evidence that elongation has not occurred. Similarly, the presence of a deacylated tRNA in the E region need not mean the ribosome is irreversibly stalled.

GTP hydrolysis and phosphate release are chemically distinguishable events. Descriptions at the level of the complete cycle normally state that EF-G hydrolyzes one GTP per successful standard translocation, and that this energy-dependent cycle promotes forward movement. The detailed timing of hydrolysis, phosphate release and individual molecular motions can depend on the experimental system and the particular state observed. A statement that every elementary movement is physically caused at the exact moment of GTP cleavage is stronger than the usual evidence supports. The safe mechanistic conclusion is that factor binding and nucleotide-dependent transitions enable rapid, ordered movement and recycling.

A useful comparison is with the delivery factor. EF-Tu in its GTP-bound form associates with a charged tRNA and participates in recognition before peptide formation. EF-G acts on a ribosomal complex that has already completed the current peptide-transfer step. Both are GTPases, but the similarity in nucleotide use does not make their substrates or their stage assignments interchangeable. A preparation supplied with abundant EF-Tu but lacking effective EF-G may complete a peptide transfer and accumulate a pre-translocation complex. More delivery factor cannot by itself restore the missing movement. Conversely, EF-G cannot supply a cognate aminoacyl-tRNA when its delivery machinery is defective.

A nonhydrolyzable GTP analog can be useful in experimental analysis because it can stabilize certain nucleotide-bound complexes and interfere with normal progression or factor recycling. The outcome depends on the factor being investigated and the properties of the analog. A complex trapped by such an analog need not be identical to the unperturbed intermediate in every structural detail. This is an important limitation when using trapped states to infer the natural sequence. Nevertheless, comparisons among GTP-bound, GDP-bound and analog-stabilized states can help establish how nucleotide-dependent factors participate in the cycle.

## 16. Maintaining the reading frame during movement

The ribosome must advance by one codon rather than by an arbitrary distance because a coding sequence contains overlapping potential triplets when no frame is specified. Take the sequence 5′-AUG GCU UUU GGA-3′. Its first selected frame groups the bases into AUG, GCU, UUU and GGA. Starting one nucleotide later produces UGG, CUU and UUG as successive complete triplets. A two-nucleotide shift produces GGC, UUU and UGG. The same chemical message can therefore encode radically different amino acid orders depending on the initial register. The function of translocation is not simply to move forward but to maintain the register established at initiation.

Stable codon–anticodon interactions help couple the mRNA position to the adaptor positions as the complex advances. The ribosome also makes contacts with the mRNA and constrains its orientation through the decoding region. Because the mRNA and tRNA interactions change as the subunits rotate and rearrange, productive movement requires coordinated release and reformation of contacts rather than permanent locking of every molecular pair. The resulting net movement normally exposes the next triplet while preserving the correct chain-to-message relationship.

A decoding error and a frameshift are distinct events. In a missense decoding error, an inappropriate tRNA can introduce the wrong amino acid for one codon, after which the ribosome may still continue in the original reading frame. In a frameshift, the register changes so that subsequent codons are read differently until a compensating change or termination event. A nucleotide insertion or deletion in the coding sequence can alter the frame at the template level, whereas translational frameshifting can occur on a physically unchanged RNA. Neither should be confused with the normal three-nucleotide translocation event.

Certain natural messages exploit programmed ribosomal frameshifting. Sequence context and structures in the mRNA can favor a defined change in the reading register, thereby altering the relative production of different proteins from the same message. Such exceptions are not evidence that ordinary elongation lacks directionality. They demonstrate that the general mechanism can be influenced by local sequence features and dynamics. The details of virus-specific regulatory strategies belong to later gene-expression topics; the relevant concept here is the distinction between a deliberate message-dependent shift and an accidental failure of normal frame maintenance.

A selective block of translocation can reduce full-length protein synthesis even when all codons are correctly recognized and the initial peptide-transfer reaction works. An experimental ribosome might accumulate peptide-bearing tRNA in a pre-translocation configuration. The next codon would not be efficiently presented to the decoding center, and repeated rounds would cease. If a test detects a new peptide bond but fails to detect the expected shift in a footprint or toeprint, the combination supports a movement defect rather than a primary inability to catalyze peptide formation. The result is most convincing when the same complex is shown to initiate correctly and accept the intended tRNA.

In a simple diagram, the message is often drawn as moving from right to left through a fixed ribosome. Another diagram keeps the mRNA fixed and moves the ribosome from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end. These are equivalent relative descriptions if the orientations are labeled consistently. A diagram's page direction is not the biochemical direction. The decisive evidence is the order in which codons enter the A site and the maintenance of the appropriate three-base register.
