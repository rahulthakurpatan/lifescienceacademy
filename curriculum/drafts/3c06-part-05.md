## 17. Counting elongation cycles, nucleotides and energy

Quantitative questions on translation become straightforward once the boundary of the calculation is defined. Let n be the number of amino acid residues in a linear polypeptide immediately after synthesis, before any processing removes residues. Assume a normal single reading frame, one initiator residue, no recoding, no failed tRNA selection attempts and a complete translation event. There are n sense codons, including the start codon, and an additional stop codon. The portion of the RNA from the start codon through the stop codon contains 3(n+1) nucleotides. Untranslated regions, introns of the original gene, and the poly(A) tail are not included in this count.

The initiation complex already contains the first residue in its P site. Consequently n−1 further residues must be delivered in ordinary elongation rounds. There are n−1 peptide bonds and n−1 completed ordinary translocations, including the last movement that brings the final peptidyl-tRNA to the P site and exposes the stop codon at the A site. In the special mathematical case n=1, the chain contains no peptide bonds and no ordinary amino-acid-addition cycle; initiation and termination are still conceptually distinct events. This boundary case is a useful check on the expression n−1, because a formula that predicts one elongation cycle for a single-residue product would be counting the initiator incorrectly.

Each of the n−1 ordinary rounds uses one GTP for delivery and one for translocation in the ideal cycle. Thus the elongation-specific GTP consumption is 2(n−1). If the problem begins with precharged tRNAs and an assembled initiation complex, the charging and initiation costs must not be charged again to the elongation phase. If instead the problem asks for synthesis from free amino acids, n ordinary aminoacylation reactions consume n ATP molecules converted to AMP. Expressed in high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents, the charging contribution is 2n. The sum of charging and elongation costs is therefore 2n+2(n−1)=4n−2 equivalents, before any initiation, termination, recycling or additional proofreading costs have been included.

For a 100-residue bacterial protein, the ordinary elongation rounds number 99. They consume 198 GTP molecules in the ideal accounting. Charging consumes 100 ATP molecules, equivalent to 200 high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents. The combined charging and elongation total is 398 equivalents. The coding sequence including the stop codon contains 303 nucleotides. If a question explicitly adds one GTP for initiation and one for termination, the stipulated total becomes 400 equivalents. This last total is not a universal value for every organism and every choice of system boundary; it follows from the additional assumptions of this particular calculation.

A typical bacterial initiation model includes IF2-dependent GTP hydrolysis. Termination and ribosome recycling can involve additional GTPases, including RF3 where applicable and EF-G in bacterial recycling. Eukaryotic initiation has its own additional GTP- and ATP-dependent steps, including nucleotide consumption by initiation factors and ATP-dependent activities associated with scanning. The exact cost of these steps can vary with the definition of the system and the machinery involved. It is therefore safer to specify which events are counted than to memorize a single formula for the total chemical expenditure of all translation-related processes.

The familiar approximation of four high-energy phosphate equivalents per residue is convenient for large proteins because the constant boundary corrections become small relative to n. It hides the fact that the first residue enters by a different route and that the cost of termination is not proportional to the number of residues. If n is very small, the approximation is less useful. If n is large, it can provide a good estimate of the minimum energetic burden of producing a protein when only the main charging and elongation reactions are considered.

Consider a protein of 250 residues. A problem asks for the number of GTP molecules consumed after initiation but before release factors enter. The answer under the ideal cycle is 2 × 249 = 498. The last translocation is included because the stop codon must become accessible before release factors enter. If the experiment instead stops immediately after the final peptide bond forms, without completing the last translocation, the number of delivery turnovers remains 249 while the number of completed translocation turnovers is 248, giving 497 GTP molecules under the simplified counting convention. In real transient intermediates, a factor may have hydrolyzed GTP before translocation has been completed; the question must define whether turnover or completed movements are being measured.

The difference of one GTP in this example may look trivial, but it reflects a major mechanistic distinction. The product can have its complete amino acid sequence without yet being in the correct register for release. By counting events rather than assuming that each stage automatically follows the preceding one, a student can reason about partially completed translation reactions. This ability is more valuable in an analytical examination than memorizing a total for an unspecified endpoint.

## 18. Translational fidelity at the level of a whole protein

The fidelity of decoding is often described as an error probability per residue or per decoded codon. A long protein involves many opportunities for errors, so the chance of at least one error is not equal to the error probability for a single residue. Let p denote a constant independent probability that each of m decoding events introduces an incorrect amino acid. The probability of a correct event is 1−p. Under the independence assumption, the probability that all m events are correct is the product (1−p)^m. The probability of at least one error is its complement, 1−(1−p)^m. In a standard elongation-only model m=n−1; a problem that includes an independently modeled initiator error may instead specify m=n.

For a hypothetical 301-residue protein in which the 300 elongation decoding events have error probability 0.0001 each, the expected number of errors is 300 × 0.0001 = 0.03. This expected value is not the probability of at least one error, although they are close when errors are rare. The probability of an error-free elongation sequence is 0.9999 raised to the power 300, approximately 0.97044. The probability of at least one error is approximately 0.02956, or 2.96 percent. For 10,000 such independently synthesized proteins, the expected number containing at least one elongation error is about 296. There is no requirement that exactly 296 will be observed in a finite sample.

The expected number of individual errors over m trials is mp, by linearity of expectation. This result does not itself require independent trials when each trial has the same marginal probability. The formula for the probability of no errors, however, uses independence. This distinction matters because molecular errors can have correlated causes. A transient depletion of a specific charged tRNA might affect several occurrences of its codon within a transcript. Some errors or perturbations might also change the probability of later stalls or decoding problems. A simple independent model is a teaching model, not an assertion that all cells and all codons have identical error behavior.

A more general independent model assigns a probability p_i to event i. The probability that every event is correct becomes the product of (1−p_i) across all events. For example, suppose a protein includes 100 ordinary sites with error probability 0.0001 and 20 unusually error-prone sites with probability 0.002 in a hypothetical altered cell. Its error-free probability is 0.9999^100 × 0.998^20, approximately 0.9512. The 20 higher-risk sites contribute an expected 0.04 errors, compared with 0.01 from the 100 ordinary sites. A relatively small subset of codons can therefore dominate the error burden when their probabilities are sufficiently different.

A distinction should be made between an incorrect amino acid sequence and a loss of biological function. Even a protein with a substituted residue may retain its normal activity, depending on the position and substitution. Conversely a completely correct sequence can fail to fold under unfavorable cellular conditions. The probability of an error-free sequence is not automatically the probability of a functional protein. A reporter assay that relies on the activity of one enzyme can be sensitive to some errors and insensitive to others. A direct measurement of error frequency requires an appropriate method and a clear definition of what counts as an error.

The accuracy cost can also be analyzed at the level of repeated GTP consumption. Suppose that each intended successful elongation delivery requires an average of 1.2 delivery-associated GTP hydrolyses because some tRNAs are rejected after hydrolysis. Assume one translocation-associated GTP per successful round and no other expenses. For 200 successful rounds, the expected delivery GTP count is 240 and the translocation GTP count is 200, giving 440 total. The ideal count without post-hydrolysis rejection is 400. The extra expected expenditure is 40 GTP. A fractional average does not imply a single ribosome can hydrolyze a fraction of a nucleotide; it represents a mean over many cycles or molecules.

Under a simplified geometric-trial model, if each GTP-consuming delivery attempt independently succeeds with probability q, the mean number of such attempts per success is 1/q. The mean wasted attempts per successful event are (1−q)/q. This follows because the probability of requiring exactly j attempts is (1−q)^(j−1)q, for j from one upward. With q=0.8, the mean number of hydrolyses per successful delivery is 1.25, and the expected wasted count is 0.25. The model excludes failures before hydrolysis because those do not contribute to this specific energy counter. It also assumes that each failed attempt resets to the same statistical starting condition. Stating these assumptions is important when translating the model into biological predictions.

## 19. Rates, bottlenecks and the duration of elongation

Elongation rate can be reported as amino acid residues incorporated per second per active ribosome. If a ribosome adds residues at an approximately constant rate v and n−1 additions are required, the idealized elongation duration is (n−1)/v. A 301-residue product with 300 additions at an assumed constant rate of 15 residues per second requires 20 seconds of elongation. Initiation, termination and intervals of inactivity are excluded. The example uses an assigned rate for calculation rather than asserting a universal speed of translation in a particular cell type.

The duration of a single round can be analyzed as a sum of sequential waiting times. If productive delivery averages 20 milliseconds, peptide transfer averages 5 milliseconds and translocation averages 25 milliseconds, the mean duration is 50 milliseconds under the assumption that these are consecutive nonoverlapping contributions to the round. The corresponding throughput for a sustained sequence of such rounds is approximately 20 residues per second. If a mutation doubles only the translocation time to 50 milliseconds, the new total becomes 75 milliseconds, corresponding to about 13.3 residues per second. The overall rate has not fallen by half, because the unaffected operations still contribute to the duration.

The slowest individual step is sometimes called rate limiting. This phrase is most useful when one operation dominates the total duration by a substantial margin. If several operations contribute comparable waiting times, changing any one of them may influence the rate without fully controlling it. In a translating cell the bottleneck can also shift with substrate supply, codon identity, local sequence and factor availability. A loss of a charged tRNA species can make delivery particularly slow at the codons it normally recognizes; a movement defect can affect many codons without the same sequence specificity. A measurement of a mean translation rate can conceal these distinct local behaviors.

A series of codons does not necessarily have one fixed duration for every addition. If 90 additions each require an average of 0.05 second and 10 difficult additions each require 0.20 second, the total expected duration is 4.5 + 2 = 6.5 seconds. The effective mean rate is 100/6.5, approximately 15.38 residues per second. Taking the unweighted average of the two local rates would give a misleading result because the two groups contain different numbers of events and different waiting times. When predicting the completion time of a sequence, sum the times rather than simply averaging instantaneous rates.

The rate of protein production from an mRNA also depends on the frequency of successful initiation and the number of ribosomes simultaneously translating it. A ribosome can take longer to complete one protein without necessarily reducing the steady-state output in exact proportion if initiation is already much slower than elongation and no ribosome traffic interferes. Under different conditions, a long pause may create queues of ribosomes and reduce output. The distinction between transit time and production flux is important. A pulse-labeling experiment that measures the time to complete newly initiated chains answers a different question from a steady-state experiment that measures how many finished molecules are produced per minute.

These quantitative examples connect elongation to broader cell physiology. A growing cell needs enough active ribosomes and charged tRNAs to maintain protein synthesis at a rate compatible with its needs. Increasing initiation alone does not guarantee a proportional increase in completed proteins if the supply of elongation substrates or the capacity for translocation is limiting. Conversely a faster translocation factor will not restore the synthesis of a protein when its mRNA is absent. Mechanistic reasoning therefore requires defining the level at which the system is constrained.
