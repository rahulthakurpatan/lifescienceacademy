## 20. Experimental separation of selection, chemistry and movement

A mechanistic experiment should be designed around an observable that distinguishes the proposed step from alternatives. Measuring the amount of a complete protein synthesized by a cell is useful as an overall functional assay, but it is not sufficient to identify the exact defect. Initiation, tRNA charging, decoding, peptide transfer, translocation and termination all contribute to this final product. To study elongation specifically, investigators can begin with a defined initiation or elongation complex whose mRNA position and tRNA cargo are known. Supplying the missing substrate or factor then initiates a much more restricted reaction than adding a DNA template to a cell.

A classical cell-free translation approach uses ribosomes, soluble translation components and a defined message to examine amino acid incorporation. Nirenberg and Matthaei's 1961 experiments with synthetic polyuridylic acid helped establish the relationship between a repeated RNA sequence and phenylalanine incorporation. That experiment is often discussed in the history of the genetic code. Its relevance here is the ability to separate protein synthesis from an intact cell and control the sequence presented to the translational machinery. It did not by itself identify all elongation factors or prove the complete pathway of GTP-dependent proofreading. Later reconstitution and kinetic studies addressed more specific molecular questions.

Imagine three aliquots of identical prepared ribosomes with an initiator tRNA in the P site and a known sense codon in the A site. All receive an appropriately charged incoming tRNA. Aliquot A receives all required factors and GTP. Aliquot B lacks delivery factor. Aliquot C receives delivery factor but lacks the translocation factor. The control is expected to progress through delivery, peptide transfer and movement. Aliquot B should show greatly reduced normal factor-dependent substrate delivery under conditions in which spontaneous entry is negligible. Aliquot C may form a dipeptide but fail to efficiently expose the following codon. The different outcomes would show that peptide formation and message movement can be separated experimentally.

The predictions are conditional, not universal statements that no peptide can ever form without either factor. At sufficiently high substrate concentration or under specially chosen conditions, some normally factor-assisted reactions can occur at a low rate without their usual protein factors. A rigorous experiment therefore measures rates or fractions of product in a specified time window and includes a negative control. Reintroducing a purified functional factor to the corresponding deficient preparation helps establish whether the loss is specific. A heat-inactivated or inactive factor control can help distinguish the effect of adding a protein from the effect of restoring its particular activity.

The detection of peptide products can use a labeled amino acid in a defined experimental system. If the label is associated with the incoming residue, its presence in the isolated peptide reports incorporation, provided unreacted aminoacyl-tRNA and free amino acid are adequately distinguished. A suitable product separation can distinguish the expected dipeptide from longer products. If only total label associated with ribosomes is measured, the signal might instead represent a bound but unreacted tRNA. Thus binding and covalent incorporation must be distinguished in the design, just as they are distinguished mechanistically.

A separate readout can follow mRNA register. Toeprinting uses reverse transcription from a primer on the mRNA; a ribosomal complex can impede the reverse transcriptase and create a characteristic stop pattern. A comparison between properly defined pre-translocation and post-translocation complexes can reveal a shift associated with the movement of the ribosome by one codon. A reverse-transcription stop is an indirect position marker rather than a direct visualization of tRNA chemistry. RNA secondary structure can also influence extension, so a message without bound ribosomes and a reference complex with a known position are important controls.

Consider a hypothetical result in which a control makes dipeptide in 90 percent of complexes and shows an expected downstream toeprint displacement in 85 percent. An experimental sample makes dipeptide in 88 percent but shows the displacement in only 8 percent. The peptide measurement argues that delivery and chemistry remain largely functional. The positional measurement suggests that progression to the post-translocation state is impaired. A factor-addition rescue would strengthen the assignment. However, the exact defective molecule cannot be identified from these two observations alone: altered tRNA–ribosome contacts, an impaired movement factor or an RNA feature that obstructs movement could generate a similar phenotype.

## 21. Testing fidelity without confusing incorrect charging with incorrect decoding

Accuracy experiments must distinguish the correct product from possible incorrect products. A simple way to frame the problem is to use an mRNA codon whose correct amino acid is known and provide two distinguishable candidate aminoacyl-tRNAs. One is cognate and correctly charged; the other is near-cognate and correctly charged with its own amino acid. If the resulting peptide is analyzed for which amino acid has been added, it becomes possible to estimate discrimination. The assay must account for the concentrations of the candidates because changing their abundance can alter product composition without changing the inherent selectivity of the ribosome.

For example, suppose equal amounts of two appropriately charged candidate tRNAs are supplied in a hypothetical prepared reaction. Out of 1,000 completed single-addition products, 990 contain the cognate amino acid and 10 contain the near-cognate amino acid. The observed wrong-product fraction is 1 percent. If a variant ribosome generates 950 correct and 50 incorrect products under matched conditions, its observed wrong-product fraction is 5 percent. The relative increase is fivefold, and the absolute increase is four percentage points. Both descriptions are correct, but they answer different quantitative questions. Replication and uncertainty estimates would be needed in a real study before drawing a conclusion about a reproducible effect.

An alternative experiment deliberately supplies a mischarged cognate tRNA. Its anticodon remains appropriate for the chosen codon but the attached amino acid is different from the expected one. If that amino acid is incorporated efficiently, the result is compatible with the division of labor between aminoacylation and decoding. The ribosome's sequence check can function as expected while the final peptide is incorrect. A comparison with a near-cognate correctly charged tRNA can reveal two different routes to the same possible sequence substitution. The result should not be interpreted as evidence that all mischarged substrates are delivered equally well; factor binding and other interactions can differ depending on the properties of the tRNA and its attached amino acid.

The distinction can be extended by measuring hydrolysis separately from peptide formation. In a controlled system containing delivery factor, the amount of GTP converted to GDP can be compared with the amount of amino acid incorporated. A situation with elevated hydrolysis and low product formation may indicate that many substrate encounters enter the GTP-consuming pathway but are rejected before completion. It could also arise from nonspecific GTPase activity or uncoupling of nucleotide hydrolysis from the productive conformational transitions. Controls that omit the candidate tRNA, the mRNA or the relevant factor help define the background signal.

A useful hypothetical dataset contains three conditions with identical amounts of starting ribosome. The cognate-only condition gives 100 delivery-associated GTP hydrolyses and 90 peptide products. A near-cognate-only condition gives 60 hydrolyses and 3 peptide products. A mixture with a ribosome variant gives 110 hydrolyses and 75 products, of which 15 are incorrect. The first two observations are consistent with substantial discrimination after some near-cognate encounters have consumed GTP. The variant data suggest altered selectivity or coupling, but do not identify a unique stage. Measuring which tRNAs were bound before hydrolysis and which were accommodated afterward would be informative. This example illustrates why the final product and the nucleotide consumption are complementary observables.

Kinetic studies of ribosomal decoding have shown that appropriate codon recognition can accelerate productive conformational reactions, not merely increase equilibrium binding. This finding explains why both residence time and forward rate matter. A strong experiment compares pre-steady-state changes when a prepared complex encounters cognate and near-cognate substrates, controlling for factor concentrations and charging. The data may show differences in the rates of GTPase activation or accommodation. The mechanistic inference is strongest when multiple independent observables fit a coherent sequence of states. It is weaker when the same endpoint could arise from several changes in reaction kinetics.

## 22. From molecular mechanisms to biological phenotypes

A defect in elongation affects all proteins that depend on the relevant translation system, but the consequences need not be identical for every gene. A reduced pool of a particular charged tRNA is expected to slow decoding of the codons it normally recognizes. A defect in the peptidyl-transferase center can affect a more general reaction, although the severity may vary with the substrates. An altered translocation factor can slow repeated movements throughout many messages. These perturbations can all reduce cellular growth because protein production is central to metabolism and cellular maintenance, yet they leave different signatures in measurements of translation intermediates.

Some elongation-associated factors have specialized roles. In bacteria EF-P helps overcome inefficient peptide formation at certain sequences, particularly consecutive proline residues. The eukaryotic factor eIF5A, despite its historical name, contributes importantly to elongation, including synthesis through difficult peptide motifs. These factors illustrate that a single general reaction can have substrate-dependent kinetic difficulties. They should not replace EF-Tu/eEF1A or EF-G/eEF2 in a diagram of the ordinary cycle. Their functions supplement that cycle by helping particular peptide-transfer reactions proceed efficiently.

A nucleotide change in a coding region may alter a protein in several ways. A nonsynonymous change alters the amino acid specified by the relevant codon under the standard reading rules. A synonymous change preserves the encoded amino acid but can alter which tRNA species participates and how rapidly an available cognate substrate arrives. Such effects are context-dependent: a synonymous substitution does not automatically speed up or slow down translation. Local RNA structure, tRNA availability, initiation and other sequence features can also influence measured protein output. The essential lesson is that the genetic code specifies sequence, whereas the cellular translation machinery determines how efficiently that sequence is decoded.

The importance of elongation can also be seen in selective inhibition. A compound that interferes with entry of a charged tRNA can prevent a new bond from forming because the acceptor substrate is missing. A compound that interferes with the catalytic center can allow some tRNA binding yet prevent normal transfer. A compound that interferes with movement can allow a chemical product to form but impair progression to the following codon. Classical examples studied in the next inhibitor chapter include tetracycline acting on bacterial A-site tRNA accommodation and diphtheria toxin disrupting eukaryotic eEF2 function. These examples are useful as mechanistic probes, not as a substitute for the separate treatment of inhibitor spectra and exceptions.

Translation in mitochondria and plastids illustrates the need to identify the compartment before generalizing from a eukaryotic cell's cytosolic factors. These organelles retain evolutionarily bacterial-related translation systems with important differences among lineages. They need not have exactly the same ribosome composition or genetic-code assignments as a standard bacterium, and they are not simply copies of the cytosolic 80S system. An experimental inhibitor selective for a bacterial-like translation feature may therefore have different effects on cytosolic and organellar protein synthesis. The relevant inference must be tied to the system being tested.

As the growing N-terminal region emerges from the ribosome, it can begin to form local structures or interact with folding and targeting machinery. The sequence of events along a translating message can influence which parts of the chain are available at a given time. A pause at one region changes the interval during which the already synthesized portion is exposed. Such a pause is not invariably beneficial for folding; it can also create difficulties for a translating complex. Later chapters address protein folding and quality control in their own right. Here the important causal connection is that elongation produces a chain progressively rather than releasing a complete sequence instantaneously.

The completed elongation framework can now be summarized as a set of constraints that an unfamiliar experiment must satisfy. A correctly positioned peptidyl-tRNA supplies an activated donor. A new charged tRNA must be selected according to the current codon and must position its amino group in the catalytic center. Peptide transfer changes which adaptor carries the chain. Relative movement restores the donor to the P register and exposes a new message symbol. When a perturbation appears to disrupt protein synthesis, identifying which of these constraints fails is the first step toward a defensible mechanistic explanation.
