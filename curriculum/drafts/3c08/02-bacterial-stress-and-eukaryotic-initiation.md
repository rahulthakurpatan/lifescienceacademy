## 5. RNA thermometers and metabolite-sensitive initiation

A regulatory RNA need not depend on a separately produced small RNA to sense a change in the environment. The structure of a transcript can itself be sensitive to a physical variable. Bacterial RNA thermometers illustrate this possibility particularly clearly. At a relatively low temperature, base pairing and stacking interactions in a messenger leader can favor a conformation that masks its initiation region. A temperature rise changes the distribution of conformations and the rates of their interconversion. If the inhibitory structure becomes sufficiently unstable, the ribosome gains better access to the message. It is the change in structural accessibility, rather than temperature directly instructing a ribosome which gene to translate, that accounts for the specificity of this control.

RNA thermometers are often described as melting hairpins, but a useful molecular interpretation is more nuanced. A hairpin can contain internal mismatches, loops or weakly paired segments. Changes in temperature may first increase flexibility at a small set of strategically placed base pairs, exposing the recognition site without unfolding the entire leader. The RNA need not switch from a perfectly closed state to a fully unstructured molecule. The probability of a productive encounter with a ribosome can rise gradually as local access improves. This behavior is advantageous when a cell requires a graded response rather than a sharp on–off threshold. Conversely, some RNA architectures can generate a relatively steep response over a useful temperature interval.

The FourU element associated with the Salmonella enterica agsA message is a well-known bacterial example. A sequence containing four uridines participates in pairing with a region that includes the Shine–Dalgarno sequence. Temperature-sensitive accessibility allows increased translation of a heat-associated protective protein. Another extensively studied family is the ROSE class of bacterial RNA thermometers, whose name refers to repression of heat shock gene expression at lower temperatures. The examples are biologically informative because they connect a physical property of RNA to the immediate physiological problem of protein folding under heat stress. Not every heat shock gene is controlled by an RNA thermometer, and some transcripts are subject to additional transcriptional and protein-level controls.

The evidence for a thermometer is stronger when its behavior can be transferred to an unrelated reporter using the candidate leader sequence. A suitable comparison tests two temperatures, maintains identical reporter coding sequences and uses a promoter whose activity is independently assessed. A destabilizing leader mutation is predicted to reduce low-temperature repression; a stabilizing mutation can reduce induction at the higher temperature. Compensatory mutations can test whether base pairing is the relevant feature. If the RNA is probed in the absence of ribosomes, temperature-dependent accessibility near the initiation region provides evidence that structural changes can occur independently of translation. A purified translation system can further test whether the temperature dependence is retained without other transcription factors. Because temperature influences many cellular reactions, a control reporter lacking the thermometer is particularly important.

A related but distinct regulatory mechanism is a translational riboswitch. Its sensory portion binds a small molecule, and ligand binding changes an RNA conformation that communicates with an expression platform. In a translationally repressive switch, ligand binding may favor a conformation that masks an initiation site. In an activating switch, binding can instead favor a conformation that exposes it. This direction is specified by the architecture of the particular RNA, not by a general rule that metabolites always repress protein synthesis. If the metabolite is the product of a biosynthetic pathway and the inhibited message encodes a pathway enzyme, repression can implement negative feedback. If the regulated product is useful specifically in the presence of the ligand, activation may be advantageous.

The distinction between a translational riboswitch and a transcriptional riboswitch depends on which downstream process is directly altered. Some switches promote formation of a transcription terminator, reducing production of the full-length transcript; others leave the transcript intact but alter ribosome access. In an experiment that measures only reporter activity, either mechanism could appear to be repression. Measuring full-length RNA and using equal amounts of preformed RNA in a translation assay helps separate them. The general ligand-binding and structural properties of riboswitches were assigned to the preceding RNA subunit; here the important point is how the structure determines use of a mature or nascent message by a ribosome. A riboswitch and an RNA thermometer both exploit conformational changes, but their principal inputs differ: occupancy by a particular ligand in one case and physical temperature in the other.

## 6. Feedback through RNA-binding proteins and translational coupling

Regulatory proteins in bacteria can bind mRNA instead of DNA. This is particularly useful when the protein being synthesized is itself part of a molecular assembly. Ribosomes, for example, require many ribosomal proteins together with rRNA. If synthesis of one assembly component continued independently while other components were scarce, large excesses of unassembled protein would be produced. A mechanism that couples production of a protein to the availability of its binding partner can limit this waste. In several well-studied bacterial ribosomal protein operons, excess unassembled ribosomal proteins act as repressors of translation of their own operon messages.

The feedback can be understood by considering competing binding partners. A regulatory ribosomal protein can bind an appropriate site during assembly with rRNA. When sufficient suitable rRNA is available, assembly consumes much of the newly synthesized protein, reducing the concentration of the free regulatory form. When rRNA supply falls relative to ribosomal protein synthesis, a larger free pool becomes available. A protein that also recognizes a regulatory structure in the operon mRNA can then occupy that structure and reduce production of the encoded proteins. This introduces an automatic link between assembly demand and synthesis. The regulatory mRNA site may mimic aspects of the protein's rRNA recognition site, but the precise structural similarity and binding arrangements differ among examples.

This feedback is autogenous in the sense that a product of the regulated genetic system contributes directly to its regulation. It should not be confused with every form of negative regulation. A separate regulator made at another locus may repress translation but need not be an autogenous feedback regulator. Nor is all autogenous feedback translational: a protein that binds its own promoter can regulate transcription. The location and function of the binding site establish which level is directly controlled. Work on E. coli ribosomal protein operons supports translational feedback for many but not all operons. The presence of a ribosomal protein gene in a cluster is not sufficient evidence that its product has this regulatory role.

A testable prediction of the competition model is that an increase in suitable rRNA availability can reduce the free regulatory protein pool, relieving repression of the mRNA. However, this prediction assumes that the added rRNA can engage the relevant binding partner and that assembly conditions allow the interaction. Adding a defective RNA lacking the necessary binding site would be a poor rescue. A mutation in the regulatory recognition region of the mRNA might abolish negative feedback without removing the assembly function of the protein. In contrast, a mutation within the protein that disrupts both RNA recognition and ribosomal assembly can cause multiple defects. Comparing regulatory-site mutants with protein mutants, and measuring both synthesis and assembled ribosomes, helps distinguish these possibilities.

The simplest binding model offers a quantitative intuition. Suppose a fixed concentration of free regulatory protein R binds a single mRNA site with dissociation constant Kd. If depletion of the protein by target binding is negligible, the equilibrium bound fraction is R/(Kd + R). If only unbound messages initiate at an unchanged rate, the normalized initiation rate is Kd/(Kd + R). At R = Kd, half the sites are bound and the predicted initiation rate is half of the unrepressed rate. Increasing R from Kd to 9Kd raises occupancy from 0.5 to 0.9 and reduces the predicted rate from 0.5 to 0.1. The model illustrates how the free regulator concentration, not necessarily its total amount, determines occupancy. It neglects cooperative interactions, ligand depletion, synthesis and decay, and possible initiation from partially occupied structures.

Bacterial polycistronic messages introduce a further level of dependence known as translational coupling. The downstream gene can have its own initiation region, yet its utilization may depend on translation of an upstream gene. One way this occurs is through RNA structure: the start region of the downstream coding sequence is buried in a stem that overlaps the upstream coding region. Passage of an upstream ribosome disrupts the structure, providing a period during which another initiation complex can recognize the downstream site. In other cases a ribosomal particle finishing one reading frame can contribute to reinitiation near a neighboring start site. These mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive, and their relative contribution is sequence dependent.

Consider two proteins A and B encoded in consecutive reading frames of the same transcript. If the first reading frame is prematurely terminated before a segment that contributes to an inhibitory stem at the second start site, production of B may decline even though the sequence of B is unchanged. The explanation is not that a ribosome translates the first gene and then chemically transfers its finished product into the second. Rather, translation of the upstream region helps determine downstream initiation competence. A premature stop can also reduce downstream transcript abundance through transcriptional polarity in certain bacterial contexts, so the experiment should measure the full-length RNA. Mutation of the downstream stem that restores accessibility without repairing the upstream reading frame can support the structural coupling explanation.

In bacteria, transcription and translation can overlap in time because the two processes are not separated by a nuclear envelope. A ribosome may engage a nascent transcript before RNA polymerase reaches the end of the transcription unit. This fact has consequences for RNA folding, transcript stability and some termination processes. It is distinct from translational coupling between adjacent reading frames, despite the shared word coupling. When a ribosome protects nascent RNA or alters its folding, it can influence subsequent transcription or translation events. These interactions illustrate why measurements of one expression layer cannot always be interpreted as isolated from the others. The detailed transcriptional attenuation and integrated operon programs are treated in the gene-expression subunit rather than repeated here.

## 7. Bacterial nutrient limitation, ribosomal activity and recovery

Protein synthesis consumes amino acids, ATP and GTP. A shortage of one amino acid may reduce the charging fraction of its cognate tRNA, even though the total number of those tRNA molecules is unchanged. An elongating ribosome encountering a codon for that amino acid requires a correctly charged tRNA; uncharged tRNA cannot donate the missing amino acid. If the supply of charged tRNA becomes low relative to demand, waiting times at susceptible codons can increase. This constitutes a direct biochemical constraint on translation and provides a signal that the cell's pattern of resource allocation needs adjustment.

In E. coli, the stringent response provides an important connection between this problem at the ribosome and broader resource management. RelA can respond to ribosomes associated with deacylated tRNA in the A site during amino acid starvation. Activated RelA synthesizes guanosine alarmones, collectively called (p)ppGpp, using ATP together with GDP or GTP as substrates. The notation refers to ppGpp and pppGpp, not to a single ambiguous type of tRNA. The alarmone response helps coordinate metabolism and macromolecular synthesis with nutrient availability. In the classical E. coli framework, synthesis of stable RNA including rRNA is reduced under stringent conditions, limiting investment in new ribosomes when resources are scarce. Other gene-expression changes support adaptation.

It is important to identify the causal step in a question about this response. RelA is not a tRNA synthetase; increasing its activity does not directly attach an amino acid to tRNA. An uncharged tRNA at a stalled ribosome is not a normal substrate for peptide-bond formation. The biochemical shortage can still cause slow elongation in a cell that lacks RelA, because deleting the signaling protein does not provide the missing charged tRNA. What is disrupted is an important adaptive signaling route. A relA mutant may retain other alarmone-related responses depending on its genetic background, and bacteria differ in the proteins used to synthesize or degrade these nucleotides. Results from E. coli should not be turned into claims about every bacterium.

A hypothetical comparison illustrates the distinction between limitation and signaling. Both wild-type cells and cells with impaired RelA activity receive an amino acid supply insufficient for their original growth rate. Direct pulse labeling shows a rapid decrease in protein synthesis in both cultures. In the wild type, the alarmone pool subsequently rises and rRNA production falls strongly. In the mutant the alarmone response to this particular input is smaller. This dataset is compatible with a direct reduction in elongation from low charged tRNA in both cultures, and a difference in the adaptive allocation response. It does not establish that all of the reduction in protein synthesis is caused by RelA. Measuring aminoacylation, ribosome occupancy and RNA synthesis at several time points makes the mechanistic distinction clearer.

Bacteria can also control the fraction of ribosomes available for productive translation. During starvation or stationary phase, some ribosome-associated factors promote inactive states. In E. coli, ribosome modulation factor (RMF) and hibernation-promoting factor (HPF) participate in formation and stabilization of inactive ribosome assemblies often described as 100S particles. These involve dimerization of 70S ribosomes. In other bacteria, different hibernation factor arrangements can promote 100S formation; some factors stabilize inactive 70S particles instead. Sedimentation terminology describes physical behavior rather than simple arithmetic addition of sedimentation coefficients.

Hibernation is not the same as ribosome degradation. A cell may benefit from conserving existing ribosomes in a protected or inactive form during a period when their immediate use is restricted. When favorable conditions return, inactive particles can be converted back into active ribosomes, allowing quicker recovery than synthesis of an entirely new pool. Nevertheless, the effect of a particular hibernation factor on long-term survival and ribosome stability depends on the species and conditions. The key exam-level point is that ribosome abundance and the fraction engaged in productive translation can vary independently. A sample with abundant rRNA can exhibit poor protein synthesis if much of its ribosome pool is unavailable.

A sucrose-gradient profile might show an increase in a 100S peak during starvation and a decline in actively translating polysomes. Following nutrient restoration, the 100S peak might diminish while polysomes increase. This pattern is consistent with reversible redistribution of the ribosome pool. However, the sedimentation peak alone is not proof that the particles were inactive in living cells; sample preparation and ionic conditions influence sedimentation. A suitable interpretation combines gradients with independent synthesis measurements and, where feasible, mutants lacking specific hibernation factors. The physiological advantage of such a response cannot be inferred solely from the size of an inactive-particle peak. Growth recovery, survival or ribosome integrity provides additional evidence.

These examples show that bacterial translation can be regulated at multiple temporal scales. Local initiation control can alter a selected message rapidly. Decreased aminoacyl-tRNA supply limits ongoing elongation and may trigger a signaling response. Changes in rRNA synthesis modify future ribosomal capacity. Redistribution into inactive complexes changes the fraction of existing capacity being used. A recovery response must reverse appropriate inhibitory events while re-establishing a balance between demand and resources. It is misleading to describe bacterial translational control as exclusively a Shine–Dalgarno phenomenon; that motif offers one important target, but the whole apparatus remains subject to physiological constraints.

## 8. Eukaryotic initiation: two separable requirements

Most cellular mRNAs translated by mammalian cytosolic ribosomes possess a 5′ cap, an untranslated leader, a coding region and a 3′ end whose organization often includes a poly(A) tail. The cap is a chemical modification of the RNA, not the start codon. It can serve as a molecular recognition site for the cap-binding factor eIF4E. The initiation machinery uses this interaction to help recruit a small-subunit-containing preinitiation complex. The complex must also carry the initiator methionyl tRNA in an appropriate factor-bound state. Cap recognition and initiator-tRNA recruitment are therefore distinct requirements. Their distinction explains why a cell can regulate the same initiation process through at least two separate sets of factors.

The eIF4F complex provides a conceptual bridge between an mRNA and the ribosomal recruitment machinery. It contains eIF4E, which recognizes the cap; eIF4G, which serves as a scaffold interacting with other factors; and eIF4A, an ATP-dependent RNA helicase involved in remodeling RNA structures during recruitment and scanning. The scaffold helps connect the cap-associated complex to the 40S-containing machinery, including interactions with eIF3. Poly(A)-binding protein can interact with eIF4G and thereby help coordinate the two ends of the transcript. Such interactions can promote initiation and influence the stability or functional organization of an mRNA. A schematic circularized message is a model for these contacts, not evidence that every translated mRNA is permanently arranged as a rigid closed circle.

After recruitment, a scanning complex normally searches the leader for an initiation codon in an appropriate context. Recognition of AUG is influenced by its neighboring nucleotides and by initiation factors that help determine the fidelity of start-site selection. Strong inhibitory RNA structures can slow recruitment or impede scanning. Their effect can depend on position, stability and availability of remodeling activities. A stem near the cap can interfere with initial engagement, whereas another further downstream can alter the scanning journey. A change in eIF4A activity can therefore affect different messages unequally. It is not sufficient to assume that every messenger has identical factor requirements because all use the same ribosome type.

A second component, the eIF2 ternary complex, contains eIF2 bound to GTP and the initiator Met-tRNAi. The initiator tRNA has a specialized role in selecting and occupying the initiation site. Elongator methionine tRNA, although charged with the same amino acid, is not an interchangeable replacement in the normal initiation pathway. The amount of available ternary complex influences the rate at which new productive preinitiation complexes can form. When a start codon is accepted, GTP hydrolysis and phosphate release are coordinated with initiation transitions, and eIF2 leaves in a GDP-bound form. Before it can efficiently deliver another initiator tRNA, its bound nucleotide must be exchanged.

In many textbook diagrams the initiation process is drawn as a linear row of factors, making it appear that regulation at any one factor has exactly the same consequences for every transcript. Actual messages differ in their leaders, binding interactions and start-site arrangements. A decrease in eIF4F assembly can especially affect recruitment of messages that depend strongly on the cap-associated apparatus. A decrease in available eIF2 ternary complex changes the availability of initiator-tRNA-bearing complexes and can modify reinitiation behavior after an upstream reading frame. Both changes may reduce bulk protein production, yet their effects on individual mRNAs can differ. The central mechanistic task is to identify which required resource has been restricted and how the target transcript uses that resource.

Some eukaryotic messages can recruit translation machinery in ways that do not require conventional cap recognition, and some initiation events have nonstandard factor requirements. These exceptions are useful for interpreting carefully designed reporter experiments. They should not be used to claim that all cap-independent translation is independent of every initiation factor, or that all stress-responsive messages use an internal ribosome entry site. The well-established canonical ATF4 response discussed later is primarily understood through the arrangement of upstream reading frames and reinitiation under altered ternary-complex availability. The detailed study of viral translation strategies lies outside the present node. The transferable concept is that a factor can be limiting for one recruitment or initiation route without being equally limiting for all routes.

## 9. The eIF2 nucleotide cycle and its regulation by phosphorylation

The nucleotide carried by eIF2 changes its functional state during initiation. The GTP-bound factor associates with initiator Met-tRNAi, forming a ternary complex that can participate in assembly of a preinitiation complex. During start-site selection, eIF2-associated GTP is hydrolyzed in the regulated initiation cycle. The factor ultimately leaves the ribosome in a GDP-bound form. GDP is not converted to GTP while it remains attached simply by adding an inorganic phosphate. Instead the bound GDP is released and another nucleotide, GTP, can bind. A guanine nucleotide exchange factor, eIF2B, promotes the exchange process.

It is worth distinguishing the energy-consuming reaction from the exchange reaction. The hydrolysis of GTP is associated with directional progress through initiation. eIF2B does not synthesize GTP from GDP as a metabolic kinase. Its catalytic contribution is to facilitate nucleotide exchange so that eIF2 can be reused. The cell's nucleotide metabolism maintains an environment in which GTP can become available for rebinding. If eIF2B activity is inhibited, GDP-bound eIF2 accumulates relative to the normal cycling state, and the supply of newly assembled ternary complexes can decline. A cell may therefore contain nearly normal total amounts of eIF2 while exhibiting a substantial loss of its initiation-supporting activity.

In mammalian cells, phosphorylation of serine 51 of the eIF2 alpha subunit is a major mechanism for controlling this exchange cycle. The alpha subunit is not itself the principal GTP-binding subunit; eIF2 is a heterotrimer, with the gamma subunit carrying the nucleotide-binding function. Phosphorylation changes the way the factor interacts with eIF2B. Phosphorylated eIF2 is inhibitory to the exchange machinery, rather than simply becoming an efficient substrate that releases GDP more rapidly. As a consequence, recycling of the available eIF2 pool is impaired and the concentration of active ternary complex decreases. A relatively limited amount of phosphorylated factor can have a substantial influence when exchange capacity is limiting.

The inhibitory interaction is an example of why phosphorylation cannot be interpreted by a universal rule of activation. Adding a phosphate changes molecular charge, interaction surfaces and sometimes conformational preferences. In this case phosphorylation of one component inhibits the productive activity of another. In the mTOR pathway discussed later, phosphorylation of a different inhibitory protein relieves repression. Both involve phosphorylation, but the signs of their effects on translation are opposite. An examination question that merely states that a translation-associated protein becomes phosphorylated is incomplete unless it specifies the target and the functional consequence of that modification.

One can reason about the exchange step with a simple illustrative model in which the effective active exchange machinery is reduced from a value of one to a fraction f. Suppose production of usable ternary complex is proportional to f and its consumption is first order in ternary-complex concentration. If all other parameters remain fixed, the steady-state ternary-complex pool is proportional to f. An inhibition that reduces effective exchange capacity to 25 percent of its initial value would then reduce that pool to 25 percent of its original value. This is a pedagogical linear model, not a measured description of the actual multimeric eIF2B system. Real exchange activity and initiation can show saturation, competition, feedback and nonlinear sensitivity. It clarifies the causal direction without assigning an unjustified quantitative law to the biological machinery.

The expected effects of mutations follow from their positions in this cycle. A nonphosphorylatable eIF2 alpha S51A mutant cannot acquire the canonical serine-51 phosphate at that residue. Under a stress whose acute initiation response depends on that phosphorylation, this mutant should diminish that specific mode of repression, assuming the mutant replaces sufficient endogenous factor and retains normal basal activity. Other inhibitory mechanisms may still reduce translation. A mutation that severely impairs eIF2B exchange activity can reduce translation even without increased eIF2 alpha phosphorylation. A variant of eIF2B that retains exchange function but is less sensitive to phosphorylated eIF2 may partially uncouple stress sensing from the normal decrease in ternary-complex availability. The outcome depends on residual activity and cellular context; these are mechanistic predictions, not blanket guarantees of viability.

Removal of the phosphate allows the system to recover its productive cycling behavior. In mammalian cells, protein phosphatase 1 complexes with regulatory partners contribute to dephosphorylation of eIF2 alpha. Constitutive and stress-induced regulatory components help balance kinase action. The induction of a stress-responsive phosphatase regulatory component such as GADD34 can form part of feedback that restores translation. This creates a temporal structure: an acute stress signal inhibits general initiation, selected adaptive products are made, and appropriate recovery mechanisms can reverse the inhibition. Prolonged inhibition may be maladaptive, and recovery before the original stress is resolved can also be costly. Translational regulation thus functions as a reversible allocation system rather than a permanent erasure of genetic information.
