## 10. Four stress-sensitive kinases converge on a common initiation factor

The mammalian integrated stress response illustrates a general design principle: different physiological problems can converge on a shared molecular event while retaining distinct mechanisms of sensing. Four well-known protein kinases phosphorylate the same regulatory residue of eIF2 alpha. They are usually designated GCN2, PERK, PKR and HRI. Their names refer to different physiological contexts or historical discoveries. In each case, the downstream effect on eIF2B and ternary-complex availability can reduce the initiation of many messages. The common downstream reaction does not imply that the upstream kinases recognize the same stimulus.

GCN2 is classically associated with amino acid limitation. As the concentration of a required amino acid falls, the charging of cognate tRNAs may become inadequate. Uncharged tRNAs and features of the stressed translation machinery contribute to activation of GCN2. The resulting eIF2 alpha phosphorylation lowers general initiation, thereby reducing the demand for aminoacyl-tRNAs. A selective increase in the synthesis of stress-responsive proteins can help the cell adjust amino acid uptake, biosynthesis and metabolism. This mechanism is conceptually related to the bacterial stringent response in that both respond to difficulties arising during protein synthesis, but the signal-transduction molecules are different. GCN2 does not produce ppGpp, and RelA is not a eukaryotic eIF2 kinase.

PERK illustrates the connection between protein synthesis and the folding capacity of the endoplasmic reticulum. Newly synthesized secretory and membrane proteins enter or associate with the ER through mechanisms treated in later chapters. When the ER folding environment is disturbed, the rate at which newly arriving proteins can be processed may become smaller than the rate at which they are produced. In this situation, PERK activation contributes to an acute reduction in general translation through eIF2 alpha phosphorylation. Lowering new protein influx can provide an opportunity for folding and quality-control systems to recover. This branch of the unfolded protein response is particularly useful as an illustration of negative feedback at the level of protein demand. The complete ER stress signaling program includes other branches and should not be identified solely with PERK.

PKR is best known for activation by double-stranded RNA in appropriate cellular contexts. Activation of this kinase during certain viral infections can restrict translation through phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha. This creates a physiological tradeoff, because a virus commonly uses host components to produce its proteins, yet host synthesis also depends on the same apparatus. The change is not restricted automatically to viral mRNAs. A cell's capacity to balance antiviral responses, adaptive protein synthesis and other functions determines the eventual outcome. Viral mechanisms that evade this response are diverse and are assigned to the subsequent gene-expression chapter; the important translation principle is the convergence of a nucleic acid-related signal on a shared initiation factor.

HRI was classically characterized in the context of heme availability and hemoglobin production in erythroid cells. Hemoglobin requires both globin protein chains and heme groups. If heme supply becomes inadequate while globin continues to be made at the original rate, the imbalance can lead to accumulation of unpaired or poorly handled globin chains. HRI-dependent phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha can reduce general initiation and thereby help adjust globin production to the availability of its nonprotein partner. HRI can respond to additional forms of stress in some cellular contexts, so the classical name does not restrict it exclusively to heme. The example nevertheless offers a memorable causal link between the synthesis of a protein and the availability of a required prosthetic group.

A common experimental strategy tests whether the downstream phosphorylation is necessary for a particular translational response. Cells with a normal eIF2 alpha allele can be compared with cells in which the relevant serine cannot be phosphorylated. After an appropriate stress, an immunoblot with a phosphorylation-specific antibody establishes whether the modification occurred, and a direct measure of protein synthesis tests the functional response. A kinase-deficient cell line can help identify the upstream branch. A negative result needs careful interpretation: incomplete deletion, compensating kinases, alternative stress pathways, and differences in the initial state can obscure the predicted response. Restoration of the wild-type kinase or an appropriate genetic rescue increases causal confidence. Measuring the total abundance of eIF2 alpha as well as its phosphorylated form distinguishes a modification change from altered protein expression.

Convergence also creates a limitation for diagnosis. Detecting elevated eIF2 alpha phosphorylation in a cell does not uniquely identify which kinase was responsible, just as observing a low synthesis rate does not uniquely identify the inhibited step. To distinguish the upstream input, one needs information about the stress, activity of candidate kinases, genetic perturbations or appropriate pathway markers. Similarly, a normal phosphorylation signal does not exclude translation inhibition through mTOR, RNA-binding proteins, aminoacyl-tRNA scarcity or other routes. A good examination answer therefore proceeds from the stimulus to the sensing branch, from the kinase to its substrate, and from the modified substrate to the exchange and initiation mechanism, without treating any single measurement as proof of an entire pathway.

## 11. Upstream reading frames and selective protein synthesis during stress

The reduction of general initiation following eIF2 alpha phosphorylation might suggest that every protein must be produced more slowly. A particularly important exception arises when the leader of a messenger contains short upstream open reading frames, often abbreviated uORFs. An open reading frame starts at a suitable initiation codon and continues in the same reading frame until a termination codon. An upstream reading frame is recognized before the principal protein-coding start site in the conventional scanning model. Translating it can change the probability that a ribosome subsequently initiates at the main coding sequence. It is therefore a structural feature of the message that can convert a global change in initiation-factor availability into a transcript-specific effect.

To understand how this occurs, distinguish leaky scanning from reinitiation. In leaky scanning, a scanning complex does not initiate at an upstream start codon and continues toward a later start site. Its ability to bypass that codon can depend on the initiation context and factor state. In reinitiation, the ribosome actually translates an upstream reading frame and terminates. Under some conditions a small-subunit-containing complex remains associated with the message, resumes movement along the leader and later acquires the factors necessary for another initiation event. Reinitiation is not a continuation of the same polypeptide. The upstream polypeptide has been released, and the new start site establishes a new protein product and reading frame.

Whether the ribosome can reinitiate depends on multiple properties. Short reading frames may permit retention of factors or interactions needed for resumption of scanning more readily than some long reading frames. The distance from the upstream termination site to a downstream start determines how much time is available to reacquire an initiator-tRNA-bearing complex. The upstream start context affects how many scanning particles translate the upstream region in the first place. The downstream context determines whether a competent scanning complex accepts that codon. The sequence of a uORF peptide can sometimes influence elongation or termination as well. Thus an upstream reading frame is not a universal inhibitory switch; its consequence depends on the entire arrangement.

The mammalian ATF4 message provides the classic stress-responsive example. Its leader contains a first upstream reading frame that facilitates the regulated reinitiation mechanism, followed by a second upstream reading frame that is inhibitory for production of the main ATF4 protein. The second reading frame begins upstream of the ATF4 start and extends into the main coding region in a different reading frame. If a ribosome initiates at this second uORF, it passes the principal start site while translating another sequence. Under the usual forward-scanning mechanism, termination downstream of that start prevents the ribosome from simply returning to initiate at the ATF4 start that it already passed.

In favorable growth conditions, ternary complexes are relatively available. After translation and termination at uORF1, a scanning small-subunit-containing complex can reacquire initiation competence in time to recognize the start of the inhibitory uORF2. Translation of uORF2 then reduces the fraction that reaches the main ATF4 coding region as a competent initiating complex. This means that efficient availability of initiation components can actually support a route that suppresses ATF4 synthesis. It is a local consequence of the arrangement of start and stop sites, not a direct inhibitory action of eIF2 on the ATF4 protein.

During the canonical integrated stress response, phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha decreases eIF2B activity and ternary-complex supply. After the first uORF has been translated, reacquisition of initiation competence can take longer on average. Some scanning complexes therefore pass the start of uORF2 before becoming able to initiate again. If they acquire the required ternary complex before reaching the main ATF4 start, they can initiate at that start instead of the inhibitory one. The overall probability of productive initiation on many other messages falls, while the routing of a subset of ribosomes along the ATF4 leader changes in a way that favors ATF4 synthesis. The resulting ATF4 protein is a transcription factor that can influence expression of adaptive genes, but its own acute increase in synthesis is an example of translational regulation.

The time window is crucial. If ternary-complex availability became extremely low, initial recruitment and subsequent reinitiation at the main start could both fail. The model does not predict an unlimited rise in ATF4 production as initiation capacity approaches zero. Rather, it predicts that an intermediate reduction can increase the relative probability of bypassing an inhibitory site while retaining sufficient initiation competence for the desired downstream site. Stress strength, message abundance and alternative modes of regulation can modify the observed absolute output. Thus a relative increase in the use of the main coding frame does not guarantee an absolute increase in protein synthesis under every degree of stress.

A simplified probability model illustrates the delay. Assume a ribosome has just completed uORF1 and resumes scanning at a constant speed. Assume acquisition of ternary complex follows a first-order waiting-time distribution with rate constant λ per second. If the inhibitory start is reached after time t2 and the main start after time t3, with t3 greater than t2, the probability of remaining unprepared at t2 but becoming prepared by t3 is exp(−λt2) − exp(−λt3). This follows from the probability of waiting longer than a given time, exp(−λt), and subtracting the subset that is still unprepared at the later site. We assume every complex prepared before the inhibitory start initiates there and every one acquiring competence in the interval initiates at the main start. Neither assumption is exactly true for real ATF4, but both make the causal prediction explicit.

Take hypothetical values t2 = 2 seconds and t3 = 6 seconds. At λ = 1 per second, the calculated probability for the desired interval is exp(−2) − exp(−6), approximately 0.1329. At λ = 0.3 per second it is exp(−0.6) − exp(−1.8), approximately 0.3835. At λ = 0.02 per second it is exp(−0.04) − exp(−0.12), approximately 0.0739. An intermediate reduction in the acquisition rate favors the main frame in this example; a very strong reduction does not. The probabilities are conditional on retention of a scanning particle following uORF1 and do not incorporate the fall in initial recruitment. They are not experimentally measured percentages of ATF4 translation. Multiplying them by the number of particles entering the pathway would be necessary to model absolute protein output.

The model also suggests a useful mutation experiment. Removing the inhibitory uORF2 start codon while preserving the main coding sequence can relieve basal repression and reduce the contrast between unstressed and stressed cells. Disrupting the first uORF may alter the regulated reinitiation route and diminish normal stress-dependent activation, although the exact residual output depends on how scanning complexes engage the remaining starts. Changing the spacing between the uORFs can alter the probability of reacquiring ternary complex before the inhibitory start. A change that adds time before uORF2 may increase initiation at the inhibitory site in conditions that would otherwise favor bypass. To attribute a result to spacing rather than a newly introduced RNA structure, the inserted sequence should be tested with appropriate alternatives.

Classic reporter experiments by Vattem and Wek in 2004 tested the function of the two ATF4 upstream reading frames and the requirement for scanning-dependent reinitiation. Independent work by Lu and colleagues in the same period also supported regulated reinitiation at alternative reading frames. The strength of such experiments is that the leader sequence can be altered separately from the principal reporter protein. Genetic control of eIF2 phosphorylation and comparisons of leader variants connect factor state to the RNA architecture. The studies establish the canonical mechanism without requiring one to assume that every stress condition or every uORF-containing message behaves identically. The distinction between a demonstrated mechanism for a specific transcript and a prediction for a new transcript is an important scientific boundary.

## 12. Yeast GCN4 as a comparative genetic model

The GCN4 system in budding yeast provides a historically important model for understanding translational regulation by upstream reading frames. Gcn4 is a transcriptional regulator that contributes to the response to amino acid limitation, including the induction of genes involved in amino acid biosynthesis. The message contains four well-studied upstream reading frames. Under nutrient-replete conditions, the availability of initiation components favors reinitiation at downstream inhibitory upstream frames rather than efficient synthesis of the main Gcn4 protein. Under amino acid starvation, signaling through Gcn2 reduces availability of the ternary complex and changes the probability of reinitiation at the different sites.

The first upstream reading frame is important because translation of it can be followed by retention of scanning competence. In the standard simplified model, a scanning particle emerging from uORF1 can subsequently reinitiate at one of the downstream upstream frames if it acquires ternary complex sufficiently early. Initiation at the more distal inhibitory frames, especially the fourth in the classical simplified comparison, generally reduces main-frame expression. During amino acid starvation, delayed reacquisition can allow particles to bypass inhibitory starts and become competent nearer the GCN4 coding region. The logic resembles the ATF4 example, but the actual numbers, spacing and properties of the upstream frames differ. One should not draw the yeast leader as the two-frame mammalian ATF4 leader.

Classical genetic work by Hinnebusch and colleagues used deletion and alteration of upstream frames and reporter constructs to distinguish the roles of upstream regions. If all upstream frames were merely equivalent barriers, each deletion should have a similar directional effect. The finding that particular upstream frames are required for efficient regulated derepression while others have inhibitory functions instead supports a model in which ribosomal history and timing matter. Further tests of the sequences around the first frame helped explain why some terminating complexes can retain the ability to resume scanning. This progression from genetic observation to a mechanistic model is a useful illustration of how translational regulation was investigated before genome-wide ribosome occupancy methods became common.

From the standpoint of comparative biology, both yeast and mammalian examples exploit changes in a shared initiation resource to adjust the synthesis of proteins that promote adaptation. However, the full mammalian set of four eIF2 alpha kinases should not be assigned to budding yeast as though all eukaryotes possess identical stress signaling. The classic yeast nutrient-response model involves Gcn2. Likewise, the downstream proteins are not identical in every organism even though the general logic is similar. Comparing conserved biochemical steps and differing RNA architectures is more informative than memorizing a list of factor names without their biological relationships.

An examination may describe a hypothetical eukaryotic reporter with an upstream positive frame followed by an inhibitory frame. If lowering ternary-complex abundance increases expression of the main reporter but reduces general protein synthesis, the most reasonable first hypothesis is altered reinitiation timing. To distinguish it from enhanced transcription, one should measure reporter mRNA. To distinguish reinitiation from direct downstream recruitment, one can change the first upstream start or insert an appropriate scanning barrier, while controlling RNA stability. To test the role of eIF2 phosphorylation, one can compare cells capable and incapable of the relevant phosphorylation response. These experimental predictions follow from the causal structure of the model and do not require memorizing the exact nucleotide sequence of GCN4 or ATF4.

## 13. Nutrient-sensitive mTORC1 and cap-associated initiation

Cells growing in favorable nutrient conditions face a different allocation problem from cells experiencing acute stress. They must decide whether the available resources justify investment in protein synthesis, cell growth and production of additional ribosomes. In mammalian cells, mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1, abbreviated mTORC1, integrates information associated with nutrient supply, growth signaling and energetic state. Its translation-related targets provide a direct route from those conditions to initiation machinery. The detailed network that activates mTORC1 belongs to cell signaling. For the present purpose, its relevant function is that of a protein kinase acting on regulators of translation.

The major mechanistic example involves eIF4E-binding proteins, commonly called 4E-BPs. In a relatively hypophosphorylated state, an inhibitory 4E-BP can bind eIF4E and compete with eIF4G for an interaction needed to assemble a productive eIF4F complex. This distinction is important: eIF4E may still bind the cap while a 4E-BP blocks the interaction with eIF4G. The repressor need not physically cover the cap itself to prevent productive recruitment. A decrease in the availability of eIF4E for eIF4G association can reduce cap-dependent initiation even when the amount of capped mRNA and total eIF4E are unchanged.

When mTORC1 activity is sufficient, phosphorylation of 4E-BPs reduces their inhibitory association with eIF4E in the conventional regulatory model. eIF4G can associate more readily, promoting assembly of the cap-associated machinery and recruitment of preinitiation complexes. Thus phosphorylation of the inhibitor removes a brake. When nutrient limitation or other appropriate conditions reduce mTORC1 activity, 4E-BPs can remain or become less phosphorylated and exert stronger repression. The result is a reduction in synthesis of many proteins, but the magnitude of the response depends on the transcript and the cellular context. The principal substrate in this regulatory example is 4E-BP; it is incorrect to say that mTORC1 stimulates translation simply by phosphorylating eIF2 alpha.

The other important branch involves S6 kinases. mTORC1 can phosphorylate and activate S6K proteins, which act on additional substrates involved in translational processes. Some of these downstream effects enhance the activity or availability of factors participating in initiation and RNA remodeling. The ribosomal protein S6 is a well-known substrate in this signaling network, but the presence of phosphorylated S6 is a marker of pathway activity rather than a direct universal measurement of protein production. Changes in phospho-S6 and actual synthesis can diverge because other limiting steps remain. A complete inference should distinguish a pathway readout, the molecular target responsible for a given effect and the measured protein synthesis flux.

Messages carrying a 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine motif, usually termed TOP mRNAs, are especially useful in this context. Many encode ribosomal proteins or other components of the protein synthesis machinery. The motif occurs at the extreme 5′ end rather than being defined simply by a random pyrimidine-rich segment anywhere in the coding sequence. Under conditions favoring growth, enhanced translation of such messages can support an increase in future translational capacity. When growth conditions are poor, restricting their translation can limit investment in additional machinery. Classical ribosome-profiling studies, including the 2012 study by Thoreen and colleagues, found strong sensitivity of TOP and related messages to mTOR inhibition and demonstrated an important contribution from 4E-BPs in the examined system. This finding should not be generalized to a claim that every mTOR-responsive message contains a TOP motif or that every cell uses exactly the same regulatory components.

The pathway is not adequately represented by a single label such as mTOR on equals all translation on. For instance, abundant cap-associated complexes cannot compensate indefinitely for a lack of charged tRNA or very low ternary-complex availability. An actively growing cell still needs amino acids and energy for elongation. Conversely, reducing mTORC1 signaling need not eliminate all protein production, because some messages have distinct recruitment requirements and the degree of inhibition can vary. A growth response is produced by a coordinated change in multiple processes, not by the assumption that one kinase is sufficient for every step of synthesis.

Genetic comparisons can connect changes in pathway activity to changes in initiation. If mTOR inhibition reduces production of a reporter strongly in cells expressing the relevant 4E-BPs but less strongly in cells lacking them, the difference supports mediation through those inhibitory proteins. It does not show that every mTOR-dependent effect has been removed. A nonphosphorylatable 4E-BP variant that retains eIF4E binding is predicted to continue competing with eIF4G even under conditions that activate the kinase. A variant unable to bind eIF4E would be expected to lack that particular direct repression mechanism. The appropriate controls include total factor amounts, reporter RNA abundance, basal synthesis and confirmation that the kinase perturbation reached its target.

Consider an illustrative equilibrium competition for eIF4E. Let G denote free eIF4G, B free 4E-BP and K_G and K_B the corresponding dissociation constants for mutually exclusive binding. Assume that eIF4E does not substantially deplete either free partner. The fraction of eIF4E associated with eIF4G in this simplified one-site model is (G/K_G)/(1 + G/K_G + B/K_B). This is obtained by summing the statistical weights of unoccupied, G-bound and B-bound states. If G/K_G = 4 and B/K_B = 5, the fraction in the G-bound state is 4/10 = 0.4. If phosphorylation lowers effective inhibitor binding so that B/K_B = 1, the fraction becomes 4/6, or approximately 0.667. This model illustrates competition; it ignores the cap, additional factors, multisite phosphorylation and changing protein concentrations. It does not justify converting those fractions directly into biological protein synthesis rates unless a problem supplies the necessary additional assumptions.
