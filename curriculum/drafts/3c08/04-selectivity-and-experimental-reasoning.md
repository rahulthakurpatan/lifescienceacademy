## 14. Specific RNA-binding control: ferritin and iron availability

The cell must balance iron acquisition with iron storage because iron is indispensable for many enzymes but excess reactive iron can contribute to cellular damage. Ferritin is a protein complex that stores iron in a relatively protected form. When iron availability is low, large investments in new ferritin are less useful than making available iron accessible to essential functions. When intracellular iron becomes abundant, increased storage capacity is advantageous. A post-transcriptional system can adjust the production of ferritin and proteins involved in iron uptake without requiring each individual change to be initiated at a promoter.

The ferritin mRNAs contain a structured sequence in the 5′ untranslated region known as an iron-responsive element, or IRE. Iron regulatory proteins, IRP1 and IRP2, can recognize such structures. Under low-iron conditions their RNA-binding activity promotes occupancy of the ferritin IRE, which impairs productive translation initiation. The relatively cap-proximal location of the element is important to the classical mechanism of interference with recruitment. When iron becomes sufficiently available, the RNA-binding activity or abundance of the appropriate regulatory proteins changes, permitting more efficient ferritin synthesis. IRP1 can switch between an RNA-binding form and a form with an iron-sulfur cluster associated with cytosolic aconitase activity, whereas the abundance of IRP2 is regulated differently. Neither protein should be described simply as iron itself binding the mRNA.

The response becomes especially instructive when compared with the transferrin receptor message. Transferrin receptor 1 participates in iron acquisition and its mRNA contains iron-responsive elements in its 3′ untranslated region. Under low-iron conditions, binding by IRPs contributes to stabilization of this message. Thus low iron can reduce ferritin translation while maintaining more of a message that supports iron uptake. The apparent opposition is not a contradiction in the molecular specificity of IRPs. The outcomes differ because the relevant RNA elements occupy different regulatory contexts. The same kind of binding event can interfere with initiation in a cap-proximal leader or influence transcript survival in a 3′ region. When iron becomes more available, decreased IRP-mediated binding can favor ferritin synthesis and allow increased turnover of the receptor transcript.

This comparison illustrates a crucial examination trap. It is sometimes suggested that an RNA-binding protein always inhibits translation, or that a regulatory motif has the same functional effect wherever it is inserted. Neither generalization is reliable. The location of an element relative to a molecular event is often essential. A ferritin reporter with a disrupted IRE that cannot bind IRP should show reduced sensitivity to the classical low-iron repression mechanism, assuming other properties remain intact. Transferring the IRE to a different region of a transcript need not recreate the same repression, because the geometry relative to ribosome recruitment is changed. A test of these predictions should compare reporter transcript abundance and newly synthesized reporter protein. Changes in cellular iron can also influence metabolism and other processes, so a reporter lacking the element is an important control.

In a hypothetical dataset, cells with low iron produce 20 units of ferritin reporter per minute from 100 units of reporter mRNA, while iron-replete cells produce 80 units per minute from the same RNA quantity. The effective synthesis-per-RNA index increases fourfold. In a separate transferrin receptor experiment the RNA level itself falls from 200 units to 50 after iron replenishment, while production per RNA unit remains constant. The first observation fits translational control and the second fits regulation of available RNA, although more evidence would be required to establish the involvement of specific IRPs. Comparing these two mechanisms teaches more than memorizing the names of the elements: it demonstrates that an identical environmental input can coordinate protein outputs through different expression layers.

## 15. Elongation, ribosomal loading and competition for resources

Initiation is a powerful control point because preventing entry of a ribosome can avoid a large downstream expenditure of amino acids and nucleotides. But a cell cannot regulate its protein output exclusively through entry. A ribosome that has initiated needs to receive the appropriate charged tRNA for each new codon, transfer the growing peptide and move to the next codon. Problems at any of these steps can reduce the probability or rate of successful completion. Translational regulation at the level of elongation and the availability of active ribosomes therefore complements initiation control. This distinction is especially important when a biochemical experiment measures ribosomes rather than finished products.

Eukaryotic elongation factor eEF2 promotes translocation of the ribosome along the mRNA. In mammalian systems, phosphorylation of eEF2 by eEF2 kinase can reduce its translocation-promoting activity. This offers another example in which phosphorylation inhibits a component of the translation apparatus rather than activating it. An increase in inhibitory eEF2 phosphorylation can slow elongation without first preventing recruitment of the 40S subunit at the cap. Nutrient and stress signaling can influence this regulatory branch, although the exact signaling inputs and their relative importance vary by context. A student should distinguish eEF2, an elongation factor, from eIF2, an initiation factor involved in delivery of initiator tRNA. Despite the similar names and use of GTP-dependent cycles, the factors act at different stages.

Consider the energy required for producing a new polypeptide. Aminoacylation of each tRNA ordinarily consumes ATP to AMP and pyrophosphate, conventionally counted as two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents when pyrophosphate hydrolysis is included. For each typical elongation addition after the first residue, one GTP is used for factor-mediated aminoacyl-tRNA delivery and another for translocation in the standard simplified accounting. Initiation, termination and recycling can require additional nucleotide use depending on the system and assumptions. For a chain of 100 residues, charging 100 tRNAs accounts for 200 equivalents, and the 99 typical elongation cycles account for 198 GTP equivalents, yielding 398 before those other costs. This convention does not imply that four ATP molecules are chemically hydrolyzed at each peptide bond, nor does it include the metabolic cost of synthesizing amino acids or maintaining cell physiology.

If a cell initiates fewer new chains, it avoids much of the future expenditure associated with those chains. If instead elongation arrests after many residues have already been incorporated, the resources spent up to that point may not yield a usable full-length product. There can also be an opportunity cost: a stalled ribosome cannot immediately initiate another productive translation. If quality control removes an incomplete polypeptide, additional resources may be required for recycling or degradation. These considerations help explain why coordinated suppression of initiation can be useful during resource shortage even when the initial biochemical problem is a reduced elongation substrate supply. The detailed molecular systems that rescue stalled complexes are outside the core scope, but the distinction between a paused, terminated and recycled ribosome is essential.

The cell has a limited pool of initiation factors, ribosomes and charged tRNAs. Distinct transcripts therefore compete, directly or indirectly, for shared machinery. Even if the affinity of one message for a recruiting factor remains unchanged, reducing the number or activity of competing messages can alter its relative access to the factor. A change in transcript-specific output need not require the physical binding of a unique regulatory protein to that message. On the other hand, this competition alone is not sufficient to explain every selective response. The specific leader architectures of ATF4, GCN4 or iron-regulated messages provide mechanisms that can be tested by mutating those features. A clear explanation distinguishes global competition effects from experimentally demonstrated cis-dependent regulation.

## 16. Measuring synthesis, occupancy and transcript abundance

To distinguish the mechanisms discussed in this chapter, measurements should be chosen according to the physical quantity of interest. An RNA blot or quantitative reverse-transcription PCR measures a property of the RNA pool. The assay can show that a particular transcript becomes more or less abundant, although its result depends on which sequence is measured, the integrity of the RNA and the normalization. A western blot measures accumulated protein recognized by the antibody. Both measurements are valuable, but their ratio need not be the instantaneous translation rate because protein maturation and loss occur at their own rates. A short pulse of labeled amino acid incorporated into nascent chains can provide information about new protein synthesis during the labeling window.

Radiolabeled amino acid incorporation is a classical approach to observing newly synthesized material. A comparison of incorporation over equal short time intervals can reveal differences in synthesis if precursor specific activity, uptake and available amino acid pools are appropriately controlled. A compound such as puromycin can also be used in suitable assays of nascent protein synthesis because its aminoacyl-like structure allows it to become associated with a growing chain through the ribosomal catalytic reaction, resulting in premature termination. An assay using an antibody against incorporated puromycin can provide a comparative snapshot of synthesis under defined conditions. The measurement is not simply a count of completed mature proteins, and the labeling reagent can itself perturb translation. The duration and interpretation of such assays must therefore be controlled rather than treating the signal as an exact universal rate.

Sucrose-gradient sedimentation separates ribosomal assemblies according to physical behavior. An RNA associated with several ribosomes sediments differently from free messenger or smaller complexes. When new initiation is suppressed while elongation and termination continue, pre-existing translating ribosomes can finish and leave the message. Over time the distribution may shift toward lighter fractions and away from heavily loaded polysomes. If elongation is slowed while initiation continues, ribosomes may remain associated for longer and the distribution may instead shift toward more heavily loaded states. These are predictions under the relevant assumptions, not universally diagnostic signatures. Global stress can change both steps simultaneously, and a heavily loaded fraction may include messages whose ribosomes are not progressing normally.

Ribosome profiling measures short RNA fragments protected from digestion by ribosomes and maps those fragments to transcript sequences. The approach introduced in a widely cited 2009 study by Ingolia and colleagues made it possible to examine translation-associated occupancy across many genes and positions. Reading-frame periodicity and the distribution of footprints relative to coding boundaries can provide evidence about where translation is occurring. A change in occupancy near a particular codon can indicate altered dwell time, but an increased local signal is not automatically evidence that more protein has been produced. A local pileup can result from slowed progress; conversely, fewer ribosomes per message can reflect more rapid transit if initiation is unchanged. The assay is particularly useful when interpreted alongside RNA abundance and an independent measurement of protein synthesis.

A reporter experiment can isolate a particular regulatory feature by placing a candidate leader before a common coding sequence. The comparison should keep the promoter, coding sequence and other relevant features constant where feasible. A control with a mutated regulatory element tests necessity; transferring a candidate element to a suitable otherwise unresponsive reporter can test whether that element confers regulation in the new context. Necessity and sufficiency are different claims. A sequence may be necessary for a response in its natural messenger but insufficient to reproduce it in a different leader. Likewise a transferred element can confer a response in one artificial arrangement without establishing that the native regulatory context is identical.

An important qualification applies to assays of translation initiation based on reporter luminescence or fluorescence. The readout may depend on the folding, maturation and stability of the reporter protein and on metabolic substrates required for its detection. A stress treatment could reduce luminescence through reduced ATP availability even when the amount of the enzyme is unchanged, depending on the assay. Using an appropriate control reporter, measuring reporter protein independently and assessing RNA abundance helps prevent a purely technical change from being mistaken for translational repression. Time courses also distinguish rapid translational effects from delayed changes in expression or cell number.

The most persuasive design uses orthogonal evidence. For instance, a stress-dependent reduction in cap-associated eIF4G interaction, decreased nascent protein synthesis, unchanged abundance of a selected reporter RNA and weaker inhibition in 4E-BP-deficient cells jointly support a cap-recruitment mechanism. No individual readout establishes the entire chain. If a factor knockout rescues reporter output but also changes the RNA pool substantially, it becomes necessary to determine how much of the rescue occurs per transcript. This approach to evidence is the foundation for Part C questions: identify what was actually measured, state what the result implies, and distinguish the favored mechanism from alternatives that remain possible.

## 17. Integrating experimental results and comparing regulatory systems

Suppose a cultured mammalian cell population is examined before and shortly after nutrient withdrawal. Measurements show that total eIF2 alpha is unchanged, phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha rises, phosphorylation of 4E-BP falls, bulk nascent protein synthesis decreases and ATF4 synthesis increases. This hypothetical set of observations is compatible with activation of an integrated stress response together with reduced mTORC1-associated signaling. Both pathways can decrease general initiation, but the increase in ATF4 can be understood by regulated reinitiation. The data do not establish that the two pathways act independently, nor do they quantify their individual contributions. To separate them one might compare a phosphorylation-resistant eIF2 alpha background, cells lacking relevant 4E-BPs and appropriately controlled combinations of perturbations.

Imagine that bulk synthesis is normalized to 100 in untreated cells. In the stressed wild type it is 30. In stressed cells with a phosphorylation-resistant eIF2 alpha variant it is 55, and in stressed cells lacking 4E-BPs it is 60. In a double-perturbed background it is 85. These illustrative measurements support contributions from both regulatory branches, assuming the unstressed baselines and experimental conditions are comparable. They do not establish a unique additive biochemical law. The cell can respond differently to chronic mutations, and nonlinear changes in common limiting resources may affect the measured outcome. Residual inhibition of 15 units in the double perturbation could reflect other stress effects or incomplete functional rescue; the data alone cannot specify which. A careful conclusion states the evidence for contributions and the limitations of the intervention.

A second hypothetical case concerns two bacterial reporters expressed from the same promoter. Their protein coding regions are identical, but one possesses a leader containing an inhibitory stem. At a lower temperature both reporters have similar mRNA amounts, while production of the stem-containing reporter is markedly lower. At higher temperature its output rises relative to the control. The result is consistent with an RNA thermometer, but the control is essential because temperature can alter general translation kinetics. If disrupting the stem leads to high production at both temperatures and restoring pairing restores the temperature response, the structural hypothesis gains support. If the mutated sequences also strongly alter RNA abundance, however, the contribution of altered translation per transcript needs to be reassessed. Structure probing of the leader can provide independent evidence for the change in accessibility.

The comparative logic of these examples is that environmental information changes the ability of a translation apparatus to use particular RNAs or shared factors. Bacterial initiation frequently provides a direct opportunity to control access to a localized ribosome-binding region. Eukaryotic cap-associated recruitment creates a different opportunity to restrict productive engagement with the message. The eIF2 system regulates delivery and recycling of the initiator-tRNA-bearing factor, while specialized leaders can exploit the timing of reinitiation. All of these mechanisms change a molecular probability or resource that influences output. The specific bacterial factors and eukaryotic factors are not interchangeable, and the absence of a nuclear envelope does not imply a universal bacterial model in every prokaryote.

Translation regulation also connects to development and physiology. Cells can retain messages whose translation is delayed until a suitable stage or cellular location, allowing production to be coordinated with biological requirements. In growing cells, regulation of ribosome production helps match the future capacity for protein synthesis to metabolic supply. In cells handling secretory proteins, regulation of new synthesis can help maintain balance with organelle folding capacity. In bacteria experiencing a changing environment, RNA structures and regulatory molecules can rapidly adjust protein output. These examples share a useful causal theme, but the detailed developmental programs, cellular signaling networks and protein-targeting pathways have their own chapters. The aim here is to understand the control points in the synthesis machinery, not to duplicate every biological pathway in which those control points are used.

In practice, a student encountering a novel mechanism can ask four connected questions. First, which molecular quantity has changed: transcript availability, recruitment, initiator-tRNA supply, elongation, active ribosome number or protein lifetime? Second, which component or RNA feature is directly responsible for the change? Third, which experiment would separate that explanation from alternatives? Fourth, does the proposed mechanism explain both the behavior of the individual transcript and the general response of the cell? The four questions provide a framework for reasoning about unfamiliar figures, genetic interactions and quantitative data. The strength of the chapter's conceptual models lies not in the ability to memorize every named example, but in their ability to generate testable predictions under clearly defined assumptions.
