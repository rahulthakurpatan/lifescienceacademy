## 6. The first line of protection near a bacterial ribosome

A useful starting point for understanding chaperone organization is the bacterium Escherichia coli. Unlike a eukaryotic cell, it does not have an endoplasmic reticulum, but it still has to handle newly emerging proteins in a crowded aqueous cytoplasm. One important bacterial folding factor, trigger factor, associates with the large ribosomal subunit near the exit tunnel. This placement means that it can encounter a chain before a freely diffusing chaperone necessarily encounters it. It is neither a ribosomal RNA component nor a translation elongation factor, even though its position places it close to the machinery of translation.

Trigger factor makes transient contacts with emerging chains and provides a protected region in which certain segments can avoid inappropriate intermolecular interactions. A binding surface need not recognize a single strict amino acid motif to perform this role. Broad recognition allows the same molecule to interact with chemically diverse newly translated proteins. The nature and duration of contact depend on the available sequence and the chain's structural state. A folded region can become less attractive as a substrate when it buries surfaces that would otherwise interact with the factor.

Trigger factor contains a peptidyl-prolyl isomerase domain, but its contribution to nascent-chain protection should not be reduced to that catalytic activity alone. A molecule can provide substantial chaperone assistance by interacting with a substrate even if a particular catalytic activity is dispensable for that protective effect. This is a useful general example of a protein having multiple mechanistically distinguishable activities. Mutating the site that catalyzes isomerization need not give the same phenotype as deleting the entire factor or disrupting its association with the ribosome.

Unlike the Hsp70 cycle discussed below, trigger factor does not require ATP hydrolysis to perform its characteristic chaperone role. Association and dissociation follow the reversible molecular interactions of factor, ribosome and substrate. An ATP-independent system can nevertheless improve folding yield because simply avoiding an inappropriate association changes the probability of reaching a productive state. Energy consumption is not the definition of a chaperone. The defining functional concept is assistance with the acquisition or maintenance of an appropriate protein conformation or assembly state.

Trigger factor and the bacterial Hsp70 system can have overlapping responsibilities. If one system is absent, another may take on a greater fraction of the vulnerable nascent chains. A moderate defect in one mutant therefore does not prove that the missing factor has no function. Testing combinations of defects, or changing the conditions under which a protein must fold, may reveal dependence that is masked in an otherwise well-equipped cell. The same principle is familiar from genetic redundancy: an observable phenotype depends on what compensatory functions remain available.

Eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are also associated with factors that influence nascent-chain handling, including Hsp70-related machinery. The nascent polypeptide-associated complex, abbreviated NAC, interacts with ribosomes and has roles in early protein biogenesis and targeting decisions. It should not be described as the eukaryotic version of bacterial trigger factor in the sense of being the same protein under a different name. The general biological problem is conserved, while the individual components and their organization may differ. This distinction becomes important in comparative questions that ask about functions rather than exact identities.

## 7. Hsp70: the molecular logic of reversible substrate binding

The Hsp70 family illustrates how nucleotide binding and hydrolysis can control an interaction between a chaperone and a nonnative protein. Members of this family occur in different organisms and compartments. In E. coli the major bacterial example is DnaK. Eukaryotic cytosolic Hsp70s perform related functions, and BiP is an Hsp70 family member in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum. Mitochondria contain their own Hsp70-related machinery. A family name describes conserved molecular principles, not a single protein with identical localization in every cell.

An Hsp70 molecule has a nucleotide-binding domain and a substrate-binding domain. The first domain interacts with ATP or ADP and participates in hydrolysis. The second contains a region that can accommodate a short peptide segment from a substrate. A movable structural element associated with the substrate-binding region helps control access and retention. The two domains communicate: changing the bound nucleotide changes the conformational behavior of the substrate-binding region. Such coupling between distant sites is an instance of allostery.

The substrate commonly presents an accessible peptide segment enriched in hydrophobic residues. In a properly folded soluble protein, many such segments are inaccessible because they form part of the internal core. A newly emerging or misfolded chain presents more of these potential binding sites. However, exposed hydrophobicity is not a perfect definition of a damaged protein. Some functional membrane proteins possess hydrophobic segments as part of their normal destination signals or transmembrane regions. Chaperone recognition must be understood in the context of substrate location, binding partners and timing.

When Hsp70 carries ATP, its substrate-binding region generally has rapid exchange behavior: peptide segments can associate and dissociate relatively quickly. The ATP-bound state is often described as open and relatively low affinity. Rapid association is helpful because the protein can sample available segments. Rapid dissociation would be a disadvantage if the aim were to retain a particularly vulnerable sequence for longer. Conversion to the ADP-bound state solves this second requirement.

Hydrolysis of ATP converts the nucleotide-bound chaperone toward a state with slower substrate dissociation and more stable peptide binding. Describing this state as closed and high affinity is a useful simplified model. Retention can protect the captured segment against inappropriate contacts while other parts of the substrate remain free to move. ATP hydrolysis does not make a new peptide bond in the client, and the nucleotide is not incorporated into its structure. The role of the chemical reaction is to bias the behavior of the chaperone's conformational cycle.

Hsp70 is not normally studied in isolation when explaining its cellular cycle. J-domain proteins, classically represented by the bacterial Hsp40 DnaJ, stimulate ATP hydrolysis by Hsp70. Many J proteins also help recognize or deliver substrates. Substrate binding and J-domain stimulation are coordinated so that a relatively transient encounter can be converted into a more persistent interaction where it is useful. The role of Hsp40 is therefore different from that of the ribosome, which synthesizes the chain, and different from that of Hsp70, which is the nucleotide-controlled peptide-binding factor.

If Hsp70 remained in the ADP-bound state indefinitely, substrate retention would create a different problem. A captured segment might need to become buried in the client's own hydrophobic core before the final conformation can be achieved. In that case the chaperone must release it. Nucleotide exchange factors promote dissociation of ADP from the nucleotide-binding domain; in a cell with sufficient ATP, ATP can then bind and restore the more rapidly exchanging state. In the standard bacterial DnaK system the exchange factor is GrpE. Eukaryotic Hsp70s use several different families of exchange factors, so GrpE should not be assigned indiscriminately to every compartment.

Following release, the substrate has an opportunity to establish intramolecular contacts or associate with the correct partner. A client that has reached a suitable conformation may no longer expose the binding segment and can leave the cycle. A client that remains nonnative may be captured again. Multiple rounds are therefore possible. There is no fixed requirement that each substrate consume exactly one ATP before becoming active. The number of productive and unproductive cycles depends on the client's behavior, the available cochaperones and environmental conditions.

The sequence of nucleotide-dependent events also provides a framework for predicting mutant phenotypes. A DnaK variant that retains ATP binding and substrate recognition but hydrolyzes ATP very slowly is expected to have difficulty stabilizing a captured segment in the normal long-lived state. This can increase the probability of aggregation for susceptible clients. By contrast, a variant that hydrolyzes ATP normally but fails to release ADP can retain substrate excessively. The latter mutant may produce a pool of soluble chaperone-bound clients without a corresponding increase in active protein. Both defects can reduce productive folding, although the underlying nucleotide state is opposite.

A J-protein defect may affect selected substrates more strongly than others because J proteins often contribute to substrate recruitment and localization. If the only measured phenotype is a reduction in total cellular activity, one cannot conclude that ATP hydrolysis has failed globally. Measuring the ATPase behavior of isolated Hsp70 in the presence and absence of its J partner, together with substrate binding and release, could distinguish these explanations. Similarly, a defect in a nucleotide exchange factor can often be reasoned about as a problem of reopening the binding cycle rather than a loss of initial recognition.

The importance of the cycle lies in its ability to couple timing to molecular interactions. A purely reversible peptide-binding factor can protect a client, but an ATP-driven cycle can regulate the residence time through stages whose rates are influenced by other proteins and the nucleotide environment. The system operates away from equilibrium when cellular metabolism continually supplies ATP. This does not mean that ATP hydrolysis directly determines the final geometry of the client. It enables a regulated sequence of capture, retention and release, through which the client can sample productive folding routes.

One can make a simple energetic distinction without introducing unnecessary biophysical mathematics. Suppose an isolated Hsp70 system performs 240 ATP hydrolysis events while 60 newly synthesized client molecules acquire measurable activity. The ratio is four ATP hydrolysis events per active molecule recovered. This is a system-level ratio for the observed interval, not a claim that each active molecule experienced exactly four cycles. Some cycles might have been spent on clients that never became active, and some clients might have folded after only one interaction. Interpreting ATP consumption requires attention to what is counted in the numerator and denominator.

When ATP becomes scarce, several processes change at once. Translation, transport, chaperone cycling and other ATP-dependent functions can all be affected. In an intact cell, ATP depletion followed by loss of protein activity cannot on its own establish a specific Hsp70 defect. A reconstituted system containing a defined client, Hsp70, its cochaperones and controlled nucleotides can isolate the dependence more directly. Even then, the experiment should distinguish the effect of ATP on the chaperone from any direct requirement of the client enzyme for ATP during its activity assay.

## 8. Chaperonins: folding with spatial separation

Hsp70 protects accessible segments of individual substrates. Some folding intermediates need a different kind of assistance: a larger fraction of the chain benefits from being physically separated from other chains while conformational changes occur. Chaperonins are large oligomeric chaperones that provide a central chamber. In bacteria the best studied system is GroEL together with GroES. Its organization makes clear how a protein complex can change the local environment of a client rather than merely shielding one short segment.

GroEL consists of two stacked rings, each built from seven subunits. The two rings create chambers at opposite ends of the complex. Within each subunit, regions involved in nucleotide binding, substrate binding and conformational transmission cooperate. The exposed surfaces near the opening of an available ring can bind nonnative protein. The substrate is not normally a permanent structural component of GroEL. Its association represents one stage in a cycle that subsequently allows release.

GroES is a separate oligomeric cochaperonin that forms a lid over a GroEL ring. In the well-established simplified cycle, nonnative substrate binds an accessible GroEL ring. Binding of ATP and GroES drives major conformational changes that reduce the affinity of the original exposed substrate-binding surfaces and enlarge the enclosed chamber. The substrate is released from the binding sites into the chamber. Its opportunity to fold is now changed because the chamber physically separates the client from other substrate molecules in the surrounding solution.

The enclosed environment is compatible with many soluble proteins, but the protective effect does not arise because GroES supplies a mold whose shape is complementary to every client. Many structurally unrelated proteins can be assisted. Within the chamber, the substrate still has to form contacts dictated by its sequence. The capacity of a chamber is finite, and not every cellular protein can be completely enclosed. Some substrates interact with GroEL in ways that do not fit the simplest full-encapsulation model. For introductory and NET reasoning, the capped folding chamber is an important standard model, not a universal description of every interaction.

ATP hydrolysis changes the nucleotide state of the capped ring and contributes to timing the cycle. ATP binding at the opposite ring can trigger release of the GroES lid, nucleotide and substrate from the first ring. The detailed coordination between the two rings can depend on experimental conditions; an exam diagram often represents them alternating between substrate-processing stages. One should identify the ring that is enclosed with the client as the cis ring and the opposite ring as trans relative to that client. These labels are relational, not permanently attached to one anatomical half of the complex.

After lid opening, a substrate that has folded can leave the chaperonin and proceed toward its biological role. One that remains nonnative may interact again with GroEL or another chaperone. Encapsulation therefore gives the substrate a folding opportunity rather than guaranteeing that a native structure emerges in one attempt. The distinction matters when interpreting experiments in which several ATP cycles are required for activity recovery. Repeated binding and release need not indicate that the protein is defective; it may reflect the kinetics of the normal maturation route.

GroEL and GroES illustrate a second use of ATP in chaperone function. Nucleotide-dependent structural changes organize the availability, closure and reopening of the chamber. The free energy supplied by the nucleotide cycle enables directional progression of the chaperone machinery through these stages. It does not substitute for the energetic interactions stabilizing the finished client. A newly folded client does not contain a new covalent bond merely because it passed through the chaperonin chamber.

Consider three hypothetical mutations. One affects the initial substrate-binding surface, one prevents effective GroES binding and one prevents the chamber from reopening. The first reduces recruitment of nonnative client. The second can permit recruitment without the normal enclosed environment. The third might permit client encapsulation but interfere with substrate release and reuse of the machinery. All three can lower the amount of free active protein, but their effects on the quantities of bound and encapsulated substrate should differ. Mapping an observed phenotype onto a step requires choosing measurements capable of distinguishing those intermediate states.

A useful biological comparison is mitochondrial Hsp60 with its cochaperonin Hsp10. These proteins are related to the bacterial group I chaperonin system and assist folding in the mitochondrial matrix. This compartment is especially relevant because many proteins arrive through membrane translocation in conformations not yet ready for biological activity. A protein can cross the membrane successfully yet fail to acquire the active conformation after import. Correct localization is therefore not sufficient proof of successful maturation.

A particularly informative genetic investigation reported in 1989 showed that mitochondrial Hsp60 was essential for proper assembly of proteins imported into yeast mitochondria. Subsequent work demonstrated the connection between complex formation with Hsp60, ATP hydrolysis and folding of imported substrates. These observations shifted attention from the question of whether proteins entered the organelle to the distinct question of what happened after entry. A mitochondrial fraction containing an imported protein is not evidence that the protein has become an active component of a respiratory or metabolic complex. The experiment needs an independent functional or assembly readout.

Chloroplasts contain related chaperonin machinery and a historically significant example is assistance in the biogenesis of the large subunit of Rubisco. Rubisco catalyzes carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, but synthesis of one of its polypeptides does not automatically create an active multisubunit enzyme. Folding of the large subunit and association with other components are distinct stages. The example connects protein biogenesis to photosynthesis without requiring that a learner memorize every plant-specific assembly factor at this stage.

Eukaryotic cytosolic CCT, also called TRiC, represents a group II chaperonin. Like group I systems it forms a double-ring complex, but the subunits of each ring are not simply seven copies of the bacterial GroEL protein. In the standard eukaryotic CCT complex each ring contains eight related but distinct subunits. The chamber can close through structural elements of the chaperonin itself rather than through a separately binding GroES lid. It is therefore incorrect to assign GroES as the universal cochaperonin for all chaperonins. Important cytosolic substrates include actin and tubulin, linking the success of folding to the integrity of cytoskeletal systems.

A comparison of Hsp70 and chaperonins should focus on how they assist rather than on which family is more important. Hsp70 is a nucleotide-regulated peptide-binding machine that can act early on emerging segments. GroEL/GroES and CCT/TRiC are larger systems that provide a chamber for appropriate clients. They can work in sequence for some substrates, but not every protein passes through both. A protein may fold independently, receive assistance from one system or require several systems with distinct functions. Cellular dependence is selective, not a compulsory identical itinerary for every chain.

## 9. Chaperones under stress and across the protein life cycle

The names Hsp70 and Hsp60 reflect historical associations with the heat shock response and approximate molecular masses, not a restriction to function at high temperatures. Many chaperones are present during normal growth because new protein synthesis is a continual source of vulnerable chains. Additional chaperone production or redistribution can become important when environmental conditions destabilize previously folded proteins. Consequently, the same basic molecular functions can assist newly made proteins and proteins that have become partly unfolded after maturation.

In an acute heat stress, the concentration of accessible nonnative binding surfaces can rise rapidly. Existing chaperones become occupied by more substrates. The proportion of free chaperone available to newly synthesized chains may therefore fall even if the total chaperone amount has not changed. Increasing chaperone abundance or temporarily reducing the influx of newly synthesized proteins can ease this imbalance. This provides an intuitive connection between proteostasis and regulation of translation; the latter is already covered in the preceding node.

Small heat shock proteins are examples of ATP-independent chaperones that can capture aggregation-prone clients under stress. They often function as holdases rather than independently guaranteeing that a captured client acquires a native structure. A held substrate may subsequently require another chaperone system for productive refolding. Such division of labor shows why the absence of ATP use by one protein does not imply that the overall pathway is ATP independent. The stages must be distinguished when drawing conclusions from an energetic experiment.

Hsp90 provides another instructive example of specialization. Many of its cytosolic clients are signaling proteins, including certain kinases and steroid hormone receptors. These clients can require assistance at later stages of conformational maturation or functional maintenance. The family participates in nucleotide-regulated interactions with clients and cochaperones. It should not be treated as a substitute name for Hsp70 or as a universal folding chamber. Loss of Hsp90 function can impair the activity or stability of selected signaling proteins, potentially affecting many downstream cellular responses without necessarily blocking ribosomal peptide synthesis.

In bacteria and certain other organisms, disaggregase systems can help recover proteins from aggregates. A distinction between preventing aggregation and reversing an established aggregate is useful. Protecting a newly exposed segment requires preventing an unfavorable interaction before it is formed. Recovering a protein from a preexisting assembly requires overcoming contacts that already hold multiple chains together. The latter process may need a different combination of chaperones and energy-dependent actions. Not all aggregates are recoverable, and recovery need not be the preferred fate for every damaged molecule.

The evolutionary conservation of molecular chaperones reflects the generality of the problem of nonnative protein interactions. It does not establish that every species possesses the same collection of cochaperones or the same division of functions. Differences in temperature, compartment organization, metabolic resources and protein repertoires affect how cells organize this machinery. Examination comparisons are most reliable when they identify a conserved molecular principle first and then distinguish the organism-specific examples.

The next aspect of maturation introduces a different kind of molecular change. Chaperones can assist a chain in rearranging noncovalent interactions; some clients also require covalent disulfide formation or catalyzed rearrangement of peptide-bond conformations. These reactions require attention to chemistry and compartment conditions, not just to the exposed surfaces recognized by chaperones.
