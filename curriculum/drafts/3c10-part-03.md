## 10. Disulfide formation: how chemical maturation reinforces structure

Among the chemical reactions associated with folding, formation of a disulfide bond is especially instructive because the reaction can create a covalent connection between two distant parts of a protein. A cysteine side chain contains a thiol group, represented as R–SH. When two appropriately positioned thiols are oxidized, they can form a disulfide represented as R–S–S–R. In a simplified half reaction, two thiols yield one disulfide, two protons and two electrons. An electron acceptor is required for the overall oxidation; the reverse reduction requires reducing equivalents. The carbon–nitrogen peptide backbone is not broken during this change.

An intrachain disulfide connects two cysteines in the same polypeptide. It may stabilize a loop or link regions that are distant in primary sequence but close in the folded structure. An interchain disulfide connects cysteines belonging to distinct polypeptides. Both types can occur in the same mature protein. When interpreting a structural diagram, identify the chain boundaries before classifying a disulfide. It is not enough to observe that two cysteines are separated by a large number of residues; if they belong to one backbone, the bond is intrachain.

Disulfides can stabilize a native conformation in part by restricting the conformational freedom of the unfolded chain. If a linkage between two regions remains intact, the unfolded state cannot sample all the arrangements that an unlinked chain could sample. This restriction can favor the folded state relative to the unfolded state under particular conditions. But stability depends on whether the linkage is geometrically compatible with the native structure. An inappropriate disulfide may instead trap a nonnative conformation. The chemical strength of a linkage does not establish the biological usefulness of its position.

Disulfide formation can occur as a chain folds, and formation of some contacts can bring particular thiols into proximity. In other cases an initial disulfide constrains subsequent structural changes. Therefore, there is no general rule that a protein must finish folding before any disulfide forms, or that all disulfides must form before any folding occurs. The chemical and conformational events can influence one another. The Anfinsen experiment demonstrates this coupling: the native arrangement of cysteines is recovered preferentially when the chain has an opportunity to adopt appropriate contacts and when nonnative bonds can rearrange.

The compartment in which a protein resides strongly affects the stability and rate of formation of its disulfides. In many eukaryotic cells the cytosol is maintained in a relatively reducing state. Cellular redox systems help sustain thiols in their reduced forms, protecting many cytosolic enzymes that require free cysteine groups. The endoplasmic reticulum lumen, in contrast, supports oxidative folding of many secreted and membrane proteins. Specific oxidoreductases and other redox partners help transfer electrons during disulfide formation. It is this chemically organized environment, rather than the mere presence of a membrane, that makes oxidative folding efficient for suitable substrates.

The reducing cytosol and oxidizing ER distinction is a useful examination generalization, but it must not become an absolute prohibition. Some cytosolic proteins can contain redox-responsive or otherwise stabilized disulfides, and stress can change cellular redox conditions. In addition, the ER must maintain a balance: indiscriminate oxidation can make it difficult to correct inappropriate linkages. Efficient oxidative folding involves both the ability to form bonds and the ability to reduce or rearrange bonds that are incorrectly positioned.

In Gram-negative bacteria the periplasm provides another important example of a compartment that supports disulfide formation. The Dsb system includes proteins that promote oxidation and others that assist rearrangement of inappropriate linkages. A bacterial secretory protein with disulfides may therefore fold successfully after export even though the bacterial cytoplasm is normally reducing. Eukaryotic ER proteins and bacterial periplasmic proteins solve related chemical problems through compartment-specific machinery. The comparison should not be confused with an assertion that bacteria have an ER.

A mutant replacing a required cysteine with serine illustrates how the chemical identity of a residue affects maturation. Both amino acids have side chains containing a heteroatom, but serine has a hydroxyl group rather than a thiol and does not participate in the same ordinary cysteine disulfide chemistry. If the missing bond stabilizes a secreted protein, the mutant may become less stable, fail quality control or be secreted inefficiently. It does not follow that every cysteine-to-serine substitution causes misfolding: some cysteines are not paired, some are involved in catalysis or metal binding, and others may be dispensable under the tested conditions.

An additional difficulty is that a mutation removing one cysteine from a native disulfide can leave its original partner unpaired. The remaining thiol might form a nonnative bond with another cysteine within the same molecule or with a different molecule. Therefore, the effect need not be limited to loss of stabilization from one missing bond. The mutation can alter the network of alternative interactions available during maturation. An experiment that detects new covalent dimers may reveal this indirect consequence, especially if the dimers disappear when reduced.

Disulfide chemistry also explains a standard use of electrophoresis. In denaturing sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, many noncovalent interactions are disrupted. If reducing reagent is absent, disulfide-linked chains can remain covalently connected. Addition of reducing reagent can separate those chains by cleaving disulfides. A band of apparent mass 60 kDa under nonreducing conditions that becomes two bands of approximately 40 and 20 kDa under reducing conditions is consistent with a disulfide-linked heterodimer. This is an interpretation under stated assumptions, not proof of the native three-dimensional structure. Other covalent connections, anomalous migration and incomplete reduction should be considered if observations are inconsistent.

An intramolecular disulfide does not join two independent peptide backbones. Accordingly, reducing an isolated monomer containing only intrachain disulfides need not create several smaller chain bands on a denaturing gel. Its migration may still change because intrachain constraints affect how it behaves in the experimental system. Distinguishing covalent chain connectivity from compactness is essential when interpreting gel data.

## 11. Protein disulfide isomerase and the correction of nonnative linkages

Protein disulfide isomerase, often abbreviated PDI, is a major folding catalyst in the ER lumen. Its role becomes clear when a protein has several cysteine residues that can form multiple possible combinations. Forming a disulfide at the first available contact is not necessarily productive. A cell needs reactions that allow exchange between thiols and existing disulfides so that the network of bonds can be corrected as the chain reorganizes.

PDI family proteins contain catalytic domains with cysteine-containing active-site motifs. The catalytic cysteines participate in reversible formation and reduction of disulfides. In a simplified oxidation mechanism, an oxidized active site carries a disulfide between its own cysteines. A substrate thiol attacks that disulfide, forming a temporary mixed disulfide between the catalyst and substrate. A second substrate thiol can resolve the intermediate to produce a new substrate disulfide and a reduced catalyst. The catalyst is regenerated through interaction with other redox partners in the ER.

This mechanism illustrates why enzyme and substrate can be covalently connected during catalysis without forming a permanent part of one another's mature structures. A transient mixed disulfide is an intermediate. Release of the substrate is necessary for the catalyst to act again. If a mutation prevents resolution of the mixed disulfide, the amount of catalyst-associated substrate can increase even while the rate of production of mature client falls. In enzyme kinetic reasoning, accumulation of an intermediate often indicates slowed conversion to a later state, not increased completion of the reaction.

The reduced form of PDI can also initiate exchange with an existing substrate disulfide. Attack by a catalytic thiol creates an intermediate that permits the original pairing to be broken. The subsequent reaction can yield an alternative substrate pairing or a more reduced substrate that has another opportunity to fold. The precise route depends on the redox state of the catalyst, the cysteines available in the substrate and the surrounding environment. It is useful to think of PDI as a mediator of reversible exchange rather than a machine that simply adds one fixed bond at a genetically predetermined position.

The cell maintains the activity of oxidative folding machinery through redox partners. Ero1 is one important example of an oxidizing system that can help restore oxidized PDI. Other pathways also contribute to ER redox control. The detailed organization of these pathways varies with organism and cell type, so an introductory diagram should not present one redox partner as the exclusive possible source of oxidative capacity. A balanced redox environment must support the ongoing production of disulfides while retaining enough capacity for correcting incorrect ones.

Some PDI family members also bind nonnative proteins and have chaperone properties in addition to catalyzing thiol-disulfide reactions. A mutant of the catalytic cysteines can therefore retain some substrate-binding activity while losing much of its exchange capacity. Such a distinction is valuable for designing an experiment. If adding a PDI variant improves the solubility of a substrate but fails to restore its native disulfides or activity, the data support a separation between binding assistance and catalysis. If adding the wild-type protein improves both outcomes, the catalytic cycle is likely to contribute to the additional improvement.

A useful controlled refolding experiment can use a purified disulfide-rich client with scrambled linkages. One sample receives active PDI under suitable redox conditions, another receives an inactive enzyme variant and a third receives no added catalyst. The initial amount of substrate, temperature, buffer and duration are matched. At several times, investigators measure native activity and the state of the disulfides using appropriate chemical or analytical techniques. Faster recovery in the active-PDI sample would support its catalytic role, particularly if the inactive variant retains binding but does not accelerate correction. Merely detecting more oxidized cysteines would not establish that the correct pairings have been achieved.

This example also demonstrates the difference between a folding catalyst and an enzyme that modifies the final protein as a regulatory switch. PDI-mediated disulfide formation changes a covalent connection that can be essential to maturation. A phosphorylation event on an already active signaling protein may instead switch activity without requiring broad refolding. Some modifications contribute to both processes, which is why the categories overlap. The following node on post-translational modification will discuss the general chemistry and regulation of such changes; here the emphasis is on obtaining an appropriate conformational state.

## 12. Peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerization as a folding bottleneck

A polypeptide's backbone is not equally flexible at every bond. The peptide bond has partial double-bond character, which restricts rotation around its carbon–nitrogen connection. Peptide bonds can be described as cis or trans according to the relative positions of the alpha-carbon groups on either side. The trans arrangement predominates for most peptide bonds because it generally reduces steric conflict. Bonds preceding proline can populate the cis configuration more often than many other peptide bonds.

Proline has a side chain connected back to the backbone nitrogen, giving its backbone geometry distinctive constraints. A particular folded protein may require a specific X–Pro peptide bond to be cis or trans, where X represents the residue preceding proline. If a denatured chain contains that bond in the incompatible configuration, much of the rest of the protein may be ready to fold while conversion of the bond remains slow. The problem is not the identity of the amino acid; the sequence is already correct. The obstacle is a conformational barrier associated with isomerization.

Peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerases accelerate interconversion of these configurations. Cyclophilins, FKBPs and parvulin family proteins are representative examples. These enzymes are different from peptidases that cleave peptide bonds. Isomerization changes the spatial arrangement around an existing bond without changing the order of residues or dividing the chain. A folding experiment can therefore show a substantial change in the time required for activity recovery without detecting a difference in polypeptide molecular mass.

Catalysis reduces the kinetic barrier between states and can accelerate approach to equilibrium. A catalyst does not, by itself, change the equilibrium free-energy difference between the states of a substrate in a closed system. If a protein's native conformation favors one configuration, accelerating the approach to that conformation can improve the rate of recovery of function. In an intact cell, where aggregation, degradation and continual synthesis create competing pathways, a faster folding step can additionally increase the fraction of chains that survive to maturity. That increase is not evidence that the isomerase changed the isolated substrate's thermodynamic equilibrium.

Consider a client that can refold rapidly when its critical proline bond is already in the appropriate configuration and slowly when the bond is not. Immediately after dilution from denaturant, a mixed population can display a rapid phase of activity recovery followed by a slower phase. Adding a relevant isomerase can speed the slow phase. A mutation replacing the important proline may remove the original bottleneck, but it can also disrupt the native structure itself. It is not safe to predict that replacing every proline will improve folding. The structural contribution of the residue must be distinguished from the kinetics of isomerization.

Unlike the Hsp70 nucleotide cycle, simple catalysis of peptide-bond isomerization does not require stoichiometric ATP hydrolysis for each cis–trans conversion. This is another example of folding assistance that is not defined by nucleotide consumption. If ATP is required in a larger reconstituted system containing both an isomerase and an ATP-dependent chaperone, the two contributions should be examined separately.

## 13. Quality-controlled folding in the endoplasmic reticulum

A secretory cell faces a particularly demanding form of protein maturation. It can synthesize large quantities of proteins that must leave the cytoplasm and acquire structures compatible with extracellular function. The ER lumen is an early environment for many of these proteins. In addition to supporting disulfide formation, it contains molecular chaperones and enzymes that help coordinate folding with other processing events. This organization makes the ER more than a passageway through which already completed proteins move.

BiP, an ER-localized Hsp70 family protein, can bind exposed segments of newly translocated or incompletely folded clients. Its ATP-dependent cycle follows the broad Hsp70 principle established earlier, although its cochaperones and environment are specialized for the ER. Incomplete assembly can leave surfaces available for BiP binding even when individual domains have acquired substantial structure. Therefore, persistent association of a protein with BiP can be consistent with an assembly problem as well as a folding problem.

Many ER clients receive an N-linked oligosaccharide during synthesis or early processing. A subset of the resulting glycan structures acts as a recognition signal for the lectin chaperones calnexin and calreticulin. Calnexin is membrane associated, whereas calreticulin is a soluble luminal protein. Their carbohydrate recognition complements the peptide recognition of chaperones such as BiP. This distinction explains how a covalent modification can become part of a quality-control system rather than merely a decoration added to a protein.

In the standard cycle, glucose residues are trimmed from a newly transferred oligosaccharide. A monoglucosylated glycan permits interaction with calnexin or calreticulin. Associated folding factors, including the oxidoreductase ERp57 in relevant cases, can assist conformational maturation. Removal of the remaining glucose releases the client from the lectin-binding state. If the protein is still nonnative, the enzyme UGGT can recognize features of its nonnative conformation and restore a glucose residue to an appropriate glycan. This allows renewed entry into the lectin cycle.

The system connects a covalent recognition mark to an assessment of conformational state. Glycan trimming is not itself a guarantee that the protein has folded. Similarly, reglucosylation does not repair the backbone sequence. It supplies another opportunity for folding assistance by making a client eligible for lectin binding again. Proteins that lack a suitable N-linked glycan cannot use this particular cycle in the same way, but they may receive assistance from other ER machinery. The presence of glycosylation-independent pathways is important when comparing different substrates.

A defect in glucosidase activity may prevent appropriate progression into or out of a lectin-binding state, depending on which reaction is disrupted. A defect in UGGT may reduce the ability of certain nonnative glycoproteins to reenter the cycle after glucose removal. Neither prediction implies that all ER proteins will cease folding. The effect depends on the client's reliance on the lectin pathway and on compensatory assistance. For a useful exam experiment, compare a glycosylated client with a nonglycosylated client while checking that their synthesis and arrival in the ER are not independently impaired.

The ER also coordinates maturation with export. A protein can remain in the compartment because it carries a retention signal, because it binds to resident quality-control machinery or because it has not achieved a state compatible with further transport. Localization alone therefore cannot identify the underlying cause. A mutant protein accumulating in the ER may be incorrectly folded despite possessing an intact targeting signal. Another mutant may fold well but lack a recognition feature required for export. Distinguishing these possibilities calls for structural, functional and localization evidence.

When the influx of vulnerable proteins exceeds processing capacity, unfolded or misfolded proteins can accumulate in the ER. Cells can respond through the unfolded protein response, adjusting synthesis, folding capacity and disposal of persistent failures. In mammalian cells, one aspect of the response can reduce general translational initiation through phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha, while other response branches increase the expression of folding and quality-control machinery. These consequences connect the chapter to regulation of translation and cell signaling, without requiring a detailed treatment of every stress sensor.

The response is not simply a mechanism for turning all protein synthesis off. Secretory cells still need to make proteins, and prolonged stress responses can have complex consequences. Reducing influx temporarily can help restore the relationship between production and maturation, while increasing folding capacity can support recovery. A student should recognize the causal problem: the relevant load is the concentration and lifetime of vulnerable clients, not merely the absolute number of ribosomes or the presence of one misfolded molecule.

Persistent failures can be directed toward degradation. ER-associated degradation provides a route for disposing of certain nonnative ER proteins through recognition, movement toward cytosolic degradation machinery and proteasomal processing. Other disposal routes may also be important for selected substrates or large aggregates. The detailed ubiquitin–proteasome machinery belongs to the later degradation chapter. Here its relevance is that failure to mature can change the lifetime of the newly synthesized protein and, therefore, the amount available for biological function.

These principles prepare the way for a different form of chemical maturation in which a protein loses part of its original amino acid chain. Unlike a disulfide exchange or a proline isomerization, proteolytic cleavage changes backbone connectivity, creating a new amino or carboxyl terminus and sometimes separate mature chains.
