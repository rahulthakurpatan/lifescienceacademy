15. Receptor distribution and the identity of the responding cell

Hormones entering the circulation can encounter many tissues, but encountering a cell and regulating its activity are different events. Imagine a hormone passing through a blood vessel supplying muscle, liver and connective tissue. The hormone does not contain a chemical address that restricts its physical presence to one target tissue. Selective action instead emerges from the distribution of receptor proteins and from the accessibility of those proteins to the hormone. A cell that lacks a particular receptor may be directly insensitive to the corresponding hormone even though neighboring cells respond strongly.

A receptor transcript is an indication that a cell has the capacity to synthesize a particular receptor, but the amount of transcript does not specify how many functional receptor molecules are present. Translation, folding, post-translational modification, assembly and turnover intervene between RNA and active protein. For a membrane receptor, trafficking to the surface is another necessary stage. A receptor protein retained in the endoplasmic reticulum may be detectable in a whole-cell extract yet unavailable to extracellular ligand. For an intracellular receptor, ligand access and localization to the appropriate compartment must also be considered. Gene expression data, total protein measurements and accessible binding-site measurements answer distinct questions.

Receptor subtype expression can cause two tissues to interpret the same hormone differently. Catecholamines illustrate this principle particularly clearly. One population of cells may express a beta adrenergic receptor that commonly stimulates adenylyl cyclase through a stimulatory G protein. Another population may express alpha2 adrenergic receptors that commonly reduce cyclase activity through inhibitory G proteins. Under suitable conditions, the same external hormone can cause opposing changes in the same intracellular messenger. These differences do not require that the hormone itself change chemical identity on entering the two tissues. They arise because its information is interpreted by different receptor-coupling systems.

Differences in cellular machinery can also produce divergent physiological outcomes when the same receptor subtype or messenger is involved. In a cardiac muscle cell, stimulation of beta adrenergic signaling can modify proteins that control contraction. In some smooth muscle contexts, beta adrenergic signaling can instead favor relaxation through the proteins available in that cell. It would be an error to equate an increase in cyclic AMP with contraction as a universal rule. A messenger influences the substrates and regulatory proteins accessible to it, and the consequences of modifying those targets depend on their physiological roles. The messenger conveys an instruction, but the cell supplies the set of operations that the instruction can regulate.

Vasopressin provides a peptide example of receptor subtype diversity. V1a receptors commonly couple to signaling that involves phospholipase C, whereas V2 receptors commonly couple to signaling that increases cyclic AMP. In appropriate kidney cells, the latter pathway regulates the availability of water channels at the apical membrane. The peptide itself does not become a component of the water channel, and it does not physically escort water into the cell. In a different tissue, activation of another receptor subtype can influence cellular contraction instead. A defect that selectively affects V2 receptors would not necessarily abolish all responses to vasopressin elsewhere.

The distribution of intracellular receptor isoforms can likewise contribute to tissue specificity. Thyroid hormone receptors arise from distinct receptor genes and transcript variants, and their expression varies among cell types. The local abundance of transporters and deiodinases can additionally change ligand availability. Thus, an experiment comparing T3 responses between tissues should not assume that receptor binding is the only variable. Two tissues with comparable nuclear receptor abundance may differ in intracellular T3 concentration or in the regulatory elements and cofactors through which the receptor acts. A complete interpretation separates hormone availability, receptor function and transcriptional competence.

The role of cellular context also explains why receptor expression in a heterologous model is powerful but not universally decisive. A scientist might introduce a receptor gene into cultured cells that lack a detectable response to the hormone. If an appropriate response appears, the result supports a limiting role for the receptor under those experimental conditions. If no response appears, the introduced receptor may not have folded correctly, may be trapped in an inaccessible compartment or may lack a compatible signaling partner. The absence of rescue is not automatically evidence that the receptor cannot recognize the hormone. The experiment needs a positive control demonstrating that the relevant downstream machinery can respond when stimulated by another route.

Hormone signaling can also alter the sensitivity of a cell over longer periods. Changes in receptor production, turnover and distribution can modify how a subsequent hormone exposure is interpreted. Such changes need not occur in all receptor systems or in the same direction. A receptor initially abundant at the surface may become less available after repeated stimulation, whereas a different hormone-regulated transcriptional program may increase the abundance of a receptor in a particular tissue. The details of desensitization and feedback are treated later, but the immediate implication is that receptor expression is a regulated state rather than an unchanging label assigned to a cell.

16. Quantitative comparison of receptor abundance, affinity and hormone access

Quantitative measurements help prevent apparently similar responses from being attributed to the same molecular cause. A minimal model considers a receptor R that reversibly binds one ligand L to form a complex RL. At equilibrium the rate of association equals the rate of dissociation, and the dissociation constant Kd equals the product of free receptor concentration and free ligand concentration divided by the complex concentration. All concentrations must be expressed in compatible units. This is an equilibrium binding model, not a universal description of all receptor systems, and assumes one class of independent, equivalent binding sites.

Let Rtotal represent the total concentration of binding sites, equal to free R plus RL. Substitution of free R as Rtotal minus RL into the equilibrium definition gives Kd multiplied by RL equal to free L multiplied by the difference between Rtotal and RL. Collecting the terms containing RL on one side gives RL multiplied by the sum of Kd and free L equal to Rtotal multiplied by free L. Dividing by the total receptor concentration and by the sum gives the occupied fraction, theta, equal to free L divided by the sum of Kd and free L. The equation has no units because both the numerator and denominator are concentrations in the same units.

When free ligand concentration equals Kd, the model predicts half occupancy. A receptor with a lower Kd has a higher equilibrium affinity than a receptor with a higher Kd when compared under the same conditions. This statement concerns the fraction occupied at a specified free ligand concentration, not the magnitude of the biological response. If the two receptors have different levels of expression or different efficiency of downstream coupling, the same fractional occupancy can lead to different responses. The distinction is particularly important when comparing hormone-sensitive tissues rather than purified proteins.

Consider a hypothetical pair of receptor populations with the same Kd of 2 nanomolar. Cell A has 20,000 accessible receptor binding sites, while cell B has 5,000 accessible sites. If the free hormone concentration is maintained at 6 nanomolar, theta equals 6 divided by 8, or 0.75. The two cells have the same fractional occupancy, but A has 15,000 occupied sites while B has 3,750. If each occupied site produced the same initial signaling output and the system remained in a linear range, A could generate four times as much initial output. This last prediction requires explicit assumptions; downstream components may saturate or become limiting.

Now reverse the comparison and consider two receptor types in a cell population with the same accessible receptor abundance. One has a Kd of 1 nanomolar, and the other has a Kd of 9 nanomolar. At 3 nanomolar free ligand, their fractional occupancies are 3 divided by 4 and 3 divided by 12, respectively. This gives 75 percent and 25 percent occupancy. With 8,000 accessible sites of each type, the predicted occupied counts are 6,000 and 2,000. Higher affinity produces more occupied sites in this particular comparison because the abundance is held constant. It does not establish that the first receptor's downstream output will be threefold greater. The receptors may couple to different effectors altogether.

The value of Kd must not be confused with EC50, the ligand concentration giving half of the maximal measured response in a defined dose-response experiment. The former is a binding parameter, and the latter is a functional parameter. If a system has substantial amplification, a half-maximal output can occur when fewer than half of its receptor sites are occupied. For example, a hypothetical receptor with Kd of 10 nanomolar would be about 9.1 percent occupied at 1 nanomolar free ligand. If its signaling system gives half of maximal output at that concentration, the measured EC50 would be 1 nanomolar, not 10 nanomolar. The difference does not violate the binding model because response and occupancy are distinct measured variables.

This separation is useful when examining receptor mutants. Suppose a mutant has the same equilibrium binding curve as the normal protein but a right-shifted functional dose-response curve and a lower maximum output. One possible explanation is impaired signal coupling rather than altered ligand recognition. A reduction in accessible receptor number could also change the functional response if the binding curves were normalized to each population's own maximum. Absolute binding capacity must therefore be examined along with normalized affinity. A graph containing only percentages of maximal binding can conceal large changes in the number of sites available.

Another quantitative complication occurs when hormones bind abundant extracellular carrier proteins. A simple model considers a ligand L that binds a single class of carrier sites C to produce CL. The dissociation constant Kc equals free carrier multiplied by free ligand divided by bound complex concentration. If the free ligand concentration is far below Kc and the carrier remains far from saturation, most carrier sites remain unoccupied and free carrier concentration can be approximated by total carrier concentration. Rearranging the equilibrium equation then gives bound hormone approximately equal to free hormone multiplied by the ratio of total carrier concentration to Kc.

The total hormone concentration in this model is the sum of free and carrier-bound hormone. Substituting the approximate expression for bound hormone gives total hormone approximately equal to free hormone multiplied by one plus total carrier concentration divided by Kc. The free fraction is therefore approximately one divided by that bracketed factor. This is a deliberately simplified model for illustrating the effects of carrier binding. It ignores other carriers, ligand depletion of binding sites, tissue uptake and physiological feedback. The approximation should be rejected if a large fraction of the available carrier sites becomes occupied.

For a hypothetical calculation, take total carrier concentration as 900 nanomolar and Kc as 100 nanomolar. Under dilute, unsaturated conditions, the free fraction is one divided by ten, or 0.10. A total hormone concentration of 2 nanomolar then corresponds to approximately 0.2 nanomolar free hormone. If carrier abundance increases to 1,900 nanomolar without changing total hormone or Kc, the approximate free fraction falls to one divided by twenty, or 0.05, corresponding to 0.1 nanomolar free hormone. Both free concentrations are much lower than the assumed Kc, and the bound concentrations are very small relative to carrier abundance, supporting the approximation in this example.

Suppose an intracellular receptor is accessible to a ligand in equilibrium with the free extracellular pool, and for the sake of the example the receptor-site free ligand concentration equals the calculated free concentration. With a receptor Kd of 0.2 nanomolar, the first condition would give half occupancy and the second would give one-third occupancy. The number of total hormone molecules measured in the extracellular samples has not changed, yet the predicted fraction of receptor sites occupied has. In real thyroid hormone signaling, transport and local metabolism may invalidate the assumption that extracellular free ligand concentration equals the concentration at the nuclear receptor. This is a reason to state the simplifying assumption clearly rather than to transfer the numerical prediction directly to a physiological situation.

A final issue is ligand depletion. The simple occupancy equation uses the free concentration of the ligand, not necessarily the amount initially added to a sample. If receptors or carrier sites bind a substantial fraction of the ligand present, the remaining free concentration may be considerably smaller than the nominal starting concentration. Adding more binding sites can then change both the number of occupied sites and the fraction occupied. The examples above stipulated a maintained free ligand concentration or dilute carrier conditions to avoid that complication. Whenever a question gives total rather than free ligand, the student must decide whether it is reasonable to approximate one by the other before substituting into an equation.

17. Comparing the first molecular event and the source of energy

The receptor classes can be organized by a single question: what happens immediately after recognition becomes productive? For a ligand-gated ion channel, the probability of an ion-conducting state changes. For a conventional GPCR, the receptor promotes activation of a separate G protein. For an intrinsic kinase receptor, a receptor catalytic domain alters the phosphorylation state of appropriate proteins. A kinase-associated receptor relies on a separate enzyme to carry out a similar chemical transfer. A receptor guanylyl cyclase changes the rate of cyclic nucleotide formation through its own catalytic region. A classical nuclear receptor changes its regulatory interactions with DNA and associated proteins.

Each event must be distinguished from the final physiological effect. If a receptor guanylyl cyclase initiates a rise in cyclic GMP, the messenger may activate a kinase that modifies proteins. Detecting that later phosphorylation does not transform the original receptor into a protein kinase. A receptor tyrosine kinase may indirectly change the concentration of a lipid-derived intracellular messenger without catalyzing its synthesis itself. A ligand-gated calcium-permeable channel can alter transcription through calcium-dependent proteins without becoming a nuclear receptor. It is possible to trace a common downstream output backward through multiple distinct architectures. The earliest class-defining event is therefore an anchor for causal interpretation.

The relevant energy sources differ as well. Opening an ion channel permits movement along an electrochemical gradient. The gradient may have been created or maintained by energy-consuming pumps, but the channel does not normally hydrolyze an ATP molecule for every ion that flows through it. In a kinase pathway, phosphorylation consumes ATP as a phosphate donor. In a GPCR pathway, the G-protein cycle uses guanine nucleotide binding and hydrolysis, while further downstream reactions can consume other substrates. In guanylyl cyclase signaling, GTP is converted into cyclic GMP and pyrophosphate. Transcription and subsequent synthesis of new proteins require nucleotide and other energy resources, but the nuclear receptor is not itself the enzyme that drives RNA chain elongation.

This comparison helps distinguish a signal from a source of chemical energy. The hormone may remain bound only transiently and may eventually dissociate without being chemically transformed by its receptor. The cellular response can nevertheless involve large changes in metabolic activity. The receptor controls the use of pre-existing chemical substrates and gradients. If a hormone-triggered reaction requires ATP, the ATP does not ordinarily originate in the hormone molecule. It comes from the target cell. This principle explains how low concentrations of signaling molecules can cause substantial biological effects.

In experiments involving metabolic inhibitors, interpretation must account for this organization. A severe reduction in cellular ATP could compromise phosphorylation, membrane potential maintenance, protein synthesis and transport at the same time. A reduced hormone response after such a treatment is not specific evidence for a kinase receptor. To establish a requirement for intrinsic receptor catalysis, a more selective perturbation, such as a receptor mutation verified to preserve ligand binding and surface expression, is more informative. Global disruption of energy supply can show that the response depends on cellular function but seldom identifies the particular molecular architecture of the receptor.

There is also a difference between a receptor's catalytic capacity and the strength of a measured output. A receptor enzyme can turn over multiple substrate molecules after activation, but its rate depends on substrate concentration, catalytic state and inhibitory regulation. A ligand-gated channel can permit many ions to cross during an open interval, but the net flux depends on the driving force and conductance. Both systems can amplify a ligand-binding event in a functional sense without using the same molecular operation. A question about amplification should specify whether it refers to messenger molecules, modified substrates, transported ions or an eventual cellular response.
