9. Labelled lines, modality and central interpretation

The electrical properties of an impulse do not by themselves establish whether it represents a visual event or a mechanical disturbance of the skin. A propagated impulse from a cutaneous afferent can resemble one recorded from an auditory afferent. The information about modality arises partly from the identity of the activated neuron and the connections through which its signals reach other parts of the nervous system. The labelled-line principle describes the idea that activity in a particular sensory pathway has meaning because of that pathway's origin, selectivity and connections. The label is not a chemical tag attached to each impulse. It is a functional property of the organised neural circuit.

Imagine two afferents delivering impulse trains with the same average firing rate. One originates in a mechanically sensitive ending in the finger, and the other is connected to a cochlear receptor. Even if the rates are identical, their activity arrives in different anatomical pathways and contributes to different patterns of central processing. A sensory circuit does not need to infer the modality from the rate alone because it receives the events through connections with different input identities. This is the reason that a stimulus applied artificially to a sensory pathway can evoke a sensation associated with that pathway even if the normal receptor has not been stimulated. The interpretation of the activity depends on the biological organisation of the circuit.

The doctrine commonly associated with the specific energies of nerves emerged historically from observations that different sensory nerves produced characteristic sensations and that unusual forms of stimulation could activate a modality-specific experience. Modern sensory physiology retains the insight that pathway identity is important while adding a mechanistic account based on receptor specialisation, synaptic organisation and central population activity. It would be misleading to interpret the historical doctrine as stating that visual impulses are composed of a fundamentally different electrical energy from tactile impulses. The basic regenerative electrical mechanism is shared; selective peripheral transduction and central organisation establish the relationship between an impulse pattern and a stimulus category.

Labelled lines are compatible with population coding. A visual pathway may carry light-related information as a broad modality, while multiple visual neurons with different receptive fields and response properties together encode location, contrast and wavelength-related features. Likewise, two tactile afferents may differ in the type of mechanical event to which they are most responsive even though both contribute to somatosensation. The nervous system can compare these labelled inputs to estimate an event's properties. A model in which every possible stimulus must have a dedicated neuron would be unrealistic, because each sensory variable can take many values and stimuli often combine several properties simultaneously. Population responses allow a finite network to represent a large range of such combinations.

The principle also has limitations. A cell can be responsive to more than one type of stimulus, and central neurons can receive inputs related to different sensory modalities. A nociceptor may respond to strongly mechanical, thermal or chemical events associated with potential tissue damage. Its discharge does not always identify the exact physical source without contributions from other receptors. In a multisensory task, visual and auditory information may both influence the interpretation of an event. These examples show that pathway identity constrains meaning but need not uniquely determine every aspect of perception. The appropriate conclusion is not that labelled lines are false, but that the nervous system can use selective channels together with distributed and convergent representations.

Central processing is required for the recognition and use of many sensory patterns. An animal may generate a rapid motor response to touch without first producing a conscious report of the event. A sensory signal can reach local circuitry and also contribute to an ascending pathway. The fact that peripheral transduction is necessary for a normal stimulus-evoked response does not imply that every physiological response requires awareness. Conversely, a normal peripheral recording does not establish that the complete sensory function is intact. A defect in the subsequent pathway could alter the perception or use of information despite preservation of receptor currents and afferent impulses. This separation of stages will be especially valuable when analysing the visual and auditory systems.

The problem of localisation illustrates why pathway identity is only part of a complete explanation. Knowing that a train of impulses comes from a tactile afferent establishes that it is related to contact or another appropriate mechanical stimulus, but an observer also needs information about the field of that afferent and its response relative to nearby afferents. Knowing that activity comes from the left eye does not identify a particular object within the visual field. The central organisation of projections, together with the distribution and timing of impulses, allows circuits to recover more specific spatial or temporal information. Thus modality, location, intensity and duration are different decoding problems, although their neural solutions can interact.

A useful reasoning exercise considers two manipulations. The first selectively disrupts a sensory transduction protein in an otherwise intact receptor. The second interrupts an ascending pathway after normal peripheral activity has been generated. A natural stimulus may fail to produce a normal percept in both cases. However, in the first case a peripheral recording can reveal reduced transduction, and direct electrical stimulation of an intact downstream segment may bypass the defect. In the second case the peripheral response may remain normal, but stimulation below the interruption may still fail to reach the relevant central circuits. An experiment must therefore specify where the signal is introduced and where it is measured. Identical behavioural outcomes can arise from very different defects in the chain of sensory processing.

10. Lateral inhibition, contrast enhancement and spatial discrimination

A sensory surface does not merely report the absolute intensity at each location. Many important features are defined by relationships between neighbouring locations. The boundary between a bright object and a dark background can be more informative about the object's outline than the average amount of light in the entire scene. A local point of pressure is more easily identified when the strongest response is distinguishable from activity generated by the surrounding tissue. Lateral inhibition is a circuit mechanism in which activity associated with one sensory location suppresses activity associated with neighbouring locations or channels. By changing the relative response of adjacent units, it can make spatial differences more prominent.

An elementary model begins with three neighbouring sensory channels. Each channel receives excitation related to its own stimulus, and each can also be influenced by inhibitory activity associated with nearby channels. In a spatially uniform stimulus, all three channels receive similar direct excitation and similar inhibitory influences. Their outputs may be reduced but remain relatively similar. When one channel receives greater excitation than its neighbours, its inhibitory influence on those neighbours is larger than the reverse influence they exert on it, under suitable connectivity. The local response maximum becomes more distinct relative to its surroundings. This is contrast enhancement; it does not require an increase in the absolute firing rate of the most strongly stimulated unit.

Lateral inhibition is usually implemented through organised synaptic connections, often involving interneurons or specialised retinal circuitry. It should not be imagined as one propagated impulse physically colliding with another in a neighbouring axon. In a typical inhibitory pathway, transmitter release alters the postsynaptic conductance or the state of another cell, thereby reducing the probability or magnitude of that cell's response to excitation. In some circuits, inhibitory effects can also act on transmitter release at an earlier stage. The anatomical sign of a synapse and the net effect of a multi-neuron pathway are distinct concepts. A circuit can include several connections whose combined effect is suppression of neighbouring responses even when the detailed implementation differs among species and modalities.

The classical experiments of H. K. Hartline, H. G. Wagner and Floyd Ratliff on the lateral eye of the horseshoe crab Limulus in 1956 supplied direct evidence for inhibitory interactions between receptor units. Each ommatidial unit contributes an identifiable impulse discharge in an optic nerve fibre. An investigator can illuminate a selected test unit while recording its impulses, and then illuminate other units while keeping the direct illumination of the test unit unchanged. A reduction in the test discharge when neighbouring units are stimulated demonstrates that the test response is not determined solely by the light falling on that unit. Subsequent work explored the relationships among inhibitory influences from different units. This is a particularly clear historical example of the value of independent control of a local stimulus and its surround.

The interpretation requires appropriate controls. If the test unit is illuminated alone and its rate is already falling because of adaptation, a later reduction in rate when another unit is illuminated could be wrongly attributed to inhibition. Repeated test-alone trials at comparable times and alternating stimulus conditions help distinguish the two effects. It is also necessary to control unwanted changes in light reaching the test unit. Light scattered from a neighbouring stimulus could alter direct excitation, potentially obscuring the effect being measured. Holding direct excitation fixed while selectively changing the neighbour is the logical basis for identifying an interaction. The exact numerical examples used below are simplified teaching models, not the published recordings of Hartline and colleagues.

Consider an illustrative feed-forward subtraction model with three channels A, B and C. Let their direct excitatory drives be xA, xB and xC, expressed as equivalent spike-rate units. Suppose the output of channel B is yB = max(0, xB − k(xA + xC)), where k is a dimensionless inhibitory weight, and the max function prevents negative firing rates. For an interior channel, the model assumes that inhibition from the two immediate neighbours is additive and that the excitatory drives are independent of the output rates. If xA = 20, xB = 60 and xC = 20 with k = 0.25, the central output is 60 − 0.25(40) = 50. If the centre's direct drive remains at 60 but each neighbouring drive increases to 60, the central output falls to 30. In the model, the centre responds differently to an identical local stimulus because the surrounding stimulus changed.

The same model clarifies edge enhancement. Imagine a long row of channels, where those on the dark side of an edge have a direct drive of 20 and those on the bright side have a drive of 60. Away from the edge, a dark-side interior channel has an output of 20 − 0.25(20 + 20) = 10, and a bright-side interior channel has an output of 60 − 0.25(60 + 60) = 30. At the border, the last dark channel has neighbours of 20 and 60, so its output becomes 20 − 0.25(80) = 0. The first bright channel also has neighbours of 20 and 60, so its output becomes 60 − 20 = 40. The output difference across the border is therefore 40, compared with the difference of 20 between the two uniform interior regions after inhibition. The border contrast is accentuated relative to the uniform-region contrast even though the bright border channel's output remains below its uninhibited direct drive of 60.

This model makes useful predictions but does not reproduce every detail of a biological inhibitory network. Real units can inhibit one another reciprocally; the inhibitory effect may depend on the actual discharge of the neighbour rather than on its direct stimulus drive. Inhibition may have a threshold, a delay or a saturating dependence on input, and the receiving cell can also have nonlinear excitatory responses. Some sensory fields include several spatial scales of excitatory and inhibitory influence. A model based on subtracting two fixed neighbour weights is useful for learning the sign and spatial consequences of inhibition, but its quantitative predictions must not be transferred automatically to an actual organ.

Centre-surround organisation describes an effective receptive field with opposing response regions. For an ON-centre, OFF-surround retinal ganglion cell, light in the centre increases its discharge relative to baseline under appropriate adaptation conditions, while light in the surround tends to decrease it. An OFF-centre, ON-surround cell shows the complementary relationship. If a spot illuminates only the centre of an ON-centre field, the excitatory effect is relatively unopposed. If a larger uniform spot illuminates both centre and surround, the opposing influences can partially cancel, producing a smaller net response. Such cells are particularly informative about local differences in illumination rather than simply the average brightness across the entire field. The detailed retinal circuit, including the signs of photoreceptor and bipolar-cell responses, belongs to 7.E.03.

An important distinction is that centre-surround antagonism is a measured functional organisation of a receptive field, whereas lateral inhibition refers to a mechanism involving interactions between spatially or functionally related channels. The two are related but not identical. One cannot determine the complete synaptic architecture merely from a centre-surround response map. Different networks can produce similar maps, so additional measurements or manipulations are required to identify the mechanism. This is a general principle of scientific inference: a useful computational description of a circuit's output does not necessarily specify the molecular or anatomical implementation that produces it.

In tactile processing, inhibitory interactions can help distinguish nearby points by suppressing activity between strongly excited regions. Suppose two points of contact initially produce broadly overlapping excitation. If spatial interactions reduce the activity of less strongly excited channels between or around the maxima, the resulting representation may contain two more distinguishable peaks. However, if the two points excite virtually the same receptor population and produce no reliably distinguishable differences in input, downstream inhibition cannot recreate spatial information that was never sampled. Lateral inhibition enhances usable contrast within the constraints of peripheral sampling. It does not give an organ unlimited spatial resolution.

Excessive inhibition can also impair detection. If a weak isolated signal occurs in a region receiving substantial inhibition from a bright or strongly activated surround, the net output may fall below a criterion even though the peripheral receptor itself remains responsive. A circuit optimised for contrast may give relatively little weight to the absolute intensity of a broad uniform stimulus. Other channels with different spatial organisation can supply complementary information. These trade-offs explain why sensory systems often include parallel pathways with different receptive-field properties. They also explain why the statement that inhibition always increases sensitivity is incorrect: improvement of discrimination between spatial features is not the same outcome as improved detection of every weak stimulus.

11. Sensory acuity and psychophysics: relating physical stimuli to detection and perception

Physiological recordings establish how receptors and neurons respond, but the function of a sensory system is ultimately evaluated in relation to what the animal can detect, discriminate or use. Psychophysics studies relationships between controlled physical stimulation and behavioural or perceptual responses. It differs from a direct recording of receptor current because the measured outcome may depend on many stages of processing, including decision and motor response. In a simple human detection task, the experimenter presents a stimulus or a blank and asks whether a stimulus was perceived. In an animal experiment, a trained subject may perform one of two responses depending on the presence or location of a stimulus. In both cases the response is a behavioural measure, not a direct measurement of the membrane potential of a receptor.

An absolute threshold is a stimulus intensity corresponding to a specified probability of detection in a defined task. If intensities are presented repeatedly, the proportion of correct detections or responses generally changes gradually rather than jumping sharply at one physical value. A psychometric curve plots the response probability against the relevant stimulus variable. In a yes-or-no task, a threshold might be described by a chosen detection percentage, provided false reports on blank trials are considered. In a two-alternative forced-choice task, chance performance is 50%, and a criterion such as 75% correct is often used to define a threshold above chance. These criteria are conventions tied to the task and cannot be compared indiscriminately across different procedures.

For example, suppose a subject must indicate in which of two time intervals a brief vibration occurred. At a very weak amplitude, the subject guesses and obtains approximately 50% correct. At increasing amplitudes, performance rises to 60%, 75%, 90% and eventually approaches 100% under favourable conditions. If the protocol defines threshold as the amplitude corresponding to 75% correct, that value estimates detection sensitivity for the stated conditions. It does not mean that a smaller vibration can never be detected or that every larger vibration will always be detected. The curve reflects variability across trials, and the threshold is a summary of this probabilistic relationship. Presenting the actual curve gives more information than reporting the threshold alone.

In a yes-or-no task, the participant may adopt a liberal or conservative decision criterion. A liberal criterion increases the tendency to report that a stimulus was present, which can increase both correct reports on stimulus trials and incorrect reports on blank trials. A conservative criterion reduces false alarms but can also increase missed detections. A measured increase in the proportion of yes responses therefore does not necessarily indicate improved peripheral sensitivity. The experiment should distinguish hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections, and control task instructions and stimulus probabilities when comparing conditions. This elementary distinction between sensitivity and response criterion is sufficient for interpreting many examination questions without invoking more specialised mathematical models of signal detection.

A difference threshold, often called a just-noticeable difference, concerns discrimination between stimuli rather than detection of a stimulus relative to its absence. A subject may reliably detect a weight placed on the hand but require a particular additional weight to distinguish it from a comparison weight. The minimum reliably discriminable increment depends on the starting intensity and on the method used to test discrimination. At many intermediate stimulus levels, an approximate empirical relationship called Weber's law states that the ratio of the just-noticeable increment ΔS to the reference intensity S is approximately constant for a specified modality and condition. This dimensionless ratio, k = ΔS/S, is the Weber fraction.

Suppose a hypothetical weight-discrimination experiment gives a just-noticeable difference of 5 g at a reference mass of 100 g. The Weber fraction is 5/100 = 0.05. If the same fraction remains valid at a reference mass of 300 g under the same experimental conditions, the predicted increment is 0.05 × 300 = 15 g. Notice that the absolute increment becomes larger at the greater reference mass while the relative increment remains constant. Mass is used as a convenient stimulus variable in this example; the gravitational force is proportional to mass at the same location. The prediction is conditional on the tested operating range and cannot be applied automatically to extremely small or large masses, to different tasks or to a different modality.

Weber's law is distinct from Fechner's historical proposal about perceived magnitude. If one assumes that each just-noticeable increment corresponds to an equal small increment in perceived sensation, and that Weber's proportionality holds over a range, then a mathematical integration gives a logarithmic relationship between an idealised sensation magnitude and physical intensity. In symbolic form, let dP = a dS/S, where P is a hypothetical perception scale and a is a proportionality constant. Integrating from a positive reference intensity S₀ to S gives P(S) − P(S₀) = a ln(S/S₀). The logarithm operates on a dimensionless ratio, and the reference cannot be zero. This derivation shows exactly which assumption connects the discrimination result to the sensation scale. Weber's law itself does not establish that subjective magnitude must follow a logarithmic function, and real perceptual relationships can deviate from both simplifications.

The distinction becomes clear when a hypothetical question compares equal increments in absolute intensity and equal ratios of intensity. Under Weber's law, a change from 10 to 11 units and a change from 100 to 110 units both have a relative increment of 0.1, so both have the same increment expressed in Weber fractions if the constant remains unchanged. Under the idealised logarithmic magnitude model, the change in P also depends on the ratio and is the same for the two cases. However, the models address different dependent variables. The former predicts discriminability under stated conditions, and the latter proposes a relation for perceived magnitude under additional assumptions. A receptor's firing rate is not necessarily equal to either of these quantities; psychophysics concerns the relation between physical stimulation and perceptual or behavioural outcomes.

Spatial acuity is the ability to discriminate fine differences in stimulus position or spatial pattern. In tactile physiology, a classical task asks whether simultaneous contact at two points is experienced as one or two. The minimum separation at which the subject reliably distinguishes the points gives an operational two-point discrimination threshold. The result depends on the physical arrangement of the probes, their pressure, timing, orientation, the region of skin, attention and the response criterion. It is not a direct measurement of the diameter of a single receptor field. A low discrimination threshold is consistent with high spatial acuity, but several mechanisms can contribute to the result.

Consider two skin regions with comparable detection thresholds for an isolated probe but different receptor densities and convergence. One region has many small overlapping fields and maintains much of the spatial detail in its central representation. The other has broad fields and extensive convergence. Both may respond when a weak probe contacts the skin, yet the second region may require wider separation before two probes generate distinguishable patterns. This dissociation between detection and discrimination is not a contradiction. It demonstrates that the total evidence for stimulation and the spatial information contained within that evidence are different properties of a sensory code.

There are limits to inferring sensory architecture from psychophysical performance alone. If training improves the ability to discriminate two nearby stimuli, the improvement might result from better use of existing neural information, altered attention or changes in central processing rather than an increase in the number of peripheral receptors. Conversely, a reduction in performance can reflect an inappropriate response criterion, fatigue or impaired motor reporting, as well as a change in peripheral sensitivity. Combining behavioural measurements with physiological recordings makes explanations more specific. The key question in an experimental interpretation is which alternatives have been excluded by the available observations and which remain possible.

12. Experimental integration: locating the origin of a change in sensory output

The previous sections describe a sequence of physical, electrical, synaptic and central transformations. A change at the end of that sequence does not by itself identify the step responsible. A useful experimental strategy is to measure signals at successive stages and then bypass particular steps with controlled stimulation. Consider a tactile ending whose spike response to a standard indentation has decreased. Possible explanations include a change in the mechanical coupling between probe and ending, a reduction in transduction current, a smaller voltage response to the same current, a failure of the spike initiation mechanism or a defect in propagation. If the behavioural response rather than peripheral firing is the measured outcome, defects in central processing and motor reporting must also be considered.

An initial experiment can compare the current evoked by the standard indentation at the same holding voltage before and after a manipulation. If the stimulus-dependent current is reduced, the difference lies at or upstream of the measured current-generating process, assuming comparable recording conditions. Further experiments can determine whether the physical deformation actually reaching the transducing membrane is reduced. If the deformation is unchanged and the current is smaller, a change in channel availability, channel regulation or the relevant ionic driving force becomes more plausible. Measuring current-voltage relations can help distinguish a decrease in conductance from a shift in reversal potential. Neither observation by itself identifies a particular gene or protein without additional evidence.

A complementary experiment injects a controlled depolarising current while recording the voltage and spikes of the sensory neuron. This stimulation bypasses the ordinary mechanism that couples the external stimulus to a change in conductance. If the neuron produces normal responses to injected current but responds poorly to natural stimulation, its spike generator can still operate under the test conditions. If both responses are reduced, altered excitability or another downstream problem becomes a possibility. The interpretation depends on where current is injected; stimulation of an axon may bypass a defect in the normal spike initiation region. The most precise conclusion is therefore about the pathway segments included in each test, not the entire neuron indiscriminately.

The same reasoning can distinguish peripheral and synaptic defects in a receptor-cell system. If displacement of an auditory hair bundle still produces a normal graded voltage response but the connected afferent no longer fires normally, transduction is preserved by that particular measure. Altered calcium entry, transmitter release, postsynaptic sensitivity or afferent excitability could account for the deficit. Direct recording of synaptic responses and stimulation of the afferent can narrow these alternatives. A normal voltage response alone cannot establish normal transmitter release because the coupling from voltage to vesicle fusion is itself a distinct physiological process. Such a result is an excellent illustration of the importance of separating a receptor potential from propagated neural output.

Experimental controls are indispensable when comparing response dynamics. Suppose an afferent discharges at 70 Hz after the onset of a step and at 20 Hz near the end. The investigator wants to conclude that adaptation has occurred. The stimulus must be verified as constant at the relevant external measurement point, and baseline and recording stability must be monitored. If the mechanical force decays during the step, the fall in response may simply follow that decrease. If the force remains constant but the deformation of the nerve terminal changes because of the capsule, this supports an accessory mechanical contribution to adaptation. If terminal deformation and transduction current remain constant while firing declines, a contribution from downstream excitability becomes more plausible. Each additional measurement excludes particular alternatives rather than automatically proving a single mechanism.

For examining receptive fields, the equivalent strategy is to hold stimulus strength constant and change position, rather than varying strength and position simultaneously. For examining lateral inhibition, one holds the direct stimulus to a recorded unit fixed and changes the surround. For examining temporal coding, one retains the spike timestamps and relates them to the stimulus time course rather than relying only on the total count. For examining behavioural sensitivity, repeated controlled trials and appropriate blank or comparison conditions are necessary. Although these experiments investigate different properties, they share a common principle: vary the independent variable relevant to the proposed mechanism while controlling other factors that could account for the outcome.

The distinction between a hypothetical dataset and published evidence must remain clear. Numerical models are valuable because their assumptions and predictions can be examined without the additional variability of a complex preparation. Their parameters, however, do not become physiological constants simply because they appear in a scientific calculation. A fitted time constant in one sensory ending need not describe another ending or even the same ending under different conditions. A subtraction model of lateral inhibition does not prove that a particular circuit implements exactly those weights. To apply a model to experimental data, compare its predictions with observations, examine discrepancies and consider alternative mechanisms. This reasoning is central to the analytical expectations of CSIR-NET Part C.

Vision, hearing and tactile sensation now provide three opportunities to apply the common framework. In vision, the optical apparatus establishes the distribution of light at the sensory surface, and retinal cells convert that distribution into a structured neural output. In hearing, mechanical processing separates and transfers components of sound to receptor cells, which communicate with afferent fibres. In touch, skin mechanics and receptor specialisations convert local deformation into patterns of afferent activity with different spatial and temporal sensitivities. In each case the next questions are the same: which physical stimulus reaches the receptor, which molecular events change the membrane signal, how is the output encoded, and what transformations allow the nervous system to discriminate the features that matter? The following chapters develop the specific organs using this shared conceptual foundation.
