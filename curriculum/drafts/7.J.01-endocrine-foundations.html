The physiological problem of distant coordination

An animal is made of cells that often perform quite different tasks. A skeletal muscle fibre converts chemical energy into movement, a liver cell manages circulating fuels, and a kidney tubule controls the composition of the internal fluid. Yet these activities must change together as the animal moves between feeding, fasting, exercise, sleep and reproduction. A muscle cell cannot simply assume that glucose will remain available while it contracts. Its demand has consequences for other organs. The endocrine system supplies a means of coordinating such distributed activities without requiring a dedicated nerve ending at every responsive cell.

A hormone is a chemical signal released by a cell that influences the activity of responsive cells through specific recognition mechanisms. In classical endocrine communication the signal enters the circulation and reaches targets at a distance. The blood transports the signal but does not determine which cells interpret it. Target specificity instead depends on receptors, access to the signal and the biochemical machinery available within the responding cell. A circulating hormone may pass through the vasculature of many tissues while producing a substantial effect in only a subset. Endocrine regulation therefore combines widespread delivery with selective interpretation.

An endocrine gland releases its products into the internal environment rather than delivering them through a duct to an epithelial surface. An exocrine gland delivers products through a duct or directly onto an epithelial surface. This difference concerns the destination of secretion, not the chemical nature of the product. The pancreas illustrates both forms of organisation within one organ: acinar cells produce digestive enzyme precursors that reach the intestinal lumen through ducts, whereas islet cells secrete regulatory peptides into the local extracellular fluid and circulation. A pancreatic duct obstruction can consequently interfere with digestive juice delivery without immediately eliminating the ability of surviving islet cells to release hormone into capillaries. It would be incorrect to classify the entire organ as exclusively endocrine or exocrine.

Many endocrine cells are not collected into discrete anatomical glands. Individual cells scattered through the intestinal epithelium produce signals that coordinate digestion with nutrient handling elsewhere. The kidney and heart also produce regulatory signals in addition to their primary physiological tasks. This distributed arrangement makes endocrine physiology a study of interorgan communication rather than merely a catalogue of glands. When considering a newly described chemical messenger, ask where it is produced, what causes its release, how it reaches a target, how the target recognises it and how its activity ends.

From a neural explanation to chemical communication

A digestive organ can begin secreting when events occur at another location in the alimentary tract. The critical scientific question is whether the distant response requires a continuous neural pathway or whether a substance carried by body fluids can transmit information. In 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling investigated pancreatic secretion after exposing an intestinal segment to acid under conditions in which its nerve connections had been interrupted. Pancreatic secretion could still be evoked. Their experiments with intestinal extracts supported a chemical mechanism and established the significance of secretin.

The logic of the evidence matters more than remembering the names. A response persisting after denervation shows that the interrupted neural pathway is not necessary under those experimental conditions. It does not establish that neural inputs never influence secretion in an intact animal. Demonstrating activity after transfer of an extract supplies complementary evidence for a transferable chemical factor. An extract, however, is a mixture, so purification and subsequent chemical characterization are required to identify the active molecule. Modern understanding of secretin links intestinal acid sensing to pancreatic bicarbonate-rich fluid secretion, helping neutralise acidic chyme.

A hypothetical modern experiment can separate the alternatives by comparing four preparations with equivalent blood flow: intestinal acid with intact innervation, intestinal acid following denervation, vehicle following denervation and acid exposure with selective blockade of the relevant hormone receptor. The first two conditions would test whether a response survives loss of local neural input, the vehicle would establish background activity, and receptor blockade would test dependence on reception of the chemical signal. The prediction is not a claim about the original numerical results; it is an experimental reasoning exercise. Failure of secretion after receptor blockade is interpretable only if viability and perfusion are maintained and the blockade is selective.

The historical lesson is that an explanation based exclusively on nerves is insufficient. Electrical activity and chemical transmission are cooperating means of regulation. Neuroendocrine cells exemplify this cooperation directly: a neuron can convert an electrical signal into release of a circulating messenger. Rather than divide the animal into unrelated nervous and endocrine systems, the student should examine how the two systems interact to coordinate different physiological time scales.

Four routes of hormonal communication

Endocrine signalling uses circulation as a distribution route. Neuroendocrine signalling is a special form in which a neuron produces a chemical messenger and releases it into a vascular compartment. Hypothalamic neurons illustrate the latter arrangement. Some release regulatory factors into portal blood that reaches the anterior pituitary, while others send axons to the posterior pituitary and release their products into systemic blood. The difference between the two arrangements is important because it separates hormone synthesis from the anatomical site of release.

Paracrine communication depends on local movement through extracellular fluid to neighbouring cells. A pancreatic islet contains multiple endocrine cell populations that can influence one another locally before their products enter general circulation. Local diffusion, extracellular degradation, vascular removal and receptor-mediated uptake affect the spatial reach of a paracrine signal. A ligand that is rapidly removed may produce an effective concentration close to its source while remaining almost undetectable in peripheral blood. Absence of a large systemic concentration does not imply absence of a biological role.

In autocrine communication a cell responds to a messenger that it releases itself. The signal can therefore alter the subsequent activity of its source. A population of similar cells may share such local communication, making the precise distinction between autocrine and paracrine effects dependent on the responding cell. Autocrine signalling need not be inhibitory; its sign is determined by the regulatory pathway. Some autocrine interactions sustain an activity while others constrain it. Local and circulating actions are not mutually exclusive properties of a molecule.

Synaptic communication differs from classical endocrine delivery because neuronal transmitter release occurs near a defined postsynaptic target. The local concentration can rise and decline sharply, and the anatomical arrangement contributes to specificity. However, the same molecular species can participate in different communication modes depending on the site and manner of release. Noradrenaline used at a sympathetic nerve ending acts locally as a neurotransmitter; catecholamines entering the blood from the adrenal medulla participate in endocrine signalling. Thus classification of a communication event requires knowledge of its route as well as its molecular identity.

Gland architecture and directional secretion

The expression ductless gland describes how the product leaves the gland rather than the appearance of a particular cell. In an exocrine epithelial gland, cells usually have an apical surface facing a lumen and a basal surface facing connective tissue. Secretory material crosses the apical membrane into the lumen, which may be continuous with the exterior or with the cavity of another organ. An intestinal lumen belongs to the latter category. A digestive enzyme that acts on food in that lumen is not thereby an endocrine hormone. Endocrine tissue instead releases its hormonal products toward the interstitial compartment and ultimately toward blood vessels. Vascular delivery exposes distant responsive tissues to the signal.

The pancreas is especially instructive because its endocrine and exocrine components share a location without sharing their destination. Acinar cells contribute enzymes to the pancreatic duct system; duct cells modify the fluid delivered to the intestine. Islet cells release hormones to adjacent capillaries. Destruction of ducts can therefore impair delivery of digestive juice without necessarily abolishing islet hormone release. Conversely, selective damage to beta cells can interfere with glucose regulation while leaving substantial digestive function intact. The anatomical name of an organ is not a reliable classification of all its secretions.

Endocrine cells often lie close to richly supplied capillaries. Rapid exchange between extracellular fluid and vascular plasma supports delivery of secreted molecules and also allows the gland to detect circulating stimuli. Capillary permeability varies by tissue, and a thin diffusion distance facilitates transfer. Fenestrated capillaries are common in endocrine organs but should not be treated as an absolute defining characteristic. The pancreatic islet, the pituitary and the adrenal cortex illustrate the advantage of extensive vascular contact, even though their arrangement and products differ.

Specialized glands are not the only sources of hormones. The heart can report mechanical stretch by releasing natriuretic peptides; adipose tissue can communicate aspects of stored energy through leptin; kidney cells produce erythropoietin in relation to oxygen availability. These examples change the interpretation of endocrine organization from a short list of classical glands to a distributed physiological communication system. The detailed organ-specific mechanisms belong to later chapters. The shared principle is that the secretory cell converts information about its surroundings into a molecule capable of changing behavior elsewhere.

An endocrine message need not enter the entire systemic circulation before reaching its immediate target. A portal vascular arrangement carries blood from one capillary bed to a second capillary bed before it returns to the general circulation. Hypothalamic regulatory hormones delivered to the anterior pituitary exploit this arrangement. A small local release can reach responsive pituitary cells at effective concentrations without requiring the same concentration throughout the whole body. In an examination question, disruption of portal vessels should therefore be distinguished from a general failure of the pituitary to synthesize its own hormones.

Chemical classes and synthesis of hormones

The classification of hormones is useful because molecular structure constrains how a signal is manufactured, stored and transported. Peptide and protein hormones consist of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Their production begins with gene expression and translation. Many enter the endoplasmic reticulum as precursors containing a signal sequence. Removal of this sequence, folding and further processing generate forms that travel through the Golgi apparatus into secretory vesicles. Proteolytic processing may release biologically active peptides from larger precursors. Distinguishing precursor abundance from active hormone abundance is particularly important when interpreting experiments that disrupt processing.

Insulin provides a concrete illustration. Preproinsulin contains a targeting sequence that directs synthesis to the secretory pathway. After removal of the signal sequence, proinsulin folds and forms disulfide bonds. Cleavage produces mature insulin and C-peptide, which are released together from secretory granules. Their different clearance properties mean that their peripheral concentrations are not necessarily identical despite equimolar release. This is one reason why measuring the circulating concentration of a hormone does not directly reveal the exact quantity released by its source.

Steroid hormones originate from cholesterol. Unlike peptides, their individual structures are not specified as directly translated gene products. Genes encode the enzymes and transport proteins that enable successive transformations of cholesterol. In many steroidogenic tissues the movement of cholesterol into mitochondria and its conversion to pregnenolone provide an early regulated step. Different patterns of enzyme expression determine which products accumulate. The same initial substrate can therefore yield distinct final signals in different endocrine tissues. A mutation affecting one enzyme may reduce a downstream product while causing accumulation or diversion of upstream intermediates. Such reasoning will become particularly important when studying adrenal and gonadal physiology.

Steroids are generally synthesized in response to demand and are not stored in large secretory vesicles containing finished hormone in the same manner as peptides. Nevertheless, cells can store cholesterol esters and regulate availability of precursors. It is misleading to infer that no storage occurs anywhere in steroidogenic cells. The relevant distinction is between the reserve of finished hormone and the reserve of material from which that hormone is synthesized. Steroid output depends on biosynthetic capacity and precursor delivery, whereas a peptide-secreting cell can often release preformed hormone rapidly even before new translation contributes to replenishment.

Amino-acid-derived hormones demonstrate why one must not use a single rule for every small molecule. Catecholamines originate from tyrosine and are relatively water-soluble; they are accumulated in secretory vesicles and released by regulated exocytosis. Thyroid hormones also originate from tyrosine-derived residues, but their iodinated structure gives them different physicochemical behaviour. They are produced within thyroglobulin and stored in extracellular follicular colloid before mobilization. Circulating thyroid hormones are largely protein-bound and access cells through transport mechanisms. Their major effects involve nuclear receptor regulation. The shared precursor therefore does not imply shared storage, transport or receptor location.

Melatonin is derived from tryptophan through serotonin-related metabolism. Its production is closely linked to temporal regulation, providing a bridge from chemical classification to circadian physiology. Lipid-derived local messengers, such as many prostaglandins, can be generated from membrane-associated fatty acid precursors and often act near their source. This helps distinguish endocrine steroids from the wider category of biologically active lipid signals. Not every lipid-derived messenger is a classical circulating steroid hormone.

Precursor processing and stored secretory capacity

A useful way to reason about peptide hormones is to follow one molecule from gene expression to the extracellular environment. An mRNA directs synthesis of a polypeptide. For many secreted peptides an amino-terminal signal sequence directs the ribosome-associated nascent chain to the endoplasmic reticulum. The signal sequence is removed as the product enters the secretory pathway, leaving a prohormone where further maturation is required. Folding, disulfide bond formation and transport through the Golgi apparatus prepare the molecule for later release. Individual hormones differ in the exact sequence of processing steps, so the general model should not be confused with a universal single molecular route.

Proteolytic cleavage is particularly important because a precursor can contain sequences that have different final fates. Proinsulin is a classic example: processing removes the connecting peptide while preserving the disulfide-linked insulin chains. A cell with defective processing can contain plenty of precursor but release less mature active hormone. Measuring only the transcript would then overestimate effective endocrine function. An antibody that binds precursor and product may likewise produce a different conclusion from an assay designed to detect the mature peptide specifically.

Storage in dense-core secretory granules gives peptide-producing cells an important physiological capacity. They can accumulate a pool of mature material before the next stimulus arrives. When cytosolic calcium rises in a regulated secretory cell, fusion of suitable granules with the plasma membrane can discharge stored contents. This is not equivalent to making new hormone at that moment. If translation is blocked briefly, existing vesicles may still support an acute secretory response. Continued stimulation eventually reveals the need for replenishment. Thus the effect of a perturbation can depend strongly on how long after treatment the experiment is performed.

A prohormone may be processed differently in different cell types. The identity of the precursor gene alone is not enough to predict the entire collection of biologically active products. Cell-specific processing enzymes, secretory pathways and maturation conditions contribute to the actual molecular output. This principle is important when interpreting expression studies that show the same transcript in two tissues: identical transcript sequences are compatible with differences in the mature products that reach target cells.

Steroid-producing cells face a different organization problem. Steroid hormones are generated enzymatically from cholesterol. Cholesterol may be acquired from circulating lipoproteins, synthesized by the cell, or mobilized from intracellular ester stores. Transport of cholesterol into the appropriate mitochondrial compartment supports the initial formation of pregnenolone, and additional reactions in mitochondria and smooth endoplasmic reticulum determine which final steroid is produced. The distribution of enzymes matters more than the mere presence of cholesterol. Two cells with comparable substrate pools can release different products because they carry different sets of steroidogenic enzymes.

An acute steroidogenic stimulus may increase substrate availability and enzyme activity, whereas longer stimulation may alter the expression of the steroidogenic machinery. In contrast with the large secretory granule pool typical of many peptides, newly synthesized steroid generally leaves the producing cell without long-term storage as finished hormone in dense-core granules. It is therefore misleading to interpret a sudden rise in steroid output as necessarily reflecting the exocytosis of previously accumulated final product. Cholesterol ester droplets are stores of precursor material, not stores of the final hormone.

Amino-acid-derived hormones demonstrate why classification by precursor cannot replace classification by physical behavior. Catecholamines are products of tyrosine metabolism; their polar groups make their aqueous behavior and regulated vesicular release more like those of many peptides than those of steroids. Thyroid hormones are also derived from tyrosine residues, but iodination and coupling produce molecules that circulate predominantly with binding proteins and act through intracellular thyroid hormone receptors. Their formation in thyroglobulin and extracellular colloid storage are further exceptions to the simplistic idea that every hormone is stored inside the cell or released immediately after synthesis. The thyroid-specific details will be developed in 7.J.04.

Chemistry therefore imposes constraints without uniquely determining every feature of the endocrine life cycle. The student should classify an unfamiliar hormone using at least three separate criteria: molecular origin, transport and storage behavior, and receptor location. One answer may place a hormone among amino-acid derivatives while another accurately places it with lipophilic signals. These are not contradictory answers when they respond to different classification criteria.

Regulated secretion and the meaning of a measured hormone

The secretion rate of a hormone is the amount entering a specified compartment per unit time. A concentration is the amount present per unit volume. Although the two are related, they are not interchangeable. A high concentration might reflect increased secretion, reduced removal or changes in distribution and binding. Conversely a hormone that is secreted rapidly but removed just as rapidly may have a modest measured concentration. This distinction is a foundation of quantitative endocrine physiology.

In peptide-secreting cells, a stimulus can alter membrane electrical activity or an intracellular signalling pathway, leading to an increase in cytosolic calcium and fusion of secretory vesicles with the plasma membrane. The short time between stimulation and secretion is possible because mature products have already been packaged. A prolonged stimulus may initially empty readily releasable vesicles faster than they can be replaced, followed by slower recruitment or biosynthesis. A biphasic secretion curve may therefore reflect several processes, and its shape alone cannot identify which particular cellular pool is responsible.

Endocrine stimuli can be grouped by the origin of the information. Humoral stimuli arise from the composition of extracellular fluid, such as glucose or calcium concentration. Neural stimuli arise from neuronal activity, and hormonal stimuli arise from signals produced by other endocrine cells. In a functioning animal these inputs can converge on a single gland. An experiment that isolates one input is useful for determining its contribution but does not establish that all other regulatory influences are absent in vivo.

Release frequently varies with time. Pulsatile secretion alternates relatively high output with intervals of lower output. A single blood sample collected during a trough can be very different from a sample obtained near a pulse peak. If the samples from two individuals are collected at random phases, their measured values can differ even if their daily production is similar. Repeated sampling is therefore central to investigating pulses. A periodic change in peripheral concentration is suggestive of a secretion rhythm but clearance and sampling limitations must be considered before inferring pulse amplitude.

Distinguishing hormone production from hormone output

A tissue may produce a large amount of hormone over an hour while showing little net change in its hormone content. The apparent paradox is resolved by considering the tissue as a reservoir. Its content rises with synthesis and falls with secretion and intracellular degradation. When synthesis replaces what leaves, the tissue content can remain stable despite high turnover. Conversely, a high tissue content may represent a large stored pool with relatively little release. Measurements of tissue extracts and circulating levels therefore answer different questions.

Consider an isolated peptide-producing tissue supplied with a constant nutrient medium. A stimulus increases the amount of hormone collected in the surrounding medium during the first five minutes. If the tissue stores mature hormone, a short observation interval does not prove an increase in gene expression. One could measure intracellular content before and after stimulation, compare responses in matched control tissue and examine whether repeated stimulation exhausts the rapid response. Those observations help distinguish a change in secretion from a change in synthesis, although no single measurement necessarily excludes changes in degradation.

A simple mass balance is useful. Suppose an illustrative gland initially contains 300 arbitrary units of mature peptide. During an observation period it synthesizes 40 units, secretes 70 units and degrades 10 units internally. The final tissue content is 260 units: 300 plus 40 minus 70 minus 10. A second gland begins with the same content and secretes 70 units but synthesizes 90 and degrades 20; its final content remains 300. Both glands generate the same extracellular output despite different tissue behavior. The numbers are hypothetical and are not biological reference values.

Some endocrine cells respond to a substance whose concentration the hormone will ultimately regulate. A fall in extracellular ionized calcium can stimulate parathyroid hormone release; a rise in blood glucose can stimulate insulin secretion in pancreatic beta cells. A hormonal stimulus is different: it conveys an instruction from another endocrine tissue. A neural stimulus can convey information through an electrical and synaptic route before the final hormone is discharged. A gland may be influenced by all three kinds of input simultaneously, and naming its dominant stimulus does not imply that other regulation is absent.

When interpreting a secretion experiment, pay attention to the reference variable. If extracellular glucose is maintained at a fixed concentration by the experimenter, the usual physiological consequence of insulin secretion cannot lower glucose as it would in an intact animal. This intervention separates the response of the secreting cell from the feedback consequences of that response. An experiment that preserves the whole animal instead gives information about the coupled system. Both approaches are valuable but their results cannot be substituted for one another without considering the missing feedback connections.

Circulation, free hormone and protein binding

The aqueous environment of plasma can carry water-soluble hormones relatively readily. Hydrophobic hormones often associate reversibly with plasma proteins. Binding reduces the fraction immediately free in solution and can influence distribution and removal. A binding protein is distinct from a receptor: its transport function does not imply that it initiates the cellular response normally produced by a signalling receptor.

Let F be the free concentration, B the concentration of hormone bound to a transport protein, and T the total hormone concentration, all in the same molar units. By conservation, T equals F plus B. The free fraction is F divided by T. In an illustrative sample with total hormone of 100 nanomoles per litre and free concentration of 2 nanomoles per litre, 98 nanomoles per litre is bound and the free fraction is 0.02 or 2 percent. A second hypothetical sample with total 150 and the same free concentration contains 148 bound, giving a free fraction of approximately 1.33 percent. The larger total concentration does not demonstrate a larger immediately available free concentration.

If the binding sites are far from saturation and the free carrier concentration is approximately constant, a simple equilibrium model gives B approximately equal to F multiplied by the free carrier concentration divided by its dissociation constant. Increasing the carrier concentration tends to increase binding at a given free concentration. However, when a binding protein rises in vivo, the response is dynamic. Free hormone may initially fall, changing feedback and clearance; a regulated system may subsequently reach a new steady state with an increased total concentration but a free concentration close to its original value. The precise outcome depends on the feedback system, binding affinity and rates of synthesis and removal.

The free-hormone principle is a useful starting point, not an excuse to ignore cellular transport and tissue metabolism. Target cells may express transporters, enzymes that activate or inactivate a ligand, and receptor systems that influence responsiveness. A change in circulating free concentration may not translate equally into a change in signalling in every tissue. This is especially important for thyroid hormone, where local conversion and transporter expression help determine intracellular exposure. The molecular details of hormone reception are developed in the following chapter.

Binding equilibria, tissue access and measured concentrations

The total concentration measured in plasma includes molecules in several states. Consider a carrier P that reversibly binds a hormone H to form PH. At equilibrium, the dissociation constant is the product of the free concentrations of P and H divided by the concentration of PH. It has units of concentration. A smaller dissociation constant corresponds to stronger binding under the stated conditions. Carrier protein and target receptor are both binding partners, but their roles differ. Carrier binding influences movement and availability; receptor binding can initiate a biological response.

If the concentration of unoccupied carrier sites is much larger than hormone concentration, the free carrier concentration can be treated as approximately constant. Rearranging the dissociation expression gives bound hormone equal to free carrier concentration multiplied by free hormone concentration and divided by the dissociation constant. Total hormone equals free plus bound. The approximate free fraction is consequently one divided by one plus the ratio of free carrier concentration to its dissociation constant. This expression assumes a single carrier class with independent binding sites and negligible depletion of free carrier. It is not a general equation for saturable mixtures of several plasma proteins.

For an illustrative carrier with a free concentration of 100 nM and a dissociation constant of 10 nM, the predicted free fraction is one eleventh, or about 9.1 percent. If unoccupied carrier rises to 200 nM while the dissociation constant is unchanged, the free fraction falls to one twenty-first, about 4.8 percent. Under an immediate closed-pool assumption, total hormone is unchanged and free hormone therefore falls. In a living feedback-controlled system, however, the gland may compensate over time. The final free concentration may recover while total concentration remains elevated. Time after the change in carrier is therefore indispensable information.

With several carrier proteins, each contributes its own bound pool. A relatively abundant weak-binding protein and a less abundant high-affinity protein may both influence the total. At low hormone concentrations, the linear approximation can often describe their combined buffering effects. When binding sites become appreciably occupied, the approximation fails. An additional increment of hormone then tends to produce a larger increment of free concentration because fewer sites are available. Such a nonlinear change should not be confused with a change in the sensitivity of the target cell itself.

The free hormone principle is a useful starting model for many circulating hormones: dissociation from carriers supplies hormone available for access to responsive tissue. Carrier-bound material can serve as a reservoir, replenish the free pool and alter apparent residence time. It is not correct to infer that every bound molecule is permanently inactive or that carriers have no biological importance. Binding is reversible, tissue transport can be regulated, and specific carrier interactions complicate the simplest free-equilibrium picture. Thyroid hormone uptake, for instance, involves membrane transport proteins rather than unrestricted movement through every lipid bilayer.

When two blood samples have identical total concentrations but different binding capacities, they need not represent equal free concentrations. When two samples have equal free concentrations but different total concentrations, the responding tissues may receive comparable effective exposure, provided that access and receptor properties are unchanged. This distinction explains why questions involving binding proteins should explicitly state whether the comparison is immediate or at a compensated steady state.

Blood sampling introduces another layer of interpretation. A concentration measured in venous blood leaving an endocrine organ can exceed that in arterial blood entering it. In a simplified steady-state calculation, the net output equals plasma flow multiplied by the venous minus arterial concentration. With plasma flow 0.2 L/min and concentrations 15 and 5 nmol/L, net output equals 2 nmol/min. This is a net exchange estimate, not necessarily gross synthesis, because the tissue might also remove some hormone. The calculation requires consistent flow and concentration units; mixing whole-blood flow with plasma concentration without considering the cellular fraction can produce a misleading result.

Quantitative clearance and half-life

A simple one-compartment model helps separate secretion from disappearance. Let C be the hormone concentration in nanomoles per litre, V the effective distribution volume in litres, S the secretion rate in nanomoles per minute and CL the metabolic clearance rate in litres per minute. If the compartment mixes rapidly and clearance is linear, the amount in the compartment is VC. Its rate of change is S minus CL multiplied by C. For constant volume the equation is V dC/dt = S − CL C. The units of both terms on the right are nanomoles per minute, which is necessary for conservation.

At steady state dC/dt is zero. Therefore S = CL C and the steady concentration is S/CL. An illustrative animal has secretion of 12 nanomoles per minute and clearance of 3 litres per minute. The model predicts a steady concentration of 4 nanomoles per litre. If clearance falls to 1.5 litres per minute while secretion remains fixed, the predicted new concentration is 8 nanomoles per litre. This calculation demonstrates that a twofold concentration rise is not sufficient evidence of a twofold increase in glandular output. In a real feedback system the secretion rate may subsequently change, so fixed secretion is an explicit modelling assumption.

If secretion ceases at time zero, the model becomes dC/dt = −kC, where k = CL/V and has units of inverse minutes. Separation of variables and integration yield C(t) = C(0) exp(−kt). Half-life is the time required for the concentration to fall to half its initial value, so exp(−kt½) = 1/2 and t½ = ln(2)/k. If V is 6 litres and CL is 3 litres per minute, k equals 0.5 per minute and the half-life is about 1.386 minutes. After two half-lives one quarter of the initial concentration remains in this idealized case.

Clearance represents the effective volume cleared of hormone per unit time; it is not the physical amount of blood removed. Enzymatic degradation, hepatic metabolism, renal elimination and receptor-associated uptake can contribute differently for different hormones. Real hormones may display multiple phases of disappearance because of exchange among compartments, reversible binding, nonlinear removal or continued production. A straight line on a semilogarithmic concentration-time plot supports an exponential decay over the observed interval, but does not prove that the organism has only one anatomical compartment.

Steady states, pulse filtering and clearance experiments

A blood concentration is a stock per unit volume, whereas secretion is a flux of material per unit time. Their relationship is established by conservation of matter. Assume a single well-mixed effective volume V and linear clearance CL. The hormone amount is V times concentration C. When V is constant, its rate of change is secretion rate S minus CL times C. Dividing by V gives the rate of change of concentration. At steady state this change is zero, so C equals S divided by CL. Clearance has dimensions of volume per time, even though no literal volume of completely cleaned plasma need exist.

Consider an illustrative hormone with S equal to 12 nmol/min and CL equal to 3 L/min. Its steady concentration is 4 nmol/L. A reduction in clearance to 1.5 L/min with the same secretion leads to a new steady concentration of 8 nmol/L. The same increase in concentration would occur if secretion doubled while clearance stayed at 3 L/min. Concentration alone cannot distinguish these two causal possibilities. Measuring disappearance following cessation of secretion, or comparing a known controlled input with steady concentration, supplies additional evidence.

The first-order rate constant k equals CL divided by V. When secretion stops, the fractional decline per unit time is proportional to concentration, giving exponential disappearance. If V is 12 L and CL is 3 L/min, k is 0.25 per minute and the half-life is the natural logarithm of two divided by k, approximately 2.77 minutes. A change in distribution volume can alter half-life even when clearance is unchanged. Conversely, equal half-lives do not guarantee equal clearance when distribution volumes differ. These distinctions are especially important in quantitative questions that ask whether a concentration-time curve reveals removal efficiency.

An endocrine pulse is not identical to the concentration peak recorded in blood. Imagine a brief release of molecules at regular intervals. Each release increases the amount present, while continuous elimination reduces the amount between releases. If half-life is very short relative to the interval, each pulse falls nearly to baseline before the next. If half-life is long, substantial residual hormone remains and the next pulse is superimposed on that residual concentration. The same secretory rhythm may therefore generate either sharply defined concentration peaks or a relatively smooth circulating curve.

An illustrative calculation makes this idea concrete. Assume pulses add 8 nM instantaneously, are separated by one half-life and there is no basal secretion. After the first pulse the concentration is 8 nM; just before the next it is 4 nM. The next pulse brings the value to 12 nM, which declines to 6 nM before the third. After many pulses the steady repeating peak becomes 16 nM because the next peak equals 8 plus one half of the preceding peak. The trough becomes 8 nM. This is an idealized model, not a description of any particular gland.

The model also explains why increasing sampling frequency cannot solve every problem of pulse identification. Frequent measurements reduce the risk of missing peaks, but elimination and distribution can still smooth the original secretory event. Assay noise can produce apparent peaks, while overlapping pulses may appear as a single broad elevation. In intact endocrine physiology, concentration time series contain information about secretion, transport and removal together. Inferring the original secretory process requires assumptions about those processes or independent measurements that constrain them.

Hormone removal is not always equivalent to irreversible destruction. A molecule may be converted to a less active form, conjugated for elimination, taken up and degraded within cells, or converted to another active hormone. Local conversion of thyroid hormone is a standard example in which disappearance of one molecular species can accompany formation of another biologically active species. The kidney and liver contribute to removal of many hormones, but the balance of organ contributions differs greatly by molecule. A disappearance curve for one measured analyte cannot automatically specify the fate of all its biological activity.

Feedback, rhythms and target response

Negative feedback occurs when a downstream consequence reduces the process that initially generated it. Consider a two-stage endocrine axis in which signal A stimulates production of hormone B, and B inhibits A. A primary failure of the B-producing gland reduces B; the resulting loss of feedback can increase A if upstream function remains intact. In contrast, loss of A reduces stimulation of the downstream gland and tends to lower B as well. Measuring the pair can therefore help locate a disturbance more effectively than measuring the final hormone alone. The interpretation assumes the normal feedback relationships, appropriate sample timing and absence of other confounding inputs.

Not every negative feedback loop uses a second gland. When extracellular calcium falls, a calcium-sensing endocrine cell can increase secretion of a hormone that promotes restoration of calcium. Rising calcium then suppresses that secretion. The regulated variable rather than the secreted messenger closes the loop. Distinguish these arrangements when drawing feedback diagrams: the direction of an arrow indicates a regulatory influence, not necessarily the path of blood circulation. A positive arrow represents stimulation and a negative arrow inhibition; neither alone identifies whether the mechanism is endocrine or neural.

Positive feedback instead reinforces a change. In the reproductive axis, sustained estradiol exposure in an appropriate physiological setting contributes to the preovulatory LH surge. The same steroid can participate in inhibitory feedback under other conditions. A molecule should not be classified as permanently a positive-feedback hormone. The sign depends on the circuit and physiological state. Positive feedback is normally constrained by events or mechanisms that terminate amplification; otherwise an initially small perturbation could produce uncontrolled change.

Circadian rhythms are endogenous rhythms with periods of approximately twenty-four hours, normally aligned to environmental timing cues. A circadian concentration pattern may coexist with faster pulses. The phase and amplitude of rhythms can shift with sleep, feeding and other inputs. Endocrine experiments comparing groups should therefore control sampling time rather than assume that a morning concentration and an evening concentration represent equivalent physiological conditions. A time series averaged across a day may conceal functionally relevant differences in phase and pulse pattern.

Target cells can also change their sensitivity. A hormone receptor recognises its ligand, but occupancy alone does not guarantee a proportional physiological effect. Under a simple single-site equilibrium assumption the fraction occupied is F/(Kd + F), where F is free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant. If F equals Kd, occupancy is one half. If F increases to nine times Kd, occupancy is nine tenths. A downstream response may reach its maximum before all receptors are occupied, and receptor number or intracellular coupling may change independently. Hence binding affinity, sensitivity and maximal response are different properties.

Hormones may interact antagonistically, permissively or synergistically. Insulin and glucagon can oppose one another in control of hepatic glucose output, without necessarily binding the same receptor. Permissiveness occurs when one signal allows a second signal to evoke its usual effect, for example by sustaining receptor expression or cellular metabolic capacity. Synergy refers to a combined effect greater than an explicitly defined additive expectation. To demonstrate synergy experimentally, compare each hormone alone and the combination against a shared baseline, and choose an appropriate response scale. Simply observing that two hormones together produce a larger effect than either alone does not demonstrate synergy.

Dynamic regulation and causal localization

A negative feedback circuit is identified by its overall sign, not by the presence of a single inhibitory arrow. A stimulus may increase hormone release and that hormone may increase an effector response. If the effector response reduces the original stimulus, the complete circuit is negative even though several of its individual links are stimulatory. Insulin secretion in response to elevated glucose illustrates the distinction. The rise of glucose stimulates insulin, and insulin-dependent changes in tissue metabolism tend to reduce glucose. The regulated variable is glucose, not the concentration of insulin itself.

A multigland endocrine axis has a different architecture. An upstream signal stimulates a peripheral gland, whose product may inhibit upstream signals. Within an idealized two-tier system, a primary peripheral gland failure decreases its product despite elevated upstream stimulation. A lesion reducing upstream secretion tends to lower both hormones, provided the peripheral gland depends on that stimulation. Autonomous secretion from the peripheral gland may elevate its product while suppressing the upstream hormone. These patterns are useful predictions of a model; actual interpretation must also consider timing, assay limitations, drug exposure, and partial rather than complete defects.

Distinguish an upstream hormone that is normal in absolute concentration from one that is appropriate for the physiological context. If the peripheral signal is abnormally low and negative feedback is intact, a vigorous upstream response would normally be expected. A value within a reference interval can therefore be inappropriately low. In an examination dataset, the reference interval may be insufficient unless both hormones and the relevant controlled variable are considered together. Conversely, a large upstream concentration can indicate a compensatory response rather than excessive action at the peripheral target.

Feedback also acts over time. An abrupt increase in a peripheral hormone need not immediately decrease its own upstream stimulus; the effects of sensing, intracellular signaling, gene regulation and secretion may unfold on different timescales. An initially elevated upstream signal together with elevated peripheral product may be consistent with a transient response even when the eventual steady-state pattern is negative feedback. A time course allows the learner to distinguish a genuine change of regulatory sign from a normal lag in correction.

Positive feedback differs from a simple sustained high concentration. A change in the initiating variable causes responses that further increase the initiating variable, creating amplification over successive steps. Such circuits usually participate in bounded physiological events, because prolonged amplification without an endpoint would not stabilize the regulated state. During parturition, increased cervical stimulation can promote oxytocin release and uterine contraction, which in turn increases cervical stimulation. Delivery removes the initiating mechanical conditions. This example illustrates why termination conditions must accompany a description of positive feedback.

A daily rhythm and a secretory pulse are not alternatives. A hormone may show brief pulses whose amplitude or frequency varies with time of day. A circadian rhythm is an approximately twenty-four-hour biological oscillation, whereas ultradian rhythms have periods shorter than a day. An animal's feeding, activity and sleep pattern can influence both. A single high value may represent normal sampling near a peak; a single low value may represent the interval between pulses. Controlled comparisons should therefore use matched physiological conditions and sampling times.

Two stimuli may act on the same target through different receptors. To decide whether their interaction is synergistic on an additive response scale, calculate each increment relative to the same baseline. Suppose control response is 20 units, hormone A yields 30, hormone B yields 26, and both together yield 39. The separate increments are 10 and 6, so an additive prediction is 36. The observed value exceeds it by 3 units. Calling that difference a demonstrated interaction in a real experiment would additionally require replication and statistical assessment. An effect larger than either single treatment is not by itself evidence of synergy.

Permissiveness is best understood by changing the order of experimental treatments. Hormone A may have little direct effect on a particular measured response but may increase the receptor abundance or other response capacity for hormone B over a longer interval. Removing A can then reduce the response to B even when B concentration is unchanged. An immediate simultaneous exposure may fail to reproduce this dependence if the required alterations take time. Antagonism can occur when two hormonal responses have opposite effects on a shared physiological variable without the hormones directly competing for the same receptor. The direction of the net response depends on the state of the tissue and the strengths of the opposing pathways.

Comparative perspective and endocrine integration

The principles of secretion, recognition and feedback extend beyond mammals, although the glands and molecules involved vary. Insects use circulating chemical signals, including ecdysteroids and juvenile hormone, to coordinate developmental transitions. The significance of a hormone concentration depends on the stage of development and the competence of the target tissues. A signal that promotes one transition in a particular developmental context does not necessarily produce the same outcome at another stage. The comparison reinforces the general principle that hormone action depends jointly on the signal and the state of the receiver.

An endocrine system can coordinate both slow changes in growth and rapid changes in metabolism. Neither the speed nor the duration of an effect alone distinguishes endocrine from neural communication. Release kinetics, distribution, receptor type, intracellular signal processing and removal all contribute. The useful way to approach an endocrine problem is to identify the information source, the stimulus controlling release, the route of transport, the responsive target and the feedback consequences. This establishes the framework needed to examine the molecular mechanisms of hormone action in the next node.

Original reasoning exercises

  1. Why can blood carrying a hormone reach a cell without evoking a direct response?
    Explanation

    The cell may lack the appropriate receptor or functional coupling machinery; delivery is not equivalent to recognition.

  2. Which route is represented by a hypothalamic neuron secreting a messenger into portal blood?
    Explanation

    Neuroendocrine signalling, because the source is neuronal and the route vascular.

  3. A peptide precursor accumulates but its mature hormone decreases after loss of a processing enzyme. What step is affected?
    Explanation

    Post-translational maturation rather than necessarily transcription; precursor synthesis can remain intact.

  4. A hormone with constant secretion of 10 nmol/min has clearance 2 L/min. Predict steady concentration.
    Explanation

    5 nmol/L, by dividing secretion rate by clearance under linear steady-state assumptions.

  5. An initial concentration of 80 units declines for three half-lives after secretion stops. What remains?
    Explanation

    10 units: 80 × (1/2)^3, assuming first-order loss.

  6. Total hormone rises from 100 to 200 nM while free hormone remains 2 nM. What happens to the free fraction?
    Explanation

    It decreases from 2% to 1%; total concentration is not an adequate surrogate for the free concentration.

  7. In a simple axis A stimulates B and B inhibits A. B is low and A is elevated. What lesion is consistent with the pattern?
    Explanation

    Primary failure of the B-producing gland, assuming an intact upstream response and no confounding factors.

  8. A hormone's Kd is 5 nM. At free concentration 15 nM, what is fractional receptor occupancy?
    Explanation

    15/(5+15)=0.75, for equilibrium independent identical sites; not necessarily 75% of maximal response.

  9. Two signals independently raise a baseline of 10 to 14 and 16. Their combination yields 20. Is that evidence for greater-than-additive synergy on this scale?
    Explanation

    No. The individual increments are 4 and 6, so the additive prediction is 20. A larger effect than either alone is insufficient.

  10. Acid evokes pancreatic secretion after denervation. Does that rule out every neural contribution in the intact animal?
    Explanation

    No. It establishes that the interrupted neural route is unnecessary for that response under the tested conditions, not that all neural regulation is absent.

CSIR-NET Part B practice

  1. A pancreatic acinar cell directs enzyme secretion into a duct. Which description fits?

    1. Exocrine secretion
    2. Endocrine secretion
    3. Autocrine secretion
    4. Neuroendocrine secretion
    Answer and explanation

    A. The defining feature is delivery through a duct to a lumen. A secreted product is not automatically endocrine, and no neuronal source or self-target is specified.

  2. Which feature makes a distant cell a direct hormone target?

    1. An identical embryonic origin
    2. An appropriate functional receptor
    3. A location near every gland
    4. A high metabolic rate
    Answer and explanation

    B. A responsive receptor and coupling machinery confer competence. Shared origin, proximity and metabolic activity do not establish specific recognition.

  3. What is the main role of a preprohormone signal peptide?

    1. Activating the mature hormone receptor
    2. Binding albumin in circulation
    3. Directing entry into the secretory pathway
    4. Terminating negative feedback
    Answer and explanation

    C. A signal sequence targets the nascent secretory protein to the ER. It is commonly removed before mature hormone release; the other choices describe different processes.

  4. Which substance is a steroid precursor?

    1. Glycogen
    2. Tyrosine alone
    3. Ribosomal RNA
    4. Cholesterol
    Answer and explanation

    D. Steroidogenesis uses cholesterol as its substrate. Tyrosine is a precursor of catecholamines and thyroid hormones, not of steroid hormones.

  5. Which amino-acid-derived hormone behaves predominantly as a carrier-bound, intracellular-receptor signal?

    1. Triiodothyronine
    2. Epinephrine
    3. Norepinephrine
    4. Dopamine
    Answer and explanation

    A. T3 has transport and receptor properties distinct from the catecholamines listed, despite their shared amino-acid origin.

  6. A peptide stored in a granule is rapidly released following a rise in intracellular calcium. What process is most directly implicated?

    1. DNA replication
    2. Regulated exocytosis
    3. Cholesterol esterification
    4. Nuclear receptor binding
    Answer and explanation

    B. Calcium can promote fusion of secretory granules with the plasma membrane. Neither new DNA nor immediate synthesis of steroid precursor is required for discharge of this stored peptide.

  7. Which is the predominant storage site for thyroid hormone precursor in a follicle?

    1. Mitochondrial matrix
    2. Nuclear chromatin
    3. Extracellular colloid
    4. Synaptic cleft
    Answer and explanation

    C. Iodinated thyroglobulin is stored in follicular colloid. This extracellular storage is a notable endocrine exception.

  8. Which example is neuroendocrine?

    1. A duct cell delivering bicarbonate to intestine
    2. A muscle fiber releasing heat
    3. A receptor binding insulin
    4. A hypothalamic neuron releasing a regulatory peptide into portal blood
    Answer and explanation

    D. Neuroendocrine secretion originates in a neuron and reaches target tissue by vascular transport.

  9. What is the free fraction if total hormone is 100 nM and unbound hormone is 4 nM?

    1. 4%
    2. 25%
    3. 96%
    4. 104%
    Answer and explanation

    A. The free fraction is free divided by total, 4/100 = 0.04. Bound concentration is 96 nM, not the free fraction.

  10. Clearance expressed as plasma volume per unit time can have which unit?

    1. nmol/L
    2. L/min
    3. min/L
    4. nmol/min
    Answer and explanation

    B. Clearance is a volume divided by time. Concentration is nmol/L and secretion rate is amount per time.

  11. Which event is a typical humoral stimulus?

    1. TSH stimulating a thyroid cell
    2. A motor axon depolarizing muscle
    3. Low extracellular calcium stimulating parathyroid cells
    4. A steroid entering its target nucleus
    Answer and explanation

    C. The calcium concentration itself is the chemical variable sensed in the extracellular fluid. TSH is a hormonal input and motor axon activity is neural.

  12. Which describes an ultradian rhythm?

    1. A rhythm with a period of several years
    2. A one-time change
    3. Exactly one annual event
    4. A repeating pattern with a period shorter than 24 hours
    Answer and explanation

    D. Ultradian rhythms recur faster than the daily circadian cycle; not every one-time concentration fluctuation is rhythmic.

  13. Which feature defines negative feedback in a complete circuit?

    1. The output tends to oppose the initiating change
    2. Every individual link must be inhibitory
    3. All concentrations remain exactly constant
    4. The final hormone never changes
    Answer and explanation

    A. The overall loop opposes the perturbation, even when intermediate links stimulate each other. Physiological variables can fluctuate.

  14. A signal acts on the same cell that released it. What term applies?

    1. Endocrine only
    2. Autocrine
    3. Exocrine
    4. Portal
    Answer and explanation

    B. Autocrine action targets the releasing cell itself. Paracrine action instead targets nearby cells.

  15. Which is an example of a permissive effect?

    1. Two hormones bind the same antibody
    2. Two hormones independently undergo renal clearance
    3. One hormone increases the target's capacity to respond to another
    4. A hormone is secreted into a duct
    Answer and explanation

    C. Permissiveness changes responsiveness to a second hormone. Direct receptor competition or sharing an elimination route is not required.

  16. At fixed secretion and linear clearance, doubling clearance does what to steady concentration?

    1. Doubles it
    2. Leaves it unchanged
    3. Quadruples it
    4. Halves it
    Answer and explanation

    D. Steady concentration equals secretion divided by clearance. Doubling the denominator halves the result.

  17. Which statement about hormone carrier proteins is correct?

    1. They can reversibly bind hormone and buffer the free pool
    2. They are always target receptors
    3. They permanently destroy every bound hormone
    4. They make all hormones insoluble
    Answer and explanation

    A. Reversible binding supplies a reservoir. Carriers differ from signaling receptors and need not chemically transform the ligand.

  18. Two hormones have opposing actions on the same controlled variable. Which term fits their functional relationship?

    1. Permissive only
    2. Antagonistic
    3. Autocrine only
    4. Irreversible
    Answer and explanation

    B. Functional antagonism refers to opposing effects. The hormones can act through entirely distinct receptors.

  19. Which observation is most directly evidence of secretion rather than gene transcription?

    1. An increase in DNA copy number
    2. An increase in nuclear size
    3. A rise in mature peptide collected from the extracellular medium
    4. A decrease in ribosomal RNA
    Answer and explanation

    C. Extracellular mature hormone reports net release into the medium, subject to degradation and sampling conditions. It does not directly measure transcription.

  20. Which system is a distributed endocrine source rather than a classical dedicated gland?

    1. Pancreatic duct lumen
    2. A dead erythrocyte
    3. A collagen fibril
    4. Adipose tissue releasing leptin
    Answer and explanation

    D. Adipose tissue contains cells capable of endocrine secretion. A lumen and structural extracellular fibers are not endocrine cells.

CSIR-NET Part C practice

  1. Two animals have secretion rates of 18 nmol/min. Clearance is 3 L/min in A and 6 L/min in B. Which steady-state concentrations follow?

    1. A 3, B 6 nM
    2. A 6, B 3 nM
    3. A 18, B 18 nM
    4. A 54, B 108 nM
    Answer and explanation

    B. Using C=S/CL, A=18/3=6 and B=18/6=3 nmol/L. Multiplying secretion by clearance is dimensionally invalid.

  2. A hormone starts at 96 nM. Secretion stops and the half-life is 12 min. What remains after 36 min?

    1. 48 nM
    2. 24 nM
    3. 12 nM
    4. 0 nM
    Answer and explanation

    C. There are three half-lives: 96→48→24→12. First-order decay approaches zero rather than reaching it in a fixed number of half-lives.

  3. A gland releases hormone into plasma flowing at 0.5 L/min. Arterial and venous levels are 8 and 14 nM. Estimate net output.

    1. 3 nmol/min
    2. 11 nmol/min
    3. 12 nmol/min
    4. 28 nmol/min
    Answer and explanation

    A. Net flux = flow × difference = 0.5×6=3 nmol/min. The mean concentration does not give net production.

  4. A peptide gland is exposed to a short translation inhibitor treatment, then stimulated. A transient near-normal response is followed by progressive failure during repeated stimuli. What is most consistent?

    1. The peptide must be a steroid
    2. Feedback is permanently positive
    3. The receptor has no ligand
    4. Stored granules support initial release but replenishment is impaired
    Answer and explanation

    D. The time dependence separates preformed hormone from ongoing synthesis. The experiment does not identify the sign of feedback or change molecular class.

  5. A new carrier is introduced to an isolated plasma sample. Total hormone is fixed and available binding capacity increases. What is the expected immediate direction of free hormone?

    1. Increase necessarily
    2. Decrease
    3. No change necessarily
    4. Become equal to total
    Answer and explanation

    B. At equilibrium, extra binding capacity moves hormone from free to bound when total amount is fixed. An intact animal may later compensate, but this sample cannot secrete hormone.

  6. A single carrier has free sites of 30 nM and Kd=10 nM. Under negligible site depletion, what fraction is free?

    1. 75%
    2. 33%
    3. 25%
    4. 10%
    Answer and explanation

    C. The free fraction = 1/(1+30/10)=1/4. The complement, 75%, is bound under the model.

  7. A hormone has V=20 L and CL=4 L/min. Which half-life is predicted by the one-compartment model?

    1. 3.47 min
    2. 5.00 min
    3. 0.14 min
    4. 13.86 min
    Answer and explanation

    A. k=4/20=0.2 min⁻¹; t½=0.693/0.2=3.465 min. V/CL is a time constant, not the half-life.

  8. Two experimental groups have identical steady concentrations. Group B has half the clearance of A. Under the linear model, what must B's secretion be?

    1. Twice A
    2. Four times A
    3. Equal to A
    4. Half A
    Answer and explanation

    D. At steady state S=CL×C, so halving clearance at fixed C requires half the secretion. Equal concentration does not prove equal secretion.

  9. A pulse adds 10 nM every one half-life, with zero basal secretion. At periodic steady state, what is the concentration just before each pulse?

    1. 5 nM
    2. 10 nM
    3. 20 nM
    4. 40 nM
    Answer and explanation

    B. Peak P satisfies P=10+P/2, so P=20; trough=10. The first pulse trough of 5 is not the long-run repeating trough.

  10. A tissue contains 200 units of peptide, synthesizes 60, secretes 80 and degrades 15 during an interval. Final tissue content?

    1. 245
    2. 185
    3. 165
    4. 135
    Answer and explanation

    C. Conservation gives 200+60−80−15=165. Secreted and degraded hormone both leave the tissue's measured pool.

  11. In a two-tier negative feedback axis, peripheral hormone is low and upstream hormone markedly high. Which hypothesis is most consistent, all else equal?

    1. Primary peripheral gland failure
    2. Autonomous peripheral overproduction
    3. Complete upstream failure
    4. Excess replacement of peripheral hormone
    Answer and explanation

    A. Loss of peripheral hormone relieves feedback, increasing upstream drive. Autonomous production and replacement tend to raise the peripheral hormone, while upstream failure reduces drive.

  12. Peripheral hormone rises quickly after a brief stimulus; the upstream hormone declines only later. What can be concluded?

    1. Feedback is necessarily positive
    2. The axis is absent
    3. Peripheral hormone has no receptor
    4. Delayed negative feedback is compatible with the observations
    Answer and explanation

    D. A lag can reflect sensing, signaling and secretion dynamics. Early coexistence of high hormones alone cannot determine the feedback sign.

  13. Control response=8, A=12, B=15, A+B=19. What is the additive-model prediction and interpretation?

    1. 27, antagonism
    2. 19, additive on this scale
    3. 15, synergy
    4. 20, complete inhibition
    Answer and explanation

    B. The increments are 4 and 7; 8+4+7=19. Adding two absolute treatment values double-counts the baseline.

  14. Control response=10, A alone=11, B alone=20, and prolonged A pretreatment followed by B=35. A increases B receptor abundance. Which interpretation best fits?

    1. B is secreted by A's receptor
    2. A and B are always antagonistic
    3. A can enhance responsiveness to B
    4. A's only role is clearance
    Answer and explanation

    C. The receptor finding provides a mechanism for permissiveness. The measured enhancement is not explained solely by A's weak direct action.

  15. A control cell expresses receptor R. A mutant cell lacks R but retains normal downstream machinery. A hormone response returns when a downstream intermediate is supplied directly. What is supported?

    1. The defect lies upstream of the supplied intermediate, consistent with loss of R
    2. The hormone is not present in the experiment
    3. The downstream machinery is destroyed
    4. The cell must be exocrine
    Answer and explanation

    A. Bypassing the receptor restores response, supporting an upstream lesion. It does not alone establish the full molecular pathway.

  16. Following an increase in carrier abundance, an intact animal shows transiently low free hormone, then normal free hormone with high total hormone. What model fits?

    1. Hormone synthesis is impossible
    2. All bound hormone is irreversibly lost
    3. Feedback must be absent
    4. Compensatory regulation restores free concentration
    Answer and explanation

    D. The total concentration may remain higher because more is bound. The time course supports compensation rather than equating total and free concentrations.

  17. An immunoassay detects both prohormone and mature peptide. A processing mutant has normal assay signal but reduced biological response. What should be tested next?

    1. Only DNA content
    2. Mature-hormone-specific concentration and precursor abundance
    3. Blood glucose alone for every hormone
    4. Only gland size
    Answer and explanation

    B. Cross-reactivity can conceal reduced maturation. A discriminating assay addresses the specific hypothesis; size and DNA do not distinguish products.

  18. A hormone has a receptor Kd of 2 nM. Free concentration falls from 6 to 2 nM. How does ideal equilibrium occupancy change?

    1. From 100% to 0%
    2. From 50% to 75%
    3. From 75% to 50%
    4. It remains 75%
    Answer and explanation

    C. Occupancy is C/(Kd+C): 6/8=0.75 and 2/4=0.5. The relationship is not linear in concentration.

  19. Researchers compare hormone concentrations from one group sampled at waking and another sampled late in the evening. A difference appears. What is the best next step?

    1. Repeat with matched sampling times and physiological conditions
    2. Infer gland failure immediately
    3. Ignore circadian timing
    4. Treat both values as peak secretion rates
    Answer and explanation

    A. Timing is a plausible confounder for rhythmic hormones. Matching times or collecting time series better distinguishes group differences from phase differences.

  20. An intestinal chemical stimulus continues to evoke pancreatic secretion after an experimentally isolated neural route is interrupted. An intestinal extract delivered by blood also evokes the response. Which inference is justified?

    1. All neural control is impossible
    2. All intestinal secretions are steroids
    3. The pancreas produces the intestinal signal
    4. A blood-borne intestinal factor can mediate the response under the tested conditions
    Answer and explanation

    D. The observations support a humoral route and show that the interrupted neural route is not required in that preparation. They do not exclude neural contributions in intact physiology.
