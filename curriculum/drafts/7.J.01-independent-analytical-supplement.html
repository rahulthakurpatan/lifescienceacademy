Analytical practice: original CSIR-NET Part C-style problems

All numerical datasets are illustrative teaching models, not published measurements.

  1. An isolated endocrine gland is perfused at 4 mL/min. At baseline the inflow and outflow concentrations are 3 and 9 pmol/mL; after stimulation the flow doubles, and concentrations are 3 and 6 pmol/mL. Which condition has greater net hormone output? What does the result establish?

    Answer and explanation

    Both yield 24 pmol/min. Net output is flow times the outlet-minus-inlet concentration difference: 4×6 versus 8×3. Concentration fell because the outflow was more dilute, not because net release fell. This calculation cannot establish gross synthesis or release if local uptake or degradation changes.

  2. In a pulse-chase study of a peptide-producing cell, labelled preprohormone enters the ER normally, prohormone reaches the Golgi, but label never appears in dense-core granules. External depolarisation raises intracellular calcium normally but does not restore mature peptide release. Localise the most likely defect and suggest one discriminating measurement.

    Answer and explanation

    A sorting or trafficking defect between Golgi and granules is consistent with the observations; a defect in depolarisation is not. Measure mature and precursor peptide in Golgi, granule fractions and extracellular medium, with fraction-purity controls. Constitutive misrouting versus failure of granule formation may produce different extracellular precursor profiles. The data alone do not identify a particular gene.

  3. Cells from a gland release a steroid at baseline. Removing extracellular cholesterol has little immediate effect, whereas inhibition of cholesterol transfer into mitochondria sharply decreases steroid production despite a normal cellular cholesterol store. What step is limiting in this experiment?

    Answer and explanation

    Access of cholesterol to the mitochondrial steroidogenic machinery is limiting under the conditions. Stored esterified cholesterol and other intracellular sources can briefly sustain production after external supply is removed. A large total cholesterol pool does not guarantee substrate delivery to the correct enzyme compartment. This differs from acute exocytosis of preformed peptide hormone.

  4. A purified hormone from an unknown gland is a tyrosine derivative. It binds plasma carrier proteins and requires a transporter for efficient cellular uptake. A second tyrosine derivative is stored in secretory vesicles and evokes rapid receptor-dependent changes at the plasma membrane. Identify plausible classes and explain why the precursor is insufficient to predict mechanism.

    Answer and explanation

    Thyroid hormone and catecholamine respectively are plausible classes. Iodination and coupling of tyrosyl residues generate thyroid hormones with hydrophobic properties, whereas catecholamines are small polar amines with distinct handling. The precursor amino acid alone predicts neither solubility, transport, receptor location nor the route of secretion.

  5. A researcher places a hormone-producing cell population on one side of a permeable membrane and reporter cells on the other, with no direct contact. Reporter activation disappears when a neutralising antibody to the secreted ligand is present. Is this proof of endocrine signalling in the animal?

    Answer and explanation

    It supports soluble ligand-mediated communication in the culture, and the neutralisation strengthens attribution to that ligand. The apparatus has no demonstrated vascular transport route. In vivo mapping of secretion, circulation and target location is needed to distinguish endocrine from paracrine delivery. Direct contact is unnecessary in this experiment, but classification must follow the route.

  6. Two glands respond to reduced blood calcium. One releases a hormone that raises calcium; the other lowers secretion of a calcium-lowering hormone. For each, specify the sign of the complete loop and explain why the two secretory responses have opposite directions.

    Answer and explanation

    Both responses can constitute negative feedback on calcium because each counteracts the original fall. The first hormone increases the controlled variable and is released more as calcium falls. The second lowers the variable and is released less as calcium falls. The feedback sign concerns the whole causal loop, not whether the secretory cell increases or decreases output.

  7. The hypothetical hormone X is secreted steadily into a well-mixed compartment of volume 8 L and has clearance 0.8 L/min. At equilibrium, X is 20 nmol/L. Determine secretion rate and half-life. If distribution volume doubles without changing clearance or secretion, calculate the new steady state and half-life.

    Answer and explanation

    S=CL×C=16 nmol/min. k=CL/V=0.1 per min; half-life=ln(2)/k≈6.93 min. Doubling V changes k to 0.05 per min and half-life to 13.86 min, but steady concentration remains 20 nmol/L because S/CL is unchanged. Assumes constant linear clearance and a single compartment.

  8. Following a step increase in secretion, hormone concentration rises from an initial steady level of 4 nM toward a new steady level of 12 nM. At 10 minutes it is 8 nM; at 20 minutes it is 10 nM. What is the inferred half-time for approaching the new steady state and the predicted concentration at 30 minutes?

    Answer and explanation

    The gap from the new equilibrium falls from 8 to 4 to 2 nM in successive ten-minute intervals. Half-time is 10 minutes; the next gap is 1 nM, predicting 11 nM at 30 minutes. This is not decay toward zero. Constant clearance and a step to fixed secretion are assumptions; the model need not describe every real endocrine transient.

  9. In a steady-state tracer-infusion experiment, a labelled hormone is infused at 6 nmol/min and its plateau tracer concentration is 2 nmol/L. Endogenous unlabelled hormone is 15 nmol/L. Assuming tracer and endogenous hormone share linear clearance and tracer does not perturb regulation, estimate metabolic clearance and endogenous secretion.

    Answer and explanation

    Clearance=6/2=3 L/min. Endogenous secretion is 3×15=45 nmol/min at steady state. The labelled tracer provides an independent estimate of removal, rather than assuming a raised endogenous concentration signifies increased secretion. If tracer kinetics differ or feedback responds to tracer dose, the inference fails.

  10. A hormone-binding protein is increased experimentally. Immediately after the change, total hormone in the sampled compartment is unchanged while measured free hormone falls. Several days later, an intact feedback axis restores free hormone toward baseline and total hormone is elevated. Explain the sequence.

    Answer and explanation

    Initially, additional binding sites redistribute hormone from free to bound pools; synthesis has not yet adjusted. Reduced free hormone weakens feedback where the free hormone determines exposure, allowing increased upstream stimulation and secretion until regulation tends to restore free concentration. The final total pool may be greater. The transient and chronic responses cannot be inferred from total concentration alone.

  11. A free hormone concentration of 3 nM is measured in both animals A and B. A has a receptor Kd of 1 nM and 100 functional binding sites per cell; B has Kd of 9 nM and 400 sites. Compute expected occupied sites under a simple equilibrium model. Can the result alone rank their physiological responses?

    Answer and explanation

    A occupancy fraction=3/(1+3)=0.75, giving 75 occupied sites. B fraction=3/(9+3)=0.25, giving 100 occupied sites. The lower fractional occupancy in B coexists with more occupied receptors. Response also depends on coupling, downstream amplification and saturation, so receptor occupancy alone does not rank functional response.

  12. A hormone response curve has an EC50 of 1 nM and a measured receptor-binding Kd of 10 nM. An irreversible receptor-inactivating treatment shifts the EC50 to 5 nM without changing Kd of remaining receptors or the maximum tissue response. Give a plausible explanation and an important limitation.

    Answer and explanation

    A receptor reserve and downstream amplification can permit a maximal response with only a subset of receptors occupied. Removing some receptors reduces sensitivity while enough remain for the same maximum. EC50 is a functional property and need not equal binding Kd. The observation is consistent with receptor reserve, not unique proof: altered coupling or assay conditions must be excluded.

  13. In three experimental animals with an idealised hypothalamus–pituitary–gland axis, measurements are A: upstream hormone high, terminal hormone low; B: both hormones low; C: both hormones high. For each, give one possible physiological cause. What observation is needed before interpreting a single sampling point?

    Answer and explanation

    A: peripheral gland failure with loss of feedback. B: central reduction in trophic input. C: autonomous upstream secretion or impaired upstream sensing of terminal feedback. These are candidate causes rather than diagnoses. Sampling time relative to endogenous rhythms, assay specificity, medications or perturbations, and repeated measurements or functional tests are needed; one pair cannot establish all causal relations.

  14. A test hormone stimulates an effector that raises blood glucose. With normal glucose feedback, the stimulus to hormone secretion diminishes. Blocking a glucose-sensing pathway produces prolonged secretion although the hormone receptor on the effector remains functional. Explain which part of the control system has been disrupted.

    Answer and explanation

    The feedback sensing or return pathway is disrupted, not necessarily the feed-forward hormonal response. The hormone can still activate the effector and alter glucose, but the controller fails to receive or translate the error-correcting information. Measure both the effector response to added hormone and secretory response to experimentally altered glucose to separate the two limbs.

  15. During a 24-hour recording, hormone peaks recur near the same biological phase on successive days even in constant conditions. Smaller peaks also recur roughly every two hours. What two time scales are represented, and why is recording only at one clock time inadequate?

    Answer and explanation

    A near-daily endogenous rhythm and shorter ultradian pulsatility are compatible with the observations. Persistence under constant conditions supports endogenous circadian timing but a sufficiently long record is needed. A single value confounds phase, pulse timing and baseline. Dense serial sampling over multiple cycles can estimate periodicity, pulse amplitude and mean separately.

  16. A repeated sampling study observes one hormone at 8 a.m. on three days in two groups; one group sleeps at night and the other works overnight. The hormone differs between groups at 8 a.m. Design a stronger test of whether overall hormone output differs.

    Answer and explanation

    Sample across the full biological day at multiple times, align data with sleep timing or an independent circadian phase marker, and use comparable activity, food and stress conditions. Estimate integrated concentration over time; where clearance could differ, measure secretion independently. A fixed wall-clock sample may compare different phases rather than different daily production.

  17. In an illustrative factorial experiment, response above baseline is 2 units with hormone P alone, 1 with Q alone and 8 with P+Q. After P pretreatment followed by washout, Q alone gives 6 units while P is undetectable. Interpret the interaction and propose a way to distinguish receptor upregulation from enhanced downstream coupling.

    Answer and explanation

    The combined increment exceeds the additive prediction of 3 on the stated scale. The lasting enhancement after P washout also supports a permissive component. Compare Q receptor abundance and binding affinity with a downstream response to receptor-independent pathway activation, using matched timing and controls. Either increased receptor abundance or altered coupling could increase sensitivity; simultaneous synergy and permissiveness can coexist.

  18. An endocrine ligand acts at the plasma membrane in tissue A and evokes secretion, whereas the same receptor is detected in tissue B but activation changes transcription instead. Both tissues bind equal ligand per cell. How can this occur, and how would an experiment test the explanation?

    Answer and explanation

    Different intracellular effectors, adaptor proteins, ion channels and transcriptional programmes can translate receptor activation into distinct responses. Equal binding does not establish equal downstream machinery. Compare early intracellular signals and test a selective downstream perturbation or bypass in each tissue. Tissue specificity is not synonymous with ligand-binding specificity.

  19. A short-lived peptide is measured by an antibody directed against a sequence shared by the mature hormone and its inactive precursor. After blocking precursor cleavage, assay concentration increases while target-cell response decreases. Explain the apparent contradiction and identify a complementary assay.

    Answer and explanation

    The immunoassay detects immunoreactive material, including accumulating precursor, whereas biological activity depends on correctly processed ligand. A mature-specific assay, chromatographic separation coupled with molecular identification, or a validated receptor-based bioassay can distinguish species or functional activity. Controls should assess cross-reactivity, recovery and matrix effects. The data do not necessarily imply receptor resistance.

  20. In an insect endocrine experiment, a circulating developmental hormone is maintained at the same concentration in two cohorts at different developmental stages. Cohort A initiates a transition; cohort B does not. Formulate two hypotheses that preserve the basic endocrine principle and describe a discriminating test.

    Answer and explanation

    Target competence may differ because receptor abundance or intracellular regulatory state changes with stage; alternatively, a second hormonal signal may provide a permissive or antagonistic context. Compare receptor abundance and response of isolated stage-matched tissues to the same ligand with and without the second candidate signal. Stage-specific output does not disprove circulating hormonal control, and insect mechanisms should not be forced into a vertebrate pituitary model.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Ductless means every endocrine cell is in a discrete gland.

    Dispersed gut endocrine cells and endocrine functions of organs show otherwise. Classify by the route of secretion, not by the size or name of the organ.

  2. All pancreatic products are hormones.

    Digestive enzymes are delivered through ducts to a lumen; islet hormones enter extracellular fluid and the circulation. Identify the destination rather than the organ name.

  3. A neuronal chemical messenger must be a neurotransmitter.

    Release into a vascular compartment can be neuroendocrine. Examine source and delivery route before classification.

  4. Hormone delivery guarantees response.

    Functional receptor and intracellular machinery are necessary. A blood-borne signal may pass nonresponsive cells without causing its specific direct effect.

  5. Peptide hormone immunoreactivity always measures active ligand.

    Precursors and degradation products may cross-react. Distinguish molecular identity, receptor activity and assay recognition.

  6. All steroid output depends on newly imported cholesterol.

    Intracellular cholesterol stores and synthesis can provide substrate. Acute steroidogenesis may instead be limited by substrate delivery to enzymes.

  7. All amino-acid derivatives behave identically.

    Thyroid hormones, catecholamines and melatonin differ in structure, storage, transport and action. Do not infer handling from precursor alone.

  8. Plasma carrier and target receptor are interchangeable.

    Reversible binding to transport proteins is not necessarily signalling. Receptor binding initiates or modifies a target response.

  9. A higher total hormone level always means greater target exposure.

    Free hormone, transporters, tissue conversion and receptor function matter. Binding changes may alter total concentration without proportional free concentration change.

  10. Concentration is secretion rate.

    Concentration depends on secretion, removal and distribution. Use clearance or flow data and stated steady-state assumptions.

  11. A larger distribution volume always lowers the steady hormone concentration.

    With fixed secretion and clearance in a linear one-compartment model, steady C=S/CL; volume changes the equilibration time.

  12. A falling hormone concentration proves secretion stopped.

    Loss may exceed ongoing input. Estimate both production and removal or examine a controlled withdrawal experiment.

  13. All hormone concentrations should be stable over a day.

    Pulses and rhythms convey physiological information. Consider biological phase and sampling frequency when comparing concentrations.

  14. Negative feedback means that every hormone in the loop falls.

    Negative feedback opposes the initiating disturbance; some signals may rise. Multiply causal signs around the closed loop.

  15. An increased combination response proves synergy.

    Compare the joint response with the baseline-corrected additive prediction on a defined scale; greater than either alone is insufficient.

Active recall

  1. Trace the information flow from a changed internal variable to a gland and back to that variable.
  2. Distinguish ductal release, systemic vascular release, local diffusion and self-directed signalling.
  3. Explain the secretin experiment using observations, inference and limitations.
  4. Trace translation, precursor processing and vesicle release of a peptide hormone.
  5. Explain how cholesterol can be abundant but unavailable to steroidogenic enzymes.
  6. Contrast catecholamine, thyroid hormone and melatonin handling.
  7. Explain why a carrier-protein increase can change total hormone more than free hormone.
  8. Derive S/CL for steady concentration and ln(2)V/CL for first-order half-life.
  9. Separate pulse frequency, pulse amplitude, circadian phase and mean concentration.
  10. Explain primary versus central changes in an ideal negative-feedback axis.
  11. Distinguish fractional receptor occupancy, receptor number and response sensitivity.
  12. Use baseline-corrected increments to test synergy and distinguish permissive effects.
