Foundations of Endocrinology and Hormonal Communication: Quantitative and Experimental Reasoning

7.J.01 · supplementary working material

Binding capacity, buffering and availability

Binding of circulating hormones is often taught as a distinction between bound and free molecules. A deeper question is how the two pools influence each other when the binding protein has a finite number of sites. Consider a plasma compartment containing a hormone H and a transport protein P. A molecule of H can associate with an unoccupied site on P and can subsequently dissociate. Neither the association nor the dissociation requires the gland to synthesize another hormone molecule. Consequently, at equilibrium there can be a rapid exchange of molecules between the two pools even when their measured concentrations remain constant. The equilibrium describes a distribution of molecules, not a permanent separation into two chemically different hormones.

Suppose the protein has one independent site per molecule and the total site concentration is Ptot. Let F represent free hormone and B represent bound hormone. The concentration of unoccupied sites is Ptot minus B. The equilibrium dissociation constant is Kd = F(Ptot − B)/B. Multiplying by B and collecting the terms containing B gives B(Kd + F) = FPtot. Thus B = Ptot F/(Kd + F). Both Ptot and F are concentrations, and the ratio F/(Kd + F) is dimensionless. The result predicts an upper limit of Ptot for the concentration of bound hormone. This is not a receptor response model: the binding sites in this example are circulating carriers, not necessarily signalling proteins.

When free hormone is much less than Kd, relatively few sites are occupied and B is approximately Ptot F/Kd. In this range, doubling F nearly doubles B if the protein concentration is fixed. When free hormone is much larger than Kd, most sites become occupied. Increasing the free concentration further produces comparatively little additional binding. These limits explain why the free fraction need not be constant across all concentrations, even if a carrier protein remains at the same abundance. They also explain why an approximation valid near the normal operating range may fail in an experiment using a very high concentration of hormone.

In an illustrative equilibrium system, take Ptot = 90 nM and Kd = 10 nM. If F = 10 nM, B = 90 × 10/20 = 45 nM; total hormone is 55 nM. If F = 30 nM, B = 90 × 30/40 = 67.5 nM and total is 97.5 nM. The free fraction rises from 10/55, or 18.2%, to 30/97.5, or 30.8%. The total concentration rises by a smaller factor than the free concentration. These values belong to an invented mathematical system, not a normal reference interval for any mammalian hormone. A useful examination question can ask whether a given free fraction is compatible with binding-site saturation rather than assuming all hormones retain a fixed percentage of free molecules.

Binding can buffer a transient change in free concentration. If a small quantity of hormone enters a compartment rich in available carrier sites, some of the incoming molecules occupy those sites. Conversely, if free hormone is removed, dissociation can replenish the free pool. The duration of buffering depends on exchange kinetics and the way hormone leaves the compartment. A carrier therefore changes the relationship between concentration, delivery and time without becoming an endocrine gland. It cannot create hormone mass. If all synthesis stops and removal continues, a reversible reservoir can delay depletion but cannot prevent eventual depletion.

Laboratory measurements must distinguish a change in the amount of binding protein from a change in the amount or activity of hormone. One way to investigate this distinction is to separate the freely diffusible fraction from protein-associated hormone using an equilibrium separation method that retains proteins while permitting small molecules to exchange. A suitable measurement of the two pools requires control of temperature, equilibration and changes in dilution because these can shift the original equilibrium. A simultaneous total-hormone measurement provides an additional constraint. The result answers an availability question; whether a tissue actually responds still depends on entry, metabolism and receptors. A hormone analogue with unusually high carrier affinity might have a different total concentration and persistence without having greater intrinsic receptor efficacy.

How secretion pulses become concentration curves

A pulse is a transient episode of increased secretion rather than a permanent shift in average gland activity. Blood collected from an animal during such an episode may show a peak later and broader than the actual secretory event because hormone takes time to enter the sampled compartment and be removed. This distinction matters when interpreting endocrine experiments. Measuring the concentration at two isolated times does not directly reconstruct secretion. Even a dense concentration record needs a model of clearance to estimate the secretory pattern that produced it.

An idealized gland releases an instantaneous quantity D into an effective distribution volume V at the start of every interval T. Suppose the hormone subsequently disappears by first-order kinetics with rate constant k and there is no secretion between pulses. Immediately after a pulse the concentration rises by D/V. By the next pulse, the fraction remaining from that peak is r = exp(−kT). A sequence of identical pulses eventually reaches a periodic steady pattern. If the peak concentration is Cmax, then the concentration immediately before the next pulse is Cmin = rCmax. Adding D/V restores the peak. Therefore Cmax = D/[V(1 − r)] and Cmin = rD/[V(1 − r)]. This mathematical steady pattern is not a flat concentration; it repeats with the pulse interval.

For a deliberately simplified example, let each release raise concentration by 6 nM and let pulses recur every 20 minutes. If the half-life is 20 minutes, r = 0.5. The eventual peak is 6/(1 − 0.5) = 12 nM and the trough is 6 nM. A single pulse delivered to an initially empty compartment would instead produce a peak of only 6 nM. The difference arises because previous releases leave residual hormone behind. If the half-life is shortened to 10 minutes while dose and period stay fixed, r becomes 0.25; the periodic peak becomes 8 nM and the trough 2 nM. These calculated patterns illustrate why a lower trough does not necessarily mean that the gland releases a smaller amount in each event.

To obtain the average concentration, integrate the disappearance curve across one interval. The area under the concentration curve is Cmax(1 − r)/k = D/(Vk), where concentration multiplied by time has units such as nM·min. Division by T gives the mean concentration D/(VkT). Since CL = Vk, the mean equals the average secretion rate D/T divided by clearance. This is the same mass balance that governs constant secretion, even though the time patterns differ. For the 20-minute half-life example, k is ln 2/20 per minute and the average is 6/ln 2, approximately 8.66 nM. A continuous input delivering the same amount per interval in this linear model would produce that concentration at steady state without peaks and troughs.

The equality of means does not imply equality of biological responses. A target cell can respond nonlinearly to concentration. A threshold-like response might occur only during peaks; a saturating receptor system might waste part of a very large peak; and adaptation during persistent exposure might reduce the response despite stable hormone concentration. Which outcome occurs depends on the response mechanism, so no universal rule says that pulsatile delivery is always more effective. Intermittent physiological GnRH delivery and sustained exposure provide a useful endocrine example of the importance of timing, but the detailed hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal mechanism belongs to subsequent nodes.

A sampling scheme must be related to the time scale of the pattern it seeks to resolve. If samples are always taken exactly twenty minutes apart from a rhythm with a twenty-minute interval, every sample could represent the same phase. A record of identical values might then be misinterpreted as evidence of constant secretion. Sampling at shorter intervals and over several cycles improves the chance of observing changes, though measurement noise, blood collection stress and biological variability remain relevant. An experiment comparing groups should use the same timing protocol and account for rhythms tied to sleep, feeding and illumination. A single sample may still answer a narrowly defined question, but it is inadequate evidence that a gland has lost its normal pulse pattern.

Separating gland output, clearance and target responsiveness

To investigate an endocrine disturbance, first identify what the observed variable represents. A low circulating concentration could result from reduced synthesis, failure of release, a change in binding or faster disappearance. A weak target response at normal circulating concentration could arise from defective entry, insufficient local conversion or defective reception. The same phenotype can therefore emerge from different points in a communication sequence. To discriminate mechanisms, an investigator needs measurements that constrain more than one step, not merely more repetitions of the original observation.

Consider two hypothetical preparations with steady hormone concentrations of 4 and 8 nM. To test whether the difference results from clearance rather than secretion, an investigator could follow a distinguishable tracer form of the same hormone at concentrations low enough not to perturb ordinary regulation, provided it has been established that the tracer behaves like the native molecule. In a simple well-mixed model, a measured tracer decay constant and an estimate of its distribution volume determine clearance. If both preparations have equal volumes but the second has half the decay constant, the measured difference is consistent with unchanged secretion and reduced clearance. If disappearance is identical, the concentration difference instead suggests different net input under the model. Both inferences depend on assumptions; a label that changes carrier binding or degradation invalidates the comparison.

A different approach to secretion is to compare blood entering and leaving a gland. For a simplified steady-flow preparation, let Q be plasma flow through the gland and Cv and Ca the outgoing and incoming hormone concentrations. The net appearance rate in the blood is Q(Cv − Ca). For Q = 0.2 L/min and a concentration difference of 15 nM, net appearance is 3 nmol/min. Plasma flow must be used with plasma concentrations; substituting whole-blood flow would require accounting for the relevant blood fractions. Furthermore, net appearance is not necessarily gross synthesis. The gland may simultaneously remove incoming hormone, store newly synthesized molecules or release material made earlier. A zero gradient is therefore not absolute evidence of zero production.

Replacement and rescue experiments test a different causal link. Suppose an animal with a poorly functioning peripheral endocrine gland has reduced target activity. Administration of a verified active hormone restores the target response. This supports the idea that inadequate hormonal stimulation contributed to the phenotype and that the target retains at least some ability to respond. It does not identify the precise upstream defect, because reduced trophic drive, synthesis and secretion could all lead to inadequate hormone exposure. If replacement fails, receptor resistance is one possibility, but insufficient delivery, an incorrect time window or irreversible damage must also be considered. A positive control demonstrating that the test preparation can still respond to a suitable independent stimulus strengthens interpretation.

To separate a change in sensitivity from a change in maximal response, compare concentration-response curves rather than a single point. A curve shifted towards larger concentrations with the same upper plateau indicates that greater ligand exposure is required to achieve comparable responses. A lower plateau suggests that the maximum obtainable response has decreased under the tested conditions. These changes can occur together or separately. Neither pattern by itself uniquely identifies the underlying molecular lesion, and an EC50 measured from a physiological response should not be substituted for a binding Kd without a justified model. This conceptual distinction prepares the detailed receptor treatment in 7.J.02.

Interpreting simultaneous hormone actions

Endocrine regulation is often distributed among several signals rather than controlled by one circulating molecule. Consider two hormones that increase the same measured response in a tissue. Their combined response can be compared with an explicitly stated null expectation. On an additive response scale, if R0 is the baseline and RA and RB are the responses to A and B separately, the predicted combined response is RA + RB − R0. Subtracting the baseline prevents it being counted twice. If the observed combined response is larger than this prediction, the result supports greater-than-additive interaction on that response scale. A statistical comparison using replicate measurements is needed before a biological interaction is established confidently.

Now suppose A increases a response from 10 to 14 and B increases it from 10 to 18, with a combined response of 25. The additive prediction is 14 + 18 − 10 = 22, so the observed interaction contrast is 3 units. The same data need not yield the same classification on a different response scale, such as proportional change. Near the upper capacity of a tissue, a simple additive prediction can even exceed the physical maximum, making the null model inappropriate. A defensible conclusion specifies the response scale, range and controls rather than treating synergy as a property that follows merely from the names of two hormones.

Permissive interactions are easier to appreciate if hormone exposure is separated in time. A tissue may be conditioned by one hormone over hours and then respond to a brief second signal. In a four-condition experiment, compare untreated tissue, tissue exposed to the conditioning hormone alone, tissue exposed to the acute signal alone, and conditioned tissue subsequently exposed to the acute signal. If the conditioning hormone has little direct effect on the measured acute response but increases responsiveness to the second signal, the findings support permissiveness. It remains necessary to rule out changes in baseline, tissue viability or effective exposure. Measurements of receptor abundance or downstream capacity can then help explain the mechanism. This design is distinct from simply adding two hormones at the same moment.

An antagonistic relationship is defined with respect to the chosen physiological variable. Two hormones can have opposite effects on hepatic glucose production without competing for the same receptor, and an individual hormone may have different effects in different tissues. The endocrine network is not a catalogue of hormones that are globally stimulatory or inhibitory. Each causal statement needs a target, a condition and a measured outcome. This is especially important for interpreting feedback, because an increase in an upstream hormone can coexist with a reduction in its downstream hormone if the downstream gland has failed. The increase is then an attempted correction, not the cause of the decrease.

Original Part C analytical practice

  1. A binding protein has 60 nM total independent binding sites and Kd = 5 nM. Free hormone is 15 nM. Calculate bound hormone, total hormone and free fraction.

    Answer and reasoning

    B = 60 × 15/(5+15) = 45 nM. Total = 60 nM and free fraction = 15/60 = 25%. The 60 nM capacity is not an amount of hormone already bound; only three quarters of the sites are occupied.

  2. At the same free hormone concentration of 10 nM, carrier sites increase from 40 to 80 nM, with Kd = 10 nM. Predict the two total concentrations.

    Answer and reasoning

    Initially B = 20 nM and total = 30 nM; subsequently B = 40 nM and total = 50 nM. Identical free concentration can accompany very different total concentrations. This comparison fixes free hormone and does not predict an uncontrolled in vivo transient.

  3. A periodic instantaneous release raises concentration by 8 nM every half-life. What are the steady periodic peak and trough?

    Answer and reasoning

    The survival factor is 1/2. Peak = 8/(1−0.5) = 16 nM. Trough = 0.5 × 16 = 8 nM. A peak of 8 nM would describe the first dose into an initially empty compartment, not the periodic steady state.

  4. A pulse adds 10 nM every 30 min. Half-life is 15 min. Calculate the periodic peak and trough.

    Answer and reasoning

    There are two half-lives per interval, so r = 1/4. Peak = 10/(1−0.25) = 13.33 nM; trough = 3.33 nM. The hormone remaining from earlier pulses accumulates until periodic input balances loss.

  5. Two treatments deliver equal hormone mass per hour with identical linear clearance. One uses pulses and the other continuous infusion. Must their mean concentration and physiological response both match?

    Answer and reasoning

    The mean concentrations match at periodic or constant steady state under the model because mean input equals mean clearance. Responses need not match: receptor saturation, threshold effects and adaptive processes may distinguish the temporal patterns. A matched average concentration is not proof of matched action.

  6. A preparation secretes hormone at 20 nmol/min. Clearance changes from 4 to 2 L/min while input is held fixed. Predict the steady concentrations. What assumption matters if the experiment is repeated in an intact animal?

    Answer and reasoning

    The values are 5 and 10 nM. In an intact animal, feedback might alter secretion after clearance changes. Fixed input is essential to the numerical prediction; an observed rise does not alone prove reduced clearance.

  7. Gland plasma flow is 0.4 L/min, arterial hormone is 8 nM, and venous hormone is 13 nM. Estimate net release. If a gland also destroys 1 nmol/min of incoming hormone, what is the gross addition to blood in this idealized balance?

    Answer and reasoning

    Net appearance = 0.4 × (13−8) = 2 nmol/min. Gross addition must be 3 nmol/min if the specified destruction is the only additional loss. Gross biosynthesis still cannot be determined without information about glandular storage.

  8. A laboratory samples a hormone every 60 minutes. A real pulse rhythm repeats every 30 minutes. All observed samples are similar. Is constant secretion established?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. The sampling interval is an integer multiple of the rhythm, so the measurements may repeatedly sample the same phase. More frequent and appropriately timed samples over several cycles are required to evaluate pulsatility.

  9. In a tissue the baseline response is 8 units; hormones A and B give 13 and 15 units respectively, and their combination gives 20. Evaluate additivity on the absolute response scale.

    Answer and reasoning

    The predicted response is 13 + 15 − 8 = 20 units. The combination is exactly additive on this scale. Being greater than either single response is not evidence of synergy.

  10. A hormone-conditioned tissue has baseline response 4; acute hormone alone raises response to 10. Conditioning alone leaves baseline at 4, but the acute response after conditioning is 19. What mechanism is supported, and what additional evidence is needed?

    Answer and reasoning

    The pattern supports a permissive or sensitizing action of conditioning because its direct effect is negligible while the second response increases. Replication, matched acute exposure, viability controls and direct examination of receptor or effector changes are needed to infer the mechanism.

  11. A concentration-response curve shifts from EC50 = 5 nM to EC50 = 20 nM, but maximum response remains 100 units. Does this prove a fourfold change in receptor Kd?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. It shows fourfold lower operational sensitivity by the EC50 criterion in the measured response. Receptor abundance, coupling, transport or other factors can produce the shift. Independent ligand-binding measurements are required to establish Kd.

  12. After gland removal, target response falls. A verified active hormone restores response; in a separate animal, replacement does not restore it. What can and cannot be concluded?

    Answer and reasoning

    Rescue supports inadequate hormonal stimulation as a contributor in the first animal. Failure of rescue in the second raises the possibility of target resistance but does not prove it; delivery, dose, time, tissue viability and irreversible changes must be evaluated. The design distinguishes hypotheses rather than uniquely identifying lesions.
