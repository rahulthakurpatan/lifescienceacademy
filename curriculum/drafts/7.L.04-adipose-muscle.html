Why muscle and fat must communicate

A vertebrate can survive the interval between meals because the sites of energy intake, storage and expenditure need not be the same. An intestinal cell absorbs nutrients during a meal, an adipocyte can retain some of their chemical energy for days, and a skeletal muscle fibre can spend energy within seconds when locomotion begins. This division of labour is economical only if the tissues respond to changing demands in a coordinated way. If an animal continues to store every incoming nutrient during vigorous activity, its working muscles may lack substrate. If it releases all stored fuel while resting after a meal, excess substrate may accumulate in the circulation. Physiological regulation solves these problems by matching substrate supply to the rate and location of expenditure.

Energy and matter must be distinguished. ATP hydrolysis supplies free energy for contraction, ion pumping and many synthetic reactions. ATP itself is continually resynthesised within the muscle fibre; adipocytes do not transport ATP to working muscles in order to power a contraction. Rather, adipocytes can release fatty acids that muscle oxidises to obtain energy for ATP synthesis. A muscle can also draw on its own glycogen, incoming glucose and intramuscular lipid. The circulation carries substrates, oxygen and regulatory molecules between these sites. Carbon atoms entering oxidative metabolism ultimately contribute to carbon dioxide, whereas the energy transferred during oxidation is redistributed into useful work and heat.

In addition to supplying fuel, adipose tissue and skeletal muscle secrete chemical signals. A molecule released by a cell may act on the same cell (autocrine action), a nearby cell (paracrine action) or a distant target reached through the circulation (endocrine action). A circulating change is not sufficient evidence for endocrine action: a target must possess a responsive mechanism, and the molecule must reach it at a physiologically effective concentration. An adipokine is an adipose-derived signalling molecule, a myokine is a muscle-derived signalling molecule, and a batokine is a signal released by brown adipose tissue. These names describe origins, not exclusive chemical families. The same protein may be produced in several tissues and may have different actions depending on the receptor and physiological setting.

Three measurements must not be conflated: the amount of energy stored, the rate of substrate transfer and the rate of energy expenditure. A large fat depot is evidence of a substantial stock, not evidence of a high current rate of fatty acid release. A rapid release of fatty acids does not guarantee rapid oxidation because the recipient tissue might re-esterify them. Exercise is an instructive perturbation because it changes demand in minutes, while repeated training changes the capacity and regulation of tissues over weeks. Studying both time scales reveals the distinction between a rapid physiological response and an adaptation.

White, brown and beige adipose tissues

White adipose tissue is a specialised organ for storing triacylglycerol. A typical mature white adipocyte contains one large lipid droplet that displaces much of the cytoplasm and nucleus toward the cell periphery. Lipid droplets are not membrane-bound aqueous vesicles; a phospholipid monolayer separates their hydrophobic contents from cytosol. Storage as neutral lipid provides a compact reservoir without imposing the osmotic load that would accompany equivalent numbers of freely dissolved small molecules. Adipose tissue is more than a collection of adipocytes. It contains endothelial cells, fibroblast-like progenitors, immune cells, extracellular matrix and nerve fibres. Consequently, a protein measured in an adipose tissue homogenate is not necessarily made by adipocytes.

Brown adipocytes generally contain multiple lipid droplets and abundant mitochondria. The vascular supply supports high oxidative metabolism and transports heat away from active tissue. The characteristic colour reflects the abundance of mitochondria and their respiratory components, together with vascularisation. In response to suitable stimulation, brown adipocytes increase substrate oxidation and use uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) to promote dissipation of the mitochondrial proton motive force. Their functional role is therefore different from the predominant role of white adipocytes, although brown cells also store lipid and white cells also consume ATP.

Beige adipocytes are inducible thermogenic adipocytes occurring within predominantly white depots. Under stimuli such as sustained cold exposure, some depots acquire cells with higher mitochondrial abundance and UCP1 expression. Withdrawal of the stimulus can diminish the thermogenic phenotype. Beige and classical brown adipocytes are not simply interchangeable labels: developmental history and depot context differ. Nor does a depot become uniformly thermogenic merely because a subset of its cells expresses UCP1. An average gene expression measurement can increase because individual cells express more transcript, because more cells acquire the phenotype, or both.

Depot location changes physiological interpretation. Subcutaneous adipose tissue and visceral adipose tissue differ in drainage, innervation, cellular composition and metabolic characteristics. Some visceral depots deliver fatty acids and other products to the liver through the portal circulation, whereas many subcutaneous depots drain into the systemic circulation. These distinctions can alter the substrate and signal environment experienced by the liver. However, location alone is insufficient to infer the exact behaviour of every depot, and observations from a particular rodent depot should not be transferred uncritically to human adipose anatomy.

A healthy storage response can protect other organs from nutrient overload. Expansion by recruitment and differentiation of new adipocytes differs from enlargement of existing adipocytes. When lipid storage capacity is poorly matched to nutrient influx, fat cells may become stressed and excess lipid-derived material may reach non-adipose tissues. This is one reason the quantity of fat alone does not fully describe metabolic health. An animal with insufficient functional adipose tissue may exhibit ectopic lipid deposition and insulin resistance despite having little visible adipose mass. Detailed metabolic disease mechanisms belong to the later chapter on obesity and diabetes; here the important principle is the protective as well as potentially disruptive function of adipose tissue.

Storage, mobilisation and the circulation

Following a carbohydrate-containing meal, insulin generally favours glucose uptake and storage in responsive cells. In adipocytes, insulin signalling promotes recruitment of GLUT4 transporters to the plasma membrane. Glucose metabolism can supply glycerol 3-phosphate, which provides the glycerol backbone for triacylglycerol synthesis. Fatty acids arriving in lipoprotein particles are made available through lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which acts at the capillary endothelial surface. Tissue fatty acid uptake, intracellular activation to acyl-CoA and esterification can then increase lipid storage. LPL is not the principal enzyme that dismantles stored triacylglycerol inside the adipocyte; the distinction between extracellular lipoprotein processing and intracellular lipid droplet lipolysis is fundamental.

During energetic demand, catecholamine signalling through appropriate beta-adrenergic receptors can increase adenylyl cyclase activity and cyclic AMP. Activation of protein kinase A changes the regulation of droplet-associated proteins and lipases. Adipose triglyceride lipase initiates much of the hydrolysis of triacylglycerol to diacylglycerol; hormone-sensitive lipase acts particularly effectively on diacylglycerol, and monoacylglycerol lipase completes hydrolysis. Complete hydrolysis of one triacylglycerol molecule yields three fatty acids and one glycerol molecule. The stoichiometry describes the reaction itself, not necessarily the ratio of products leaving a tissue, because some fatty acids may be retained and re-esterified.

Insulin suppresses lipolysis in part through signalling that activates phosphodiesterase 3B and lowers cyclic AMP. Falling insulin during fasting removes part of this restraint. In human adipose tissue, catecholamines and insulin are major short-term regulators; it is misleading to make glucagon the universal direct activator of adipocyte lipolysis. Receptor subtype distribution also matters: alpha-2 adrenergic receptor signalling can oppose cyclic AMP accumulation. Net responses depend on the balance of inputs, and different depots can respond differently to the same circulating catecholamine concentration.

Most released non-esterified long-chain fatty acids travel in plasma bound reversibly to albumin. Binding permits transport of hydrophobic molecules in an aqueous environment without requiring their packaging into new triacylglycerol-rich particles. Albumin-bound fatty acids are metabolically different from esterified fatty acids carried in chylomicrons or very-low-density lipoproteins. Glycerol released from adipose tissue can reach the liver, where it may enter gluconeogenesis or other metabolic pathways. Muscle uptake of fatty acids depends on substrate delivery, membrane transport, intracellular binding and the capacity to oxidise or esterify them. An increase in plasma fatty acid concentration cannot by itself identify which of these steps has changed.

Consider a simplified adipose depot in which lipolysis generates 12 micromoles of fatty acids per minute. If 5 micromoles per minute are re-esterified, only 7 micromoles per minute remain available for net release, assuming negligible local oxidation or other fates and a steady intracellular free fatty acid pool. A second depot could release the same net amount while generating 20 and re-esterifying 13. Measuring net export would not discriminate between these two situations. This example illustrates how substrate cycling can change energy use and gross biochemical rates without an equivalent change in net transfer.

Skeletal muscle demand and metabolic signalling

Skeletal muscle converts chemical energy into mechanical work through cross-bridge cycling. ATP also supports the calcium pumps that restore low cytosolic calcium following activation and the ion pumps that maintain excitability. At the start of intense contraction, demand for ATP can rise faster than oxidative metabolism can respond. Phosphocreatine buffers ATP availability through the creatine kinase reaction, and glycolysis supplies ATP rapidly as demand increases. Oxidative phosphorylation becomes increasingly important when exercise is sustained and oxygen delivery and mitochondrial capacity are sufficient. The energy systems overlap in time rather than switching on and off at sharply separated intervals.

Muscle fibres differ in contractile and metabolic properties. Slow oxidative fibres generally have high mitochondrial content, myoglobin and capillary support and are adapted to sustained activity. Fast fibres span a range of oxidative and glycolytic characteristics. Recruitment pattern and exercise intensity therefore influence both ATP turnover and fuel selection. A tissue sample containing mixed fibre types cannot establish that every fibre increased fatty acid oxidation equally. Training may change mitochondrial enzyme activity and substrate-handling capacity even without changing the number of fibres of each type.

Insulin stimulates muscle glucose transport through a receptor tyrosine kinase pathway involving IRS proteins, PI3K, Akt and regulators of GLUT4 vesicle trafficking. Contraction also stimulates glucose uptake, through partially distinct signals involving changes in energy state, calcium and mechanical activation. AMPK, an energy-sensitive kinase, contributes to regulation of several contraction-associated processes, but is not the sole explanation for every contraction-induced glucose transport response. Both pathways can converge on regulation of GLUT4 trafficking, which explains why glucose uptake can increase during exercise even while circulating insulin falls. The presence of GLUT4 in a cell is not equivalent to its insertion into the surface membrane.

Activation of AMPK during energetic stress can favour ATP-generating processes and restrain energy-consuming biosynthesis. In muscle, inhibition of acetyl-CoA carboxylase by phosphorylation can reduce malonyl-CoA, relieving inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 and facilitating entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups into the mitochondrial oxidation system under appropriate conditions. Oxidation still depends on demand, substrate delivery and respiratory capacity. Thus phosphorylation of a regulatory enzyme predicts a shift in biochemical permissiveness, not an unlimited rate of oxidation. Fatty acids can also be esterified into intramuscular lipid stores; a high lipid content need not imply a high rate of formation of harmful lipid intermediates.

The balance of fuels varies during exercise. At moderate intensity, a sustained muscle contraction may use a substantial mixture of fatty acids and carbohydrate. At high intensity, rapid ATP requirements tend to increase reliance on carbohydrate metabolism. Catecholamines may be high at the same time as carbohydrate oxidation dominates. There is no contradiction: a signal can promote lipid availability while another limitation, such as the rate of mitochondrial ATP production required by contracting fibres, shapes the actual fuel mixture. A measurement of released fatty acids therefore cannot establish that the muscle uses them as its dominant fuel.

After activity, increased glucose handling supports restoration of glycogen. Repeated exercise can increase mitochondrial content, improve capillary supply and alter the ability of muscle to respond to insulin. The acute molecular response to one bout and the rested phenotype after repeated training should be separated experimentally. Sampling immediately after the last session combines acute effects and long-term adaptation; sampling after an adequate recovery interval is more informative about persistent adaptation, although the interval itself must be specified.

Adipose-derived signals

The identification of leptin helped establish that adipose tissue functions as an endocrine organ. Leptin secretion generally relates to adipose energy reserves, while nutrient status also affects its production. Leptin acts prominently in the central nervous system, where it contributes to regulation of food intake and energy expenditure through hypothalamic pathways. A fall in leptin during negative energy balance signals reduced energy availability, influencing behavioural and neuroendocrine responses. Leptin is not a simple substance that directly melts fat. Its physiological effects require receptor signalling and depend on nutritional state and the responsiveness of target circuits.

Leptin-deficient animals illustrate how a missing endocrine signal can alter behaviour and body composition even though large amounts of fat are present. In contrast, common obesity often features increased circulating leptin accompanied by reduced effectiveness of leptin signalling. A measurement of high hormone concentration cannot be interpreted as evidence of an equally strong biological action. A receptor defect, altered transport or downstream signalling impairment can weaken the response. A comparison of hormone-deficient and receptor-deficient animals is especially useful for separating ligand availability from target sensitivity.

Adiponectin is another important adipocyte-derived protein. In many settings it is associated with greater insulin sensitivity, and its circulating abundance tends to be lower in obesity, in contrast to the typical behaviour of leptin. Adiponectin receptors in metabolic tissues can support signalling pathways related to fatty acid oxidation and glucose regulation. AMPK-associated signalling is a useful example in skeletal muscle. This relationship does not mean that the concentration of adiponectin is a complete surrogate for insulin sensitivity: receptor abundance, post-receptor events, protein forms and the physiological environment influence its biological effect.

Inflammatory mediators provide a different communication route. Expansion and stress of adipose depots can alter immune-cell recruitment and cytokine production. TNF and other mediators can interfere with insulin-responsive signalling in some experimental settings. A tissue source must be assigned carefully, because resident or recruited immune cells may contribute substantially to secretion. The terms adipokine and adipose-derived cytokine should not be used to imply that every adipose-associated mediator originates from a mature adipocyte. Chronic inflammation differs from a brief, regulated cytokine response to exercise, a distinction that becomes particularly important when interpreting interleukin-6.

An endocrine experiment needs evidence beyond correlation. If animals with higher adiponectin also have higher fatty acid oxidation, this does not establish direction of causality. Their activity, nutrient intake, body composition or other hormones may influence both. A receptor-specific perturbation that attenuates a response, combined with a suitable rescue or independent confirmation, is more informative. Even then, direct action on skeletal muscle must be distinguished from an effect mediated indirectly through altered appetite, liver metabolism or circulating substrate availability.

Contraction-dependent myokines and their interpretation

A contracting muscle fibre is not only a consumer of fuel. It can alter the synthesis and release of proteins that communicate its metabolic state. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a well studied example. Prolonged exercise can increase muscle IL-6 expression and release; the magnitude of the response depends on exercise duration, intensity, muscle mass recruited and glycogen availability. A temporary rise in circulating IL-6 during exercise differs in context from chronically elevated inflammatory signalling. The concentration of a cytokine at a particular moment does not define its origin, the duration of exposure, the receptor system involved or the biological outcome.

Muscle-derived IL-6 can participate in communication with metabolic organs, including effects on substrate availability and lipid metabolism. Effects attributed to IL-6 must be interpreted in the context of simultaneous changes in catecholamines, insulin, blood flow and energy demand. If administration of IL-6 changes fatty acid mobilisation under controlled conditions, it supports a possible signalling contribution; it does not establish that all exercise-induced lipolysis requires IL-6. Demonstrating necessity involves selectively removing or blocking the relevant signal and asking which portion of the response remains.

Irisin is a proposed exercise-associated signal derived by processing of the membrane protein FNDC5. Experimental work has linked muscle transcriptional responses involving PGC-1alpha to FNDC5 and thermogenic changes in adipocytes, particularly in rodent models. These results motivate study of muscle–fat communication, but their translation to humans requires care. Measurements of circulating irisin have differed by assay, and the contribution of this pathway to human whole-body thermogenesis during exercise remains less certain than classical sympathetic activation of brown fat. A student should learn the proposed pathway and its evidence limitations rather than treat every arrow in a schematic as an equally established human mechanism.

Myostatin is a member of the transforming growth factor beta superfamily that restrains skeletal muscle growth. Interference with myostatin activity can change muscle mass and thereby alter energy demand and body composition. When a myostatin-deficient animal has less fat, however, the change may arise indirectly from its larger muscle compartment or altered whole-body metabolism rather than from a direct effect of myostatin on adipocytes. A direct action could be evaluated in isolated adipose cells, but an effect in culture would still need a demonstration of physiological significance in the animal.

The category of myokines is therefore useful without implying that every muscle-derived molecule must travel through blood to fat. Some signals act locally on muscle fibres, connective tissue or neighbouring cells. Others can reach distant organs. A protein detected in blood after exercise may be released by skeletal muscle, liver, adipose tissue, vascular cells or leukocytes. Measuring secretion by isolated contracting muscle or a difference between venous and arterial concentrations across an active limb provides more source-specific evidence than systemic blood measurement alone.

Brown fat, beige cells and heat production

The electron transport chain uses energy from oxidation to drive proton translocation across the mitochondrial inner membrane. Proton motive force includes a membrane potential and a chemical concentration gradient. ATP synthase allows protons to move down this electrochemical gradient while coupling the movement to ATP synthesis. In a brown adipocyte, activated UCP1 provides an alternative route for proton movement, permitting more of the energy associated with substrate oxidation to appear as heat rather than being conserved in ATP. The term uncoupling means that electron transport and ATP synthesis are less tightly linked; it does not mean that the cell stops respiring.

Cold-sensitive neural pathways increase sympathetic input to thermogenic adipose tissue. Noradrenaline activates adrenergic receptors, elevates cyclic AMP and stimulates lipolysis and thermogenic responses. Released fatty acids can provide oxidative fuel and promote UCP1 activity. Increased oxygen consumption can accompany uncoupling because the respiratory chain must work to maintain the gradient against increased proton conductance. This prediction assumes an adequate supply of oxygen and substrates. An inactive cell with depleted fuel cannot sustain high oxygen consumption merely because UCP1 protein is present.

Cold exposure can produce two distinct effects: rapid activation of thermogenic cells already capable of heat production and slower recruitment or remodelling of thermogenic capacity. A rise in oxygen consumption within minutes is compatible with acute activation. Greater UCP1 abundance, mitochondrial biogenesis or increased numbers of beige adipocytes after prolonged exposure indicates a change in capacity. The two processes may occur together but their time scales and appropriate measurements differ. An experiment assessing UCP1 transcript after several days cannot establish the exact amount of heat produced during the first minutes of cold exposure.

Brown adipose tissue also produces signals sometimes termed batokines. FGF21 is a representative molecule that has been studied in this context, but the liver and other tissues can also contribute to circulating FGF21. Therefore, a cold-induced rise in plasma FGF21 is not proof that brown adipocytes are its source. In an experiment designed to establish a brown-fat endocrine signal, one needs evidence for release from brown adipocytes, delivery to an appropriate target and a target response attributable to that signal. Multiple mediators may influence the same outcome, and redundancy can make the phenotype of a single deletion relatively modest.

Studies published in 2009 established metabolically active brown adipose tissue in adult humans using imaging and, in selected samples, tissue evidence of UCP1-positive adipocytes. Cold exposure can increase uptake of a radiolabelled glucose analogue in characteristic regions. FDG-PET indicates tracer uptake and trapping, not directly the rate of heat generation or total fatty acid oxidation. A region with little FDG uptake may still use other substrates. Combining imaging with measurements of oxygen consumption, temperature responses, tissue markers and controlled environmental conditions can strengthen physiological interpretation. The existence of adult human brown adipose tissue is well established, but its quantitative contribution to total daily energy expenditure varies and should not be assumed from a single imaging result.

Muscle shivering and brown-fat non-shivering thermogenesis illustrate different strategies for heat generation. Shivering increases ATP turnover through involuntary muscle activity; UCP1-mediated thermogenesis reduces the fraction of oxidative energy conserved in ATP. Both ultimately require fuel and oxygen. Exercise also generates heat as a consequence of muscle ATP turnover and inefficiency of mechanical work. Exercise-induced heat production is not evidence of activation of brown fat, especially because a rising body temperature changes thermoregulatory demand. The distinction between metabolic expenditure due to contractile work and thermogenic expenditure due to uncoupling is essential.

Quantitative reasoning and evidence

The net rate of substrate uptake by a perfused organ can be estimated using the Fick principle. If blood flow is Q in litres per minute and arterial and venous substrate concentrations are Ca and Cv in micromoles per litre, net uptake is Q multiplied by (Ca minus Cv), in micromoles per minute. The derivation follows conservation of matter: arterial inflow carries Q times Ca per minute and venous outflow carries Q times Cv. A positive difference represents net uptake, while a negative difference represents net release under this convention. Concentration measurements and blood flow must refer to the same compartment; plasma concentrations should be paired with plasma flow when relevant.

In a hypothetical experiment, a resting limb receives plasma at 0.5 litres per minute and arterial and venous non-esterified fatty acid concentrations are 600 and 500 micromoles per litre, respectively. Net uptake is 0.5 times 100, or 50 micromoles per minute. During exercise, plasma flow rises to 2 litres per minute while the concentration difference narrows to 50 micromoles per litre. Uptake becomes 100 micromoles per minute. Although the concentration difference decreased, net uptake doubled because flow increased fourfold. Neither value establishes the rate of oxidation: some extracted fatty acids could enter storage and some locally released fatty acids could obscure gross uptake.

Whole-body indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production. The respiratory exchange ratio, RER, is carbon dioxide output divided by oxygen uptake measured at the mouth; both gases must be expressed in comparable units and conditions. At steady state, values near 0.7 and 1.0 are consistent with predominant fat and carbohydrate oxidation respectively, assuming negligible protein contribution. Non-steady-state exercise, altered ventilation and bicarbonate buffering can make RER diverge from the cellular respiratory quotient. RER cannot identify which organ oxidised a particular substrate.

For an illustrative steady-state example, oxygen uptake is 1.5 litres per minute and carbon dioxide output is 1.2 litres per minute. RER equals 0.8. Using the simplified Weir relation, expenditure in kilocalories per minute is approximately 3.941 times oxygen uptake plus 1.106 times carbon dioxide output, with both gas flows in litres per minute and negligible urinary nitrogen contribution. The calculation yields 5.9115 plus 1.3272, or about 7.24 kilocalories per minute. Over 30 minutes at unchanged rates, this corresponds to about 217 kilocalories. It is gross expenditure, not the amount attributable specifically to muscle above rest, and certainly not a direct measurement of fat mass lost.

A study comparing an exercise group with a sedentary group should distinguish changes caused by exercise from changes caused by food intake, ambient temperature and body composition. Matching absolute exercise work can impose different relative intensities on animals of different fitness, while matching relative intensity can result in different absolute work. Choice of comparison depends on the scientific question. Measuring baseline and post-exercise values within each animal helps reduce between-animal variability but does not eliminate time-dependent confounders. A randomised intervention with standardised feeding, temperature and sampling interval is stronger than a cross-sectional comparison for establishing the effect of training.

The most useful experimental distinction is between sufficiency and necessity. If a muscle-derived factor added to adipocytes increases UCP1 expression, the factor may be sufficient under the chosen culture conditions to elicit that response. To test whether it mediates an exercise effect in an animal, selective removal of the factor or its receptor is needed, with appropriate controls and evidence of effective disruption. If the exercise response persists, the factor may be redundant or unnecessary under that condition; the result does not erase all possible actions of the factor. Rescue experiments, measurement of physiological concentrations and confirmation in independent systems help narrow the interpretation.

Reasoning practice

1. Part B

Which enzyme primarily initiates hydrolysis of stored adipocyte triacylglycerol?

Answer and explanation

Adipose triglyceride lipase; LPL instead processes lipoprotein triglyceride at capillary surfaces.

2. Part B

What is the major circulating carrier for non-esterified long-chain fatty acids?

Answer and explanation

Albumin; it reversibly binds fatty acids released into plasma.

3. Part B

What is the principal thermogenic mitochondrial protein in classical brown adipocytes?

Answer and explanation

UCP1, which provides a regulated proton conductance across the inner membrane.

4. Part B

Which adipose-derived protein is generally reduced rather than increased in common obesity?

Answer and explanation

Adiponectin; leptin generally increases with adipose mass, although its effectiveness may decline.

5. Part B

Why can skeletal muscle glucose uptake rise when insulin falls during activity?

Answer and explanation

Contraction activates partly insulin-independent signals that promote GLUT4 trafficking.

6. Part C

A tissue has unchanged arterial–venous concentration difference but twice the plasma flow. Assuming steady state, how does net substrate uptake change?

Answer and explanation

It doubles because uptake equals flow multiplied by concentration difference.

7. Part C

After a cold stimulus, UCP1 transcript doubles but oxygen consumption is not measured. Can the rate of heat production be calculated?

Answer and explanation

No. Transcript abundance reflects potential regulation, not actual protein activity, substrate oxidation or heat flux.

8. Part C

An intervention increases circulating IL-6 after exercise. What measurement could help establish that active muscle contributes to the rise?

Answer and explanation

Measure arterial and venous IL-6 concentrations across the limb together with blood flow; net venous release supports a local contribution but does not uniquely identify the secreting cell type.

9. Part C

A mouse lacking a myokine still shows exercise-induced beige-cell recruitment. Does this prove the myokine cannot affect adipose cells?

Answer and explanation

No. It indicates that the factor is not indispensable under the tested conditions; compensatory mechanisms or redundant signals may exist.

10. Part C

A participant has higher BAT FDG uptake during cold exposure than during warmth. Why is it incorrect to report that fatty acid oxidation doubled?

Answer and explanation

FDG measures glucose tracer handling, not fatty acid oxidation. The relationship to total heat production requires additional evidence.
