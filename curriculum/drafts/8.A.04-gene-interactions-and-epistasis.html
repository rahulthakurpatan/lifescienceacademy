From inherited alleles to interacting biological functions

In the simplest Mendelian experiment, a genetic difference at one locus separates two observable categories. A pea plant with one functional copy of an allele required for a particular trait may resemble a plant with two functional copies. The relationship between the two alleles is called dominance. Yet most biological structures require the products of more than one gene. A colored petal, for example, cannot arise merely because a plant inherits an allele for a pigment enzyme. The cell must also possess substrates, the machinery that makes the substrate available, and the conditions in which the pigment is produced and retained. Failure at any necessary step can change the appearance of the petal. Genes can therefore assort independently while their effects on the final phenotype are dependent.

A locus is a position in the genome, and alleles are alternative versions of that locus. A diploid with genotype AaBb carries two alleles at each of two loci. It need not contain four different forms of a single gene. The notation A_ will be used for AA or Aa, and B_ for BB or Bb. Whenever a dominant functional allele is sufficient for the stated process, A_ represents the functional class and aa the nonfunctional class. This is an explicit simplification, not a universal property of capital letters. An allele can instead confer excessive activity, alter the specificity of an enzyme, or produce an inhibitory protein. The notation does not decide which of these mechanisms operates; the biological observations do.

Dominance describes what a heterozygote looks like compared with the corresponding homozygotes at one locus, with other variables fixed. Classical epistasis describes how the phenotype associated with a locus is masked or altered by a genotype at another locus. The masking genotype is called epistatic and the locus whose phenotypic effect is hidden is hypostatic in that comparison. The two relationships answer different questions. If a functional A allele makes Aa resemble AA when B is functional, A is dominant to a under those conditions. If bb prevents either AA or Aa from giving the expected phenotype, bb is epistatic to the difference at A. There is no contradiction between these descriptions. One concerns the alleles within a locus; the other concerns the dependency between loci.

Gene interaction is broader than complete masking. Two products may contribute independently to a measurable output, combine to produce a novel state, or compensate for one another. Some interactions are obvious in visible phenotypes, while others require a more sensitive measurement. A flower classified as white in two genotypes may contain different colorless metabolites, different enzyme activities, or different levels of pigment below the visible threshold. A genetic ratio is a statement about the way the experimenter has classified progeny. It does not necessarily capture all molecular differences. This distinction becomes essential when using genetic crosses to reconstruct pathways.

The four dihybrid genotype classes

Consider AaBb crossed with AaBb, assuming the loci assort independently, both parents produce equally frequent AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes, fertilization is random, all zygotes survive equally to scoring, and the alleles have full dominance within each locus for the functions being tested. At the A locus the genotypes AA, Aa and aa have probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. Hence P(A_) is 3/4 and P(aa) is 1/4. At B, the corresponding probabilities are 3/4 and 1/4. Independence allows us to multiply these probabilities. The joint probabilities of A_B_, A_bb, aaB_ and aabb are respectively 9/16, 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16. They sum to one. These fractions describe groups of genotypes, not yet phenotypes.

If the expression of the two loci can be scored independently, the four groups can give four observable categories in a ratio 9:3:3:1. If two groups become indistinguishable for the trait being studied, their probabilities must be added. This addition is permitted because the genotypic groups are mutually exclusive. A 9:7 ratio can therefore mean that A_B_ has one phenotype and all three remaining groups have a second phenotype. A 9:3:4 ratio can arise if the 3/16 group aaB_ and the 1/16 group aabb share a phenotype. No gamete has disappeared. Independent assortment and Mendelian segregation can still hold exactly.

This way of deriving ratios is more reliable than memorizing the names of interactions. First write the genotype classes. Then determine the phenotype of each group from a specified mechanism or observations. Finally add the class weights for groups with the same phenotype. In a testcross AaBb × aabb the weights instead become 1/4 each because the double recessive parent contributes ab to every progeny. For the same phenotype map, the resulting ratio may be different. The gene interaction has not changed; the distribution of genotypes has. If the heterozygote is selfed instead, the standard F2 fractions apply provided the biological assumptions remain valid.

A probability of 9/16 means that an offspring sampled independently from the stated cross has that chance of belonging to the corresponding group. It does not promise that exactly nine of the next sixteen offspring will have the phenotype. A small family can deviate appreciably by sampling alone. Large samples permit a more precise comparison between observed and predicted distributions. Genetic evidence should therefore include the actual number of progeny as well as the ratio proposed to summarize them.

Recessive epistasis and Labrador coat color

In a simplified Labrador retriever model, two loci influence the color of the hair. The B locus distinguishes black and brown eumelanin when that pigment can be deposited, while the E locus influences whether dark eumelanin is expressed in the coat. B_ E_ dogs have a black coat, bb E_ dogs a brown coat, and ee dogs have a yellow coat regardless of the B genotype. The yellow phenotype should not be equated with complete absence of all pigment: other pigmentation can remain, including pheomelanin in the hair. This is why the biological description matters more than the superficial color mnemonic.

Crossing BbEe individuals gives a black probability of (3/4)(3/4) = 9/16. Brown requires bb and E_, a probability of (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Yellow requires ee, regardless of whether B_ or bb is present, so its probability is 1/4 = 4/16. Equivalently, B_ee contributes 3/16 and bbee contributes 1/16. The F2 ratio is black:brown:yellow = 9:3:4. If a textbook lists the colors in a different order, it may write 9:4:3. The biological model is unchanged. The e allele is recessive with respect to the E locus in this simplified cross, because a functional E allele permits dark coat expression. The ee genotype is epistatic to the B locus because it hides the black-versus-brown difference in the hair.

Suppose a yellow dog is crossed with a genetically known bbee tester. Both parents have ee, so every pup will also have ee. All pups will be yellow in the model irrespective of the yellow parent's B genotype. This is a useful demonstration that an apparently simple testcross can fail to reveal a hidden allele if the other locus remains in a masking state. In contrast, an E/E, b/b tester ensures every offspring has a functional E allele. If the yellow parent is BB, all progeny will be BbEe and black. If it is Bb, half the expected progeny will be black and half brown. If it is bb, all will be brown. The difference between the two experiments is not how much genetic material the tester contributes but whether its E allele opens the phenotypic readout of B.

The genotype-to-phenotype map is specific to the character scored. A molecular assay of B or a pigmentation characteristic outside the coat may distinguish genotypes grouped as yellow in this classroom model. Moreover, other loci may modify shade, pattern and intensity. The simple ratio predicts broad coat color under stated assumptions and is not a complete model of all canine pigmentation.

Complementary gene action: 9:7

In complementary gene action, both of two functions are required to obtain a particular phenotype. Imagine a colorless precursor converted into a colorless intermediate by enzyme A. Enzyme B converts the intermediate into a colored product. With sufficient activity at both loci, A_B_ individuals accumulate the colored product. A_bb has the first activity but lacks the second. aaB_ has the second activity but cannot generate the substrate for it. aabb lacks both activities. If the intermediate has no visible color, each of these three mutant classes is scored as colorless.

The expected colored fraction in the dihybrid F2 is 9/16. The remaining 7/16 is colorless, composed of 3/16 A_bb, 3/16 aaB_ and 1/16 aabb. This is called complementary interaction or duplicate recessive epistasis in many textbooks. Classical flower-color crosses in sweet peas illustrate the principle: two pure white lines carrying recessive defects at different required loci can yield colored F1 progeny because the hybrid inherits a functional allele at both loci. The term sweet pea here denotes Lathyrus odoratus, not Mendel's garden pea Pisum sativum. One must not mistake the two historical experimental systems.

To understand the parental cross, write AAbb × aaBB. Each pure line is colorless because one activity is missing. The first parent makes Ab gametes and the second makes aB gametes, producing AaBb offspring. Under the sufficient-activity assumptions, all F1 individuals are colored. Selfing the F1 creates the 9:7 partition. Crossing two white plants from the F2 does not necessarily produce colored progeny: their hidden genotypes matter. AAbb × aaBB restores both functions, whereas AAbb × Aabb leaves every progeny bb and colorless. Thus an observed phenotype does not determine the possible progeny without additional information.

Complementary gene action and a complementation test are related but not identical concepts. Complementary action describes the dependence of a phenotype on multiple functional genes. A conventional complementation test crosses two recessive mutant lines of the same phenotype and asks whether the resulting diploid or heterokaryon restores function. Restoration often suggests defects in different genes, while failure often suggests defects in the same gene. There are exceptions, including intragenic complementation and nonallelic noncomplementation. A 9:7 segregation ratio is consistent with two required functions but does not by itself establish that their proteins catalyze consecutive reactions. They might be components of the same complex or independent functions that converge on one necessary developmental outcome. Direct evidence is needed to discriminate those mechanisms.

Dominant epistasis and dominant inhibition

Dominant epistasis occurs when the presence of at least one allele at one locus masks the observable effects of another locus. A useful model is a dominant inhibitor I and a pigment-type locus C. If I_ prevents pigment production, both I_C_ and I_cc have the same pale phenotype. Among ii individuals, C_ may produce one color and cc a different color. Under independent assortment, pale is (9+3)/16 = 12/16, the first color is 3/16 and the second color is 1/16. The expected ratio is therefore 12:3:1. Classical summer squash fruit color is frequently taught using a dominant white class together with yellow and green classes. The illustrative inhibitor model explains the partition without requiring that the molecular mechanism be assumed from the ratio.

A related two-phenotype model yields 13:3 rather than 12:3:1. Let C_ permit a particular pigment and cc prevent that pigment, while I_ prevents its accumulation in either genotype. A colored phenotype requires iiC_. The other classes are all colorless: I_C_, I_cc and iicc. Their combined weights are 9+3+1 = 13. The colored group has weight 3. Notice the difference from the three-phenotype dominant epistasis model. In the 12:3:1 model, the absence of C in the permissive background generates a distinguishable alternative phenotype. In the 13:3 model, the same absence is indistinguishable from inhibition by I.

The word inhibitor describes a proposed functional effect, not necessarily direct contact between two proteins. The inhibitor could reduce production of a necessary substrate, prevent the transcription of a structural gene, or alter a cell differentiation state needed for pigment accumulation. These alternatives can generate the same phenotypic ratio. Measurements of gene expression, protein activity, intermediate metabolites and restoration of the phenotype can help distinguish them. A high-yield examination trap is to treat 13:3 and 12:3:1 as interchangeable because both contain a dominant epistatic allele. Instead, list the phenotypes assigned to all four genotype classes and count distinct states.

Dominance of an inhibitor also does not automatically imply that its normal biological function is to inhibit. A dominant mutation may introduce a new inhibitory activity or create a protein that interferes with the wild-type product. Conversely, a normally inhibitory gene can have recessive loss-of-function mutations. The mechanism must be specified before mapping genotype symbols to molecular activities.

Duplicate dominant and cumulative gene action

In duplicate dominant action, either of two functions is sufficient to produce a phenotype. A_ B_, A_bb and aaB_ all produce the same state, while aabb produces an alternative state. The expected ratio is (9+3+3):1 = 15:1. An intuitive model is a pair of enzymes that catalyze alternative routes to the same essential product. If either route is functional, enough product accumulates to exceed the scoring threshold. Only the double loss eliminates both routes. This redundancy can explain why a single mutant appears normal even though the affected gene contributes to an important function. The absence of an obvious single-mutant phenotype is not proof that the gene has no biological role.

A 15:1 cross is also compatible with two structurally unrelated genes whose outputs converge at a downstream process. The name duplicate dominant does not demonstrate that the genes arose by gene duplication, that they have similar sequences, or that they encode enzymes of identical specificity. Those are separate molecular and evolutionary hypotheses. Even when the genes are genuine paralogues, their expression may differ between tissues or developmental stages. Redundancy can be complete in one phenotype and incomplete in another.

A different interaction generates three classes with a 9:6:1 ratio. In a threshold model, A_B_ produces a combined effect, either A_bb or aaB_ produces the same intermediate effect, and aabb produces the baseline phenotype. The single-functional classes contribute 3/16 each, giving 6/16. The double-functional class is 9/16 and the double-recessive class 1/16. This is often called duplicate gene action with cumulative effects or additive interaction in classical ratio terminology. A classic teaching example is squash fruit shape, where the two dominant functions together produce a disc-shaped phenotype, either alone a spherical phenotype, and neither an elongated phenotype.

The word additive needs special care. Classical 9:6:1 describes a three-category map of presence or absence of dominant functions. It does not imply that each individual allele contributes an identical numerical increment. A model in which each functional allele independently adds a unit of pigment would distinguish zero, one, two, three or four functional alleles and can yield five F2 categories with a 1:4:6:4:1 distribution. That distribution follows from four independent allele transmissions with probability one half per allele, and is not equivalent to the three-category 9:6:1 map. The latter collapses AA and Aa into one functional class. Quantitative genetics later generalizes these distinctions to continuous traits and estimates contributions on a specified measurement scale.

The Bombay phenotype as a substrate-dependent interaction

The ABO blood group provides an especially clear molecular explanation of a dependency between loci. A and B alleles encode glycosyltransferases that add different terminal sugars to a precursor structure called the H antigen. In the usual red-cell pathway, FUT1 encodes the fucosyltransferase required to generate the H structure. The A transferase adds N-acetylgalactosamine to the H structure, whereas the B transferase adds galactose. Common O alleles lack the corresponding effective transferase activity, so the H structure remains unmodified by A or B transferase.

If red cells fail to form H antigen because of loss of FUT1 function, they cannot produce the usual A or B surface antigens merely by carrying a functional ABO allele. In the simplified classical H/h model, hh masks the expression of the ABO genotype. Classical Bombay phenotype involves absence of A, B and H antigens; secretor-related FUT2 function is relevant in distinguishing Bombay from para-Bombay presentations. Ordinary group O cells retain H antigen, so the two phenotypes must not be equated. This example concerns the red-cell antigen pathway; other tissue-specific routes and secretion of ABH-related substances complicate the complete biological picture.

Consider the idealized cross Iᴬi Hh × Iᴮi Hh, with independent assortment and complete functional sufficiency of H. At ABO the four genotypes IᴬIᴮ, Iᴬi, Iᴮi and ii each occur with probability 1/4. At H the probability of H_ is 3/4. Thus each ordinary ABO phenotype has probability 3/16, while hh has probability 4/16 irrespective of ABO genotype. The phenotype distribution is A:B:AB:ordinary O:H-deficient = 3:3:3:3:4. If a flawed test merges the last two categories, the apparent O class would be 7/16. This arithmetic illustrates how phenotype classification affects genetic inference.

The interaction also explains why an apparently O-like red-cell result cannot automatically establish an ii genotype. A functional allele may be present yet lack the biochemical substrate required to express its antigen. The classroom model is intended to teach genetic reasoning rather than make transfusion decisions; actual blood group identification depends on validated laboratory procedures.

Pathway order, suppressors and experimental discrimination

A mutant phenotype can reveal where a gene is needed in a sequence of events. In a simple biosynthetic model, precursor S is converted by enzyme A to intermediate M, which enzyme B converts into final product P. If A is absent, M and P are not made. If B is absent, M can accumulate while P is absent. A double mutant lacking both activities resembles the A mutant when the readout is intermediate accumulation. When the readout is final product alone, both single mutants and the double mutant resemble one another; final-product scoring does not establish order. Therefore pathway inference depends on the chosen observable.

Supplying a downstream intermediate provides a second approach. If M is taken up and reaches the appropriate compartment, providing M to the A mutant may bypass the missing A activity and restore P when B is functional. Providing M to the B mutant cannot replace B's conversion of M into P. Supplying P directly could bypass either enzymatic block if the final phenotype depends only on P and the supplied product can reach the relevant site. Rescue does not always occur in real cells because membrane permeability, degradation, toxicity, compartmentation and feedback can interfere. Failure to rescue is consequently not definitive evidence that the intermediate lies upstream of the mutation.

In regulatory pathways, epistasis cannot be interpreted by simply asserting that the masking gene must be downstream. For example, assume receptor R positively activates transcription factor T and T activates a reporter. A loss of R prevents T activation; a constitutively active T allele may still induce the reporter despite the R defect. This supports a model in which T acts downstream of R, provided the active T allele truly bypasses R and the reporter faithfully reports the pathway. In another network containing inhibitory steps, the same reasoning requires explicit signs of regulation. A severe upstream biosynthetic block can mask a downstream block when the readout is intermediate accumulation. Published genetic analyses emphasize that the nature of the mutations and the architecture of the pathway determine which epistatic phenotype is observed.

A suppressor is a second genetic change that reduces or reverses a mutant phenotype. Intragenic suppression occurs through a second change in the same gene, whereas extragenic suppression occurs through another gene. A suppressor may restore the activity of an altered protein, bypass a blocked step, remove an antagonist, or increase an alternative function. Restored appearance alone does not demonstrate reversal of the original DNA mutation. If an original mutant a remains present in an individual whose phenotype has been restored by s, molecular genotyping can distinguish suppression from reversion. Modifier genes are loci that alter the expression or severity of another genotype's effect. They need not fully suppress the phenotype; a modifier could increase, decrease or change its manifestation.

To test alternative models, use crosses that give different expected distributions and measurements that discriminate causal states. For instance, two independently assorting recessive white mutations give a colored hybrid under a complementary-function model. A 9:7 F2 is then expected. A competing model might involve one locus controlling pigment production and another controlling transport, with both necessary for visible color. Both predict the same cross outcomes. Pigment measurement in the cells, examination of the location of pigment, or rescue with a downstream compound could distinguish these mechanisms. An examination question that provides only the ratio may justify a genotype-class partition but not the biochemical identity or order of the proteins.

Quantitative model testing and deviations from standard ratios

The chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic compares observed class counts O with expected counts E according to the sum of (O−E) squared divided by E over all classes. Consider an illustrative dataset of 160 F2 progeny with 91 black, 31 brown and 38 yellow individuals. A 9:3:4 model gives expected counts 90, 30 and 40. The contributions are 1/90, 1/30 and 4/40, approximately 0.0111, 0.0333 and 0.1000, so the total is 0.1444. With three categories and no parameters estimated from these data, there are two degrees of freedom. The value is much smaller than the conventional 5% critical value of 5.991. We fail to reject the proposed model; we have not proved it uniquely correct.

A valid comparison requires that the observations be independent and expected counts sufficiently large for the chi-square approximation. If progeny from different families were pooled while the parents had different genotypes, the expected distribution could be wrong. If embryos of one genotype die before the scoring stage, the surviving phenotype frequencies need not match conception frequencies. If the trait is scored at different ages or under different environmental conditions, genotype classes can be misclassified. These biological complications should not be automatically called epistasis. A linkage explanation may also be plausible when joint genotype classes fail to occur at their expected frequencies.

To see the distinction from linkage, imagine a coupling-phase AB/ab double heterozygote with a recombination fraction r. Its AB and ab gametes each have probability (1−r)/2; Ab and aB each have probability r/2. In a testcross to ab/ab, suppose only the A_B_ class has the complementary phenotype. Its probability is (1−r)/2 rather than 1/4 unless r equals one half. With r = 0.20 it is 0.40. In repulsion phase Ab/aB, the same phenotype would have probability r/2 = 0.10. The genotype-to-phenotype rule is identical but the joint transmission probabilities differ. Detailed linkage mapping belongs to the subsequent nodes; the essential lesson is that a modified phenotype ratio can reflect altered genotype frequencies, altered phenotypic mapping, or both.

A practical reasoning sequence therefore starts with the cross, specifies gamete probabilities, forms the genotype distribution, maps each genotype to the phenotypic categories, and checks assumptions against observations. When comparing possible models, choose a cross or independent biochemical measurement for which predictions diverge. Neither an attractive textbook ratio nor a small statistical discrepancy substitutes for the biological evidence needed to infer mechanism.

Conditional probabilities and informative crosses

A purple F2 plant in a complementary gene system has genotype A_B_, but the relative frequencies of its four exact genotypes are not equal. AABB accounts for one of sixteen outcomes, AABb and AaBB for two each, and AaBb for four. Conditional on purple, these probabilities are 1/9, 2/9, 2/9 and 4/9. When selfed, the first class breeds true, the middle two classes give a three-to-one ratio, and the last gives nine-to-seven. Thus the expected proportions of the three types of selfed families are 1/9, 4/9 and 4/9. An all-purple sample of five offspring from an AaBb parent is possible: its probability is (9/16) to the fifth power, approximately 0.0563. A finite family cannot certify that a parent breeds true.

The cross AaBb × aabb produces four equally probable genotype classes. In a complementary model only AaBb is colored, so the expected ratio is 1:3. Crossing AaBb to AAbb instead ensures that every offspring receives A; only B segregates into functional and nonfunctional states, producing a 1:1 ratio. Crossing AaBb to aaBB probes A similarly. These crosses deliberately hold one locus in a permissive state to reveal the effect of the other. In a masking background, even an informative segregation at the second locus may produce no visible segregation.

In the Labrador model, a randomly selected yellow F2 animal has BB, Bb or bb with conditional probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4 because independent assortment leaves the B genotype distribution unchanged by selection on ee. Cross this animal to bbEE. The corresponding expected black fractions are 1, 1/2 and 0. Averaged over the random parental genotype, the expected black fraction is 1/2. This does not mean each family has an equal probability of black and brown offspring: all siblings share the same parental genotype. Observing multiple offspring provides information about that genotype. Distinguish the probability averaged over possible parents from the probability conditional on one particular parent.

In duplicate dominant action, the only exceptional F2 phenotype is aabb. Its appearance establishes that each parent was capable of transmitting an ab gamete, but its absence in a small family does not establish the opposite. The probability of observing at least one exceptional progeny among n independent F2 offspring is 1 minus (15/16) raised to n. At n = 16 this is about 0.644. A ratio describes the distribution from repeated sampling, not an obligation for every family to contain the predicted integer numbers.

Testing causal models and alternative explanations

A genotype-class table specifies which inherited states correspond to a particular phenotype; a pathway model additionally proposes a molecular cause. A two-step biosynthetic pathway, a two-subunit enzyme complex and two essential convergent signaling functions can all produce a 9:7 ratio. To distinguish them, measure intermediate metabolites, relevant enzyme activities, or the effects of selectively restoring a function. A ratio alone cannot establish pathway architecture.

Consider an illustrative system in which wild type contains moderate intermediate M and abundant pigment P, mutant a contains little of either, mutant b contains abundant M but little P, and the double mutant resembles a for intermediate accumulation. This is consistent with A producing M and B consuming M to make P. Alternative explanations include changes in intermediate degradation and feedback regulation. A functional rescue using the normal allele can help establish whether the mutation is causal. Independent enzyme measurements and appropriately matched controls can strengthen the mechanistic inference. Genetic rescue addresses the phenotype in its biological setting; biochemical assays address the function of the gene product.

Suppression provides another way to interrogate a system. A second mutation may restore a lost phenotype by increasing an alternative route, removing an antagonist, or stabilizing a partially functional protein. When a mutation in the original gene is still present, restoration is not a true reversion. An extragenic suppressor may act in the same pathway or affect a separate process that converges on the same observable trait. Genetic interaction does not necessarily imply direct protein binding. The experimental conclusion should be no stronger than the evidence.

Finally, the four-class framework depends on complete dominance at both loci. If A exhibits incomplete dominance and B complete dominance, the initial categories AAB_, AaB_, aaB_, AAbb, Aabb and aabb have weights 3, 6, 3, 1, 2 and 1 out of sixteen. If bb masks the A-dependent phenotype, the last three groups collapse into one category of weight four, giving 3:6:3:4. When penetrance is incomplete, each genotype contributes to more than one possible phenotype according to its penetrance. When viability differs, calculate conception probabilities before renormalizing among surviving offspring. These examples show why standard ratios are not universal rules: they are special cases of genotype probabilities combined with a specified phenotype map.

Misconceptions and reasoning corrections

  1. Epistasis violates segregation

    Normal segregation can coexist with a modified phenotype map.

  2. Dominance is epistasis

    Dominance compares alleles of one locus; epistasis compares effects across loci.

  3. Recessive implies null

    Recessive inheritance does not establish biochemical activity.

  4. 9:7 proves consecutive enzymes

    A protein complex or convergent pathway can yield the same ratio.

  5. 15:1 proves duplicated genes

    Redundancy can occur between unrelated genes.

  6. 9:6:1 implies equal per-allele effects

    The model merges dominant homozygotes and heterozygotes.

  7. 9:4:3 and 9:3:4 are necessarily different

    Class labels determine the interpretation.

  8. Dominant inhibition implies direct binding

    The molecular relationship requires independent evidence.

  9. Epistatic always means downstream

    Mutation type, pathway signs and measured phenotype matter.

  10. Yellow Labrador means no pigment

    The simplified coat model concerns dark eumelanin, not all pigmentation.

  11. Bombay is ordinary O

    Ordinary O retains H antigen; classical Bombay lacks it.

  12. Suppression proves reversion

    A second mutation can restore phenotype while retaining the original mutation.

  13. A normal single mutant proves no function

    Redundant functions can mask single loss.

  14. Small chi-square proves a model

    Failure to reject does not prove uniqueness.

  15. An F2 ratio holds in a testcross

    Genotype frequencies must be derived from the actual cross.

Original practice questions

  1. Part B: Which genotype masks B in the simplified Labrador model?

    Answer and reasoning

    ee The absence of the permissive E function prevents dark coat expression, regardless of B.

  2. Part B: Which class alone is colored in the complementary model?

    Answer and reasoning

    A_B_ Both required functions must be present.

  3. Part B: Which F2 class has a probability of 1/16 under independent assortment?

    Answer and reasoning

    aabb Both loci must be homozygous recessive: 1/4 × 1/4.

  4. Part B: What is the F2 ratio when either dominant function is sufficient?

    Answer and reasoning

    15:1 Three classes totaling 15/16 have the common phenotype.

  5. Part B: What is the usual H-antigen enzyme in red cells?

    Answer and reasoning

    FUT1-encoded fucosyltransferase Its activity produces the substrate for ABO transferases.

  6. Part B: What is the difference between a suppressor and a reversion?

    Answer and reasoning

    Suppression may retain the original mutation. Reversion restores the original sequence in the strict sense; suppression can bypass or compensate.

  7. Part C: An AaBb plant requiring A_ and B_ is crossed to Aabb. What fraction is colored?

    Answer and reasoning

    3/8 At A, Aa × Aa gives 3/4 A_; at B, Bb × bb gives 1/2 B_. Multiply.

  8. Part C: Of purple F2 plants in a 9:7 model, what fraction are double heterozygotes?

    Answer and reasoning

    4/9 AaBb represents 4/16 of all progeny; purple represents 9/16.

  9. Part C: A pathway A then B generates an intermediate before final product. Which mutant can be rescued by the intermediate if uptake is sufficient?

    Answer and reasoning

    The A mutant The intermediate bypasses the first reaction but still requires B.

  10. Part C: For independent BbEe × BbEe, what proportion of all progeny are yellow and BB?

    Answer and reasoning

    1/16 P(ee) × P(BB) = 1/4 × 1/4.

  11. Part C: A coupling heterozygote AB/ab with r=0.2 is testcrossed to ab/ab. Only A_B_ is purple. What fraction is purple?

    Answer and reasoning

    0.4 Only AB gametes produce A_B_, and the parental gamete has probability (1−0.2)/2.

  12. Part C: A 160-progeny F2 is predicted to have 13:3 pale:colored. What counts are expected?

    Answer and reasoning

    130 pale and 30 colored Multiply the total by 13/16 and 3/16. Compare actual observations with these expected counts, not with integer ratio labels.
