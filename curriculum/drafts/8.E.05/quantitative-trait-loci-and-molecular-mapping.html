Why quantitative traits need molecular mapping

A plant breeder may cross two varieties that differ in seed weight and find that their offspring occupy almost every measurable value between the parental means. Some offspring may even exceed both parental values. Unlike a simple cross involving a readily distinguished pair of flower colours, the phenotype does not identify the genotype of a particular plant. A relatively heavy seed can result from favourable alleles, favourable growing conditions or both. A light seed does not necessarily lack every favourable allele. If several loci influence the trait and environmental variation is substantial, grouping the offspring into a few phenotypic categories would conceal much of the information needed to understand inheritance.

Quantitative genetics separates the population variation of a phenotype into components attributable to genetic differences and other sources. Heritability provides an estimate of how much variation is attributable to genetic differences under stated conditions; it does not provide the chromosomal addresses of the contributing variants. Molecular mapping asks a different question. Given the inheritance of identifiable DNA segments and a measured phenotype, which segments are associated with predictable changes in that phenotype? The answer can guide further experiments aimed at discovering individual genes or regulatory sequences.

A quantitative trait locus (QTL) is a genomic locus or region whose genetic variation contributes to variation in a quantitative phenotype in the population being studied. The word locus denotes a genomic position, not necessarily an entire gene. A detected region may contain one causal variant, several linked causal variants, or a regulatory sequence acting on a nearby or distant gene. Since mapping produces an estimate with uncertainty, the term QTL is commonly applied to the region supported by statistical evidence, even before the causative change is known.

A QTL can influence a quantitative trait without being a large-effect locus. For example, a change in the rate of an enzyme might alter a metabolic intermediate slightly, and a change in expression of a transporter elsewhere might modify the same phenotype. The total phenotype reflects the effects of these loci together with environmental and developmental factors. A locus with an important molecular function can remain undetected in a mapping study if the parents carry the same functional allele. Mapping tests the consequences of differences that segregate; it does not compile an inventory of every gene required for a biological process.

Consider two equally tall plants. One might carry a height-increasing allele at the first locus and a height-decreasing allele at the second, while the other has the opposite combination. Recombination between parental chromosomes can assemble two height-increasing alleles in a descendant. This provides a genetic explanation of transgressive segregation. It also illustrates why parental phenotypic differences alone do not determine how many QTL will be found. The investigator needs offspring in which genetic combinations vary, and a sufficiently informative way to identify inherited segments.

Markers, linkage and the experimental cross

A molecular marker is an identifiable DNA feature for which alternative states can be distinguished among individuals. A single nucleotide polymorphism is a position at which DNA sequences differ by one nucleotide. Other marker types include short tandem repeat length variants and restriction fragment length polymorphisms. A useful marker must distinguish the chromosomes whose inheritance the experiment aims to follow. A marker that is identical in both parents provides no information about parental origin in their descendants, even if it is highly polymorphic in the species as a whole.

A marker need not have a biological effect on the trait. If a marker and a causal locus are close to each other on the same chromosome, they are likely to be inherited together through a limited number of meiotic divisions. The marker therefore acts as an indicator of a chromosomal segment. The relationship is analogous to identifying a familiar landmark near a destination, but the analogy has a limitation: the probability that a marker indicates the causal allele depends on recombination and genetic phase, and may differ between populations.

Suppose an inbred high-yielding parent carries a chromosome segment M–Q, while an inbred lower-yielding parent carries m–q. The F1 is heterozygous for both positions, with M and Q in coupling phase. If a fraction r of its gametes is recombinant between the two positions, the expected gamete frequencies are (1−r)/2 for MQ, (1−r)/2 for mq, r/2 for Mq and r/2 for mQ. In a backcross to the mq parent, the marker genotype identifies whether the F1 transmitted M or m. The trait genotype is nevertheless uncertain: an M-bearing gamete has Q with probability 1−r, whereas an m-bearing gamete has Q with probability r.

Let the mean phenotype of a Qq offspring be μ+b and that of a qq offspring be μ. Under a single-locus model with no systematic environmental differences between marker classes, the expected mean among offspring receiving M is μ+(1−r)b. The expected mean among offspring receiving m is μ+rb. Subtracting gives (1−2r)b. This expression explains why a marker further from a QTL generally has a weaker expected association with the trait. If b=8 units and r=0.125, the expected marker contrast is 6 units. At r=0.5 the contrast is zero, even though the underlying locus has an effect of eight units. A negative observed contrast can also reflect reversed phase rather than an inherently negative effect of the marker nucleotide.

The relation is specific to the stated backcross model. It is not a universal correction for regression coefficients in arbitrary populations. In an F2, three QTL genotypes may segregate and dominance may contribute. In a naturally sampled population, allele frequencies and haplotype structure need not match those of a two-parent cross. Recognising the design behind an equation is essential before applying it.

Genetic map distance measures recombination along a chromosome, whereas physical map distance counts base pairs. For short intervals, one percent recombinant gametes approximates one centimorgan. For longer intervals, multiple crossovers can restore the parental marker combination and the observed recombination fraction approaches one half. Different chromosome regions can also have very different recombination rates per megabase. A five-centimorgan QTL region cannot be converted into a fixed number of genes without a physical map and knowledge of local recombination.

An F2 population is produced by intercrossing or selfing appropriate F1 individuals. At an autosomal locus with ordinary Mendelian segregation, its expected genotype proportions are one-quarter of each homozygote and one-half heterozygotes. This enables estimation of an additive contrast between homozygotes and a dominance deviation of heterozygotes. A backcross generally provides only two genotype classes at the locus, so additive and dominance effects cannot usually be disentangled from that cross alone. Recombinant inbred lines contain largely homozygous mosaics of the parental genomes; repeated phenotyping of the same line can improve estimates of line means and permit evaluation across environments. Doubled haploid lines similarly provide fixed genotypes, but their formation and accumulated recombination history differ from recombinant inbred lines.

The design should match the biological question. Replicable lines are valuable for traits that vary with season or growing conditions. F2 individuals allow direct comparison of heterozygotes with homozygotes. Backcrosses can simplify segregation analysis, and near-isogenic lines can help validate a mapped region in a largely shared genetic background. Every design samples a particular set of alleles. Increasing marker density cannot reveal effects of alleles absent from the mapping population.

Phenotype measurement and single-marker analysis

A marker genotype is informative only when its measurement is linked accurately to the phenotype of the same individual or line. Researchers should define the phenotype before collecting data, including the developmental stage, measurement unit and relevant environmental conditions. Plant height measured before flowering need not represent final height. Disease resistance scored under variable pathogen exposure may reflect opportunity for infection instead of biological resistance. A trait that is difficult to measure benefits from repeated observations, appropriate controls and standardisation.

A randomised experiment distributes individuals or lines among positions or experimental blocks without systematically favouring one genotype. This is important when light, nutrient availability or temperature changes across the experimental area. If every line carrying marker A is grown on the sunnier side of a greenhouse, a difference in height cannot cleanly be attributed to genetics. Blocking groups comparable experimental conditions and allows block differences to be modelled. Replicate measurements reduce the influence of random error on the estimated mean, although they do not automatically eliminate systematic measurement bias.

In single-marker analysis, phenotypic measurements are grouped by marker genotype. A simple backcross analysis might compare the average values of the two classes using a t test or an equivalent regression model. In an F2, three genotype means can be compared with an analysis of variance or regression containing additive and dominance terms. The null hypothesis is that marker genotype is unrelated to the expected phenotype, subject to the model assumptions. Rejection provides evidence for association, not proof that the marker itself causes the difference.

A useful F2 parameterisation is Y=μ+ax+dz+ε, where x takes values −1, 0 and +1 for aa, Aa and AA, and z takes values 0, 1 and 0 for the same genotypes. The error ε includes unmodelled sources of variation. The expected means are μ−a, μ+d and μ+a. Thus a is one half of the difference between homozygous means, and d measures the heterozygote's deviation from the midpoint. If mean seed masses for aa, Aa and AA are 18, 23 and 26 mg, respectively, μ=22 mg, a=4 mg and d=1 mg. The heterozygote lies one milligram above the midpoint, indicating partial dominance towards the higher value in this illustrative dataset.

These coefficients have units of the phenotype. They do not directly describe the fraction of phenotypic variance explained. Under ideal F2 genotype frequencies, the mean of x is zero, its variance is one half, the mean of z is one half and its variance is one quarter. The covariance of x and z is zero by symmetry. The variance of the fitted genotypic values therefore equals a²/2+d²/4. For the preceding hypothetical values, it is 8.25 square milligrams. If total phenotypic variance is 25 square milligrams under a compatible model, the genotypic component attributable to that locus is 33 percent of the total. The assumption of ordinary F2 frequencies and the lack of confounding are material to the calculation.

In an ideal random-mating population with a purely additive causal allele of per-copy effect β and frequency p, the allele dosage variance is 2p(1−p). The corresponding additive phenotypic variance is 2p(1−p)β². Consequently, a rare allele with a large effect per copy may explain less population variance than a common allele with a smaller effect. This distinction is especially important when comparing association studies. Effect size measures the expected change per specified genetic contrast; explained variance depends on the effect, genotype frequencies, other loci and the total phenotype variance.

For a fitted regression, the coefficient of determination is R²=1−SSE/SST, where SSE is the sum of squared residuals and SST is the total sum of squared deviations from the observed mean. If an intercept-only fit has SST=1,000 and a marker regression has SSE=800, the marker model explains 20 percent of the sample variation. That does not mean 20 percent of every individual's phenotype was caused by the marker. It describes the fit to variation among individuals in that sample. A selected peak may overestimate explained variance, especially in small samples, and independent prediction should be used when assessing transferability.

Interval mapping, likelihood and genome scans

A single-marker comparison restricts tests to positions at which marker genotypes are observed. A causal variant may lie between two such positions. Lander and Botstein's 1989 work developed a framework for analysing quantitative phenotypes together with linkage maps across genomic intervals. The principle is to treat the QTL genotype at a proposed location as unobserved, while using the genotypes of nearby markers and the genetic map to estimate its probabilities. Testing successive locations gives a profile of relative statistical support along the chromosome rather than a collection of isolated marker tests.

Suppose a backcross has two flanking markers that both indicate the first parent's chromosome. A QTL between them probably has the first parent's allele, although a double recombination or other uncertainty may break the correspondence. If the left marker indicates the first parent and the right indicates the second, a crossover somewhere between the two is plausible. A candidate QTL very close to the left marker is then more likely to have the first parent's allele than a candidate close to the right marker. The probability is not determined solely by physical distance: it derives from the recombination model and observed marker states.

In a simplified backcross with independent recombination in the two subintervals, let r1 be the recombination fraction between the left marker and candidate QTL, and r2 between the candidate QTL and right marker. Given opposite flanking parental origins, the probability that the QTL matches the left origin is (1−r1)r2 divided by [(1−r1)r2+r1(1−r2)]. The numerator describes no switch in the first interval and a switch in the second. The denominator sums the two possible single-switch patterns. With r1=0.05 and r2=0.15, the numerator is 0.1425 and the denominator is 0.185, giving approximately 0.7703. The complementary probability is approximately 0.2297. The calculation is conditional on the observed marker pattern and the simplified independent-switch model.

A likelihood expresses how well a set of parameter values accounts for observed data under a specified statistical model. In interval mapping, an individual's phenotype can be represented by a mixture of possible QTL genotype-specific distributions. For a two-class backcross, the contribution at a candidate location is πf(y; μ+b, σ²)+(1−π)f(y; μ, σ²), where π is the conditional probability of the Q allele based on marker data and f is an assumed density for the phenotype. Combining contributions from independent individuals produces the likelihood. The alternative model allows a QTL effect; the null model constrains the genotype means to equality. The parameters are fitted separately under each model.

The logarithm of odds score, usually called the LOD score, is log10(L1/L0), where L1 and L0 are the maximised likelihoods under the compared models. A score of 3 corresponds to a likelihood ratio of 1,000; a score of 2 corresponds to 100. These ratios refer to the compared models and observed data. They are not posterior probabilities that a QTL exists. They also do not specify the genome-wide false-positive probability. If the natural log likelihoods are −220 and −228, the difference is eight natural-log units, so the LOD is 8/ln(10), approximately 3.47. Since the models are nested and fitted to the same observations, the optimised alternative likelihood cannot be smaller than the null likelihood in the ideal calculation.

For ordinary Gaussian regression with independent observations and residual variance estimated by maximum likelihood under both models, the LOD is (n/2)log10(RSS0/RSS1). Here n is the sample size and RSS is residual sum of squares. If n=100, RSS0=1,000 and RSS1=800, LOD=50log10(1.25), approximately 4.85. This expression is useful for simple marker-regression examples; a full mixture-likelihood interval scan need not be reducible to these same residual sums.

The horizontal axis of a QTL profile usually represents genetic position and the vertical axis represents the test statistic. At the highest local maximum the proposed location has the strongest support under the scanned model. A peak is not a direct physical observation of a gene. Nearby positions may produce similar likelihoods because their conditional genotype probabilities are nearly identical. Several genes within that interval may have plausible functions, and additional recombinants may be required to distinguish them.

A broad peak can arise from limited recombination, modest effect size or environmental noise. It may also be influenced by two linked causal loci. A second nearby peak need not prove that two independent QTL exist because correlated tests can form complex profiles. Comparing models containing one and two loci, together with independent evidence, is preferable to simply counting maxima. A dense marker map improves tracking of existing recombination events but cannot create extra crossover events in the population.

Thresholds, confidence and the multiple-testing problem

A genome scan evaluates many potential positions. Even when no causal QTL segregates, random variation in phenotype can create apparent differences between some genotype groups. If each of 100 independent null tests is conducted at a nominal significance level of 0.05, the probability of at least one false positive is 1−0.95^100, about 0.994. QTL tests at neighbouring positions are typically correlated, so the independence calculation is an illustration rather than a literal genome-wide prediction. It nevertheless explains why a pointwise threshold is insufficient for an unrestricted scan.

Churchill and Doerge's 1994 work introduced an empirical approach based on permutations for determining study-specific thresholds. For an exchangeable set of observations, the association between genotype and phenotype is broken by randomly reassigning the observed phenotypes to individuals while retaining the marker data. The complete scan is repeated on each rearranged dataset. Recording the maximum LOD from each scan yields an empirical distribution of the strongest spurious signals expected under the permutation null. Its upper five-percent tail defines an approximate five-percent genome-wide significance threshold for the specified scan.

The procedure preserves the correlations among markers and the observed marginal phenotype distribution. It should not be applied blindly to observations that are not exchangeable. For example, if genetically identical lines are measured repeatedly, treating every replicate as an independent line would misrepresent the sampling structure. Likewise, experimental blocks or related individuals may require a design-appropriate restricted permutation or alternative method. The basic examination principle is that the null-generating procedure must preserve the structure of the experiment apart from the association being tested.

A genome-wide threshold answers whether the observed statistic is sufficiently extreme under the chosen null scan. It is not an estimate of location uncertainty. One elementary way to summarise location uncertainty is a LOD support interval, such as the region within a specified LOD drop of the maximum. Its endpoints depend on the profile shape and selected drop. A fixed LOD-drop interval does not automatically have exactly 95 percent frequentist coverage for every design. Bootstrap, model-based and other interval procedures have their own assumptions. A marker at the interval centre is not necessarily causal, and an interval with fewer markers is not necessarily biologically smaller.

Power is the probability of detecting a real effect under a stated alternative. It depends on sample size, effect size, residual variation, marker information and the threshold. A small QTL may fail to cross the threshold despite having a genuine effect. Conversely, the detected effects in a low-power experiment tend to be those whose sample estimates are inflated by chance. This selection phenomenon is often called the winner's curse or, in QTL studies, the Beavis effect. Independent validation is important both for whether a signal reproduces and for the size of its estimated effect.

Association mapping and GWAS

Linkage mapping in experimental crosses traces parental chromosome segments transmitted through a known breeding design. Genome-wide association studies typically compare genotypes and phenotypes across individuals sampled from a population. Their power to localise effects relies substantially on linkage disequilibrium, the non-random association of alleles at different positions. Historical recombination may have separated a causal variant from many nearby markers while leaving some strongly correlated. Testing correlated markers can therefore identify a region even if the causal variant was not directly genotyped.

For two biallelic loci, define D=f(AB)−pA pB, where f(AB) is the haplotype frequency and pA and pB are the marginal allele frequencies. The squared correlation r² equals D²/[pA(1−pA)pB(1−pB)] when both loci are polymorphic. If pA=pB=0.5 and f(AB)=0.4, D=0.15 and r²=0.36. This differs from the recombination fraction r of a family linkage analysis. Strong LD can exist over different physical scales in different populations, and weak LD can occur between physically close variants. Neither LD nor physical proximity alone proves a common biological function.

In an introductory additive GWAS, genotype dosage is coded 0, 1 or 2 according to the number of copies of an allele. A quantitative phenotype is regressed on that dosage and relevant covariates. The coefficient estimates the expected phenotype change per extra allele under the model. A p-value evaluates the compatibility of the observed association with the null hypothesis, conditional on the model and assumptions. The statistical evidence is often displayed as a Manhattan plot, with chromosomal position on the horizontal axis and minus log10(p) on the vertical axis. A p-value of 10 to the power −8 corresponds to height eight. This plot is not a LOD profile: the vertical axes represent different statistics.

A major difficulty is population stratification. Consider two hypothetical groups with different diets and different frequencies of a genetic marker. Group one has higher average values of a nutritional phenotype and an allele frequency of 0.8; group two has lower average values and an allele frequency of 0.2. Even if the allele has no effect within either group, a pooled analysis can produce an association. The marker partially identifies group membership, and group membership predicts the phenotype through environmental or other genetic differences. Comparing individuals within ancestry groups, including genetic ancestry covariates, or using suitable relatedness-aware models can mitigate confounding. No single correction guarantees perfect removal of bias.

A conventional threshold of 5×10⁻⁸ is commonly used in human common-variant GWAS, but it is not a universal threshold for every organism, variant set or experimental design. It reflects a stringent multiple-testing standard for a particular context. A significant variant can have a tiny estimated effect if the sample is large enough. A large observed effect can have weak evidence if very few individuals carry the variant. Statistical significance, effect magnitude and biological importance must be evaluated separately.

Polygenic architecture means many loci contribute to phenotype variation. A Manhattan plot with a few conspicuous peaks does not establish that only those loci matter. Numerous smaller effects can remain below the detection threshold. Summing explained variance from associated markers that are in LD can double-count the same signal, so joint models or carefully defined independent signals are needed. Likewise, a polygenic score is an estimate based on genetic associations and may not transfer well to populations with different allele frequencies, LD patterns or environmental contexts.

Linkage mapping and association mapping are complementary rather than interchangeable. Controlled crosses can simplify confounding and permit reproducible environmental experiments, but sample only alleles represented in their parents and often provide broad regions. Association studies can sample much more allelic diversity and exploit historical recombination, but face population structure, relatedness and multiple-testing challenges. The best choice depends on available populations, the trait, the relevant variation and the question being asked.

From an associated region to a causal mechanism

An association identifies statistical evidence linking genetic variation with phenotype variation. To move towards a biological mechanism, researchers must distinguish the causal variant from neighbouring markers, correlated variants and plausible but uninvolved genes. A significant marker within a coding exon can still be a passenger in LD with a regulatory causal variant. A marker in noncoding DNA may itself be functional, for example by altering a regulatory sequence. The physical position alone cannot settle the issue.

Fine mapping seeks to refine the region or set of candidate variants. In experimental populations, additional recombinants that divide the associated segment can help identify a smaller portion necessary for the phenotypic contrast. Near-isogenic lines with overlapping introgressions can be compared to assess whether their differences consistently track a candidate segment. Residual background differences must still be considered. In association studies, examining correlation between variants and comparing evidence across populations can help distinguish variants that are highly correlated in one population but less correlated in another.

Candidate-gene evidence may include expression in the relevant tissue or developmental stage, sequence changes with plausible functional consequences and previous knowledge of a pathway. Such evidence is valuable for prioritisation but not definitive proof. A gene whose transcript abundance changes in high-yield plants may be responding to increased yield-related development instead of causing the difference. Functional testing that isolates the candidate genetic change, combined with suitable controls, provides more direct evidence. Replication in independent crosses or populations strengthens confidence that the association is not a sample-specific artefact.

An example of examination reasoning is a QTL for leaf area that spans three genes. Gene A encodes a known regulator of cell division, gene B encodes an enzyme in carbohydrate metabolism, and gene C has no annotated function. It is reasonable to prioritise gene A for investigation, but not to declare it causal. The leaf may expand through changes in cell number, cell size or developmental duration. Gene B could influence substrate availability and gene C could participate in an unknown regulatory process. A convincing conclusion would require evidence that distinguishes their variants and connects the actual genetic change to the phenotype.

Marker-assisted selection can exploit a validated marker–trait association even before the causative gene is completely understood, especially for a difficult or costly phenotype. However, recombination between marker and QTL can break the predictive relationship. The direction of association may differ between parental backgrounds, and effects may depend on environment. A functional marker that directly assays a proven causal variant avoids recombination between the assayed nucleotide and itself, but its phenotypic predictive value can still vary with genetic background or environmental conditions.

The conceptual endpoint is not a list of genes ranked by p-value. It is a logically connected chain from a well-defined phenotype through genetic variation, experimental or observational association, localisation uncertainty and validation to a plausible biological mechanism. Quantitative genetics and molecular mapping complement each other: the former explains the distribution and inheritance of variation, while the latter provides a means of connecting parts of that variation to genomic positions.
