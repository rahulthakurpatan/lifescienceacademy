# 3.C.07 — Translation Termination, Ribosome Recycling and Polysomes

## 1. The problem of completing a protein

The beginning of protein synthesis establishes which nucleotide will be read first, while elongation maintains the correspondence between successive nucleotide triplets and amino acids. Neither process, however, tells the cell where a useful polypeptide ends. An RNA molecule can contain thousands of nucleotides beyond the sequence needed for a particular protein. If a ribosome simply proceeded until it physically reached the end of the RNA, it would often synthesize a polypeptide with an incorrect carboxyl-terminal extension. The resulting chain might have abnormal folding properties or interfere with a binding interaction that requires a precisely positioned terminus. A reliable mechanism for recognizing the end of a coding region is therefore an integral part of the genetic code, not a secondary housekeeping event.

The cellular solution is to reserve particular codons for an instruction that differs fundamentally from amino acid incorporation. In the standard genetic code, UAA, UAG and UGA generally act as termination signals. When one of these codons occupies the decoding position of a normally translating ribosome, the usual cycle of aminoacyl-tRNA delivery is replaced by a reaction that liberates the previously synthesized polypeptide. Proteins called release factors participate in this change of activity. They recognize the termination signal and promote hydrolysis of the bond linking the chain to its final tRNA. The final amino acid has already entered the chain before the termination signal is recognized. There is no extra amino acid corresponding to an ordinary stop codon.

It is useful to separate completion of the chain from recovery of the machine that produced it. A protein can be chemically complete yet remain attached to its tRNA. After that attachment is broken, the ribosome can still be associated with the messenger and the discharged tRNA. Only after the appropriate complexes are dismantled are the components readily available to support a new round of translation. Thus completion of elongation, peptide release and ribosome recycling are three distinguishable molecular events. They occur in close succession in healthy cells, which can make their differences easy to overlook.

An analogy with a reusable assembly line is helpful if its limits are understood. Finishing the last operation on a product does not automatically remove the product from its holder or reset the assembly apparatus. The holder must be released and the equipment returned to a suitable starting arrangement. In a ribosome, these changes correspond to covalent bond hydrolysis and specific molecular rearrangements. Unlike a mechanical assembly line, the ribosome does not independently know the intended length of a protein: its position on the mRNA and the local interactions with factors determine the next chemical event.

A cell benefits from reuse because the ribosome itself is a substantial ribonucleoprotein complex. Its RNA and protein constituents require considerable biosynthetic investment. Producing a new ribosome for every polypeptide would divert resources toward constructing the synthetic machinery rather than toward synthesizing the proteins required for cellular activity. The existing ribosome pool can instead cycle repeatedly through initiation, elongation, termination and recycling. This arrangement makes protein synthesis a continuous process even though each individual protein molecule has a definite start and finish.

Protein production also becomes more efficient when several ribosomes use the same RNA at once. The collective arrangement is called a polyribosome or polysome. Individual ribosomes within it occupy different positions along the coding region, and each generally constructs its own copy of the encoded chain. A polysome is not a single giant ribosome, nor does each ribosome synthesize just a different piece of one protein. To understand polysomes properly, the student must distinguish the number of ribosomes currently working, the time needed by each ribosome to finish, and the number of completed molecules released per unit time. These quantities can change in different directions.

## 2. Reading-frame position and the meaning of a stop signal

An mRNA is a directional polymer. Its sequence is conventionally written from the 5′ end toward the 3′ end, and the translational apparatus moves in this direction along the coding region. A codon is a sequence of three nucleotides interpreted as one unit in the selected reading frame. There are sixty-four possible triplets because each of three positions can contain any of four standard RNA bases. In the standard code, sixty-one are sense codons that specify amino acids, and three are ordinarily used for termination. This classification describes the genetic code and does not imply that each sense codon has its own completely distinct tRNA.

The reading frame is established during initiation. Imagine an mRNA whose coding sequence begins 5′-AUG-GCU-UUC-UGA-3′. In the frame that starts with AUG, methionine, alanine and phenylalanine are added in that order, followed by termination at UGA. In a bacterial cytosolic system using the usual initiator, the first residue is initially formylmethionine rather than ordinary methionine. The stop triplet is read as a signal but makes no contribution to the residue count. The chain has three residues and two peptide bonds before any subsequent processing removes or alters its amino-terminal group.

A different partition of exactly the same nucleotide string can produce different codons. It follows that the appearance of the letters UAA somewhere in a transcript does not guarantee termination. Those three letters must be aligned in the decoding site according to the active reading frame. If a sequence contains UAA across a boundary between two sense codons, the ribosome does not normally treat that overlapping triplet as an instruction. This feature explains why an insertion or deletion of one or two nucleotides can produce a qualitatively different protein even if the downstream RNA sequence itself is mostly unchanged. The entire downstream grouping of bases is altered.

A stop codon also need not be at the absolute 3′ end of a messenger. In a typical eukaryotic protein-coding RNA, a region called the 3′ untranslated region follows the normal stop signal. It may contain sequences that influence RNA stability, localization or translation. These sequences are not normally incorporated into the polypeptide. The physical end of the RNA can be far from the end of the coding sequence. In a bacterial polycistronic message, another coding region may lie downstream of the first termination signal. A ribosome terminating the upstream open reading frame does not thereby destroy the downstream coding region.

Translation termination must be distinguished from transcription termination. RNA polymerase responds to transcriptional termination mechanisms, not to the conventional genetic-code interpretation of UAA, UAG and UGA. For example, an RNA polymerase transcribing a bacterial gene can continue into a downstream noncoding region after copying the DNA segment corresponding to the translation stop codon. A translation defect caused by mutation of a stop triplet therefore need not change the length of the primary RNA transcript. The relationship between RNA production and protein production is central to the interpretation of gene expression experiments.

The three conventional stop triplets have traditional names. UAG is called amber, UAA ochre and UGA opal or umber. These terms are particularly useful in genetics because certain suppressor mutations are classified according to which termination codon they can read through. The names are not chemical descriptions of the RNA bases and do not imply a color difference among the products. The triplet sequences are the information that matters in an examination question.

A protein's stop codon can be represented on DNA as well as RNA, but the strands and orientation must be stated. For UAG in an mRNA, the corresponding DNA coding-strand sequence is 5′-TAG-3′, whereas the complementary template triplet is 3′-ATC-5′. If a question gives the template in the conventional 5′ to 3′ orientation, it is necessary to reverse the direction as well as complement the bases. Merely replacing T with U is appropriate when converting a coding DNA strand to the matching RNA sequence, not when converting the template without accounting for antiparallel geometry.

## 3. The ribosome immediately before termination

The two-subunit architecture of the ribosome enables coordination between decoding and chemistry. The smaller subunit participates principally in mRNA decoding, whereas the larger subunit contains the peptidyl transferase center. The tRNA binding sites span the functional interface. The aminoacyl or A site normally accepts an incoming charged tRNA during elongation. The peptidyl or P site positions the tRNA carrying the growing polypeptide. The exit or E site accommodates a discharged tRNA on its way out. A simple diagram that assigns each tRNA to one of these sites can be sufficient for many examination problems, although moving tRNAs can also transiently occupy hybrid positions.

Immediately after the final sense codon has been used, the newly extended peptidyl-tRNA initially lies in the A site as a consequence of the peptide bond formation reaction. Translocation subsequently advances the mRNA and transfers this peptidyl-tRNA into the P position. The stop codon, which was previously downstream, is now exposed in the A decoding site. The final sense codon remains aligned with the peptidyl-tRNA anticodon in the P site. This arrangement is the classic pretermination complex.

It is important to identify the ribosomal position of the final amino acid rather than simply state that a stop codon is present somewhere on the messenger. If a diagram shows the stop codon one codon downstream of the A site, normal decoding of the last sense codon has not yet been replaced by termination. If the stop codon is in the A site and the P-site tRNA carries the full-length chain, the appropriate release factor can act. If a diagram shows a stop codon aligned with a normal charged tRNA in the P site, the question may instead be describing an unusual readthrough event or may contain an erroneous representation.

At this stage the polypeptide has been synthesized from its amino terminus toward its carboxyl terminus. The last incorporated residue has a carboxyl group participating in an ester linkage to the ribose at the terminal adenosine of tRNA. The polypeptide is not attached to the mRNA by this bond. The mRNA serves as the information-bearing template, and the tRNA serves as the adaptor and temporary covalent carrier. Recognition of the stop signal has to communicate across the ribosome from the decoding site to the location of this ester bond.

The nascent polypeptide occupies the exit tunnel to an extent determined by its length and structure. Much of a sufficiently long protein can already extend outside the ribosome and begin to fold while the newest portion remains near the peptidyl transferase center. The term nascent chain therefore does not mean that the entire protein is hidden inside the ribosome. Release gives the newly made chain freedom from its tRNA attachment, but folding or targeting can have begun long beforehand. Conversely, a released polypeptide is not necessarily a fully mature, active protein. Its later maturation will be considered in subsequent chapters.

The immediate substrate for termination is thus an assembled ribosome carrying mRNA and a peptidyl-tRNA, rather than an isolated RNA codon. A free stop triplet in solution lacks the spatial relationship to a peptidyl-tRNA that gives the signal its physiological meaning. This fact underlies biochemical experiments that use defined ribosomal complexes. Researchers can alter which triplet occupies the decoding site while holding the peptidyl substrate constant, thereby testing whether a particular factor recognizes a particular signal.

## 4. Why ordinary stop codons are interpreted by proteins

Most sense codons are decoded through base pairing with a complementary or wobble-compatible tRNA anticodon. The tRNA molecule has already been charged with the amino acid associated with its identity. Once accepted at the ribosome, it delivers an amino group that can participate in extension of the chain. Termination presents a different chemical requirement: the cell must not add another amino acid but must instead make the finished carboxyl terminus available. A normal aminoacyl-tRNA is therefore not the appropriate substrate for the reaction.

Release factors solve both the recognition problem and the change in chemistry. They occupy the functional region where a charged tRNA would otherwise operate and establish interactions with the stop codon and ribosomal components. They also extend into the large-subunit catalytic region, enabling the peptidyl-tRNA ester to be hydrolyzed. The two ends of this action must be coordinated because hydrolysis at an arbitrary sense codon would prematurely release a chain, while efficient binding at a stop codon without hydrolysis would simply leave the ribosome arrested.

The action of a release factor illustrates a broader principle of molecular recognition: the interpretation of an RNA sequence need not involve another RNA molecule that forms a complete anticodon duplex. Proteins can discriminate among nucleotide bases through combinations of hydrogen bonding, steric fit, local electrostatics and stabilization of particular nucleotide conformations. The ribosome supplies part of the recognition environment. A student's mental picture of the genetic code should therefore include protein-mediated recognition alongside ordinary codon–anticodon pairing.

Bacterial class I release factors and the eukaryotic class I factor eRF1 contain conserved structural elements that allow them to coordinate decoding with hydrolysis. Although they share an overall functional strategy, the detailed recognition apparatus differs between the bacterial and eukaryotic systems. Similar factor names should not be taken as proof of identical evolutionary origin or identical mechanisms. The useful common feature for the syllabus is the coupling of a stop-sensitive recognition event with a chemical reaction in the peptidyl transferase center.

There is an important distinction between the absence of a normal cognate aminoacyl-tRNA and the active presence of a termination factor. If a stop codon were left without either type of substrate, the ribosome would tend to pause rather than automatically generate a released protein. The availability of a suitable release factor is therefore necessary for efficient normal termination. An organism can suffer from a defect in termination despite having an entirely normal set of stop codons in its genes. The codons encode an instruction, but the decoding machinery implements that instruction.

Stop suppression shows that the meaning of a termination codon depends on the competing cellular machinery. A mutant tRNA may acquire an anticodon capable of recognizing a stop codon and deliver its attached amino acid there. In such a case the normal stop instruction competes with continued elongation. The unusual tRNA is not part of the ordinary standard-code termination mechanism. Its action is important because it gives an experimental way to test whether a stop is merely a passive absence of sense decoding or a site of active competition.

## 5. The chemistry of releasing the chain

During elongation, the reaction at the peptidyl transferase center transfers the existing chain from one tRNA to another. The incoming aminoacyl-tRNA provides a nucleophilic amino group, and formation of a new amide linkage lengthens the chain. The energy required to activate the amino acid was supplied during the preceding aminoacylation reaction. The immediate elongation chemistry does not require a separate ATP molecule to be hydrolyzed directly in the peptidyl transferase center.

Termination instead substitutes water for the incoming amino group as the reacting species. The ester bond that joins the carboxyl end of the completed chain to the terminal ribose of its P-site tRNA is cleaved by hydrolysis. In simplified chemical notation, peptidyl–C(O)–O–tRNA plus H₂O yields peptidyl–COOH plus HO–tRNA. At physiological pH the released carboxyl group is often deprotonated, and the protonation states depend on the local environment. The simplified equation represents the bond changes rather than every proton transfer in the catalytic mechanism.

This reaction does not split a peptide bond between two amino acids. The sequence of residues built during elongation is preserved. If the final amino acid is phenylalanine, hydrolyzing the ester gives a chain ending in phenylalanine; it does not remove phenylalanine to generate a shorter chain. The bond that is lost is the covalent linkage between the protein and its adaptor. The difference matters when a problem asks whether the final product has one less residue than the number of translated sense codons.

A conserved Gly-Gly-Gln sequence, abbreviated GGQ, is associated with the hydrolytic function of class I release factors. Its placement at the peptidyl transferase center promotes the environment needed for cleavage of the ester. The amino acids in this motif are part of the release-factor protein; they are not inserted into the growing chain. One should not draw the GGQ motif as if it were a tRNA carrying glutamine to a stop codon. The motif supports catalysis in cooperation with the ribosomal active site.

The decoding region and the GGQ-containing region of a release factor are functionally separable. This separation explains how a mutation can preserve recognition of a stop codon yet interfere with peptide release. Such a mutant might remain associated with the termination complex because it binds the correct site, but the chain would remain covalently attached to its tRNA. A mutation that primarily disrupts codon recognition could instead reduce the factor's recruitment even when its catalytic motif remains chemically competent. Measurements of binding and peptide release should therefore be considered separately when diagnosing defects.

The chemical event and the energy-consuming movements of the translation apparatus also need to be distinguished. In normal termination the ester bond is hydrolyzed by reaction with water; an additional GTP molecule is not required as a stoichiometric reactant in that bond-cleavage equation. GTP hydrolysis by accessory factors can nevertheless be important for the correct sequence, efficiency and turnover of termination. ATP-dependent steps can subsequently help dismantle the eukaryotic posttermination complex. Energy accounting becomes misleading if all these events are described as a single unspecified hydrolysis.

Hydrolysis also changes the chemical availability of both products. The polypeptide can now diffuse or continue interacting with folding or targeting machinery without its covalent tRNA attachment. The discharged tRNA has a free terminal ribose hydroxyl group and can eventually be charged again by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. This does not mean that the tRNA immediately leaves the ribosome in the same instant as ester cleavage. The recovery of the adaptor and the recovery of the ribosomal subunits involve subsequent structural changes.

## 6. Historical evidence for stop-specific release factors

The genetic identification of termination signals raised a biochemical question that could not be answered by reading a sequence table alone. Did the appearance of a nonsense codon merely prevent further amino acid incorporation, or did a separate factor actively release the existing chain? Genetic observations of shortened proteins were compatible with termination, but a shortened product alone did not establish the chemical catalyst. Cell-free experiments provided a way to isolate this stage from the other steps of protein synthesis.

A classical study by Scolnick, Tompkins, Caskey and Nirenberg, published in 1968, demonstrated bacterial release factors with different specificities for terminator codons. The important conceptual result was that terminator triplets were not interchangeable for every individual protein factor. The bacterial decoding system could be divided into overlapping recognition classes. A component that promoted release in response to UAG also acted on UAA, whereas a component acting on UGA also recognized UAA. This was evidence for specific protein-mediated interpretation of the termination signals, rather than a simple nonspecific instability of ribosomes encountering any triplet without a normal tRNA.

The logic of these experiments can be reconstructed with a deliberately simplified teaching model. Consider preparing ribosomes with an attached, labeled peptidyl-tRNA substrate. Introduce a suitable triplet into the decoding environment and add a purified candidate release factor. If the labeled peptide is freed from its tRNA, this indicates that the reaction has gone beyond merely stopping elongation. Repeating the same comparison with different triplets and factors establishes a specificity pattern. A control lacking the factor determines the background level of spontaneous release, and a sense triplet provides a control for inappropriate hydrolysis.

There are limitations to such an assay. In a minimal system using a very short peptide substrate, the measured reaction may not capture all structural features of a normal nascent chain. Factor concentrations and ionic conditions can differ from those in cells. A positive release reaction establishes the competence of a component under the experimental conditions, but it does not alone reveal the exact rates of its association and dissociation in the cytoplasm. Classical reconstitution is powerful because it identifies necessary or sufficient components in a defined system, not because it reproduces every detail of cellular life.

Further studies separated terminator recognition from peptidyl-tRNA hydrolysis and examined the requirements imposed by the ribosome and protein factors. This conceptual separation was especially valuable: if a factor recognizes a termination triplet, one still needs to establish how that recognition is transmitted to the catalytic region. Later structural work placed release factors in the ribosome and helped explain how their domains span the decoding center and the peptidyl transferase center. Structural observations provide positions and conformations, while biochemical measurements provide the functional effects of perturbing these interactions. Neither type of evidence alone is as informative as the two considered together.

The historical experiments also illustrate the difference between an artificial teaching dataset and actual published observations. A numerical problem might assign a release yield of eighty percent to one factor–codon combination and five percent to another. Unless those numbers come from a cited experiment, they should be explicitly described as hypothetical. The qualitative specificity relationship can be based on established evidence without pretending that invented numbers were measured by the historical researchers.

## 7. Bacterial stop recognition: RF1 and RF2

In the canonical bacterial system, two class I release factors share the task of interpreting the three standard stop codons. RF1 recognizes UAA and UAG. RF2 recognizes UAA and UGA. UAA is therefore recognized by either of the two factors, whereas UAG and UGA each depend on a different class I factor in the standard model. In bacteria such as Escherichia coli, the genes prfA and prfB encode RF1 and RF2, respectively. These gene names are useful for connecting molecular genetics to the interpretation of mutant phenotypes.

The overlapping specificity immediately generates several predictions. If RF1 function is selectively diminished while RF2 remains active, termination at UAG should be directly affected. Termination at UAA may still occur through RF2, although it need not remain quantitatively normal because cellular factor concentrations and their individual rates also matter. UGA recognition does not directly require RF1 in the canonical specificity model. Conversely, selective loss of RF2 should disproportionately compromise UGA termination. The system's partial redundancy at UAA does not mean that the factors are completely interchangeable.

This specificity can be understood by comparing the second and third positions of the codons. All three begin with U. UAA and UAG share A at the second position, while UAA and UGA share A at the third position. The protein recognition domains distinguish permitted arrangements of these bases through their interactions with the ribosomal decoding center. This is not a simple RNA wobble rule. RF1 and RF2 do not carry anticodon loops that pair with codons in the same manner as charged tRNAs. Although compact sequence motifs in bacterial factors are often used as memory aids, complete specificity arises from a broader structural context.

The functions of RF1 and RF2 are also not restricted to identifying the letters of the stop signal. Once a productive interaction has formed, the hydrolytic domain must act on the P-site peptidyl-tRNA. A binding-competent but catalytically defective factor can occupy the termination site without releasing the peptide efficiently. In that situation a second functional factor could potentially compete at UAA, but competition with the defective factor may slow the reaction. At UAG, RF2 cannot simply substitute for RF1 on the basis of being catalytically active.

A bacterial culture containing a defective release factor may show slow growth even if most of its proteins remain correctly encoded at the DNA level. Ribosomes accumulate at problematic stop sites, the available pool of free subunits can decline, and some abnormal products can arise from competing near-cognate decoding or quality-control mechanisms. Growth is therefore a downstream integrated phenotype. To establish that the immediate cause is defective termination, an experiment needs additional evidence such as codon-specific effects in reporter constructs or retention of peptidyl-tRNA in appropriately defined ribosomal complexes.

The exact consequences of reducing release factor concentration depend on demand. A growing bacterial cell translates many messages, and release factors have to be used repeatedly. A factor molecule that remains trapped after catalysis cannot efficiently serve other ribosomes. The balance of recruitment, hydrolysis and factor departure therefore influences the apparent availability of RF1 or RF2 even if their total cellular protein concentrations remain unchanged. This leads to the role of the class II bacterial release factor, RF3.

## 8. RF3 and turnover of bacterial release factors

RF3 is a GTP-binding protein. Unlike RF1 and RF2, it does not provide the standard class I stop-codon specificity or the GGQ-dependent hydrolytic function. Its principal role in the canonical termination cycle is to facilitate turnover of the class I factors on posttermination ribosomes. A release factor that has already promoted hydrolysis must subsequently vacate the ribosome so that later events can proceed. RF3 assists this transition through nucleotide-dependent conformational interactions with the ribosome.

For examination purposes the sequence can be represented as recruitment of RF1 or RF2 at the stop codon, release of the peptide, RF3-assisted departure of the class I factor, and preparation of the posttermination complex for ribosome recycling. This sequence expresses the dominant functional distinctions without asserting that every molecular binding event follows one absolutely invariant timing order. In living systems the relative rates and nucleotide states of accessory factors can influence the pathway, and bacterial species differ in their complement of translation factors.

The existence of RF3 makes clear that release factor recycling and ribosome recycling are related but different tasks. The first returns RF1 or RF2 to the soluble pool so another termination event can use it. The second dismantles the residual ribosome–mRNA–tRNA complex and restores the components needed for initiation. If a question asks which bacterial factor promotes release of RF1 and RF2, RF3 is the expected answer. If the question instead asks which factor cooperates with EF-G in splitting the posttermination ribosome, the expected answer is ribosome recycling factor, RRF.

A simple kinetic argument explains the biological effect of RF3. If a class I release factor spends a large fraction of its lifetime associated with completed termination complexes, fewer molecules are available to respond to new stop signals. Reducing the dwell time increases the number of termination events a fixed population of factors can support. This is analogous to increasing the turnover of an enzyme without necessarily increasing its concentration. An alteration of the factor departure rate can thus affect total protein production even when the chemical step that releases an individual peptide remains normal.

RF3 is not universally present in all bacteria, so the classic three-factor scheme should not be elevated to a claim that every bacterial species requires precisely these three proteins. RF1 and RF2 can catalyze peptide release without RF3 in appropriate defined systems. RF3 is best understood as a facilitator of efficient factor turnover and termination dynamics in organisms that possess it. The fact that GTP hydrolysis accompanies this process should never be used to infer that RF3 itself reads UAA, UAG or UGA.


## 9. A complete bacterial termination cycle

A useful way of studying the bacterial reaction is to begin with an elongation complex that has completed translocation after incorporating its final amino acid. Its P-site tRNA carries the finished chain and its A site contains one of the three normal stop codons. The E site may contain a discharged tRNA temporarily, depending on the state of the complex. The important point is that the A site no longer calls for normal aminoacyl-tRNA delivery. The ribosome is ready to exchange its elongation substrate for a release factor.

Suppose the stop is UAG. RF1 is the appropriate class I factor. Its initial engagement with the ribosome allows the recognition domain to sample the bases and their arrangement. A productive recognition event positions the factor to act in the large subunit. The GGQ-containing region reaches the peptidyl transferase center, where it helps establish a hydrolysis-competent configuration. The ester joining the chain to tRNA is then cleaved. The product may still interact noncovalently with the ribosome or other cellular molecules, but it is no longer joined to the adaptor by the ester.

The same overall chemical reaction occurs at UGA with RF2, despite the difference in recognition specificity. The peptide length and the identity of its final residue are determined by the sense codons preceding the stop. If two otherwise identical mRNAs differ only in whether the stop is UAG or UGA, they should produce identical amino acid sequences under accurate standard-code termination. Differences in the amount of protein or the proportion of readthrough products can nevertheless arise because the two factors are not present in identical amounts or because their recognition kinetics differ.

At UAA, either RF1 or RF2 may carry out the class I function. The stop signal does not ordinarily require both factors to bind successively to the same ribosome. Either factor can support the release reaction. If a simplified experiment contains both factors, their combined activities depend on their effective concentrations and their competition for the decoding site. The observed release rate is not necessarily the arithmetic sum of two independently measured maximum rates because both factors act on the same population of ribosomes.

Once peptide release has occurred, the posttermination complex contains a different chemical substrate from the pretermination complex. The P-site tRNA is no longer peptidylated. This distinction provides a particularly useful biochemical test for the stage of a translation defect. Accumulation of ribosomes with chains still esterified to tRNA indicates an obstacle before or at release; accumulation of ribosomes with discharged tRNA and little attached peptide can instead indicate an obstacle after release. In either case the messenger may remain associated with the ribosome, so merely detecting ribosome-bound RNA does not diagnose the chemical stage of arrest.

RF3 can assist the departure of the class I factor, where present. This creates access to the ribosome for subsequent recycling machinery. Its GTPase cycle involves nucleotide-associated conformational transitions, which make factor turnover responsive to the physical state of the ribosome. It is reasonable at introductory level to describe GTP hydrolysis as supporting efficient RF3 cycling, but the detailed order of nucleotide exchange, conformational changes and factor departure is more complex than a simple one-step power stroke. The key exam distinction is between recognition and hydrolysis by RF1 or RF2 and turnover assistance by RF3.

Consider a hypothetical reconstituted bacterial system in which RF1 is present but EF-G is omitted after formation of a pretermination complex. If the stop codon is already positioned in the A site, it may still undergo peptide release. EF-G is not the class I release factor. However, efficient subsequent recycling of the posttermination ribosome requires a different action of EF-G together with RRF. If EF-G were omitted earlier, while the final sense codon was still in the A site, that omission could prevent the translocation needed to establish the termination complex in the first place. The timing of the perturbation is therefore essential. A factor can appear necessary for release in one experimental design because it is necessary to produce the substrate on which release acts, even though it is not directly responsible for the hydrolytic event.

This principle extends to all translation factor questions. A complete translation assay is a linked series of reactions, and blocking an earlier event prevents downstream events even when their own catalysts remain functional. A stage-specific experiment instead supplies a substrate that is already past the earlier step. Both designs are useful, but they answer different questions. A student who can identify the substrate state can distinguish direct mechanistic requirements from indirect dependence.

## 10. Eukaryotic stop recognition and the role of eRF1

Cytosolic translation in eukaryotes uses the same broad genetic-code convention but a different arrangement of release factors. One class I release factor, eRF1, normally recognizes UAA, UAG and UGA. This contrasts with the two overlapping bacterial specificity classes. Thus, a table asking which eukaryotic protein recognizes UGA should not be answered by transplanting bacterial RF2 into the eukaryotic system. The standard eukaryotic answer is eRF1 for each of the three stop codons.

Eukaryotic ribosomes are conventionally described as 80S particles assembled from 40S and 60S subunits. The decoding activity of the small subunit and the peptidyl transferase activity of the large subunit remain organized around corresponding functional sites. The chemical substrate for termination is still an ester joining the completed chain to a P-site tRNA. Consequently, the fundamental distinction between sense decoding and peptide release remains valid even though the factors and the overall ribosomal architecture differ.

eRF1 is commonly described in terms of three major domains. The amino-terminal domain participates in stop-codon recognition. The middle domain bears the GGQ motif required for efficient peptidyl-tRNA hydrolysis. The carboxyl-terminal domain contributes to interactions with eRF3 and other components. A schematic containing these three regions allows the student to make predictions without memorizing an extensive list of residues. A mutation in one domain may alter one functional property disproportionately, although the folding and interactions of the complete protein must also be considered.

The shape and arrangement of eRF1 allow it to span the functional distance between the decoding site and the large-subunit catalytic region. In the early state of recruitment the hydrolytic region is not simply assumed to be fully inserted into its final position. Recognition and rearrangement make it possible to couple the signal to hydrolysis. Such coupling protects against the undesirable possibility of releasing a chain at an ordinary sense codon. The correct response is a dynamic consequence of interactions among eRF1, the ribosome, the stop triplet and its immediate environment.

Recognition is often influenced by surrounding nucleotides. The stop triplet is central to the instruction, but its neighborhood can affect the probability and efficiency of termination. A sequence immediately downstream of a stop can stabilize or destabilize recognition arrangements, and nearby RNA or protein interactions can alter the effective competition with decoding tRNAs. Therefore a mutation outside the three stop bases can sometimes change readthrough. This should not be mistaken for a claim that every nucleotide surrounding every stop signal forms a fixed universal extension of the genetic code. Context effects vary between sequences and organisms.

A loss-of-function alteration in eRF1 is potentially broad in its direct consequences because the same class I factor normally recognizes all three stop codons. The consequences need not be numerically identical for every stop context. An alteration of one recognition property can impair one signal more severely than another, and a partially active protein may retain sufficient activity under some conditions. A complete mechanistic interpretation should distinguish total removal of the factor from a selective mutation, a reduction of concentration or a change in binding affinity.

Many proteins have similar amino acid sequences at their carboxyl termini in different eukaryotic species because correct termination and subsequent folding have been preserved during evolution. The role of eRF1 is to prevent additional unencoded residues from being appended under normal circumstances. This contribution is different from the specificity of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, which ensures that the adaptor carries the correct amino acid, and from decoding proofreading during elongation. All three mechanisms contribute to the fidelity of protein production but act at different stages.

## 11. eRF3, GTP and the sequence of eukaryotic peptide release

eRF3 is an accessory GTPase that works with eRF1. The canonical eukaryotic cytosolic pathway is often described in terms of delivery or recruitment of eRF1 together with GTP-bound eRF3. This association places the stop-recognition machinery in a condition favorable for productive termination. Successful recognition is coupled to the GTPase cycle, enabling a rearrangement in which the hydrolytic domain of eRF1 reaches the peptidyl transferase center and supports release of the polypeptide.

eRF3 should not be labeled the factor that independently recognizes each stop codon. That function belongs principally to eRF1 in cooperation with the ribosome. Nor should eRF3 be treated as the enzyme that directly cuts the ester linkage. Its influence is mediated through the dynamics of the termination complex. The factor eRF3 and the factor RF3 both participate in translation termination and both bind guanine nucleotides, but their principal positions in the canonical mechanisms are different. Bacterial RF3 mainly promotes removal of class I release factors, whereas eukaryotic eRF3 assists eRF1 in productive recognition and release.

A hypothetical experiment can illustrate this difference. Imagine that a eukaryotic translation extract is supplied with pretermination complexes and eRF1. Peptide release can occur under suitable reconstituted conditions, but addition of functional eRF3 and GTP can increase the efficiency of the normal reaction. A mutation of eRF3 that disrupts its nucleotide cycle may therefore impair termination even when eRF1's GGQ motif is intact. Conversely, adding more eRF3 cannot necessarily restore an eRF1 whose hydrolytic domain is incapable of performing its function. The accessory factor does not replace the class I catalyst.

Nonhydrolyzable nucleotide analogues can be informative because they allow the investigator to distinguish a nucleotide-binding state from completion of a normal nucleotide cycle. If a complex forms in the presence of a GTP analogue but peptide release is delayed, the observation suggests that nucleotide hydrolysis or a subsequent rearrangement is important for efficient progress under those conditions. Interpretation requires a control with authentic GTP and comparison of binding with product release. An analogue may also alter binding affinity or stabilize unusual conformations, so its effects should not be interpreted as an exact picture of the unperturbed kinetic pathway.

After hydrolysis of the peptidyl-tRNA bond, eRF1 can remain on the ribosome. This persistence is not simply a failure of the termination reaction. In eukaryotic cells the remaining factor provides a functional connection to the machinery that splits the posttermination ribosome. It is therefore inappropriate to assume that eRF1 must completely depart before the next stage begins. The bacterial pathway involves a different handover to RRF and EF-G, which makes the timing of factor departure an important comparative point.

The most useful conceptual sequence for eukaryotic cytosolic termination is thus stop exposure, eRF1/eRF3 recruitment and productive decoding, GTP-dependent rearrangements, peptidyl-tRNA hydrolysis and formation of a posttermination complex capable of undergoing recycling. This sequence is a functional model. It is not a claim that every ribosome in every organism exhibits a completely identical intermediate with the same lifetime. Examination reasoning is improved by learning what each factor accomplishes and what substrate each successive event requires.

## 12. Ribosome recycling as a distinct biological task

The termination reaction releases the finished chain but leaves the machinery in an arrangement that was constructed for reading a particular coding sequence. The small subunit still engages the messenger, and a discharged tRNA can remain aligned with a codon. The larger subunit is still associated with the small subunit. Simply waiting for every interaction to dissociate spontaneously would make the availability of these components depend on the passive stability of a large molecular complex. Cells instead employ factors that help disassemble and reset the posttermination ribosome.

Recycling does not ordinarily mean degradation of the ribosome into individual ribosomal proteins and nucleotides. The subunits are preserved as functional ribonucleoprotein particles. The messenger may remain intact and be translated again. The discharged tRNA may be reused after recharging. These are separate macromolecular resources, and productive recycling allows them to re-enter their appropriate pools. A common conceptual error is to assume that termination destroys the mRNA because the ribosome reaches a stop codon. In reality, a stable message can support many successive rounds of synthesis.

The functional importance of recycling becomes especially clear in a cell with a limited ribosome pool. Let the total population be partitioned among free initiation-competent subunits, initiating complexes, elongating complexes and completed complexes awaiting dismantling. The exact categories overlap during transient states, but the principle is that a ribosome trapped in one stage cannot simultaneously participate in a new translation event. If recycling becomes slower, the fraction held in posttermination complexes can rise, leaving fewer resources for initiation. The effect can reduce protein output across many messages even when their individual initiation sequences remain intact.

The need for active rearrangement also follows from the structural characteristics of the ribosome. Its subunits associate through numerous noncovalent contacts. Their stability depends on ionic conditions, nucleotide interactions, bound tRNAs and the conformational state of the complex. Certain laboratory treatments, such as magnesium chelation, can dissociate ribosomal particles, but this does not reproduce physiological recycling. In the cell the goal is controlled disassembly that preserves the integrity and future competence of both subunits. Dedicated factors act in this context rather than simply destroying all stabilizing interactions indiscriminately.

The distinction between a factor that promotes splitting and one that prevents reassociation is experimentally important. A factor could make separated subunits remain apart without directly increasing the rate of initial splitting. In a measurement taken only after a long incubation, both effects might increase the apparent amount of free subunits. A time-resolved experiment can distinguish them by measuring the early transition and the subsequent stability of products. This distinction is particularly relevant to the bacterial initiation factor IF3, whose anti-association function helps maintain the recycled small subunit in an appropriate state.

## 13. Bacterial recycling: RRF, EF-G and IF3

Bacterial ribosome recycling factor, abbreviated RRF, interacts with the posttermination complex. RRF is distinct from the stop-recognition factors RF1 and RF2 and from the accessory factor RF3. Similar abbreviations often cause mistakes, especially when a student memorizes names without connecting them to substrates. The biological substrate of RRF is normally a ribosome that has already released its polypeptide. It does not identify the stop codon and does not catalyze the release of the peptide from its tRNA.

RRF has an overall shape that recalls certain features of a tRNA, which is useful when considering how it occupies regions of the ribosome normally used by translation substrates. Nevertheless, it is a protein, lacks a nucleotide anticodon and is not charged by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. Its structural resemblance to an adaptor reflects the constraints of interacting with a common molecular machine rather than identity of chemical function. The ribosome can recognize compatible three-dimensional shapes without requiring every ligand to belong to the same chemical class.

The GTPase EF-G cooperates with RRF to promote disassembly of the posttermination complex. EF-G is already familiar from elongation, where it drives or facilitates translocation of tRNAs and mRNA. Its participation in recycling does not mean that ordinary elongation simply continues past the stop. The presence of RRF and the discharged state of the ribosome provide a different molecular context. The same factor can support different rearrangements when the other components of its substrate complex change.

The result of the RRF/EF-G pathway is separation of the large and small subunits and recovery of the components associated with the posttermination complex. The 50S subunit is released, and the 30S subunit can be cleared of its remaining tRNA and messenger. Different experimental conditions can affect the apparent ordering of tRNA release, mRNA release and subunit dissociation. For standard examination purposes it is sufficient to distinguish the major activities rather than insist that every product leaves in one fixed universal order.

IF3 is particularly relevant after the subunits have been separated. It binds the bacterial small subunit and disfavors premature reassociation with the large subunit. It also contributes to preparation for a new initiation event. A useful mechanistic distinction is that RRF and EF-G promote splitting, while IF3 helps maintain an initiation-competent small-subunit state by preventing reassociation and can aid clearance of residual ligands. IF3 should not be called the principal stop-codon recognition factor or the enzyme that hydrolyzes peptidyl-tRNA.

Imagine two experiments that use preformed bacterial posttermination complexes. In one, RRF and EF-G are supplied without IF3. In the other, the same factors are supplied with IF3. Time-resolved observations could reveal splitting in both experiments but more persistent separated subunits in the second. Such a result would support a product-stabilizing or anti-association role for IF3. It would not justify assigning IF3 the entire splitting reaction. This reasoning parallels the distinctions revealed by kinetic analysis of bacterial recycling in the literature.

The use of GTP by EF-G during recycling adds to the nucleotide expenditure associated with a complete translation cycle. The exact count assigned to a protein-production problem depends on what the problem includes. Some elementary exercises count only aminoacylation and elongation, whereas more complete exercises count initiation, final peptide release and recycling separately. When an exam question specifies the number of GTP-dependent transitions, that specification takes precedence over a memorized total. The chemical bond-cleavage event should still be distinguished from the energy-dependent rearrangements that surround it.

A defect in RRF provides a different prediction from a defect in RF1 or RF2. If RRF is defective, chain release may proceed normally at all three stop codons, but ribosomes can remain associated with completed messages or discharged tRNAs for too long. The main direct defect is failure of efficient posttermination disassembly rather than recognition of a particular stop codon. Reduced availability of free subunits may secondarily inhibit new translation. A reporter measurement made only at steady state could show reduced overall protein accumulation in both mutants, but biochemical assays distinguishing attached and released peptide would help identify which step is affected.

## 14. Eukaryotic recycling and the ATPase ABCE1

The principal cytosolic eukaryotic recycling pathway uses ABCE1, an ATP-binding cassette family protein also known as Rli1 in budding yeast. This factor acts on the posttermination ribosome in coordination with eRF1. Unlike the bacterial combination of RRF and EF-G, the eukaryotic pathway depends on an ATPase working in an arrangement that includes the class I release factor after peptide release. ABCE1 is not the same type of ATP-binding cassette protein as a typical membrane transporter; its role here concerns ribosomal dynamics.

The association of eRF1 with the posttermination complex creates a useful handover. The factor that connected the stop signal to ester hydrolysis now helps establish a configuration that can recruit or engage the recycling machinery. ABCE1 uses nucleotide-associated structural transitions to promote separation of the 60S and 40S subunits. At an introductory level, this is appropriately described as ATP-dependent ribosome recycling. More detailed studies distinguish ATP binding from hydrolysis and resetting of the factor, so one should avoid stating a universal ATP stoichiometry for the splitting event unless the model in question specifies it.

As the large subunit is separated, the residual small-subunit complex can still retain messenger RNA and discharged tRNA. Additional factors facilitate clearance or rearrangement of this residual complex. Initiation-associated factors, including eIF1, eIF1A and eIF3-related activities under defined conditions, contribute to preparation of the small subunit for another productive engagement. The appropriate broad concept is that release of a large subunit alone does not guarantee that every RNA ligand has already left the small subunit. Complete recycling includes the recovery of components in states suitable for their subsequent roles.

A comparison of eRF3 and ABCE1 is useful in an energy question. The former uses guanine nucleotides in association with peptide release and its preceding rearrangements. The latter uses adenine nucleotides in the recycling process. If an assay contains preformed posttermination complexes but lacks functional ABCE1, the most direct expected problem is subunit separation. If the same assay measures only the quantity of released peptide, it may miss this defect because the peptide was liberated before the assay began. The choice of measured endpoint determines which part of the mechanism is actually being tested.

A eukaryotic small subunit that remains associated with an mRNA after release of the large subunit may sometimes participate in reinitiation, particularly in suitable arrangements involving short upstream coding regions. This possibility should be treated as an exception to the simplest complete-reset model, not as proof that recycling is unnecessary. Reinitiation requires appropriate initiation competence and a suitable downstream start site. A ribosome does not normally begin another polypeptide merely because a stop codon lies next to a sense codon.

The comparison with bacteria highlights a conserved task and different implementations. Both systems have to free the completed chain and restore reusable ribosomal components. Both employ class I release factors to support hydrolysis of peptidyl-tRNA. Both employ nucleotide-dependent accessory machinery. Yet the bacterial RRF/EF-G arrangement and the eukaryotic eRF1/ABCE1 arrangement are not equivalent sets of named proteins. A reliable comparison table must show separate columns for stop recognition, peptide release, class I factor dynamics, subunit splitting and residual small-subunit clearance. Collapsing all functions into a single heading called release factors obscures the mechanistic distinction.

## 15. Evolutionary context and organelle exceptions

The canonical bacterial and eukaryotic cytosolic examples provide an excellent framework for understanding translation, but their terminology should not be imposed indiscriminately on every cell compartment. Archaea possess ribosomes with bacterial-like sedimentation classifications but numerous translation factors related to those in eukaryotes. Archaeal termination involves a class I factor usually designated aRF1, and archaeal recycling uses ABCE1-related machinery. This pattern is one reason why a simple statement that all prokaryotes use the complete bacterial RF1/RF2/RF3 scheme is too broad. Bacteria and archaea are both prokaryotic in cellular organization but differ in important aspects of information processing.

The evolutionary comparison illustrates that a common problem can be solved by partly distinct molecular systems. Recognizing the end of a coding sequence, hydrolyzing the ester and recovering the ribosomal components are broadly conserved tasks. Individual protein families, accessory factors and their modes of interaction have diversified. Students should first master the experimentally well-characterized bacterial and eukaryotic cytosolic cases and then use archaeal examples to avoid overgeneralizing their distinctions.

Mitochondria and plastids also have specialized translation systems. Many of their genes and translation components reflect their evolutionary relationships with bacteria, but organelle genomes and their decoding machinery have undergone lineage-specific change. The genetic code used within some mitochondria is not identical to the standard nuclear code. For example, UGA specifies tryptophan in the vertebrate mitochondrial code rather than serving as a conventional stop. This exception is especially relevant in examination questions that explicitly identify the compartment from which a messenger originates.

The important inference is that one cannot determine the meaning of a codon independently of the translation system. The same RNA triplet can be interpreted differently in distinct biological contexts. A question describing a vertebrate nuclear-encoded cytosolic protein and one describing a mitochondrially encoded protein do not necessarily apply the same code table. The standard cytosolic rule remains reliable when the standard genetic code is specified or clearly implied. The organelle exception is not a contradiction of the rule but a limitation on its domain of validity.

Specialized incorporation of selenocysteine at selected UGA codons is another reason to avoid saying that UGA can never specify an amino acid. Selenocysteine incorporation depends on dedicated cellular machinery and RNA context, rather than ordinary recognition by the standard set of tRNAs at any arbitrary UGA. Certain organisms can also incorporate pyrrolysine at UAG using specialized systems. These examples are relevant as boundaries to normal termination, not as reasons to replace the fundamental rule with an unrestricted statement that stop codons generally encode amino acids.
