## Ten progressive reading chapters

### 2.C.01 — Genome and Gene Organization in Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

**Core question:** How is genetic information arranged inside different kinds of cells before we examine individual gene patterns?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Gene, locus, genome and chromosome terminology; chromosomes versus extrachromosomal DNA (plasmids at structural overview level); prokaryotic nucleoid, typically circular bacterial chromosome with important exceptions; eukaryotic nuclear DNA distributed over usually linear chromosomes; coding and noncoding stretches, broad gene density and chromosome organization.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Label a generic bacterial versus eukaryotic genome diagram; identify gene, locus, nucleoid, chromosome and plasmid; compare basic DNA organization without universalizing exceptions.

**Content boundary:** DNA replication and plasmid replication, genome sequencing and large-scale genomics belong to Units 3 and 12; detailed nuclear envelope already in 2.B.01.

### 2.C.02 — Operons and the Organization of Prokaryotic Genes

**Core question:** Why are several bacterial genes often organized into one coordinated transcriptional unit?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Operon as linked gene arrangement; promoter, operator, structural genes, regulator gene and transcription unit distinction; polycistronic RNA idea; lac and trp operon architecture as classic examples; orientation, shared control sites, the difference between operons and nonoperonic genes; limits and exceptions to simple prokaryote–eukaryote contrasts.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Interpret labeled lac/trp gene maps, count transcription units, distinguish promoter/operator/regulatory gene and predict broad structural consequences when gene-map elements change.

**Content boundary:** Detailed induction/repression dynamics, attenuation mechanisms, transcription machinery and control of gene expression belong to Unit 3.D; use only the minimum necessary examples to explain operon organization.

### 2.C.03 — Interrupted Genes and Eukaryotic Gene Architecture

**Core question:** How does an interrupted gene differ structurally from a continuous gene?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Continuous versus interrupted genes; discovery by RNA–DNA hybrid observations at introductory level; exons, introns, exon/intron boundaries, untranslated regions, transcription start and termination landmarks, promoter as positional context; broad alternative exon arrangements as a structural consequence, not detailed processing; intron presence in eukaryotes and important exceptions.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Read gene diagrams and distinguish genomic DNA, primary transcript and mature RNA schematics; calculate the number or length of exons/introns in straightforward exam questions.

**Content boundary:** Spliceosome mechanisms, splicing biochemistry, RNA processing, editing and alternative splicing regulation are Unit 3.B topics.

### 2.C.04 — Unique, Moderately Repetitive and Highly Repetitive DNA

**Core question:** Why does a genome contain both mostly unique sequences and DNA motifs repeated many times?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Single-copy/unique sequences and repetitive DNA; low/moderate/high copy ideas; tandem versus dispersed repeat arrangements; satellite DNA, minisatellites, microsatellites, rDNA/histone repeat arrays as appropriate examples; centromeric/telomeric repeat overview; qualitative renaturation kinetics and C0t curves as standard illustrative evidence if relevant; distinguish repeated sequence from gene-family identity.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Identify repeat classes from sequence arrays, examine simple repetition counts and interpret a conceptual DNA reassociation graph without excessive biophysical methods.

**Content boundary:** LINES/SINEs as mobile elements receive their mechanism in 2.C.10; DNA fingerprinting protocols and population marker analysis are methods/genetics areas, not a standalone chapter here.

### 2.C.05 — Gene Families, Gene Duplications and Pseudogenes

**Core question:** Why do organisms have several closely related genes rather than one copy of every gene?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Gene family definition and sequence similarity; gene clusters versus dispersed families; tandem duplication and divergence, a basic conceptual distinction from alleles; globin gene family as a classic example, rRNA/histone genes as context; pseudogenes and basic functional versus nonfunctional copies, simple evolutionary outcomes of duplicate genes.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Classify simplified gene maps, distinguish alleles from paralogous family members in a simple setting, reason from sequence similarity with clear limitations.

**Content boundary:** Advanced phylogenetic inference, detailed molecular evolution models and whole-genome comparative genomics belong to Evolution and Genomics units.

### 2.C.06 — Histones, Nucleosomes and the Fundamental Structure of Chromatin

**Core question:** How can long eukaryotic DNA molecules be packaged without losing all accessibility?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Chromatin as DNA–protein complex; core histones H2A/H2B/H3/H4, nucleosome octamer and DNA wrapping (~147 bp), linker DNA, histone H1 as a linker-associated component; nucleosome repeat length variability, beads-on-a-string model, histone tails and basic structural differences between chromatin and naked DNA.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Identify histone components, interpret nucleosome sketches and perform straightforward bp accounting with assumptions; classic micrococcal nuclease protection evidence at a nontechnical level.

**Content boundary:** Detailed histone modification pathways and epigenetic regulation belong to Unit 3.D; avoid specialist cryo-EM structures.

### 2.C.07 — Chromatin Packing and Higher-Order Chromosomal Organization

**Core question:** How does nucleosome-based chromatin become organized into larger chromosome-scale structures?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Progress from nucleosome arrays to variable chromatin folding, loop domains, chromosome-associated scaffold/axis and condensation at descriptive NET/SET level; structural roles of cohesin and condensin at overview level; distinguish simplified classical textbook 30-nm models from their variable and not universally observed reality; structural relationships between chromatin and chromosomes.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Arrange levels of DNA packaging, compare organization at different compaction states and interpret simple diagrammatic packaging questions.

**Content boundary:** Advanced chromosome-conformation capture, topological domain research and molecular condensin kinetics are research-level or genomics topics; mitotic regulation belongs to 2.D.

### 2.C.08 — Chromosome Architecture: Centromeres, Telomeres and Chromatids

**Core question:** Which structures distinguish a chromosome, and how do they help preserve chromosomal organization?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Chromosome arm, chromatid and sister chromatid as physical structures; centromere/kinetochore positional distinction; telomeres and their protective structural role; chromosome morphology according to centromere position; homologous chromosomes versus sister chromatids; autosomes/sex chromosomes as generic classifications; basic karyotype ideas.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Label standard metaphase chromosome cartoons, identify centromere types, distinguish chromosome from chromatid counts with careful phase assumptions, compare homologous and sister chromatids.

**Content boundary:** Mitosis/meiosis dynamics, segregation and checkpoints belong to 2.D; aneuploidy, karyotype abnormalities and inheritance problems are primarily Genetics.

### 2.C.09 — Euchromatin and Heterochromatin: Organization and Differences

**Core question:** Why are different portions of the same chromosome packaged and accessed differently?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Euchromatin and heterochromatin definitions; condensed versus less condensed chromatin with qualified links to transcriptional accessibility; constitutive versus facultative heterochromatin; basic examples including pericentromeric heterochromatin and the Barr body; replication timing and staining differences at introductory level; dynamic chromatin states and context dependence.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Interpret simplified staining and accessibility schemes, classify constitutive versus facultative examples, explain why condensed does not invariably mean completely silent.

**Content boundary:** Full chromatin-mediated expression control, detailed histone/DNA modifications and gene silencing pathways belong to Unit 3.D; X-inactivation inheritance/evolutionary detail belongs to Genetics.

### 2.C.10 — Transposons and Retrotransposons: Mobile Genetic Elements

**Core question:** How can genetic sequences change genomic location, and what structural effects can follow?

**Precise in-syllabus coverage:** Transposable element definition and historical discovery by Barbara McClintock at exam level; insertion sequences and simple/composite bacterial transposon architecture; DNA transposons with terminal repeats and transposase; cut-and-paste versus replicative movement in simplified examples; retrotransposons, LTR and non-LTR examples, LINEs and SINEs; autonomous versus nonautonomous elements, genome insertion outcomes and effects on gene organization.

**Standard NET/SET analysis and evidence:** Identify DNA-versus-RNA intermediate in transposition schemes, classify basic element structures, predict insertional effects and compare transposons with ordinary tandem repeats and gene families.

**Content boundary:** Detailed retroviral life cycles, transposon engineering, genome editing, antimicrobial resistance mechanisms and specialist mechanisms are outside this subunit.

## Progressive flow and hand-offs

1. **From nucleus to genome (2.C.01):** 2.B established organelle structure; here students locate the different genomic systems inside cells.
2. **From genome to gene organization (2.C.02–03):** Contrast common bacterial operon arrangements with eukaryotic interrupted genes while keeping gene regulation details for Unit 3.
3. **From genes to sequence organization (2.C.04–05):** Distinguish single-copy, repeated and related gene families using sequence and chromosome examples.
4. **From DNA sequence to chromatin (2.C.06–07):** Nucleosomes, histones and chromosome-level packing explain how long DNA is physically arranged.
5. **From chromatin to recognizable chromosomes (2.C.08):** Centromeres, telomeres, arms and chromatids provide landmarks that help the learner interpret chromosome diagrams.
6. **From chromosome structures to regional states (2.C.09):** Euchromatin and heterochromatin explain differences in packaging, accessibility and basic cellular outcomes.
7. **From fixed organization to rearrangeable sequences (2.C.10):** Transposable DNA explains an important source of variation in genome structure, handing off to Unit 3 processes and Genetics without teaching those units early.

This order is a **teaching sequence**, not an asserted historical timeline of molecular discoveries or a statement about the evolution of genomes.
