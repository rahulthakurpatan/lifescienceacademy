## Eleven sequential chapter briefs

### 4.B.01 — Fundamentals of Cellular Communication: Signals, Contacts and Responses

**Website menu description:** Understand how cells communicate through direct contact, local mediators, distant signals and junctional connections to coordinate biological responses.

**Learning question:** How can one cell influence another cell, and why are different communication ranges and mechanisms needed?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Cell-to-cell recognition and communication as physical or chemical interaction; juxtacrine/contact-dependent signaling, paracrine, autocrine, endocrine and synaptic routes as comparative categories; signal source, target-cell specificity, receptor and response in general terms; direct cytoplasmic coupling, cell–cell adhesion, ECM-based communication and soluble messengers as the map of subunit 4.B; a simple distance/timing comparison without confusing range with ligand chemistry.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Classify communication types from short scenarios, distinguish contact-mediated from secreted and junction-mediated communication, reason about signal range, strength and target specificity.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Intracellular receptor cascades and second messengers belong to 4.A; full endocrine physiology belongs to Unit 07 and immune signaling to 4.C. This is a framework, not a second GPCR chapter.

### 4.B.02 — Cell Adhesion Molecules I: Cadherins and Stable Cell–Cell Contacts

**Website menu description:** Explore calcium-dependent cadherin adhesion, homophilic recognition and how cell–cell anchoring helps maintain tissue organization.

**Learning question:** What makes neighboring cells form selective, relatively stable attachments?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Cell adhesion versus cell signaling, adhesion molecule (CAM) classes as overview; cadherin extracellular Ca²+-dependent homophilic binding, E-cadherin/N-cadherin as examples, catenin connection to actin as necessary mechanical context; adherens junctions and desmosomal cadherin roles in a functional comparison, recognition, sorting and coordinated tissue integrity; relationship of CAM expression to cell movement and contact without specialist development pathways.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Predict loss of calcium or cadherin expression on adhesion, identify homophilic contacts, compare adherens/desmosomal adhesive functions from diagrams.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Detailed junctional ultrastructure, actin/desmosome anatomy are Unit 02; cancer invasion from adhesion loss is 4.E, morphogenesis is Unit 05. Avoid advanced junction assembly biochemistry.

### 4.B.03 — Cell Adhesion Molecules II: Selectins and Immunoglobulin-Superfamily CAMs

**Website menu description:** Compare transient selectin-mediated contacts with immunoglobulin-superfamily adhesion, using leukocyte adhesion as a representative communication process.

**Learning question:** How can cells first recognize and contact one another transiently before forming stronger associations?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** E-, P- and L-selectins as familiar examples of lectin-like carbohydrate recognition and rolling contacts; calcium-dependence of selectins as common standard; immunoglobulin-superfamily cell adhesion molecules including ICAM and VCAM as examples of binding partners; calcium-independent Ig-superfamily adhesion at protein-family level, without generalizing every interaction; leukocyte rolling/adhesion as an integrated biological illustration; distinguish selectin and Ig-like CAMs from integrins without developing integrin details here.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Match CAM family to ligands and adhesive behaviors, reason about loss of selectin/ICAM under shear flow and compare transient versus firm adhesion.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Lymphocyte activation and detailed leukocyte trafficking/immunological effector mechanisms belong to 4.C; integrin activation and firm matrix contacts to 4.B.05.

### 4.B.04 — Extracellular Matrix: Composition, Organization and Cellular Roles

**Website menu description:** Understand collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, fibronectin and laminin in the structure and communication functions of extracellular matrices.

**Learning question:** How can material outside cells support tissues and also convey information to those cells?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Extracellular matrix (ECM) as a cell-secreted molecular network; interstitial ECM and basement membrane as broad types; collagen fibers, elastin, glycosaminoglycans, proteoglycans and adhesion glycoproteins (fibronectin and laminin); mechanical, structural, hydration and cell-interaction roles; concept of substrate binding, attachment and environmental cues, selected tissue examples and basic effects of ECM degradation/remodeling.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Identify matrix components from functions, distinguish basement membrane from interstitial matrix, predict qualitative changes after collagen/elastin/proteoglycan defects.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Protein synthesis and secretion mechanisms belong to 3.C and membrane architecture to Unit 02; specialist ECM pathologies and engineering are outside NET/SET scope.

### 4.B.05 — Integrins: Cell–Matrix Adhesion and Bidirectional Communication

**Website menu description:** Follow integrin αβ receptors through ECM recognition, cytoskeletal linkage, inside-out activation and outside-in signaling.

**Learning question:** How does attachment to the extracellular matrix influence cell behavior, and how can a cell regulate its own adhesiveness?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Integrins as α/β heterodimeric transmembrane proteins, binding selected ECM proteins and cell-surface Ig-family ligands; divalent-cation requirement as a general principle; affinity/avidity and inside-out activation versus outside-in signals, links to actin via talin or other adaptor proteins at overview level; focal adhesions and hemidesmosomes as function-based comparisons; matrix signals affecting cell survival/migration at basic level; link with CAM interactions without duplicating node 03.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Recognize integrin heterodimers, predict altered attachment after receptor or ECM defects, compare inside-out and outside-in examples, distinguish focal adhesion from gap junction.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** General kinase cascades are covered by 4.A; cytoskeleton and junction structures in Unit 02; cancer invasion in 4.E and immune migration in 4.C are contextual examples only.

### 4.B.06 — Gap Junctions and Direct Cytoplasmic Cell–Cell Coupling

**Website menu description:** Examine connexins, connexons, gap-junction channels and the regulated passage of ions and small molecules between neighboring cells.

**Learning question:** How can two neighboring animal cells communicate directly without secreting a messenger into extracellular space?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Gap junction as direct channel-mediated cytoplasmic communication; connexin assembly into connexons (typically hexamers), docking of two hemichannels in vertebrates, regulated permeability to ions and selected small signaling metabolites, electrical and metabolic coupling; gating by voltage, pH and intracellular Ca²+ as context-sensitive concepts; contrast chemical synapse and gap junction/electrical synapse; note innexins in many invertebrates and pannexins as related but non-identical channels, without exhaustive taxonomy; tight/adherens junctions mentioned only to distinguish function.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Trace molecule movement across a paired-channel diagram, predict uncoupling consequences, classify junction function, compare electrical and chemical communication.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Membrane channel biophysics in 2.A.07–09 and junction anatomy in 2.B; gap junction electrical coupling is distinct from neurotransmitter-driven chemical synapses studied in 4.B.07–09.

### 4.B.07 — Chemical Neurotransmission I: Synaptic Organization and Vesicle Release

**Website menu description:** Trace an arriving nerve impulse to presynaptic calcium influx, synaptic-vesicle fusion and quantal neurotransmitter release.

**Learning question:** How does an electrical signal in a presynaptic cell become a chemical message across a synaptic cleft?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Presynaptic terminal, synaptic cleft and postsynaptic membrane; chemical vs electrical synaptic modes; arrival of action potential as prerequisite, voltage-gated Ca²+ channel opening, synaptic vesicle docking and priming; SNARE proteins and Ca²+-sensing synaptotagmin as standard molecular examples, exocytosis and quantal release; representative acetylcholine or glutamate synapse, with well-labeled directionality and no detailed electrophysiology.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Order presynaptic events and predict consequences of Ca²+ channel blockade, SNARE cleavage or loss of vesicle filling; interpret a labeled synapse diagram.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Action-potential propagation and detailed voltage/current models belong to Unit 07, membrane potentials to 2.A.09, trafficking fundamentals to 2.A.10; postsynaptic receptors follow in 4.B.08.

### 4.B.08 — Chemical Neurotransmission II: Receptors and Postsynaptic Responses

**Website menu description:** Compare ionotropic and metabotropic neurotransmitter receptors, excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic responses and basic signal integration.

**Learning question:** How does transmitter binding change the receiving cell’s electrical or biochemical state?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Synaptic neurotransmitter classes as selected examples (acetylcholine, glutamate, GABA, glycine, monoamines) without building a full neurochemistry catalog; ligand-gated ion channels (ionotropic) versus G-protein-linked metabotropic neurotransmitter receptors; response timing, excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (EPSP/IPSP) as qualitative graded responses; temporal/spatial summation at a simple conceptual level, receptor specificity and the fact that transmitter effect depends on receptor/ion gradients; elementary neuromuscular junction example.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Identify ionotropic versus metabotropic responses from time courses, predict direction of potential change from specified channel/ion movement, distinguish presynaptic release from postsynaptic reception.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** No extensive ion-channel electrophysiology or brain circuit anatomy, muscle contraction physiology belongs to Unit 07 and GPCR biochemistry to 4.A.03–05.

### 4.B.09 — Regulation of Neurotransmission: Signal Termination and Synaptic Modulation

**Website menu description:** Explain neurotransmitter reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, diffusion, autoreceptor feedback and control of presynaptic release.

**Learning question:** How is neurotransmission kept brief, adjustable and repeatable rather than allowing receptors to be activated continuously?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Cycle of synthesis/packaging, release, receptor activation, transmitter clearance; termination by transporter-mediated reuptake, enzymatic breakdown such as acetylcholinesterase, diffusion and glial uptake in familiar examples; autoreceptor feedback, presynaptic inhibition/facilitation, postsynaptic receptor sensitivity, vesicle recycling as broad control; distinction between increased transmitter availability and increased receptor response; short-term synaptic facilitation/depression only when needed to illustrate modulation.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Predict effects of blocked reuptake, altered acetylcholinesterase, or presynaptic inhibitory feedback, interpret changes in amplitude and duration of postsynaptic responses.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Drug-by-drug neuropharmacology, learning/memory circuit plasticity and nervous system physiology are Unit 07, clinical neurotransmitter therapies beyond scope.

### 4.B.10 — Haematopoiesis I: Stem Cells, Blood Cell Lineages and the Marrow Niche

**Website menu description:** Understand haematopoietic stem-cell self-renewal, major blood-cell lineage choices and the supportive bone-marrow microenvironment.

**Learning question:** How can a relatively small stem-cell population continuously replenish the many kinds of blood cells?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Haematopoiesis and regulation as a homeostatic process; adult bone marrow and simple fetal-liver context; haematopoietic stem-cell self-renewal, quiescence, progenitors and broadly myeloid/lymphoid differentiation pathways as a useful schematic rather than a fully rigid hierarchy; representative major output lineages (erythrocytes, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes and lymphocytes); cell–cell adhesion, stromal contacts and local soluble factors in bone marrow niche, CXCL12 and SCF as standard illustrations; definitions of proliferation, commitment and maturation.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Interpret simple stem-cell hierarchy/niche diagrams, distinguish self-renewal from lineage differentiation and predict basic consequences of a niche defect.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Comprehensive blood physiology is Unit 07, lymphocyte-specific antigen-dependent differentiation is 4.C, developmental stem-cell potency frameworks are Unit 05; detailed hematopoietic disease taxonomy excluded.

### 4.B.11 — Haematopoiesis II: Cytokines, Growth Factors and Feedback Control

**Website menu description:** Trace erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, colony-stimulating factors and niche signals in regulated blood-cell production.

**Learning question:** How does the body increase or limit production of particular blood cells in response to changing needs?

**Complete microtopic assignment:** Growth-factor and cytokine roles in regulating survival, expansion, differentiation and release of haematopoietic cells; erythropoietin (EPO) and oxygen-demand feedback, thrombopoietin (TPO) and megakaryocytes/platelet output, G-CSF and neutrophil granulopoiesis, GM-CSF and IL-7 as contrasting representative signals, SCF/CXCL12 niche support; simple cell-contact versus soluble-factor regulation, common receptor/signaling principles as already taught in 4.A.07 (no full JAK-STAT duplication); normal demand-dependent homeostasis and simple loss-of-factor examples.

**CSIR-NET/SET analytical and diagrammatic practice:** Map growth factors to major cell output, interpret low-oxygen/EPO negative feedback qualitatively, predict simplified progenitor response to absent growth factor, identify niche-dependent versus circulating hormone actions.

**Academic boundary and overlap avoidance:** Molecular JAK–STAT cascade taught in 4.A.07; immune cytokine networks and B/T differentiation in 4.C; full hematological disease and therapy protocols, bone marrow transplantation techniques and specialist HSC genomics excluded.

## Balance, narrative, chronology and hand-off QA

1. **4.B.01:** Answer how communication occurs in principle before introducing specialist interfaces.
2. **4.B.02–03:** Cell-adhesion molecule families: stable cadherin contacts and dynamic selectin/Ig-family contacts. Integrin signaling is a later, separate focus rather than repeatedly explained.
3. **4.B.04–05:** Explain ECM components before integrins, because integrins must first have meaningful extracellular ligands. Connect adhesion to cytoskeletal forces and signaling using standard functional examples.
4. **4.B.06:** Distinguish direct gap-junction communication from adhesion junctions; anticipate electrical coupling before discussing synapses.
5. **4.B.07–09:** Trace the causal chemical synaptic cycle: stimulus/Ca²+/vesicle release → receptor-mediated postsynaptic responses → transmitter clearance and modulation.
6. **4.B.10–11:** Finish by scaling cell communication to tissue homeostasis through HSC/progenitors, marrow signals, erythropoietin and other differentiation factors.

The sequence is a **teaching chronology**, not a literal developmental timeline for all organisms.

## Specific scientific accuracy checks

- Cadherins use Ca²+-dependent homophilic adhesion in standard examples; selectins often bind carbohydrate-containing ligands. Adhesion members of the immunoglobulin superfamily do not as a family require extracellular Ca²+, though individual receptor interactions vary.
- Integrins are αβ heterodimers. They contribute to cell–matrix adhesion and can bind some cell-surface partners. Distinguish ligand binding, cytoskeleton-linked adhesion and inside-out/outside-in communication.
- Vertebrate connexin gap junctions use two docked connexons, typically six connexins per hemichannel. Invertebrate gap-junction channels commonly use innexins; do not claim connexins are universal.
- Chemical synaptic transmission typically requires a voltage-gated Ca²+ influx preceding vesicle fusion; SNARE and synaptotagmin functions are standard examples. Electrical coupling via gap junctions is different.
- A neurotransmitter need not be intrinsically excitatory or inhibitory in all cells: effects depend on receptor type and ionic context. Ionotropic and metabotropic responses differ in the mechanism/timing.
- Clearance can involve uptake, degradation or diffusion; avoid falsely stating a single route applies equally to all transmitters.
- Haematopoiesis is regulated by niche cell contacts and soluble cytokines/growth factors. Distinguish adult bone-marrow niche from systemic EPO feedback; simplified myeloid/lymphoid family trees are schematic, not universally rigid fate paths.
- Neurophysiology and muscle physiology belong to Unit 07; immune effector and antigen-driven lymphocyte differentiation to 4.C; kinases/GPCR pathways to 4.A; specialized developmental stem-cell specification to Unit 05.
- Always use logically connected explanatory prose, correct diagram labeling, textbook-supported examples and original reasoning questions. Avoid filler, artificial word quotas, specialist pharmacology, exhaustive disease lists, clinical procedures and advanced experimental methods.
