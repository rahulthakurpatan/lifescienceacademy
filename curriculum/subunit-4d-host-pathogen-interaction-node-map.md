# Life Science Academy — 4.D: Host–Pathogen Interaction

## Syllabus authority and architecture

- **Official revised 2026 CSIR–UGC–DBT NET Life Sciences & Biotechnology**, Unit **4.D** (printed p.5): https://www.csirhrdg.res.in/SiteContent/ManagedContent/ContentFiles/20260121115025355Merged_Syllabus_CSIR-DBT_21Jan2026.pdf

- **MP SET Life Sciences**, Unit **4.A — Host parasite interaction** (printed p.3): https://cdn-images.prepp.in/public/image/mp-set-paper-2-life-scinces-1760795800.pdf
- **Revised wording to cover completely:** Recognition and entry of different pathogens such as bacteria and viruses into **animal and plant host cells**; alteration of host-cell behaviour by pathogens; virus-induced cell transformation; pathogen-induced diseases in **animals and plants**; and cell–cell fusion in **normal and abnormal cells**.
- **Chosen chapter count:** Exactly **12** sequential one-page learning nodes `4.D.01`–`4.D.12`. This is a teaching choice within the user's 10–13 limit, **not** an official fixed count. Chapter academic responsibilities and overall completeness matter more than equal word counts.
- **Narrative progression:** Recognition and host specificity → bacterial adhesion and animal-cell entry → bacterial manipulation → viral attachment, entry and alteration → viral oncogenic transformation → animal disease case reasoning → plant entry and disease/spread → normal versus pathological cell–cell fusion.
- **Scientific scope:** Thorough undergraduate foundations and MSc/NET/SET standard, simple academic English, carefully introduced textbook examples, diagrams and analytical reasoning. No thin chapters or padding. Avoid specialist PhD research, taxonomic surveys, medical treatment or agricultural protocols.
- **Important biology caveat:** *Entry into a host organism/tissue is not always entry into a host cell.* Many plant bacteria live in the apoplast and deliver effectors; plant viruses often require wounds/vectors and later spread via plasmodesmata. Neither should be inaccurately described as ordinary animal-virus membrane fusion.
- **Menu contract:** One node = **one future substantive reading page** with multiple internal microtopics. No individual URLs or sitemap entries until a genuine chapter has been published. The menu is a planned curriculum, not 12 live reading pages.

## Complete twelve-node reading-page briefs

### 4.D.01 — Foundations of Host–Pathogen Interaction: Recognition, Tropism and Infection Stages

**Student-facing menu description:** Learn pathogen–host specificity, attachment, colonization, entry, tissue tropism and the sequence from exposure to cellular damage.

**Driving learning question:** How does an encounter between a host and microorganism lead—or fail to lead—to infection?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Define host, pathogen, commensal and opportunistic pathogen only where needed; exposure, adhesion, colonization, invasion, survival, spread and disease as distinct stages; animal and plant barriers and host-specificity concepts; receptor–ligand compatibility and tissue tropism; pathogen versus host contributions to the interaction; localized versus systemic spread at a functional level; infection versus colonization versus disease; simple animal–plant entry contrast. Introduce extracellular versus intracellular lifestyles without asserting that all pathogens enter cells.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Identify the stage disrupted by loss of adhesin/receptor or breach of barrier; distinguish infected cell, colonized tissue and resulting disease; compare host range and tissue tropism in simple scenarios.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** 4.C owns immune PRRs/TLR details and defense-cell pathways; 4.A owns general signaling; microbiological taxonomy and life-cycle catalogs are not in 4.D.

### 4.D.02 — Bacterial Recognition, Adhesion and Colonization of Animal Hosts

**Student-facing menu description:** Examine bacterial adhesins, host-cell receptors, surface attachment, biofilm context and barriers to successful animal-tissue colonization.

**Driving learning question:** How does a bacterium select and remain associated with an animal tissue before it causes damage?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Fimbrial/pilus and non-fimbrial adhesins, host cell glycans, proteins and extracellular-matrix receptors as common ligand classes; adhesion specificity and host/tissue tropism; mucosal and epithelial surfaces, resident microbiota as competition in brief; colonization vs internalization; bacterial biofilm only as a host-associated persistence example, not complete microbiology; representative E. coli adhesive mechanisms or appropriate textbook model; how adhesin or receptor alteration affects infection potential. Include that not all bacterial pathogens must be intracellular.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Match an adhesin to attachment stage, reason about loss of surface receptors and distinguish physical adherence from invasive uptake.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Cell adhesion molecular-family chemistry is already covered in 4.B; bacterial growth kinetics in 2.E, detailed immunity and antimicrobial strategies in 4.C; no specialist strain/genotype inventories.

### 4.D.03 — Bacterial Entry into Animal Cells: Zipper and Trigger Mechanisms

**Student-facing menu description:** Follow receptor-driven uptake and effector-induced membrane remodeling through the classical zipper and trigger bacterial invasion models.

**Driving learning question:** How can bacterial pathogens cross an animal cell membrane that does not normally engulf them?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Receptor-mediated zipper uptake through host surface ligands and local actin rearrangement, using Listeria internalin/E-cadherin as an appropriately specific example; bacterial Type III secretion-mediated trigger uptake in Salmonella and actin ruffling; uptake by professional phagocytes versus pathogen-induced entry into nonphagocytic cells; phagosomal or vacuolar residence versus escape as immediate post-entry consequences; cytoskeletal and endocytic steps explained causally at NET/SET level; compare zipper/trigger by source of the trigger, not by superficial appearance alone.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Reconstruct zipper versus trigger diagram sequences; predict consequences of a lost invasin, actin-blocking perturbation or defective secretion machinery.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Advanced secretion apparatus structure or genetic manipulation protocols are out of scope; general endocytosis in Unit 02, bacterial host manipulation and vacuolar survival in 4.D.04.

### 4.D.04 — Bacterial Virulence Factors and Pathogen-Driven Host-Cell Alteration

**Student-facing menu description:** Explain secretion-system effectors, toxins, cytoskeletal changes, intracellular niches and altered host signaling or survival.

**Driving learning question:** After reaching a host tissue or cell, how can a bacterium redirect normal host processes to support infection?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Distinguish secreted extracellular toxins from direct injection of bacterial effector proteins; receptor-binding toxins or classic signaling effects at explanatory level (cholera toxin's persistent Gs/cAMP example), T3SS effectors and actin-cytoskeleton manipulation, altered vesicular trafficking, phagosome maturation and intracellular niches with Listeria/Salmonella/Mycobacterium as labeled illustrative strategies; cell damage, apoptosis modulation and nutrient access without advanced microbiological metabolic detail; extracellular pathogen effects and intracellular strategies contrasted. Host response is discussed as an outcome, not a second complete immunology syllabus.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Predict effects of toxin receptor absence, effector/secretion loss and host trafficking disruption; classify intracellular survival vs extracellular toxin-mediated pathogenesis.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Phagocyte immune killing in 4.C, complete cAMP pathways in 4.A and pathogen-specific animal disease case comparisons in 4.D.09; no toxin production recipes, clinical management or advanced pathogen secretome research.

### 4.D.05 — Viral Host Recognition: Attachment Receptors, Coreceptors and Tropism

**Student-facing menu description:** Understand how viral surface proteins bind host receptors or coreceptors to determine attachment, cell specificity and host range.

**Driving learning question:** Why can a virus infect some host cells but not others?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Animal viral capsid proteins or envelope glycoproteins interacting with host receptors; receptor and occasional coreceptor roles, ligand specificity, attachment and tissue tropism; selected influenza hemagglutinin–sialic acid and HIV gp120–CD4/coreceptor examples (mechanistic, not clinical); receptor distribution versus additional intracellular compatibility as determinants of permissiveness; distinguish adsorption/attachment from actual penetration, productive infection and disease.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Interpret virus-binding and receptor-knockout scenarios, distinguish receptor recognition from the later uncoating and replication steps, contrast enveloped and non-enveloped attachment.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Antiviral antibody and HIV immune responses belong to 4.C; viral classification belongs to Unit 9; viral penetration owned by 4.D.06.

### 4.D.06 — Viral Entry, Membrane Penetration and Uncoating in Animal Cells

**Student-facing menu description:** Trace receptor-triggered entry by membrane fusion or endocytosis, endosomal escape and delivery of the viral genome into host cells.

**Driving learning question:** Once attached, how does an animal virus pass host membranes and expose its genome?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Enveloped virus fusion at the plasma membrane versus endosomal membrane, fusion protein conformational changes and suitable pH/receptor triggers; non-enveloped virus penetration and endosomal disruption or membrane pore as representative classes; endocytosis and trafficking only at a functional level; uncoating and nucleic acid delivery, basic intracellular destination differences relevant to host-cell entry; examples of influenza low-pH-dependent fusion and HIV plasma-membrane fusion, emphasizing mechanism specificity rather than universal rules; distinguish virus–cell membrane fusion from cell–cell fusion.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Order binding→entry→uncoating, identify impact of acidification or fusion inhibition on specified virus model, compare enveloped and non-enveloped mechanisms.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Full viral replication/transcription programs are not the learning target; cellular endocytosis is Unit 02 and membrane molecular physics is Unit 01/02; virus-induced host changes are 4.D.07.

### 4.D.07 — Viral Manipulation of Host Cells and Cytopathic Changes

**Student-facing menu description:** Explore how infection redirects host gene expression, membranes, organelles, survival pathways and cell morphology during viral propagation.

**Driving learning question:** How can a virus turn a functioning animal host cell into a cell that produces viral components or undergoes injury?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Host machinery exploitation at infection stage without detailed replication chemistry; suppression or redirection of host protein synthesis as representative example, endoplasmic-reticulum/vesicle remodeling, altered host signaling and cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis as appropriate outcomes; cytopathic effects including rounding, lysis, inclusion bodies and syncytia as introductory morphological patterns with clearly labeled virus examples; changes that improve release or local spread; virus-host antagonism described without restating all innate or adaptive immune defenses.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Distinguish entry, replication-associated host remodeling and cytopathic effect; interpret a simplified host translation-shutoff or apoptosis perturbation experiment.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Immune response and interferon defense mechanisms are 4.C; viral gene expression machinery is 3.D; virus-induced stable transformation separate in 4.D.08; cell–cell fusion in depth in 4.D.12.

### 4.D.08 — Virus-Induced Cellular Transformation: Persistent Changes in Host Growth Control

**Student-facing menu description:** Explain viral oncogenic proteins, altered cell-cycle checkpoints and the distinction between infection, immortalization and transformation.

**Driving learning question:** How can a subset of viral infections cause lasting changes in cell proliferation instead of only acute cell injury?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Definition of cellular transformation in context; virus-induced alterations of growth signals, cell-cycle regulators and apoptosis; textbook high-risk HPV E6/p53 and E7/Rb interactions at the exam-relevant conceptual level, EBV or SV40 as brief comparative examples when biologically grounded; persistent oncogenic viral expression and variable long-term outcomes; distinguish productive infection, immortalization, transformed phenotype and actual malignancy without treating them as synonyms; role of viral persistence as a model, not every infection leads to cancer.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Predict effects of viral oncoprotein disabling a checkpoint, distinguish cell transformation from cell–cell fusion, map HPV E6/E7 to relevant host regulator.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** 4.E owns overall cancer genetics, oncogenes/tumor suppressors, metastasis and therapy; this node is the **virus–host transformation mechanism**, not a full oncology chapter.

### 4.D.09 — Pathogen-Induced Diseases in Animals: Mechanisms and Representative Cases

**Student-facing menu description:** Connect bacterial and viral entry or host-cell alterations with tissue injury using toxin-mediated, invasive and cytopathic animal-disease examples.

**Driving learning question:** How do the molecular interactions learned so far produce recognizably different animal disease patterns?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Pathogenesis as the causal sequence from entry and colonization to host-cell alteration and tissue dysfunction; contrasting cases such as Vibrio cholerae toxin-mediated intestinal fluid secretion without invasion, Salmonella epithelial invasion/inflammation at overview level, and influenza viral epithelial damage with host contribution; distinguish direct toxin-mediated injury from invasion-related and virus-associated cytopathic injury; tissue specificity and local versus systemic consequences; concise mechanism-first case comparison with animal pathology interpretation, not disease lists.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Compare pathogen→host target→mechanism→cell effect→symptom-level outcome tables, identify an expected cellular lesion from a given entry or toxin defect.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Detailed immune responses to TB, malaria and HIV already in 4.C; detailed infectious-disease clinical diagnosis/treatment, epidemiology and extensive species taxonomy excluded.

### 4.D.10 — Plant Pathogen Recognition, Entry Routes and Initial Colonization

**Student-facing menu description:** Compare plant cell-wall barriers, stomatal or wound entry, bacterial apoplastic colonization, fungal penetration and vector-assisted viral entry.

**Driving learning question:** How do microbes reach susceptible plant tissues when plant cells are protected by rigid walls?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Plant cuticle, cell wall, stomata, hydathodes, wounds and root interfaces as anatomical entry routes; distinction between entry into a **plant tissue**, the apoplast, and actual penetration into a plant **cell**; bacteria often colonize apoplast rather than entering host cytoplasm; fungal and oomycete penetration/haustorium as simplified examples; many plant viruses require mechanical wounds or insect/vector delivery, rather than animal-style direct receptor-mediated membrane fusion; initial plant pathogen recognition through cell-surface PRRs/PAMPs only as context, with detailed plant immune signaling belonging elsewhere; host specificity and vector transmission as mechanistic context.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Match pathogen class to plausible plant entry route, distinguish apoplastic colonization from intracellular viral delivery and compare animal versus plant membranes/cell-wall obstacles.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Plant physiology immunity and defense cascades belong to Unit 06 and innate receptor taxonomy to 4.C; viral entry cannot be described as equivalent across animal and plant hosts; no agricultural control protocols.

### 4.D.11 — Plant Host-Cell Manipulation, Pathogen Spread and Disease Examples

**Student-facing menu description:** Follow bacterial effectors, fungal invasion and viral movement proteins to altered plant-cell function, spread and classic disease symptoms.

**Driving learning question:** How do plant pathogens modify their hosts, move between tissues and generate blight, wilt, galls or mosaic symptoms?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Plant bacterial Type III secretion and effector delivery altering host function without implying bacterium enters the cytoplasm; representative Pseudomonas syringae plant disease or Xanthomonas blight as appropriate examples; Agrobacterium T-DNA transfer and crown-gall formation as a classic host-cell reprogramming case; fungal secreted effectors/haustoria where appropriate, simplified rust or wilt symptom context; plant-virus intracellular replication only as prerequisite, movement proteins modifying plasmodesmata, local cell-to-cell and systemic movement, tobacco mosaic virus as labeled example; pathogen-mediated plant symptoms (mosaic, chlorosis, necrosis, gall, wilt) connected causally to cell physiology, not a pathology encyclopedia.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Identify plant cell targets of an effector or movement protein, distinguish bacterial extracellular action from transferred effectors, map gall/mosaic/blight to appropriate conceptual mechanisms.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Plant adaptive/molecular immune defense networks, gene-for-gene signaling, agricultural crop protection and plant biotechnology T-DNA engineering protocols are beyond 4.D; viral gene-expression detail belongs to 3.D/other units.

### 4.D.12 — Cell–Cell Fusion in Normal and Abnormal Biology: Mechanisms and Syncytia

**Student-facing menu description:** Compare regulated cell fusion in fertilization, muscle or placental development with virus-induced syncytia and other abnormal fusion events.

**Driving learning question:** How can two separate cells merge their plasma membranes, and when does this process become pathological?

**Required internal teaching and scientific microtopics:** Definition of cell–cell fusion as merging plasma membranes and cytoplasmic continuity; recognition, close membrane apposition, fusogenic proteins and fusion-pore opening as general physical sequence (no advanced biophysics); physiological cases sperm–egg fusion in animals, myoblast fusion to form multinucleated skeletal muscle, trophoblast fusion forming syncytiotrophoblast, and optional osteoclast fusion for comparison; abnormal or pathogen-induced virus glycoprotein–mediated neighboring-cell fusion, syncytium or multinucleated giant cell formation with a representative enveloped-virus example; distinguish virus–cell entry fusion from infected-cell–neighbor fusion, and true fusion from multinucleation caused solely by nuclear division without cytokinesis; biological consequences and simple context-specific examples.

**CSIR NET/SET diagrammatic and analytical practice:** Classify normal vs abnormal syncytium cases, distinguish membrane merger from endocytosis and virus–host fusion, reason what a viral fusion-protein defect might change.

**Academic scope and no-overlap boundary:** Fertilization/developmental chronology belongs to Unit 05 and muscle physiology to Unit 07; specialist cancer cell fusion is 4.E if needed; do not present every multinucleate cell as evidence of fusion.

## Why these twelve are chronological and reasonably balanced

1. **4.D.01** gives the distinct stages of exposure, recognition, colonization, entry, host manipulation and disease, so students can distinguish cause from consequence.
2. **4.D.02–04** follow bacterial encounter with an animal host: first attachment, then entry when relevant, then bacterial effectors/toxins and intracellular survival. Note that some bacteria never need to enter host cells.
3. **4.D.05–08** follow viral encounter with an animal host: attachment and tropism, penetration and uncoating, changes in host behavior, then a separate mechanistic example of persistent virus-induced cell transformation.
4. **4.D.09** is an integration exercise explaining representative animal diseases after their microbial mechanisms have been established, not an encyclopedic disease list.
5. **4.D.10–11** use the different anatomy and microbiology of plant hosts to explain arrival/entry, apoplastic versus intracellular presence, injected effectors, viral movement, and plant-specific disease examples.
6. **4.D.12** compares regulated physiological fusion with infection-associated abnormal cell–cell fusion and syncytium formation, explicitly distinguishing viral particle–cell membrane fusion from fusion of two cells.

## Scientific QA and inclusion safeguards

- **Revised NET vs older NET/SET:** Although current subunit is 4.D, the old host-parasite interaction syllabus is 4.A. Do not rename current 4.A Cell Signaling or shift earlier curriculum menus.
- **Host recognition vs immune PRRs:** The 4.D focus is recognition/attachment **by or between pathogen and host for entry/colonization**. Detailed host immune sensing (TLRs, antigen recognition, B/T cells, immunity against TB/malaria/HIV) belongs to 4.C.
- **Bacterial entry:** Distinguish host receptor-induced *zipper* uptake and injected-effector-induced *trigger* uptake using named organisms only when applicable; never assert all bacteria enter host cells or all Type III secretion systems cause invasion.
- **Bacterial modification:** Type III effectors and secreted toxins have different delivery modes; bacterial toxins can alter host signaling from outside cells, while other pathogens manipulate intracellular vacuoles or cytoskeleton.
- **Animal viruses:** Binding to a host receptor does not guarantee productive replication; entry can require membrane fusion, endocytosis and/or endosomal escape depending on the virus. No universal rule that every virus fuses at a cell surface or uses low pH.
- **Virus-induced transformation:** Infection, cytopathic change, cellular immortalization, transformation and malignancy are distinct; high-risk HPV E6/E7 impacts on host p53/Rb offer a clear NET-level example. General cancer progression, oncogene/tumor suppressor catalogues and therapy are taught in 4.E.
- **Plants:** The plant cell wall means virus entry differs from animal viral entry. Use accurate concepts of wounds/vectors, bacterial apoplastic colonization, effector delivery into plant cells, fungal penetration, and movement through plasmodesmata; no false generalization that plant bacteria must be intracellular.
- **Disease examples:** Keep each animal and plant pathogen case as a mechanism demonstration; do not turn 4.D into separate unrelated animal/plant disease catalogs or treatment material.
- **Fusion:** Sperm–egg, myoblast and placental trophoblast fusion are physiological; enveloped-virus-induced syncytia can be pathological. A single cell with multiple nuclei is not necessarily a product of cell–cell fusion.
- **Prevent duplicated syllabus:** 4.A generic signaling; 4.B adhesion and junction biochemistry; 4.C immune recognition/defense and infection-specific immunology; 4.E general cancer; Unit 02 endocytosis and cytoskeletal structures; Unit 05 fertilization and development; Unit 06 full plant physiology; Unit 09 diversity and taxonomy; Methods advanced laboratory techniques.
- **QA of every future chapter:** one detailed reading page with real narrative, chronological subheadings, fully explained standard illustrations, evidence-based mechanism comparisons and original exam questions. Strictly no page-count padding or doctoral tangents.
- **Deployment safety:** Modify only this new curriculum file and the `4.D` menu region in current `index.html`; use the latest index blob SHA, preserve 4.A/4.B/4.C, Unit 02 and Unit 03, and do not add fake reading-page links or sitemap entries.
