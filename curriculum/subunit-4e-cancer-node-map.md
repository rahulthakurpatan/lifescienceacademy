## Twelve logically ordered chapter specifications

### 4.E.01 — Fundamentals of Cancer Biology: From Normal Growth to Malignancy

**Student-facing menu description:** Understand regulated cell growth, benign and malignant tumors, multistep transformation, clonal expansion and the major cancer-associated cell behaviors.

**Driving scientific question:** How can a normal cell acquire features of a malignant population over time?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Normal tissue homeostasis and proliferation; definitions of neoplasm, tumor, benign versus malignant and carcinoma/sarcoma only as basic illustrations; tumor initiation, clonal expansion, progression and selection as a simple sequence; heritable somatic changes versus normal physiological adaptation; distinctions among hyperplasia, dysplasia, invasion and metastasis at exam-level without histopathology classification; cancer-associated capabilities such as growth-signal autonomy, evasion of growth restraints and cell death, replicative persistence, angiogenesis and invasion, treated as an integrating roadmap, not detailed 'hallmarks research' review; why cancers are molecularly heterogeneous.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Recognize benign versus malignant properties, order multistep progression examples, separate biological hallmarks from a particular gene or tissue label.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Basic mitosis and controlled cell cycle are in 2.D; genetics and driver changes in 4.E.02–06, invasion/metastasis in 4.E.09–10. Avoid clinical staging and diagnostic pathology atlases.


### 4.E.02 — Genetic Alterations and Rearrangements in Cancer Progenitor Cells

**Student-facing menu description:** Follow somatic mutations, chromosomal translocations, deletions, amplifications and their accumulation in progenitor cells during cancer initiation.

**Driving scientific question:** What kinds of heritable genetic changes can turn a dividing progenitor into the ancestor of a tumor clone?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Progenitor/stem-like cells as possible origins without asserting every cancer begins in a classical stem cell; somatic versus germline predisposition, driver versus passenger changes; point mutations, deletion, copy-number gain/amplification, chromosomal translocation, inversion and gene fusion as cancer-relevant rearrangements; examples Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) and BCR–ABL fusion as a causal model; DNA damage, replication defects and inaccurate repair as an overview of mutation sources; chromosomal and genomic instability and multistep clonal selection; distinction between a rearrangement and all activating mechanisms of oncogenes.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Interpret simple chromosome/fusion diagrams, explain why a stable fusion can alter kinase signaling, contrast driver/passenger mutations and predict clonal expansion from selective advantage.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** General DNA-repair enzyme chemistry belongs in 3.A; chromosomal rearrangements as inheritance events are Unit 08; molecular details of proto-oncogenes are in 4.E.03, tumor suppressors in 4.E.04.


### 4.E.03 — Proto-Oncogenes and Oncogenes: Mechanisms of Gain-of-Function

**Student-facing menu description:** Explain how point mutation, amplification, rearrangement or inappropriate expression converts growth-regulatory proto-oncogenes into oncogenic drivers.

**Driving scientific question:** How can too much or persistently active growth-promoting signaling drive abnormal division?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Proto-oncogene versus oncogene, gain-of-function and usually dominant effect at cellular level; oncogene classes from ligand/receptor to cytoplasmic switch, kinase and transcription factor; exemplar RAS activating mutations, MYC transcriptional activity, HER2/ERBB2 amplification and BCR–ABL fusion in appropriate labeled cancer contexts; gene dosage, translocations, mutation and promoter dysregulation as different activation routes; constitutive pathway signaling and growth advantage; contrast oncogene activation with inactivation of tumor suppressor genes.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Classify mutation/amplification/translocation examples, infer consequences of constitutively active Ras, compare cellular dominance of oncogene gain-of-function with tumor suppressor loss, identify gene-level driver from a schematic.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Detailed RTK–Ras–MAPK biochemical steps are taught in 4.A.06, viral oncoproteins in 4.E.07 and targeted drugs in 4.E.12; avoid exhaustive oncogene catalogues.


### 4.E.04 — Tumor-Suppressor Genes: Growth Restraints, p53, Rb and the Two-Hit Concept

**Student-facing menu description:** Compare tumor suppressors, loss-of-function mutations, loss of heterozygosity and the p53/Rb growth-control pathways.

**Driving scientific question:** What happens when molecular brakes on cell division and genome damage responses are lost?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Tumor-suppressor concept and functional types (cell-cycle brakes, DNA-damage checkpoints, differentiation and growth restraint); RB1–E2F and restriction point as a classic model, TP53 response to genome damage, APC/Wnt linkage at a short relevant example; Knudson's two-hit model and retinoblastoma inheritance as basic demonstration, somatic/germline alleles, loss of heterozygosity; typical recessive cell-level effect contrasted with single-allele exceptions (haploinsufficiency, dominant-negative TP53) only as caveats; differentiation of caretaker and gatekeeper roles at exam-level.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Work two-hit pedigrees/allelic models, predict RB1/TP53 loss impact, identify why inherited one-hit predisposition is not sufficient for tumor formation.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Checkpoint integration follows in 4.E.05 and apoptotic responses in 4.E.06, full Mendelian pedigree calculation in Unit 08, detailed Wnt signaling/developmental networks outside this subunit.


### 4.E.05 — Cancer and the Cell Cycle: Cyclins, CDKs and Checkpoint Failure

**Student-facing menu description:** Trace how altered cyclins, cyclin-dependent kinases and G1/S, G2/M or spindle checkpoints allow uncontrolled cell proliferation.

**Driving scientific question:** Why do cell-cycle safeguards fail in a proliferating cancer clone?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Minimal normal G0/G1/S/G2/M overview as prerequisite; mitogen-dependent cyclin D–CDK4/6, p16INK4a and Rb–E2F restriction-point interactions; cyclin E/CDK2 as transition context; DNA-damage checkpoints linked to ATM/ATR, p53–p21 only at NET/SET-level functional overview; spindle assembly checkpoint and aneuploidy as representative genome instability consequence; loss of contact inhibition, mitogen independence and cell-cycle deregulation in a causal progression; distinguish excess positive regulator activity from missing negative regulators.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Read checkpoints in pathway diagrams, predict effects of cyclin amplification/CDK inhibition or p21 loss, identify why spindle checkpoint failure increases chromosome errors.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Complete normal cycle machinery in Unit 2.D; full p53 genetics in 4.E.04; advanced CDK regulation and specialist cancer sequencing panels excluded.


### 4.E.06 — Apoptosis Escape, Senescence and Survival of Cancer Cells

**Student-facing menu description:** Explain how evasion of programmed cell death, altered Bcl-2-family regulation and failure of senescence support tumor survival.

**Driving scientific question:** How can damaged or abnormally dividing cells persist when normal tissue would remove or arrest them?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Apoptosis as a regulated safeguard, intrinsic mitochondrial and extrinsic receptor pathways at a short cancer-relevant refresher; DNA damage→p53→proapoptotic responses and BAX/BAK versus BCL-2-family survival balance; cytochrome c/caspase activation at illustrative level; changes in pro-/antiapoptotic proteins and death receptors in tumors; cellular senescence and replicative limitation, telomere maintenance and telomerase as relevant cancer survival/immortalization concept; contrast apoptosis escape with fast proliferation and with uncontrolled necrosis; rationale for therapies that restore damage-induced cell death.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Predict relative apoptosis following TP53 loss or BCL-2 upregulation, distinguish growth arrest versus apoptosis escape and interpret simplified survival/viability plots.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Core apoptosis/necrosis/autophagy mechanistic lectures are in Unit 2.D, cell-cycle control in 4.E.05, targeted therapy in 4.E.12. Do not expand into unrelated aging diseases or specialist telomere biophysics.


### 4.E.07 — Virus-Induced Cancer: Viral Oncoproteins and Growth-Control Disruption

**Student-facing menu description:** Connect oncogenic viruses with persistent infection, viral proteins, p53/Rb disruption and virus-associated transformation.

**Driving scientific question:** How can a virus contribute to cancer, and why is viral infection alone usually not equivalent to malignancy?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Virus-associated oncogenesis as one route among multiple cancer causes; viral persistence/integration or sustained viral gene expression as context-specific mechanisms; human papillomavirus (high-risk HPV) E6–p53 and E7–Rb as classic example; EBV-mediated B-cell proliferation and HBV/HCV-associated liver-cancer mechanisms mentioned at exam-level, distinguishing direct viral protein disruption from chronic-inflammation pathways; infection versus transformation versus tumor progression and additional cooperating events; animal tumor-virus models only when clearly relevant.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Interpret HPV oncoprotein/p53/Rb diagrams and predict checkpoint consequences, compare direct oncogenic protein and chronic infection mechanisms, avoid implying all HPV infection causes cancer.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** 4.D.08 owns pathogen–host transformation and viral entry context; here emphasis is **integration with general cancer molecular biology**, host tumor suppressors and persistence. Immune responses to virus remain in 4.C.


### 4.E.08 — Tumor Microenvironment: Interactions of Cancer and Normal Cells

**Student-facing menu description:** Explore interactions among tumor cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, blood vessels and extracellular matrix that shape growth and survival.

**Driving scientific question:** Why is a tumor influenced by neighboring normal tissue instead of behaving as isolated cancer cells?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Cancer cells interacting with stromal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, ECM and soluble mediators; tumor-associated fibroblasts, extracellular matrix remodeling and integrin adhesion as functional interfaces; angiogenesis and VEGF as a representative signaling example, nutrient/oxygen effects and simple hypoxia response; tumor-promoting versus tumor-limiting immune interactions, basic immune evasion at principle level; paracrine growth signals and microenvironmental selection; distinguish tumor microenvironment from strictly cell-autonomous oncogene mutations.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Predict consequences of impaired angiogenesis, identify which cell populations contribute to ECM/microenvironment, reason about opposite roles of stromal/immune cells.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Integrin chemistry in 4.B, basic receptor pathways in 4.A, innate/adaptive immunology in 4.C and full immunotherapy in 4.E.12; avoid specialized tumor single-cell genomics.


### 4.E.09 — Local Invasion: Cell Adhesion, Epithelial–Mesenchymal Changes and Matrix Breakdown

**Student-facing menu description:** Follow loss of cell–cell restraint, altered integrins, extracellular-matrix degradation and cancer-cell migration during tissue invasion.

**Driving scientific question:** What changes permit a tumor cell to breach its original tissue boundary?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Difference between local growth and invasion, basement-membrane barriers, cadherin changes and cell–cell detachment, integrins and altered cell–matrix attachment, protease/MMP-assisted ECM remodeling, motility and cytoskeletal polarity; epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) as a model of increased invasive properties with context and reversibility, not an absolute requirement of every tumor; stromal assistance and locally invading cells; transition from carcinoma in situ to invasive carcinoma as a standard biological concept.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Order invasion steps, predict effect of preserved epithelial adhesion or blocked MMPs in a simplified system, distinguish local invasion from metastatic spread.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Cell adhesion/ECM base chemistry in 4.B, 4.E.08 covers tumor-normal interaction, distant spread and seeding in 4.E.10; no detailed pathology grading or research-level EMT networks.


### 4.E.10 — Metastasis: Intravasation, Survival, Extravasation and Colonization

**Student-facing menu description:** Trace the metastatic cascade from local invasion to vascular entry, circulation, distant tissue exit, seeding and secondary tumor growth.

**Driving scientific question:** How can cancer cells spread to and establish growing colonies in distant organs?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Definition of metastasis versus local invasion, major metastatic routes (blood, lymphatic, transcoelomic in brief) and why route matters; tumor-cell intravasation, circulation survival, immune/mechanical stress, extravasation, dormancy or failed colonization, and eventual outgrowth as separate bottlenecks; organ tropism/seed-and-soil idea as classic conceptual framework; angiogenesis/ECM/stromal support integrated briefly; primary versus secondary tumor identification by origin rather than the new organ; distinguish dissemination from successful metastasis.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Arrange the metastatic cascade and classify an intervention by stage, interpret organotropism and distant lesion scenarios, distinguish primary and metastatic tumor nomenclature.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Mechanism of local invasion already explained in 4.E.09; no clinical imaging, grading, staging or treatment decision algorithms.


### 4.E.11 — Therapeutic Control I: Surgery, Radiation and Cytotoxic Chemotherapy Principles

**Student-facing menu description:** Relate tumor removal, DNA-damaging radiation, cytotoxic drugs and cell-cycle vulnerability to controlled or failed cancer-cell growth.

**Driving scientific question:** How do classical cancer interventions exploit abnormal proliferation or remove malignant tissue?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** Therapy as biological attempt to remove/control proliferating cells, localized removal through surgery and limits for occult metastatic disease as an overview; radiotherapy as DNA damage with repair and cellular death/senescence mechanisms, differences between normal and cancer cells; categories of cytotoxic chemotherapy: DNA damaging/crosslinking, antimetabolites and mitotic inhibitors as classes without drug-by-drug catalogues; proliferative fraction, cell-cycle sensitivity, systemic adverse effects and the basic reason for combination or resistance; therapeutic intervention versus immune prevention distinctions.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Match broad intervention with cellular mechanism, reason why rapidly dividing normal cells may also be harmed, compare local versus systemic treatment principles.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** Not clinical advice, dosing, current guidelines, toxicity management or patient-specific therapy selection. Molecular/immune-directed interventions in 4.E.12.


### 4.E.12 — Therapeutic Control II: Targeted Therapy, Immunotherapy and Resistance

**Student-facing menu description:** Understand molecularly targeted growth inhibition, hormone-directed approaches, immune-based tumor control and the basic causes of treatment resistance.

**Driving scientific question:** How can tumor-specific molecular dependence or immune recognition be used to restrain growth, and why can responses fail?

**Complete internal microtopic responsibility:** General targeted-therapy principle: link activated oncogenic driver to inhibition, using BCR–ABL kinase inhibition as a simple mechanistic example; targeted receptor/kinase inhibition, hormone-dependent growth control, antiangiogenic rationale; immune-mediated tumor growth control: monoclonal antibodies, checkpoint blockade and cellular immune approaches at introductory biological mechanism level; difference between direct tumor signaling inhibition and relieving immune inhibition; innate/acquired resistance as selection of pre-existing variants, secondary target mutation, bypass signaling or cellular context changes; combination strategies as a conceptual response; distinguish curative eradication from control or relapse without unsupported efficacy claims.

**Original NET/SET explanation, comparison and problem-solving requirements:** Link a molecular target to a mechanistic intervention, predict simple resistance route, distinguish a kinase inhibitor from checkpoint immune modulation, evaluate schematic growth curves after treatment.

**No-overlap, accuracy and academic-depth boundary:** 4.C owns immunology pathways, 4.A signaling and 4.D virus–host interaction; detailed clinical regimens, marketed drug comparisons, clinical trial data, gene therapy protocols, CAR-T engineering and specialist personalized oncology are excluded.

## Chronological teaching arc and balance audit

1. **4.E.01–02 — Initiation and genetic origin.** Establish how malignant cell behavior differs from controlled tissue growth, then explain the somatic or inherited changes and rearrangements that make a progenitor clone abnormal. Include genome instability only in its cancer-specific consequences rather than repeating 3.A.
2. **4.E.03–04 — The molecular accelerator and brakes.** Contrast activated proto-oncogenes and inactivated tumor suppressors with carefully distinguished dominant/recessive cellular effects and representative cancer examples.
3. **4.E.05–06 — Loss of internal safeguards.** Connect genomic alterations to checkpoint failure, sustained division and escaped programmed cell death/senescence; preserve apoptosis as explicit MP SET/legacy content.
4. **4.E.07 — Virus-associated oncogenesis.** Revisit the viral transformation concept from 4.D only to explain how viral persistence/onco-proteins contribute to cancer pathways; avoid repeating viral entry and infection mechanisms.
5. **4.E.08–10 — Tissue interactions and spread.** Explain the normal stromal/immune/endothelial environment, local invasion, then systemic dissemination, survival and secondary organ colonization as *distinct stages*.
6. **4.E.11–12 — Biological principles of cancer interventions.** Contrast removal and conventional DNA/mitotic cytotoxicity with rational molecularly directed and immune-based growth control; introduce resistance only at NET/SET explanatory level.

## Scientific accuracy and exclusion rules

- **Cancer initiation:** Not every tumor must arise from a conventional stem cell; discuss progenitor cells, somatic clonal evolution and cell-of-origin at appropriate generality.
- **Terminology:** Benign masses are not automatically malignant; *invasion* is distinct from *metastasis*, and dissemination is not the same as successful colonization.
- **Mutations:** Gain-of-function oncogene activation and loss-of-function tumor suppressors are mechanistically distinct. The two-hit model explains classic RB1 contexts but is not an absolute claim for all suppressors or TP53 mutant states.
- **Checkpoint logic:** Keep G1/S and DNA-damage responses grounded in normal cycle prerequisites; do not overgeneralize that every tumor has identical cyclin or p53 defects.
- **Apoptosis:** BCL-2/BAX/BAK and p53 can change apoptotic susceptibility; do not conflate apoptosis with necrosis or classify all cancer cells as universally apoptosis-resistant.
- **Viral cancer:** Viral infection, immortalization, viral transformation and cancer are separate endpoints; HPV-associated E6/E7 effects are a standard example, not a universal mechanism of all tumor viruses.
- **Cancer–normal interactions:** Not all microenvironmental immune cells promote cancer; the immune system can also inhibit tumors. Integrin/ECM pathways should be explained as communication without repeating all 4.B adhesion chemistry.
- **Metastasis:** Learn local invasion → intravasation → survival in circulation → extravasation → dormancy/colonization, with organ specificity and known inefficiency; do not equate presence of circulating tumor cells with an established secondary tumor.
- **Interventions:** Teach cellular mechanisms, not medical recommendations, dosing or current efficacy comparisons. Clinical outcomes vary across cancer type; no treatment should be presented as universally curative.
- **No drift:** High-dimensional cancer omics, specialized cancer stem-cell cultures, detailed CAR-T fabrication, cutting-edge oncogene modeling, clinical guidelines and medical imaging interpretation are not syllabus-required separate nodes.
- **Reading-page protocol:** Each eventual page should develop its subtopics with friendly, precise, chronological English, labeled diagrams and NET/SET exam reasoning; no fixed character or token cap, no thin artificial word-filling and no child-topic pages.
