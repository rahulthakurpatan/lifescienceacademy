## Five full educational chapter contracts

### 6.B.01 — Plant Respiration and the Citric Acid Cycle

**Student-facing menu description:** Trace sugar-derived respiratory substrates through pyruvate oxidation, acetyl-CoA entry and the eight reactions of the citric acid cycle, linking NADH, FADH2, carbon balance and biosynthetic precursors.

**Guiding scientific question:** How does a plant transfer carbon energy from sugars to the reducing equivalents that fuel mitochondrial ATP production, even when photosynthesis is active?

**Complete mandatory topics, pathway detail, experimental evidence and examples:** Link photosynthesis-derived sucrose, stored starch and glycolysis to pyruvate import into mitochondria (review glycolysis only as a prerequisite); mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase generating acetyl-CoA and NADH; plant mitochondrial location and matrix/cytosol interplay; eight canonical TCA reactions in correct sequence: citrate synthase, aconitase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, succinyl-CoA synthetase, succinate dehydrogenase, fumarase and malate dehydrogenase; stoichiometry per acetyl-CoA: two CO2, three NADH, one FADH2 and one substrate-level NTP (GTP/ATP by organism/context); note succinate dehydrogenase/Complex II sits in inner membrane, distinct from matrix soluble enzymes; oxaloacetate regeneration, shared carbon skeletons for amino acids, anaplerotic replenishment and carbon flow; plant respiration in both light and dark, day/night effects, photorespiratory glycine mitochondrial substrate as forward link; regulation by substrate availability, adenylates/redox; exceptions in plant noncyclic/branched TCA flux without making a universal reverse pathway claim.

**NET/SET diagrams, calculations and explained problem targets:** Trace carbon fate with labeled pyruvate or acetate; distinguish one TCA turn vs glycolysis yield, calculate NADH/FADH2/CO2 per acetyl-CoA, infer accumulation after succinate dehydrogenase deficiency, interpret citrate/TCA cycle diagram and NAD+/NADH-linked control.

**Scope boundary, accurate terminology and no-duplication principle:** Detailed glycolysis and fundamental TCA bioenergetics belong to Unit 01, but plant-specific substrate supply/compartmentation and citric acid cycle exam mastery belong here. Respiratory electron transfer is 6.B.02; ATP synthase 6.B.03; photorespiratory C2 recycling 6.B.05.

### 6.B.02 — Plant Mitochondrial Electron Transport Chain

**Student-facing menu description:** Follow electrons from matrix NADH and succinate through Complexes I–IV, ubiquinone and cytochrome c to oxygen, including plant alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases and proton translocation.

**Guiding scientific question:** How do electrons from carbon oxidation reach oxygen through plant mitochondrial carriers and establish an electrochemical gradient?

**Complete mandatory topics, pathway detail, experimental evidence and examples:** Inner membrane, matrix and intermembrane space topography and orientation, redox reactions and mitochondrial outer membrane as context; conventional respiratory Complex I NADH dehydrogenase, FMN/Fe–S, ubiquinone Q/QH2 and H+ pumping; Complex II/succinate dehydrogenase linking TCA-derived succinate oxidation to Q with no proton pumping; Complex III/cytochrome bc1 Q-cycle overview, cytochrome c mobile protein, Complex IV/cytochrome c oxidase reducing O2 to H2O with proton movement and chemistry; membrane carriers Q versus mobile cytochrome c and correct order; location of plant alternative type II NAD(P)H dehydrogenases on internal/external inner membrane surfaces, non-proton-pumping electron feeding into Q and some rotenone-insensitive entry as qualified example. Redox direction, coupling, ROS at Complex I/III in context; distinguish conventional ETC terminal O2 reduction from mitochondrial ATP synthase, which does not directly transfer electrons. Plant-specific structural organization and regulatory flexibility as scope without exhaustive supercomplex assembly.

**NET/SET diagrams, calculations and explained problem targets:** Label ETC and membrane faces, work carrier-order or inhibitor-vignette reasoning, predict changes to membrane potential and O2 consumption after Complex I/II/III/IV disruption, explain why Complex II does not pump H+, compare alternative entry with conventional Complex I qualitatively.

**Scope boundary, accurate terminology and no-duplication principle:** 6.B.01 owns TCA and NADH production, 6.B.03 owns ATP synthesis/stoichiometry, 6.B.04 owns alternative oxidase replacing terminal cytochrome pathway. Unit 01 teaches general oxidative phosphorylation; plant chain topology/alternatives are primary here.

### 6.B.03 — Oxidative Phosphorylation and ATP Synthesis in Plants

**Student-facing menu description:** Explain how mitochondrial electron transport generates the proton motive force that drives F0F1 ATP synthase, and assess coupling, ATP yield, inhibitors and uncouplers.

**Guiding scientific question:** What links electron flow to ATP formation, and why is the efficiency of plant respiration not one fixed ATP number?

**Complete mandatory topics, pathway detail, experimental evidence and examples:** Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation as electron transfer linked to ATP phosphorylation, respiratory proton pumping at I, III and IV (not II) from matrix to intermembrane space; proton motive force, membrane potential Δψ and ΔpH, proton return through ATP synthase (Complex V) to matrix where ATP is synthesized, rotor/stator and F0F1 conceptual machinery, ADP/Pi transport and adenine nucleotide translocase/Pi carrier costs; contrast substrate-level phosphorylation in TCA versus OXPHOS; respiratory control by ADP availability and oxygen, coupled vs uncoupled membrane, classic ATP synthase inhibitors (oligomycin) and uncouplers, directional membrane potential effects; illustrative P/O ≈2.5 per NADH and ≈1.5 per FADH2 in textbook mitochondria as approximate non-universal values, altered by plant alternative entry/exit, proton leak and actual transport costs; proton pumping and P/O reasoning versus fixed 36/38 ATP claim; relationship to high respiration at night, maintenance and carbon skeletons.

**NET/SET diagrams, calculations and explained problem targets:** Draw Δp across inner membrane, interpret respiratory control/oligomycin/uncoupler traces, explain why Complex-II electrons yield fewer ATP, distinguish O2 use with or without ATP synthesis, calculate indicative ATP given explicitly stated assumptions rather than assigning universal yield.

**Scope boundary, accurate terminology and no-duplication principle:** Unit 01 generic biophysics, 6.B.02 carrier biochemistry, 6.B.04 AOX reduction of ATP coupling; 6.A.04 contrasts thylakoid photophosphorylation, where proton and ATP orientation differ.

### 6.B.04 — Alternative Oxidase and Alternative Respiratory Pathways

**Student-facing menu description:** Explore plant mitochondrial AOX as a non-proton-pumping ubiquinol-to-oxygen bypass of Complexes III and IV, and explain redox flexibility, reduced ATP yield and stress-related roles.

**Guiding scientific question:** Why would a plant route respiratory electrons through a pathway that conserves less energy as ATP?

**Complete mandatory topics, pathway detail, experimental evidence and examples:** Plant mitochondrial AOX in inner membrane, ubiquinol (QH2) oxidation and direct O2→H2O reduction, bypass of cytochrome-chain Complexes III and IV, non-proton pumping at AOX itself and reduced ATP yield for those electrons; distinction from Complex I upstream proton pumping, which can still occur depending on entry point; alternative internal and external NAD(P)H dehydrogenases that also bypass Complex I with no proton pumping, combinations with AOX/normal cytochrome pathway and their ATP implications. AOX activation/redox regulation by plant physiological conditions and organic acid effects at broad exam level, cyanide-resistant but AOX-sensitive respiration as classical evidence, AOX inhibitors SHAM or n-propyl gallate as textbook tools with off-target limitations; reduction of ubiquinone over-reduction, maintaining TCA flux, redox balance and possible ROS attenuation, cold/heat and biotic/abiotic stress links context-dependent not a universal claim that AOX always prevents oxidative stress. Differentiate bypass physiology from increased total photosynthetic carbon fixation.

**NET/SET diagrams, calculations and explained problem targets:** Draw AOX branch from reduced Q pool and quantify relative energy loss by bypassed proton sites under stated assumptions; predict cyanide vs AOX inhibitor response, compare Complex-I bypass vs terminal AOX, interpret ROS/redox state qualitative outcomes.

**Scope boundary, accurate terminology and no-duplication principle:** 6.B.02 supplies the full cytochrome ETC route, 6.B.03 coupling and ATP efficiencies, 6.H broad stress physiology. This node teaches AOX physiological regulation and tradeoffs—not a second whole ETC or advanced inhibitor experimental design.

### 6.B.05 — Photorespiration: The C2 Glycolate Pathway

**Student-facing menu description:** Trace Rubisco oxygenation and 2-phosphoglycolate recycling through chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria to 3-phosphoglycerate, including glycine-to-serine conversion, CO2/NH3 release and metabolic costs.

**Guiding scientific question:** How does a C3 leaf recover carbon trapped by Rubisco oxygenase, and why does that process consume energy and release CO2?

**Complete mandatory topics, pathway detail, experimental evidence and examples:** Rubisco bifunctionality and competing oxygenation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate giving one 3-PGA and one 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG), 2-PG detoxification and salvage; chloroplast phosphoglycolate phosphatase producing glycolate, peroxisomal glycolate oxidase forming glyoxylate and H2O2 with catalase detoxification, aminotransferases generating glycine, glycine transfer to mitochondria and glycine decarboxylase/serine hydroxymethyltransferase (two glycine→one serine + one CO2 + one NH3 with reducing equivalents), serine to peroxisome and hydroxypyruvate reductase producing glycerate (NADH use), glycerate returns to chloroplast and glycerate kinase uses ATP to regenerate 3-PGA; chloroplast reassimilation of photorespiratory ammonium through GS/GOGAT with ATP and reductant demand, relationship with nitrogen metabolism 6.C. Overall two Rubisco oxygenations generate two 2-PG and return three carbons as 3-PGA while releasing one carbon as CO2, with one ammonium in canonical C2 cycle; oxygen/CO2 ratio, leaf temperature, stomatal limitations, and lowered flux in C4/CAM CO2-concentrating systems from 6.A; role in metabolic homeostasis/photoprotection in context, not simplistic 'always wasteful' absolute; distinguish mitochondrial glycine decarboxylation involved in photorespiration from plant TCA respiration.

**NET/SET diagrams, calculations and explained problem targets:** Sequence compartmental enzymes and products, reason 2-PG→glycolate→glyoxylate→glycine→serine→hydroxypyruvate→glycerate→3-PGA, trace CO2/NH3 release and ATP use, compare low versus high O2/CO2 conditions and C3/C4 photorespiration, interpret inhibited glycine decarboxylase or photorespiratory enzyme mutants.

**Scope boundary, accurate terminology and no-duplication principle:** 6.A.06–08 owns Calvin cycle and C3/C4/CAM pathways, 6.B.01–04 mitochondrial respiratory energy pathways, 6.C NH3 assimilation, 6.F stomata and 6.H stress. Do not confuse photorespiration with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation; both intersect metabolism but are not one sequential process.

## Causal continuity between the five chapters

1. **6.B.01 makes NADH and FADH2:** Begin with plant sugars feeding glycolysis/pyruvate and the mitochondrial TCA cycle. Understand energy-carrying reducing equivalents, CO2 release, carbon skeleton outputs and day/night context.
2. **6.B.02 passes electrons to oxygen:** Connect TCA-produced NADH/FADH2 to respiratory Complexes I–IV, membrane carrier Q and cytochrome c, and proton pumping. Plant mitochondria also have alternate electron entry proteins.
3. **6.B.03 conserves the gradient as ATP:** Proton pumping generates Δp, and Complex V drives ATP formation. Approximate yields depend on how protons and electrons travel; no fixed all-plant ATP number.
4. **6.B.04 adds a flexibility branch:** AOX accepts QH2 electrons and reduces oxygen while bypassing Complex III/IV proton pumping. It trades ATP conservation for pathway/regulatory flexibility without guaranteeing reduced stress in every context.
5. **6.B.05 returns to the Rubisco problem introduced by 6.A:** Chloroplast oxygenation generates 2-PG, then peroxisome/mitochondrion/chloroplast reactions recover 3-PGA with CO2/NH3 release and associated energy costs. Mitochondria participate in C2 glycine metabolism here, **not** as proof that photorespiration is the last stage of the mitochondrial ETC.

## Nonnegotiable scientific accuracy checklist

- Complex II is both TCA succinate dehydrogenase and an ETC Q reducer; **it is not a proton pump**. Complexes I, III and IV support proton translocation in the conventional cytochrome pathway.
- Matrix NADH electron entry through Complex I and succinate electron entry through II are not equivalent in proton pumping and potential ATP yield.
- Plant alternative internal/external type-II NAD(P)H dehydrogenases lack the proton pumping function of conventional Complex I; they can feed electron transfer without identical coupling.
- Respiratory ATP synthase sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane and synthesizes ATP toward the **matrix side**, using proton return from the intermembrane space; do not conflate this with chloroplast ATP synthase orientation.
- P/O numbers and ATP balance require explicit assumptions; approximate textbook 2.5 and 1.5 values are **not universal plant mitochondrial constants**.
- AOX itself **does not translocate protons** and diverts electrons from QH2 to O2 instead of through III/IV; some upstream proton pumping at Complex I may still occur in the presence of AOX.
- Cyanide-insensitive respiration is characteristic of AOX branch but inhibitor responses have limits and must not be generalized without experimental interpretation.
- Mitochondrial respiration and photorespiration both interact with mitochondria but are **not synonyms**. Photorespiration is a **three-compartment carbon-salvage route** that begins with Rubisco oxygenation.
- In the canonical C2 photorespiratory pathway, two oxygenation products eventually yield **one 3-PGA (three carbon atoms)** with **one CO2 released**, and the mitochondrion produces **one NH3 per two glycine**; follow compartmental carbon flow and energy requirements carefully.
- 2-phosphoglycolate inhibits photosynthetic metabolism if unrecycled; photorespiration has costs but also important physiological functions, so avoid unconditional "useless waste process" claims.
- Photorespiratory NH3 recapture through chloroplast GS/GOGAT belongs here as pathway integration; 6.C owns complete nitrogen assimilation.
