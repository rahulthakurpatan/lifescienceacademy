## Eight connected academic node contracts

### 6.F.01 — Water Potential, Osmosis and the Driving Forces of Plant Transport

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Build water-potential reasoning from osmotic, pressure, gravitational and matric components, then predict water movement, turgor, plasmolysis and plant-cell responses to changing environments.

**Driving scientific question:** Why does water move from soil into roots, up tall plants and into living cells without being actively pumped as individual water molecules?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Define chemical potential of water, water potential Ψw relative to pure water (MPa), components Ψs/Ψπ (osmotic; typically negative), Ψp (pressure, positive in turgid living cells, commonly negative in transpiring xylem), Ψg (gravitational, material for tall plants), Ψm (matric adsorption, especially soils/cell walls) with conditional approximate sum Ψw=Ψs+Ψp+Ψg+Ψm and note soil formulas may group terms differently. Explain dilute solution van't Hoff approximation Ψs≈−RTC_osm with R consistent units, absolute temperature, ideal-solution limitation and ion dissociation/osmotic particle concentration; Ψw comparisons from less negative to more negative, pressure/vapor phase as context. Osmosis requires differential water potential across selectively permeable membrane, cell wall/turgor plasmolysis and incipient plasmolysis, hypotonic/isotonic/hypertonic effects, flaccidity and water movement of root/hydrated tissues; contrast osmotic potential and total Ψw. Basic soil–root–leaf–atmosphere gradient, water potential as a unifying principle; why living cell osmotic equilibration differs from xylem bulk water flow; negative pressure means xylem tension not a negatively charged solute.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Verify units in worked numerical examples: Ψs=−0.6 MPa and Ψp=+0.3 MPa gives cell Ψw=−0.3 MPa if other terms negligible; predict net water direction for surrounding Ψw=−0.2 or −0.5 MPa, compare two xylem heights and gravity. Diagram cell in isotonic/hypertonic solution and explain plasmolysis vs loss of turgor; reinterpret water-potential maps for given gradients, without assuming all soil potentials are simple ideal solute solutions.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** Unit 01 owns general solution thermodynamics, Unit 02.A general membrane/osmosis, 6.F.02 plant-specific transport protein behavior, 6.F.04 bulk xylem hydraulics and 6.H drought-response signaling. This node owns quantitative plant water-potential reasoning.

### 6.F.02 — Plant Membrane Transport, Aquaporins and Cell-to-Cell Movement

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Explain plant H⁺-ATPases, channels, carriers, cotransporters and aquaporins, then compare apoplastic and plasmodesmata-mediated symplastic transfer of ions, solutes and selected macromolecules.

**Driving scientific question:** How can a root or leaf cell selectively transport mineral ions and sugars while letting water and some intercellular signals move efficiently?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Plasma membrane and tonoplast selective permeability, diffusion/facilitated diffusion vs primary ATP-driven H+ export and vacuolar H+ pumps, electrochemical gradients, membrane potential and secondary H+-coupled symport/antiport; examples nitrate uptake (NPF/NRT-type in contextual form), H+/sucrose symport SUT/SUC (preview to 6.F.07), K+ channels and mineral selectivity; avoid conflating passive water channels with active pumping. Plant PIP/TIP and other aquaporins, gating/trafficking, membrane water permeability and bidirectional flux set by Ψ gradient, no claims that all channels carry the same solutes/gases. Plant cell-wall apoplast versus cytoplasmic symplast, plasmodesmata structure (plasma-membrane-lined channels with desmotubule), size-exclusion and regulated gating including callose, cellular passage of selected signaling proteins, RNAs and other macromolecules in some contexts—NOT unrestricted translocation of arbitrary large biomolecules from soil through the entire xylem. Distinguish phloem mobile macromolecules from root-soil uptake of intact macromolecules; root and companion cells regulate transport and gate permeability.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Calculate direction of an ion from combined concentration and electric gradients qualitatively; compare H+-ATPase inhibition versus aquaporin gating; explain plasmodesmata dye tracing and callose-dependent transport perturbations, identify what a radiolabeled ion uptake experiment establishes versus mere adsorption to cell walls, and contrast transcellular versus plasmodesmata routes.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** Unit 02.A teaches universal ion-channel and membrane biophysics; 6.F.03 owns root endodermal barriers and whole-root movement; 6.F.07 phloem sugar loading pathways. 6.C full nitrate assimilation is a biochemical process, not membrane uptake; 6.F.02 should not retell those reactions.

### 6.F.03 — Soil-to-Root Water and Mineral Uptake: Apoplast, Symplast and Endodermis

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Trace water and mineral ions from the rhizosphere through epidermis, cortex and endodermis to the stele, emphasizing root transport selectivity, Casparian strips and aquaporin-controlled radial flow.

**Driving scientific question:** How can roots acquire soil water and minerals while regulating which dissolved substances reach the vascular tissues?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Soil water availability, soil texture/matric and ionic effects, root hairs, rhizosphere and contact area, root epidermis/cortex/endodermis/pericycle and xylem location with mature tissue variation. Three radial routes: apoplastic via cell walls/intercellular spaces, symplastic via plasmodesmata, transmembrane/transcellular alternating membrane crossings; endodermal Casparian strip lignin barrier interrupts apoplastic continuity, necessitating selective membrane passage into the living symplast for typical uptake and later xylem loading; suberin lamellae and passage cells as nuanced context, exodermis in some species; mineral selective uptake with H+ pumps and transporters, rhizosphere pH and interactions, mycorrhiza as nutritional context without duplicating mutualism ecology. Aquaporin regulation and root hydraulic conductivity, developmental plasticity across young/mature regions, xylem loading of ions and osmotically influenced root pressure (preview), precipitation/soil salinity caveats. Distinguish cell-to-cell water route from long-distance xylem movement and nitrate uptake from nitrate reduction.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Root cross-section labeled pathway maps, tracer/dye experiments contrasting apoplastic entry and endodermal blockade, predict effects of missing Casparian strip or low aquaporin permeability, compare nitrate transporter deficiency versus nitrate reductase deficiency, reason water flow using soil and root Ψw values.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.F.02 owns channels and transport thermodynamics, 6.F.04 owns xylem ascending hydraulics; 6.C nitrogen assimilation and 6.H salinity stress response; Unit 05 root development and differentiation. Keep this as *radial resource uptake* not total vascular transport.

### 6.F.04 — Xylem Transport and Ascent of Sap: Cohesion–Tension and Hydraulic Limits

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Follow water ascent through vessels and tracheids using cohesion–tension theory, root-pressure exceptions, xylem resistance and embolism risks from soil to leaves.

**Driving scientific question:** How can a tall tree raise water against gravity without a mechanical pump in its xylem conduits?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Xylem tracheids and vessel elements as specialized dead-at-maturity conduits in typical mature vascular plants, pits and perforation plates, lignified wall strength; xylem water plus dissolved mineral ions/hormones and some solutes, bulk flow as pressure-gradient-driven under tension, atmospheric evaporation at mesophyll cell walls producing negative water potential that draws the continuous xylem column through cohesion, adhesion/capillarity as supportive physical phenomena not independently adequate mechanism for tall-tree rise. Soil–leaf hydraulic resistance, Ψ gradients and gravitational offset (~0.01 MPa per meter height) with unit caution, hydraulic conductance vs resistance, cavitation/embolism, air seeding and pit membrane bottlenecks, vulnerability to drought, restricted refilling and maintenance caveats; positive root pressure at low transpiration, guttation through hydathodes as evidence, and why root pressure cannot explain most daytime transpiration pull in tall plants. Distinguish xylem mass flow driving pressure from metabolic transport pumps and from phloem positive-pressure flow.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Explain the classical potometer vs actual xylem flow limitations, root-pressure/guttation vs transpiration cases, tension and hydrodynamic resistance diagrams, water-potential profile root→stem→leaf and xylem embolism inference, numerical examples for height/gravity under stated simplifications.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.F.01 provides Ψw math; 6.F.03 root delivery and Casparian strip; 6.F.05 leaf evaporation/stomata; 6.F.06 phloem comparison. Full xylem lignification anatomy is Unit 5.D, drought embolism survival is 6.H.

### 6.F.05 — Transpiration, Stomatal Regulation and Whole-Plant Water Balance

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Explain evaporation through leaves, stomatal and boundary-layer resistance, environmental control of transpiration and its consequences for cooling, CO₂ exchange and water-use efficiency.

**Driving scientific question:** Why do leaves lose water while photosynthesizing, and how do weather, stomata and leaf structure govern this trade-off?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Soil–plant–atmosphere continuum, evaporation from wet mesophyll cell walls, diffusion through substomatal airspace, stomatal pore and leaf boundary layer, stomatal and cuticular transpiration and limited lenticular evaporation context, distinction between evaporation and transpirational bulk water uptake. Vapor-pressure gradient/deficit (VPD) and stomatal conductance with bounded approximate relationship E≈g_w×(vapor mole-fraction deficit) where measurement units and boundary layer resistances matter, roles of temperature, relative humidity, wind and light, transpiration stream water supply and evaporative cooling, nutrient mass flow and potential hydraulic failure; stomatal number/distribution, sunken stomata, cuticle/leaf morphology; guard-cell K+, anions, turgor and H+-ATPase as a short reprise of 6.E.05, ABA closure as 6.D.07 cross-reference, CO2 and circadian influences with exceptions. Stomatal/leaf conductance versus photosynthesis assimilation and intrinsic or whole-plant water-use efficiency (A/g_sw vs A/E) clearly distinguished, CAM night openings as exception; distinguish potometer water *uptake* measurement from direct transpiration measurement, gravimetric, porometer and gas-exchange measurements and real experimental interpretation.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Interpret changes in transpiration when humidity or wind changes with boundary-layer caveat, compute simple E/g cases with defined units, compare leaf size/surface traits, contrast potometer uptake with leaf mass-loss method, explain when stomatal closure reduces CO₂ uptake even if it protects Ψw.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.E.05 owns blue-light phototropin–guard cell molecular pathway, 6.D.07 owns ABA receptor signaling, 6.A owns C3/C4/CAM photosynthetic carbon fixation, 6.H drought tolerance and 6.F.04 xylem ascent. Here focus on physical evaporation, water-cost economics and transport integration.

### 6.F.06 — Phloem Architecture, Source–Sink Relations and the Pressure-Flow Hypothesis

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Understand sieve elements, companion cells, source and sink tissues, then follow the Münch pressure-flow model linking osmotic loading, positive turgor and long-distance assimilate movement.

**Driving scientific question:** Why do sugars generally travel away from mature photosynthetic leaves to growing or storage tissues, and what drives movement inside sieve tubes?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Mature source leaves photosynthesizing and exporting assimilates (often sucrose), developing roots, shoots, seeds, fruits and storage organs as sinks; organs can transition source↔sink by stage, source/sink relative not fixed by anatomy. Phloem sieve elements and companion cells, sieve plates, enucleate but living angiosperm sieve elements, metabolic support from companion cells; gymnosperm sieve cells/albuminous cells as qualified difference, vascular layout and xylem–phloem exchange. Phloem sap sucrose, amino acids, potassium, hormones and selected systemic regulatory RNAs/proteins (species and selective trafficking) with caveats, positive turgor pressure versus negative-tension xylem. Münch pressure-flow/hypothesis: loading at source lowers local phloem solute potential, draws water from xylem, raises turgor; unloading at sink permits water return and lowers pressure, producing long-distance pressure-gradient mass flow through sieve tubes. Loading energy costs arise mainly at membranes when required, not an ATP-driven motor moving each sucrose within the tube. Different sieve tubes can simultaneously conduct different directions between distinct sources/sinks; avoid claiming unrestricted simultaneous bidirectional bulk flow within one tube. Classical ringing/girdling, aphid stylet sap sampling and autoradiographic 14C labeling as evidence, limitations for living systems.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Draw pressure–osmotic source/sink loop, explain isotope sugar tracer and girdling effects, interpret aphid-stylet phloem turgor evidence, reason whether a fruit transitions from sink to source with development, compare xylem and phloem pressure and direction.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.A production of photosynthate, 6.F.07 mechanism of entry/loading, 6.F.08 unloading and partitioning, 6.F.04 xylem hydraulics and 6.D hormone chemistry. This node owns phloem structure and mass-flow logic.

### 6.F.07 — Phloem Loading: Apoplastic, Symplastic and Polymer-Trapping Mechanisms

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Compare SWEET-mediated apoplastic sucrose export and SUT/SUC proton-coupled uptake with plasmodesmatal symplastic loading and raffinose-family polymer trapping.

**Driving scientific question:** How does sucrose produced in mesophyll cells cross into the sieve-element/companion-cell complex efficiently enough to build transport pressure?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Mesophyll sucrose formation and minor-vein collection phloem, phloem parenchyma, companion cells and sieve elements; apoplastic loading as sucrose movement to cell wall space via facilitative SWEET-type carriers then active H+-coupled SUT/SUC import into companion cell or sieve-element/companion cell complex driven by H+-ATPase-generated electrochemical gradient. State SWEET exporter-mediated movement is generally facilitated and *not itself ATP-hydrolyzing active export*, even where whole process uses energy through symport. Symplastic loading via dense plasmodesmata (diffusion dependent on sugar chemical gradient), and polymer trapping in specialized intermediary cells converting imported sucrose to raffinose and stachyose to maintain concentration gradients and retention; biochemical reaction and molecular size caveats in a simplified model, not assume all species use one mechanism. Companion cell ultrastructure/transfer cells and wall ingrowth in apoplastic loaders, cell-specific SUT/SWEET localization, leakage and retrieval via transporters as limited context. Distinguish loading from long-distance pressure flow and compare likely species examples with qualifiers; genetically altered SUT or SWEET as evidence for import route and compensation caveats.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Diagram three loading routes from mesophyll to sieve element and identify where membranes are crossed, distinguish plasmodesmatal route, predict H+-ATPase/SUT inhibition vs SWEET loss versus plasmodesmata sealing, analyze 14C sucrose tracer and raffinose accumulation experiments with stated assumptions, articulate role of proton gradient and source pressure.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.F.02 membrane transporter fundamentals, 6.F.06 positive-pressure transport and companion cell basics, 6.F.08 unloading/sink unloading routes, 6.G secondary sugar chemistry (when relevant). Do not claim SWEET itself actively consumes ATP or every plant is apoplastic loader.

### 6.F.08 — Phloem Unloading, Sink Strength and Whole-Plant Assimilate Partitioning

**Student-facing syllabus menu description:** Trace photoassimilate unloading into roots, developing leaves, fruits and seeds via symplastic or apoplastic routes, then connect sink metabolism, storage and mobile signals with allocation across the plant.

**Driving scientific question:** What determines whether sucrose leaving a sieve tube fuels respiration, growth, fruit filling or storage rather than accumulating at the wrong site?

**Detailed core content, mechanisms, caveats and examples:** Source-to-sink unloading: phloem unloading from sieve-element/companion cell complex into post-phloem cells through symplastic plasmodesmatal routes, apoplastic release via transporters with sucrose uptake by SUT or hydrolysis by cell-wall invertase followed by hexose uptake, different sink tissues developmental states and tissue-specific transfer cell organization. Sink metabolism of sucrose via sucrose synthase (SuSy) or invertases, osmotic effects, starch/sugar conversion and storage, cell division/growth and cambial allocation, competition between growing leaves, roots, seeds, tubers and fruit; sink strength as a combination of transport capacity, unloading, metabolic demand and developmental/ hormonal cues, not just sugar concentration. Dynamic source↔sink transitions, sugar feedback on photosynthesis, stress-induced source allocation as context while detailed hormonal stress is 6.H; phloem-mobile signals include selected proteins and RNAs passing selectively through plasmodesmata/sieve tube and other mobile hormones, distinguish whole-plant signaling via phloem from direct soil uptake of large intact macromolecules. Xylem–phloem water coupling, distribution to different sinks, long-distance tracer evidence and transport route specificity. Compare photoperiodic FT long-distance flowering signal from 6.E.07 without retelling its gene regulation; wrap with integrated plant water/ion/assimilate map and scientifically bounded osmotic/pressure examples.

**Diagrams, quantitative reasoning and classical NET/SET evidence:** Use 14C/13C labeled sucrose or source-leaf CO2 to trace partition to developing fruit/roots, interpret invertase or SUT loss and diminished sink growth, quantify simplified source-sink mass balance under given assumptions, differentiate symplastic vs apoplastic unloading from callose/transport mutant evidence, compare source-sink reallocation before and after leaf maturation.

**Scope boundaries and precise cross-links:** 6.F.06 long-distance pressure-flow theory and anatomy, 6.F.07 loading route, 6.A sucrose production, 6.C nitrogen distribution, 6.E FT signaling, Unit 05 organogenesis and 6.H stress tradeoffs. Keep unloading and allocation as integrative final topic rather than duplicating the other chapters.

## Chronological/casual learner progression

1. **6.F.01 → 6.F.02:** Begin with a measurable driving force (water potential). Then cross selective living membranes using channels, transporters, pumps or symplastic continuity, keeping water movement thermodynamically passive even if gradient generation is energized.
2. **6.F.02 → 6.F.03:** Apply those routes to real root tissues. The Casparian strip interrupts the apoplastic corridor and creates a selective checkpoint before solutes and water reach the stele and xylem.
3. **6.F.03 → 6.F.04:** After radial root entry, transport becomes long-distance through mostly dead-at-maturity xylem conduits. Transpiration-generated tension and water cohesion explain ascent; root pressure offers a limited additional route under certain conditions.
4. **6.F.04 → 6.F.05:** At leaves, water leaves mesophyll through evaporation and diffusion. VPD, stomata and boundary layer shape both hydraulic tension and the cost of photosynthetic gas exchange.
5. **6.F.05 → 6.F.06:** Water flow permits carbon fixation, but sucrose export uses a **different living phloem system**. Source loading creates positive turgor that drives bulk solution flow down pressure gradients to sinks.
6. **6.F.06 → 6.F.07:** The pressure-flow model requires a source-loading mechanism. Compare membrane-crossing SWEET→SUT/SUC loading, passive plasmodesmatal transfer and polymer trapping, including metabolic energy and species-dependent alternatives.
7. **6.F.07 → 6.F.08:** Delivered sugars must unload and be metabolized or stored to sustain sink demand. Explain how unloading route, invertase, sink growth and mobile macromolecular signals control whole-plant partitioning.
8. **Integrated map:** Soil→root→xylem→leaf (water and minerals) and leaf source→phloem→growing/storage sinks (mostly sucrose and additional metabolites/signals). Water exchanges with phloem and flows through plant tissues according to local pressure/potential gradients—**not a single circulation pipe as in vertebrate blood flow**.

## Scientific accuracy and QA gate

- Water potential is a **chemical potential per volume** expressed in pressure units (often MPa), and water typically moves from higher to lower Ψw; a more negative value is lower potential under defined reference.
- The convenient sum `Ψw≈Ψs+Ψp+Ψg+Ψm` depends on the system and component conventions; in simple isolated plant-cell problems, `Ψw≈Ψs+Ψp` is usually adequate. Do not double-count soil matric terms if models combine potentials.
- Osmosis and aquaporin-facilitated flux do not mean ATP directly pumps individual water molecules up a water-potential gradient; active ion transport can *indirectly* create osmotic driving forces.
- Root apoplastic, symplastic and transmembrane routes are distinct and mix across tissues; mature endodermal Casparian strips typically enforce membrane crossing to enter the stele, but developmental/anatomical variations exist.
- The dominant tall-tree xylem ascent model is **cohesion–tension**, with transpiration-generated negative pressure, not root-pressure-only push. Embolisms limit conductance and refilling is physiologically context-dependent.
- Evaporation, transpiration and water uptake are related but not identical instantaneously. Stomatal versus cuticular transpiration, humidity/VPD and boundary-layer effects all matter.
- The phloem is living conducting tissue in angiosperms, assisted by companion cells; source-to-sink **positive-pressure bulk flow** is not the same force as xylem negative-pressure water ascent. Different sieve tubes may have opposite flow directions, but not necessarily simultaneous opposite bulk flows within one individual tube.
- SWEET-family proteins generally mediate facilitated sugar efflux; **H+-coupled SUT/SUC inward transport** is often secondary active. Do not claim the SWEET exporter itself hydrolyzes ATP to pump sucrose.
- Symplastic loading may be passive or involve polymer trapping (raffinose-family sugars), depending on plant taxa; no universal single loading model. Endosperm/fruit/root unloading may differ in route and metabolic control.
- Xylem mainly carries water/minerals and some organic compounds; phloem carries assimilates and selected signals, including certain proteins/RNAs. Macromolecules do **not** move unrestricted through arbitrary channels or from soil all the way to every leaf.
- Source and sink roles change over development; transport allocation depends on unloading, metabolism and growth demands, not simply organ name.
