## Student-facing subunit descriptions and syllabus scope

### 4.A — Cell Signaling

**Final menu description:**

Learn how cells respond to hormones and other cues through hormone receptors, cell-surface receptors, G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), second messengers and signal-transduction pathways. Examine how signaling is regulated, including bacterial and plant two-component systems, light signaling in plants, bacterial chemotaxis and quorum sensing.

**Required in-syllabus knowledge:** Hormones and their receptors; cell-surface receptor classes; GPCR signaling and second messengers; signal-transduction pathways and regulation; two-component systems in bacteria and plants; plant light signaling; bacterial chemotaxis; quorum sensing.

**Boundary avoiding duplication and off-syllabus content:** Whole-body endocrine physiology belongs to Unit 7; plant hormone/photoreceptor physiological detail belongs to Unit 6. Teach receptor-to-response principles rather than duplicating those units or adding advanced research-only networks.

### 4.B — Cellular Communication

**Final menu description:**

Study the general principles of communication among cells and their surroundings, including cell adhesion, adhesion-molecule classes, gap junctions, extracellular matrix (ECM) and integrins. Follow neurotransmission and its regulation, then examine intercellular control of haematopoiesis.

**Required in-syllabus knowledge:** Cell contact and paracrine/endocrine communication; cadherins, selectins, Ig-superfamily adhesion molecules and integrins at NET/SET level; gap junctions; ECM and integrin functions; neurotransmitter release, signal reception and regulation; growth factors, cytokines and the cellular regulation of haematopoiesis.

**Boundary avoiding duplication and off-syllabus content:** Membrane and organelle structures belong to Unit 2; detailed neural and blood physiology to Unit 7. Avoid disease-specific hematology and specialist stem-cell methods.

### 4.C — Innate and Adaptive Immune System

**Final menu description:**

Build innate and adaptive immunity from immune cells, recognition molecules, antigens, antigenicity, immunogenicity and B-/T-cell epitopes. Study antibody structure and diversity, monoclonal antibodies, basic antibody engineering and antigen–antibody interactions; MHC molecules, antigen processing and presentation; B-/T-cell receptors, activation and differentiation; humoral and cell-mediated responses, primary and secondary immune responses, immune modulation, complement, Toll-like receptors, cellular effector functions and inflammation. Cover hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, immune responses to bacterial, parasitic and viral infections, congenital and acquired immunodeficiencies, and vaccines.

**Required in-syllabus knowledge:** Innate and adaptive immune cells/molecules; antigen and epitope properties; immunoglobulins, antibody diversity, monoclonals and introductory engineering; antibody-antigen recognition; MHC I/II and antigen processing/presentation; BCR/TCR and lymphocyte activation/differentiation; humoral and cell-mediated responses, primary/secondary immunity and modulation; complement, TLRs, inflammation and cellular effectors; hypersensitivity, autoimmunity; immune responses to bacteria, parasites and viruses; congenital/acquired immunodeficiencies and vaccination. MP SET textbook contexts include TB, malaria and HIV.

**Boundary avoiding duplication and off-syllabus content:** Pathogen entry/manipulation belongs to 4.D; antibody production lab engineering to biotechnology/methods; no unrelated clinical treatment or postgraduate research immunology.

### 4.D — Host–Pathogen Interaction

**Final menu description:**

Explore how bacteria, viruses and other pathogens recognize, attach to and enter animal and plant host cells. Explain pathogen-driven changes in host-cell behaviour, virus-induced cellular transformation, representative pathogen-induced diseases in animals and plants, and cell–cell fusion in normal and abnormal biological contexts.

**Required in-syllabus knowledge:** Recognition and entry of bacterial and viral pathogens into animal and plant host cells; host surface receptors and pathogen attachment; basic entry mechanisms; altered host-cell behavior; viral transformation; representative pathogen-induced animal and plant diseases; cell–cell fusion in physiological and abnormal contexts.

**Boundary avoiding duplication and off-syllabus content:** Immunological defense mechanisms in 4.C; cancer progression in 4.E; pathogen diversity in Unit 9 and plant defense physiology in Unit 6. Avoid catalog-style disease surveys.

### 4.E — Cancer

**Final menu description:**

Examine genetic rearrangements in progenitor cells, oncogene activation, tumor-suppressor genes and loss of normal cell-cycle control. Connect virus-induced cancer, altered apoptosis and interactions between cancerous and normal cells with metastasis, then study the basic principles of therapeutic interventions against uncontrolled cell growth.

**Required in-syllabus knowledge:** Genetic alterations and rearrangements in progenitors; proto-oncogenes/oncogenes, tumor suppressors; cell-cycle disruption and apoptosis evasion; virus-induced cancer; invasion/metastasis and interactions with normal cells; NET/SET-level principles of cancer therapy and growth control.

**Boundary avoiding duplication and off-syllabus content:** Normal cell-cycle and apoptosis fundamentals in 2.D, viral transformation/entry in 4.D, immune mechanisms in 4.C. Exclude individualized clinical advice, advanced drug protocols or staging details.

## Academic progression

1. Cell signaling: reception and intracellular responses to hormones and environmental cues.
2. Cellular communication: interactions among cells, cell adhesion, ECM and direct or neurotransmitter-based communication.
3. Innate and adaptive immunity: a complete framework for immune recognition, cellular coordination, effector response, memory and disorders.
4. Host–pathogen interaction: pathogen entry, host-cell alteration and biological effects on animal and plant cells.
5. Cancer: breakdown of growth regulation, transformation, normal-cell interactions, tumor progression and therapeutic principles.

This is the logical curriculum sequence, not a claim that these biological events always occur chronologically in a single cell.
