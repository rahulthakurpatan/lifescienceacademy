1. The biological problem: coordinating proliferation and organ identity

A plant remains capable of making new organs long after its embryo has formed. A seedling establishes roots and leaves, an established stem produces axillary buds, and a damaged plant may replace tissues through renewed growth. These events require more than a supply of carbon, nitrogen and mineral elements. Cells must decide whether to continue dividing, begin differentiating, or enter a developmental program that will produce a new organ. A nutrient can provide the atoms needed to construct a new cell, but a regulatory signal can alter the probability that a competent cell enters a division cycle. Cytokinins are among the chemical regulators that connect these two aspects of growth. Their importance becomes particularly clear when one compares tissues that have access to similar nutrients but follow very different developmental trajectories.

Imagine a small piece of tobacco stem placed on a suitable sterile nutrient medium. Its cells have water, sugars, salts and other essentials, but their behavior depends on the developmental state of the explant and on the regulators in the medium. A change in the auxin and cytokinin supplied can transform the outcome from root production to continued cell proliferation or shoot formation. The information supplied by a hormone is therefore not equivalent to the energy or structural material supplied by the medium. The tissue interprets the signals by activating particular genes and changing its developmental competence. In an intact plant, the same principle operates without an artificial nutrient medium: neighboring cells communicate and the available hormone pool reflects local biosynthesis, transport and metabolism.

The term cytokinin describes a class of plant growth regulators, many of which are derivatives of adenine. The name reflects the discovery of compounds able to promote cell division in suitable experimental tissues. The name does not imply that every cytokinin-treated cell undergoes cytokinesis or that all effects of the hormone are mediated solely by cell division. An existing leaf can respond by retaining photosynthetic capacity for longer, even when the majority of its mature mesophyll cells are no longer actively dividing. A developing root may respond by moving cells toward differentiation earlier, rather than enlarging its proliferative region. These contrasting effects are not contradictory if cytokinin is understood as a signal whose consequences depend on the responding tissue.

To reason about such differences, distinguish three levels of a hormone response. First, there is the abundance of the relevant active molecules in a compartment. Second, there is the competence of receptors and downstream signaling proteins to convert that chemical input into an intracellular response. Third, there is the physiological output, which depends on the genes expressed in the cell, its developmental history and its relationship with surrounding tissues. An increase in the active molecule may fail to induce a particular gene in a receptor mutant. A receptor signal may reach the nucleus but fail to initiate an organ in a differentiated cell that lacks the appropriate developmental program. A change in organ size may arise from more cells, larger cells, altered duration of growth, or a combination of these.

Cytokinin is especially useful for illustrating the distinction between local and systemic regulation. Roots can communicate their nutrient environment to shoots by exporting cytokinin-related compounds into the vascular system. At the same time, cells within a shoot meristem can synthesize or activate their own cytokinins. Long-distance delivery cannot by itself specify a small group of stem cells; positional information requires local regulation as well. Likewise, local synthesis cannot necessarily replace a signal that reports nitrogen availability to a distant organ. A complete model therefore needs both the chemistry of the hormone and the spatial organization of its metabolism.

In the following sections, we first establish the chemical identities and origins of cytokinins. This allows us to distinguish precursors, mobile derivatives, active ligands, temporary conjugates and products of breakdown. We then examine how a plant cell detects the ligand through a multistep phosphorelay. Finally, we connect those molecular events with cell division, shoot regeneration, meristem maintenance, root differentiation and leaf longevity. The central question is not whether cytokinin promotes or inhibits growth in the abstract, but which process is being changed, in which tissue, through which mechanism, and under what experimental conditions.

2. From tissue culture to a natural plant hormone

The early evidence for cytokinin action arose from experiments in which researchers attempted to sustain proliferation of isolated plant tissues. The ability to maintain viable cells on a nutrient medium was important because it made environmental factors and chemical regulators experimentally separable. Instead of inferring hormone activity from changes in a whole plant, investigators could give comparable tissue pieces different chemical treatments. They could then examine the growth of the cultures, the formation of organs and the persistence of dividing cells. This experimental approach helped show that auxin alone did not explain all forms of plant cell proliferation.

Kinetin was identified in the 1950s during work on factors that stimulated tobacco tissue growth. A source of activity was aged or heat-treated DNA preparations; the resulting compound was characterized as a substituted adenine. Kinetin is valuable in the history of plant physiology because it demonstrated that a chemical related to a familiar nucleic acid base could have strong regulatory activity. It should not be treated as the principal naturally occurring cytokinin in most higher plants merely because it was important in early experiments. Its chemical origin in those preparations and its activity in a bioassay are different questions from its abundance and physiological role in an intact plant. A growth-promoting response identifies biological activity under assay conditions, not necessarily the major endogenous ligand.

A subsequent discovery of zeatin from developing maize kernels demonstrated that plant tissues contained naturally occurring compounds with cytokinin activity. The name zeatin derives from the genus Zea. The finding provided a link between the response of isolated cells and the chemistry of living plants. Developing seeds are useful examples because their growth requires intensive coordination of cell proliferation, nutrient allocation and tissue differentiation. Nevertheless, the extraction of a hormone from a seed does not imply that it acts only in seeds or that seeds are the only site where it can be synthesized.

The experiments of Folke Skoog and Carlos Miller, reported in 1957, established an influential principle of plant tissue culture. By altering the supply of auxin and cytokinin, the investigators could influence whether a cultured tissue proliferated or produced particular organs. Under suitable conditions, a medium relatively rich in auxin favors roots, whereas a medium relatively rich in cytokinin favors shoots. Intermediate combinations can sustain unorganized proliferation. These findings revealed that organogenesis can depend on interactions between chemical signals, rather than on a single hormone that acts as an unconditional command to form an organ. This is a particularly important distinction in examinations, where an apparently precise ratio may be presented as if it were universal.

The word callus refers to a proliferating mass of cells formed in certain culture or wound conditions. A callus is not necessarily composed of identical, developmentally blank cells. In some experimental systems its developmental program has similarities to root initiation, and different regions of a culture may possess different potentials. The historical observation that a tissue can regenerate roots or shoots does not mean every isolated plant cell will regenerate an entire plant under every condition. Tissue competence, genotype, culture history and the regulator used all influence regeneration. These qualifications preserve the importance of the classic experiment without converting its result into an overly broad rule.

A sound reconstruction of the evidence begins by asking what the treatment groups actually differ in. Suppose one explant is cultured with auxin alone and another receives auxin plus cytokinin, with equal sugar, light, temperature and culture duration. If proliferation occurs primarily in the second group, the result supports a regulatory contribution by cytokinin under those conditions. It does not establish that cytokinin directly catalyzes cell division. The compound might induce transcription of a cell cycle regulator, alter nutrient utilization, change developmental identity or influence an inhibitory pathway. Identifying the biochemical link requires subsequent molecular work. If the two cultures also differ in the amount of nitrogen, one cannot isolate the hormone effect; the design must control that confounding variable.

The transition from bioassays to analytical chemistry and genetics extended the model. Rather than scoring tissue growth alone, researchers could measure different cytokinin forms, trace their movement, alter biosynthetic enzymes and test plants with defective receptors. Molecular genetics makes it possible to distinguish failure to produce a signal from failure to perceive it. Modern cytokinin biology integrates the original physiological observations with this molecular evidence. The continuing value of the historical experiments is that they establish the developmental problem any molecular mechanism must explain: comparable tissues can interpret combinations of regulators in different ways and establish distinct organs.

3. Chemical forms: adenine derivatives, precursors and active ligands

Adenine is a purine base with a fused ring system and an amino group at the sixth position of the purine skeleton. Many natural cytokinins are adenines substituted at the nitrogen of this amino group, conventionally called N6. This substitution is central to their molecular identity. Ordinary adenine participates in nucleotide metabolism but does not have the same biological activity as an appropriately substituted cytokinin. The adenine ring provides a recognizable chemical scaffold, while the side chain contributes to the specificity of enzymes, receptors and transport proteins. The plant therefore distinguishes molecular structures that are closely related from the perspective of general metabolism.

Isopentenyladenine, abbreviated iP, contains an unsaturated five-carbon side chain. Zeatin contains a related chain with an additional hydroxyl group. In trans-zeatin, or tZ, the geometry around the side-chain double bond is trans; in cis-zeatin, or cZ, it is cis. The two forms have the same molecular formula but differ in spatial arrangement. The distinction is meaningful because enzymes and receptor binding sites interact with three-dimensional molecular shapes. One cannot infer equal biological effects simply by counting the atoms in the compounds. In many Arabidopsis contexts, iP and tZ are prominent active cytokinins. Cis-zeatin can also have physiological functions; its relative contribution varies across species, organs and developmental or environmental conditions. Dihydrozeatin, abbreviated DHZ, has a saturated side chain and consequently differs in susceptibility to certain metabolic enzymes.

There are also aromatic cytokinins. Benzyladenine, often called BA or BAP, is a common synthetic cytokinin used in tissue culture, and kinetin has a furfuryl side chain. Some naturally occurring aromatic cytokinin-related compounds are known. Aromatic and isoprenoid compounds need not behave identically in a tissue. A molecule that survives degradation for longer can produce a different integrated response even if its initial receptor activation is similar to that of another ligand. Thus a tissue culture result with benzyladenine is useful evidence of cytokinin action but does not necessarily describe the concentration or metabolism of endogenous trans-zeatin.

The name of a cytokinin often contains a suffix identifying its chemical state. A free base has no sugar attached at the relevant purine nitrogen. A riboside has a ribose moiety, and a ribotide or nucleotide additionally contains phosphate on the ribose. Isopentenyladenosine and its ribonucleotides are therefore chemically distinct from isopentenyladenine even though they share the modified adenine portion. This distinction is analogous to the difference between adenine, adenosine and adenosine monophosphate. The analogy is useful for remembering the structures; it does not imply that cytokinin ribotides simply act as conventional energy-carrying nucleotides. Their modified base is part of a specialized signaling metabolism.

Most canonical cytokinin receptors in Arabidopsis strongly recognize free-base ligands, while ribosides and ribotides often serve as mobile or metabolic forms that can contribute to signaling through conversion. A measurement of total cytokinin may therefore conceal a major shift in signaling potential. Consider two tissues with identical total amounts of zeatin-related compounds: one contains mostly free trans-zeatin, while the other contains mostly conjugated or phosphorylated species. Their measured total pools can be the same even though their accessible active ligands differ substantially. A useful analytical report separates the forms rather than assigning the entire pool a common receptor activity.

The free base is not the only possible measure of physiological significance. A large riboside pool moving through the xylem can matter greatly to the shoot if it is subsequently converted into a form that activates receptors. Conversely, a small local pool can be decisive if generated in a particularly responsive cell population. The concentration of a molecule averaged across a whole organ may be a poor indicator of its local effect. For example, a small meristem forms only a fraction of a shoot sample. A high concentration in the meristem might be diluted below obvious detection when the entire shoot is homogenized, whereas a low-activity storage form in mature leaves might dominate the measured total. Spatial regulation is therefore a recurring theme.

Glycosylation adds sugars at particular sites of a cytokinin molecule. In zeatin, the side-chain hydroxyl group can serve as an attachment site for O-glycosylation. Nitrogen atoms of the purine ring, commonly N7 or N9, can also be modified. The position of the sugar attachment affects the biological fate of the conjugate. Certain O-glucosides can be hydrolyzed to regenerate active hormone; N-glucosides are commonly discussed as inactivated forms, though their metabolism and possible reactivation are not identical in all species or circumstances. It is inaccurate to classify every glycoside as an irreversible degradation product. A conjugate may be a temporary reservoir, a route of deactivation, or a metabolically persistent compound.

For an examination problem, the key is to classify a compound by its structure and experimentally supported fate, not by its name alone. A cytokinin nucleotide upstream of activation and an O-glucoside downstream of conjugation may both be weak direct receptor ligands, yet the first can be a biosynthetic precursor while the second can be a reversible reserve. An assay that measures only free bases would miss their contribution to the potential supply. A measurement after chemical hydrolysis could include them but would not directly reveal the active concentration before hydrolysis. The purpose of the method determines which distinction is essential.

4. Biosynthesis and local activation: IPT, CYP735A and LOG

Plants synthesize isoprenoid cytokinins by transferring a prenyl group to adenine-containing substrates. The reaction connects nucleotide metabolism with the production of isoprenoid precursors. Dimethylallyl diphosphate, abbreviated DMAPP, is a five-carbon activated isoprenoid unit. In the principal de novo route of many higher plants, adenylate isopentenyltransferases, or IPTs, use adenine nucleotides as acceptors. Several plant adenylate IPT enzymes preferentially use ATP or ADP; the precise substrate preferences vary among enzymes. This detail distinguishes their activity from a simplified textbook equation in which all cytokinin synthesis is represented as one reaction using AMP. The important common principle is the introduction of an isoprenoid side chain at the N6 amino group of an adenine nucleotide.

The resulting compounds are isopentenyladenine-type nucleotides. A plant can use the nucleotide pool to produce active iP, or alter the side chain to form another class of cytokinin. CYP735A enzymes, members of the cytochrome P450 family, catalyze hydroxylation of iP-type cytokinin nucleotides to produce trans-zeatin-type nucleotides. The added hydroxyl group is chemically important: it changes the identity of the signaling molecule that can eventually be released, creates a possible site for O-glycosylation and influences the balance among cytokinin forms. In Arabidopsis, the relative distribution of tZ-type and iP-type cytokinins is physiologically meaningful for root and shoot communication.

The initial IPT reaction is not identical to the final formation of the active free base. The product still carries sugar and phosphate. Activation can occur through LONELY GUY enzymes, known as LOG cytokinin phosphoribohydrolases. LOG catalyzes direct removal of the phosphoribose portion from a cytokinin riboside monophosphate, releasing the corresponding free base. The name derives from a rice mutant with defects in meristem maintenance. A simplified representation of the pathway should therefore distinguish between side-chain introduction, side-chain modification and liberation of the active base. These transformations correspond to different regulatory steps and different possible mutant phenotypes.

The importance of activation becomes clear if the precursor and receptor are considered separately. Suppose a tissue produces large amounts of cytokinin nucleotide but expresses little LOG activity. Its precursor pool could be abundant, yet its local release of an active free base may be constrained. If an adjacent region expresses LOG strongly, it can create a local domain of cytokinin action without requiring the first biosynthetic step to occur exclusively there. A hormone gradient can therefore be generated partly by spatial differences in conversion rates. A concentration maximum may identify a site of activation rather than a site of initial synthesis. The location of IPT transcription alone cannot establish where the highest active cytokinin concentration will occur.

The 2007 rice study of Kurakawa and colleagues linked loss of LOG function to premature termination of shoot meristem activity and identified a cytokinin-activating enzyme. The spatial expression of LOG in the meristem supported the importance of local production of active hormone. This evidence connects a molecular conversion with the developmental need to maintain a group of dividing stem cells. It also illustrates why interpreting a mutant requires attention to the exact metabolite measured. If a mutation changes the ratio of inactive nucleotide to active base, a large total pool does not rescue a failure in local activation. The expected quantitative relationship depends on transport and alternative routes, so the phenotype is not proof that every cytokinin in the entire plant has disappeared.

Another route to cytokinin-related compounds involves the modification of transfer RNA. Specific tRNA isopentenyltransferases add an isoprenoid modification to an adenine residue in some tRNAs, contributing to translation-related functions. Turnover of modified tRNAs can supply cis-zeatin-related compounds. This pathway should be distinguished from the de novo synthesis of iP-type and tZ-type cytokinin nucleotides by adenylate IPTs. Although both routes involve the addition of an isoprenoid group to an adenine moiety, their substrates and primary cellular contexts differ. In different plants, the relative significance of tRNA-derived cytokinin can vary. It is particularly misleading to conclude that all cytokinins originate from degraded DNA because of the historical discovery of kinetin in treated DNA preparations.

Biosynthetic enzymes are regulated in response to developmental demands and environmental information. An increase in available nitrate can stimulate cytokinin production in roots, including increased expression of particular IPT genes in model plants. Such regulation provides a link between the nitrogen supply and the capacity for shoot growth. A growing shoot requires nitrogen for proteins, nucleic acids and photosynthetic machinery. A hormonal report of favorable nutrient availability helps coordinate growth with the actual resource environment. However, a nitrate-induced increase in IPT transcript is not itself a direct measurement of free-base cytokinin. The enzyme must be translated and functional, its substrates must be available, and the products must survive transport and metabolism.

When analyzing mutants, separate the effect of an enzyme defect from compensatory metabolism. Reduction of adenylate IPT activity can limit precursor formation, but multiple IPT genes may act in overlapping domains. A single mutant might have a modest phenotype, whereas simultaneous disruption of several functionally overlapping genes could reveal a stronger dependence on the pathway. Similarly, a mutation in CYP735A can change cytokinin composition without eliminating every iP-type molecule. A LOG mutation may alter activation even when nucleotide biosynthesis persists. Exogenous application of an active cytokinin can bypass a deficient synthetic or activating step if the administered molecule reaches the appropriate cells and the downstream signaling machinery is functional.

This rescue logic is powerful but not absolute. Failure of an applied cytokinin to restore a mutant phenotype can result from poor penetration, incorrect timing, a concentration that is toxic or otherwise unsuitable, or irreversible developmental defects that arose before application. A successful rescue supports a particular interpretation but must be compared with appropriate controls. Wild-type plants exposed to the same treatment establish whether the chosen dose itself has abnormal effects. Applying a free base and a precursor in parallel can help distinguish a defect in activation from a general defect in perception, although other metabolic routes must be considered. These experimental distinctions turn a list of enzyme names into a causal model of hormone synthesis.

5. Cytokinin homeostasis: storage, inactivation and degradation

An active cytokinin pool cannot be understood from its synthesis rate alone. The concentration available to a receptor reflects all the reactions that add or remove the active molecular form. Some of those reactions conserve the modified adenine scaffold and permit its eventual recovery. Others chemically destroy the side chain responsible for cytokinin activity. This distinction is essential because a plant may respond rapidly to a stimulus by mobilizing an existing pool rather than making every new ligand from a nucleotide precursor. Conversely, a plant may terminate a response by increasing breakdown rather than stopping synthesis entirely.

Glycosyltransferases transfer sugar residues from activated donors onto suitable substrates. In cytokinin metabolism, the site of attachment affects the resulting chemical properties. A zeatin molecule has a hydroxyl group on its side chain that can be glycosylated. The resulting O-glucoside generally has much lower direct receptor activity than the free base. A compatible beta-glucosidase can hydrolyze the bond and restore the free hormone. Such a reserve differs from an irreversibly degraded molecule because it retains the structural features needed to regenerate the active base. Whether a particular reserve is actually mobilized depends on the location of the conjugate, accessibility to the enzyme and physiological regulation of hydrolysis.

Other glycosyltransferases modify nitrogen atoms of the purine ring, producing cytokinin N-glucosides. These compounds are traditionally regarded as strongly inactivated products. The description is useful for distinguishing them from readily mobilized O-glucosides, but should not become a universal assertion that no plant can ever metabolize or reactivate any N-glucoside. Different conjugates have different metabolic fates. A student interpreting an experiment should identify which compound is measured rather than assume that all sugar conjugates represent one interchangeable pool. The existence of many conjugates helps explain why total cytokinin content can remain high even when cytokinin signaling falls.

Cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase, abbreviated CKX, provides another important route of homeostatic control. CKX enzymes oxidatively cleave the susceptible N6 side chain of certain cytokinins. This destroys the structural feature of those molecules that contributes to their hormonal activity. The reaction is unlike simple conjugation because removing a sugar cannot reconstruct the cleaved side chain. A cell must synthesize another appropriate cytokinin molecule or receive one from elsewhere to replace the lost ligand. CKX thus acts as a potentially effective regulator of the duration, magnitude and spatial distribution of signaling.

Substrate specificity matters when predicting the effect of CKX. Different CKX isoforms do not degrade every cytokinin species equally. The unsaturated side chains of iP and zeatin-type molecules can be susceptible, whereas saturated dihydrozeatin and many aromatic cytokinin compounds resist cleavage by typical CKX enzymes. Some conjugates or nucleotide forms may be poor substrates; the precise spectrum depends on the isoenzyme. Consequently, a tissue with elevated CKX activity may respond differently to a natural susceptible cytokinin and to an experimentally applied resistant synthetic cytokinin. A failure to consider this distinction could lead to an incorrect inference that a plant with high CKX activity has defective receptors.

A further complication is that CKX isoforms can act in different cellular locations. Hormone concentrations in a cytosolic pool, endoplasmic reticulum lumen or extracellular region need not change identically when one isoform is manipulated. A transporter may restrict substrate access to the enzyme. A tissue-specific CKX transgene can therefore alter a local response even if the total cytokinin concentration in the entire plant changes only slightly. This is a central reason why the geographical distribution of enzymes and receptors is an important part of hormone homeostasis. The cell is not a single, uniformly mixed reaction vessel.

A simplified quantitative model helps clarify how synthesis and degradation interact. Let C represent active cytokinin concentration in nanomoles per litre within a compartment of constant volume. Assume an effective input rate S in nanomoles per litre per hour and an effective first-order removal coefficient k in inverse hours. If synthesis or import is approximately constant and removal is proportional to C, the concentration changes according to dC/dt = S − kC. At a steady state, net change is zero, so C equals S divided by k. This equation represents a teaching model, not a universal kinetic law for every CKX enzyme. Real tissue may exhibit enzyme saturation, feedback, dilution by growth, multiple metabolic pools and changing transport.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose S is 12 nanomoles per litre per hour and k is 0.30 per hour. The steady concentration would be 40 nanomoles per litre. If CKX overexpression increases effective removal to 0.60 per hour while input remains unchanged, the new steady concentration is 20 nanomoles per litre. The concentration is halved even though the production rate has not changed. If production also doubles to 24 nanomoles per litre per hour, the steady concentration returns to 40 nanomoles per litre. This explains why an apparently normal pool can coexist with substantial changes in both production and removal. Concentration and metabolic turnover are distinct observables.

In a separate hypothetical experiment, suppose synthesis is stopped and a freely mixed active pool initially contains 80 nanomoles per litre. With first-order removal at 0.35 per hour, the half-life is ln(2)/0.35, approximately 1.98 hours. After about four hours, the pool would contain approximately 20 nanomoles per litre. The calculation assumes no further import or release from reserves. If the experimentally measured decline is slower than predicted, several explanations become possible: the removal coefficient might change, a conjugated reserve might release hormone, transport from neighboring tissues might continue, or the initial first-order approximation may be inadequate. A deviation from the model can motivate an experiment rather than automatically proving any one explanation.

The practical importance of cytokinin degradation is illustrated by plants engineered to increase CKX activity. In many experimental contexts, lowering cytokinin impairs shoot growth while increasing aspects of root growth, demonstrating that the two systems do not respond identically. A phenotype following CKX overexpression is evidence that the availability of its substrates matters to development, but the interpretation should include measurements of cytokinin forms and signaling competence. The complementary approach of altering cytokinin biosynthesis can strengthen the causal inference. If reduced cytokinin production and increased cytokinin degradation produce related phenotypes, the convergence supports a model based on active hormone availability rather than an unrelated role of either enzyme.

Homeostasis also includes feedback. Cytokinin can regulate genes involved in its own metabolism, including certain CKX genes, and the signaling pathway has additional negative regulators. Increasing cytokinin can therefore increase the capacity of the plant to reduce the hormone response. Such feedback can stabilize a developing tissue against fluctuations and prevent persistent stimulation after the original signal has disappeared. However, stabilization need not mean the concentration is perfectly constant. The plant may require a transient signal, and the timing of the response can be as important as its magnitude.

6. Local distribution and long-distance transport

Cytokinin transport is necessary because many physiological responses take place away from the site where the relevant molecules were initially synthesized. Roots absorb nutrients from a spatially variable environment, while leaves supply much of the fixed carbon needed for growth. The shoot cannot directly sample every region of soil explored by the root system. Chemical communication allows the plant to integrate information across these compartments. Cytokinins participate in this communication, but they also function within individual organs, where local synthesis, activation and transport determine which cells respond.

The xylem provides a route for root-to-shoot movement of water and dissolved compounds. Cytokinin-related molecules can enter this transport stream and be delivered to aerial tissues. In many vascular plants, trans-zeatin-type cytokinins, including the riboside form, are prominent in root-to-shoot communication. The phloem provides another route through which sugars and other solutes move from sources toward sinks. Isopentenyladenine-type compounds are often associated with phloem transport, including shoot-to-root signaling. These associations are useful organizing principles, but they are not exclusive rules. Both the molecular composition of vascular sap and the direction of phloem flow vary with species, developmental stage and source–sink relationships.

It is important to distinguish loading a compound into a vascular pathway from carrying the compound once it is loaded. The upward flow of xylem sap is largely driven by plant hydraulics, including transpiration. A membrane transporter need not propel a molecule all the way from the root to a leaf. Instead, it may be required for uptake into a particular cell, release into a vascular compartment, or redistribution at the destination. A mutation that interferes with loading can reduce long-distance delivery even when water flow remains substantial. Conversely, normal expression of a transporter does not guarantee normal delivery if the vascular flow rate is very low.

The Arabidopsis ABCG14 transporter is an important example. Genetic disruption of ABCG14 impairs the distribution of cytokinins from root to shoot and can produce strongly reduced shoot growth. Mutant plants show an altered distribution of cytokinin between roots and shoots, and application of trans-zeatin can restore aspects of the growth phenotype. These observations support a role in the transport of particular cytokinin forms rather than simply a reduction of the entire plant's synthetic capacity. Later work has refined the understanding of ABCG14 activity and tissue-specific transport steps. For NET-level reasoning, the safest conclusion is that ABCG14 contributes critically to efficient root-to-shoot cytokinin distribution; it should not be portrayed as the sole universal transporter of every cytokinin in all plants.

To distinguish transport from biosynthesis experimentally, compare at least two compartments. If a mutant has low cytokinin in the shoot but accumulates relevant cytokinin forms in roots, an impaired export or redistribution mechanism becomes plausible. In a mutant defective in a major biosynthetic enzyme, reduced abundance of the newly synthesized products in the production tissue itself would be expected, though compensation or uptake from another compartment can complicate that pattern. The measurement of only one organ cannot establish which explanation is correct. A tracer study can add direct evidence by following an identifiable cytokinin-related molecule from its point of supply to distant tissues.

Reciprocal grafting is another useful experimental approach. A shoot from a mutant can be grafted onto a wild-type rootstock, and a wild-type shoot can be grafted onto a mutant rootstock. The two combinations separate the genotypes of the source and destination compartments. If a defect in rootstock function prevents normal shoot growth even when the shoot genotype is wild type, the result supports a contribution of the root to the phenotype. If a wild-type rootstock substantially rescues the mutant shoot, it supports the existence of a mobile or systemic influence. Such results do not prove the exact identity of a mobile compound without supporting chemical measurements, because the rootstock may alter water uptake, nutrient acquisition and other signals as well.

Transport over a short distance also influences signaling. Purine permease family proteins, including PUP14 in Arabidopsis, have been implicated in cytokinin uptake and spatial restriction of responses. Equilibrative nucleoside transporters can transport nucleosides, including certain cytokinin ribosides in particular contexts. Moving a cytokinin across a membrane can either increase its accessibility to a receptor or remove it from a compartment where it was available. The direction of the physiological effect depends on transporter orientation, substrate specificity, concentration gradients and receptor localization. One should not assume that increased expression of any transporter must increase signaling throughout the tissue.

A useful thought experiment is to imagine a source cell releasing a mobile cytokinin into a shared extracellular space. Neighboring cells could take up the compound, convert it, degrade it or detect it through receptors. Increasing uptake in a band of cells might enrich those cells but reduce the amount that reaches cells farther away. If the uptake is coupled with rapid inactivation, that band could behave as a sink for the signal. If the imported molecule is efficiently activated, it could instead generate a local response domain. The two effects may occur simultaneously in different places. This is why a transporter phenotype cannot be interpreted simply as hormone concentration increasing everywhere.

A quantitative transport example illustrates the distinction between concentration and flux. Assume xylem sap contains a cytokinin riboside at a concentration of 25 nanomoles per litre and flows at 0.4 millilitres per hour into a particular shoot. Multiplication by the volume flow gives 25 × 0.0004, or 0.010 nanomoles per hour, equivalent to 10 picomoles per hour. If the concentration doubles to 50 nanomoles per litre while flow falls to 0.2 millilitres per hour, the delivery rate is unchanged. These are hypothetical values. The calculation assumes steady flow and that the measured concentration represents the entering sap. It does not account for metabolism during transport or release from tissues along the route.

This calculation is useful when interpreting nutritional or environmental treatments. An altered xylem cytokinin concentration need not imply a proportional change in the amount delivered per unit time. If drought decreases sap flow, an increased concentration can coexist with unchanged or decreased delivery. Conversely, a modest concentration at high flow can deliver a substantial quantity. To establish a causal role for vascular cytokinin, the experiment should distinguish the amount per unit volume, the volume flow, the chemical forms carried and the downstream physiological response. Sampling procedures themselves can alter sap composition and pressure, so the method and the conditions need to be considered.

Cytokinin signaling can therefore be spatially distributed in at least three ways: the precursor is transported and converted near the receptor, the active molecule is transported directly, or a local synthetic response is induced by an upstream mobile signal. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. Their distinction prevents a common misconception that cytokinin found in a shoot must have been made in the root. The shoot possesses its own metabolic machinery and can respond to local developmental cues. A whole-plant model must explain both autonomous growth and systemic coordination.

7. Perception by histidine kinase cytokinin receptors

A cytokinin molecule becomes a signal only when it is recognized by a suitable cellular component. Plant hormone receptors vary considerably in their molecular design. Auxin signaling commonly involves a ligand-dependent interaction between an F-box protein and a repressor. Cytokinin uses a different architecture: receptors related to histidine kinases of two-component signaling systems. This distinction is an important part of plant physiology because a small chemical signal can produce a transcriptional response through several entirely different biochemical mechanisms.

Arabidopsis has three canonical cytokinin receptors commonly designated AHK2, AHK3 and AHK4. AHK4 is also known as CRE1 or WOL in particular genetic contexts. Their names reflect their identification as Arabidopsis histidine kinases and their association with developmental phenotypes. AHK receptors are hybrid histidine kinases: they possess a sensor domain, a kinase region and a receiver domain. The cytokinin-binding region includes a CHASE domain, which helps recognize the appropriate ligands. The kinase and receiver regions enable phosphate transfer after receptor activation. Although receptors share this general organization, they can differ in expression, ligand preference and contribution to particular physiological outcomes.

Many cytokinin receptors are found predominantly on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, with some evidence for localization at the plasma membrane under particular conditions. The location is significant because the ligand must reach the binding domain on the appropriate side of the membrane. For an ER-localized receptor, the sensor domain is exposed toward the lumen, whereas the phosphotransfer machinery communicates with the cytosolic side. The orientation couples detection of a small molecule in one compartment to biochemical events in another. This does not mean that all cytokinin activity necessarily originates in the ER or that extracellular transport is irrelevant. The cellular distribution of receptors and ligands determines how the signal reaches the detection machinery.

An exam question may compare a receptor mutation with a biosynthetic mutation. A lesion in the cytokinin-binding domain can decrease recognition of the ligand, even if the active hormone is abundant. A lesion in the kinase domain may allow binding but prevent transmission of information to downstream proteins. A mutation in the receiver domain can interrupt the phosphate relay after the kinase is activated. These are mechanistically distinct failures. In each case, the phenotype can include reduced cytokinin responsiveness, but the location of the defect determines which biochemical readouts would remain normal. A ligand-binding assay, a kinase assay and a transcriptional reporter test different levels of the same pathway.

The basic logic of a histidine kinase begins with a regulated transfer of phosphate from ATP. A suitable ligand can shift the receptor toward a signaling-active state, enabling phosphorylation of a conserved histidine residue in the kinase region. The phosphate is then transferred to an aspartate residue within the receptor's receiver region. In a hybrid kinase, these two components belong to the same receptor system. The phosphoryl group subsequently travels through an intermediate histidine phosphotransfer protein to a final response regulator. This multistep relay can connect membrane-associated sensing with gene regulation in the nucleus.

One must distinguish the biochemical phosphoryl transfer from the movement of the hormone. The cytokinin molecule does not have to travel along the sequence of signaling proteins, and it is not converted into phosphate. Binding changes the state of the receptor; the phosphoryl group carried through the signaling pathway derives from ATP. Similarly, a response regulator does not become a cytokinin molecule when it receives a phosphate. It becomes a phosphorylated protein whose activity or interactions may differ. Recognizing this distinction makes the arrow notation in pathway diagrams much less ambiguous.

The receptor proteins have partially overlapping functions. If several receptors are expressed in a given tissue and recognize related cytokinin molecules, removal of one may not abolish the response. Loss of multiple receptors can have a more severe developmental effect. The apparent weakness of a single mutant is therefore not evidence that its gene is unimportant. Redundancy can protect an essential function while allowing individual receptors to specialize in certain tissues or ligand ranges. Genetic experiments should take this possibility into account when comparing receptor mutants with wild type.

Receptor specificity also changes how one interprets dose responses. A tissue with a high abundance of a receptor that efficiently recognizes a particular ligand may respond at relatively low concentrations. Another tissue may require a higher concentration or respond through a different receptor. Binding affinity contributes to sensitivity but is not its sole determinant, because transport, metabolism and downstream feedback alter the relationship between external dose and transcriptional output. The concentration applied to a culture medium is not necessarily the free concentration at the receptor binding site.

A simple equilibrium model helps separate binding from physiological response. Suppose a ligand L binds reversibly to one class of independent receptor sites R, with dissociation constant Kd. If the free ligand concentration is known and binding has reached equilibrium, the fraction of occupied receptors is [L]/(Kd + [L]). At a free ligand concentration equal to Kd, half the receptors are occupied. At a concentration nine times Kd, the model predicts 90 percent occupancy. For a hypothetical receptor with Kd of 10 nanomolar, 10 nanomolar ligand gives 50 percent occupancy and 90 nanomolar gives 90 percent occupancy. The example does not claim measured affinity for any particular AHK receptor.

The binding equation is not an equation for plant growth. A transcriptional response could saturate before receptor occupancy is complete, because a small fraction of receptors may generate sufficient downstream phosphorylation. Negative feedback could lower the response after prolonged treatment even if occupancy remains high. Excess hormone could cause developmental disruption rather than a proportional increase in growth. The difference between a ligand-binding curve and an organ-growth curve is fundamental. A student should be able to predict the binding curve for the simple model, but must use additional biological information to predict the phenotype.

A comparison with animal signaling clarifies the unusual residues used in this pathway. Many familiar eukaryotic signaling pathways rely on phosphorylation of serine, threonine or tyrosine residues in proteins. Cytokinin receptors use a histidine–aspartate relay, resembling the architecture found in bacterial two-component systems. This does not mean that plants lack serine/threonine or other kinase signaling, or that animals cannot contain histidine phosphorylation chemistry. The claim is more specific: the canonical cytokinin receptor pathway uses a hybrid histidine kinase and a histidine–aspartate phosphorelay, whereas many common animal hormone receptor pathways use different molecular architectures.

8. AHK–AHP–ARR phosphorelay and transcriptional output

A cytokinin receptor is only the first step in the signaling system. The plant must connect a change at a membrane-associated protein to changes in nuclear transcription. Arabidopsis histidine phosphotransfer proteins, abbreviated AHPs, provide an intermediate relay. A functional AHP accepts phosphate on a conserved histidine from the phosphorylated receiver region of the receptor and participates in transmission to response regulators. The AHPs can be present in both cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments. Their role links the receptor-associated reaction with transcription factors in the nucleus. They should not be confused with membrane proteins that transport cytokinin molecules.

The sequence of phosphotransfer residues is often summarized as His–Asp–His–Asp. The first histidine belongs to the kinase region of the hybrid receptor. The first aspartate belongs to its receiver domain. The second histidine is located on a functional AHP protein. The final aspartate belongs to a response regulator. Each step represents a transfer of a phosphoryl group between protein components, not a transfer of the ligand. This sequence provides a powerful framework for analyzing mutations. Replacing a necessary phosphoacceptor with a residue that cannot accept the phosphate can impair transmission even if the proteins are normally expressed.

Arabidopsis response regulators are abbreviated ARR. Type-B ARRs include transcription factors with a receiver domain and a DNA-binding output domain. In a typical cytokinin response, phosphorylation increases their ability to activate a set of cytokinin-responsive genes. Type-B ARRs connect the phosphorelay to transcription of genes involved in developmental and homeostatic processes. The transcription factor does not normally synthesize the cytokinin molecule itself; it can change the expression of enzymes or regulators that influence future hormone availability or developmental behavior.

The term transcriptional activation does not mean every gene in the genome is activated. A type-B response regulator must act in a cell where its target genes can respond, where appropriate regulatory sequences are accessible, and where the necessary transcriptional environment exists. A gene induced in a shoot meristem may not be induced in the same way in a mature leaf. Other regulatory proteins and hormones can influence the outcome. A biochemical signal that is widely shared across plant cells can therefore produce distinct organ-specific transcriptional programs.

Type-A ARRs differ functionally from the type-B class. They are rapidly induced by cytokinin in many contexts and commonly act as negative regulators of cytokinin signaling. The pathway therefore produces some of its own inhibitors. Initially, a rise in ligand availability can increase phosphorelay activity and type-B-dependent transcription. As type-A ARR abundance increases, the capacity of the pathway to sustain that output may be reduced. The resulting negative feedback can constrain the magnitude or duration of the response. Some type-A ARR proteins participate in this control through phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms, but it is not necessary to assign all members a single detailed biochemical mode of inhibition.

This feedback is a common examination trap because both classes contain response regulators and both can receive phosphate, yet their principal regulatory functions differ. Type-B proteins generally mediate positive transcriptional responses. Type-A proteins generally limit the response. A loss-of-function mutation in several type-A ARRs can produce enhanced cytokinin sensitivity, rather than the weak response expected from removal of positive transcription factors. Conversely, loss of important type-B ARRs can reduce cytokinin-responsive gene expression. The exact phenotype depends on which family members are removed and in which tissue the response is measured.

Not all AHP-like proteins act as positive relay intermediates. AHP6 is a pseudo-histidine phosphotransfer protein that lacks the canonical histidine required for phosphotransfer. It can inhibit cytokinin signaling in specific developmental contexts, notably in vascular patterning. This exception is informative because it demonstrates that protein similarity does not guarantee equivalent biochemical activity. A predicted protein with a missing essential phosphoacceptor residue should not automatically be placed in the positive signaling pathway simply because its name resembles that of other AHP proteins. Its function must be inferred from sequence, biochemical activity and genetic evidence together.

A time-course experiment can reveal the consequences of feedback. Consider a hypothetical culture treated continuously with cytokinin. A primary response reporter is measured at 0, 15, 30, 60 and 180 minutes. It rises rapidly, reaches a maximum and subsequently falls to an intermediate level. The ligand concentration is experimentally maintained. The pattern is compatible with negative feedback, but it does not prove that type-A ARRs caused the decline. Receptor desensitization, changes in intracellular ligand accessibility and other metabolic or transcriptional regulators could also contribute. A comparison with a suitable multiple type-A arr mutant can test whether the negative regulators contribute to adaptation of the reporter response.

If a mutant lacking type-A negative regulators has a similar initial increase but maintains a higher reporter output at later times, the result supports an important feedback function. If both wild type and mutant show the same decline, the missing regulators may not be essential to this particular response or other feedback mechanisms may compensate. A reporter time course alone should be interpreted with the genotype, ligand measurements and viability controls. A constitutive reporter unrelated to cytokinin can help detect general changes in transcription or cell viability that would otherwise be mistaken for specific signaling changes.

Common cytokinin reporters include promoter fusions for genes such as ARR5 and synthetic transcriptional reporters such as TCSn driving a fluorescent protein. These tools measure an output of the signaling system rather than directly measuring the mass of cytokinin present. A bright reporter indicates that a particular transcriptional circuit is active under the conditions of the assay. It does not guarantee that the concentration of free cytokinin is greater than in every dim region, because receptor expression and signaling competence may differ. Fluorescent protein accumulation and turnover also introduce a temporal delay between transcription and visible fluorescence.

Chemical measurement and reporter analysis complement each other. A tissue with high active cytokinin but weak reporter output may have impaired perception or downstream regulation. A tissue with lower chemical concentration but strong reporter activity may be highly sensitive, possess a favorable receptor repertoire or have weak negative feedback. A reporter in a small region can reveal spatial patterns that a bulk metabolite assay averages away. Conversely, chemical profiling can distinguish whether an altered reporter reflects the absence of free bases or changes in signaling machinery. Reliable pathway interpretation often requires combining both levels of evidence.

A final comparison with bacterial two-component signaling provides a useful conceptual anchor. A simple bacterial system commonly consists of a sensor histidine kinase and a response regulator that receives phosphate on an aspartate. Plant cytokinin signaling elaborates that architecture through the receiver domain of a hybrid receptor and a separate phosphotransfer intermediary. The final response regulators can influence nuclear gene expression. The plant version is therefore often described as a multistep two-component-related system, not a literal two-protein system. The name refers to the evolutionary and biochemical logic of histidine kinase and receiver modules rather than the total number of proteins in the complete pathway.

9. From transcription to the cell division cycle

The production of a new cell requires a coordinated series of events. Before chromosome segregation, the cell must replicate its DNA, acquire sufficient biosynthetic resources and prepare the machinery for mitosis. During cytokinesis the cytoplasm is partitioned, and in a plant cell a new cell plate forms between the daughter nuclei. Cytokinin influences this process primarily as a regulatory signal. It does not function as a structural component of the cell plate or a substrate for DNA polymerase. Its effect can begin before the visible division event, by altering expression or activity of proteins controlling entry into the cell cycle.

A useful starting point is the G1 phase, the interval preceding DNA synthesis. Cells in G1 can integrate information about nutrients, developmental signals and their own physiological condition. Cyclins bind to cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), helping to regulate transitions between cell cycle states. D-type cyclins are especially relevant to the interpretation of growth-promoting signals and cell cycle entry. In Arabidopsis, cytokinin can increase expression of CYCD3-type cyclins. These proteins contribute to the regulation of the G1-to-S transition. The connection gives a molecular explanation for the older observation that cytokinin can stimulate proliferation in suitable plant cultures.

In a simplified G1 control mechanism, cyclin–CDK activity influences the phosphorylation state of regulatory proteins, including the plant retinoblastoma-related protein, commonly called RBR. The interaction of RBR with E2F transcription factors can limit expression of genes required for DNA replication. Changes in CDK activity can modify this repression and support entry into S phase. This model explains how a signal that initially acts at a membrane receptor can eventually influence the expression of genes involved in chromosome duplication. It is not necessary to suppose that an AHK receptor physically contacts a replication fork or that ARR transcription factors directly substitute for cyclins.

Riou-Khamlichi and colleagues reported in 1999 that cytokinin induced a D-type cyclin in Arabidopsis and connected its expression with the G1/S transition. Experiments in which a cyclin was expressed constitutively showed that altering a downstream regulatory component could reduce the requirement for exogenously supplied cytokinin in particular culture contexts. The result supports the idea that cytokinin acts upstream of cell cycle machinery. It does not establish that CYCD3 is the only target of cytokinin or that every aspect of cytokinin-dependent development can be bypassed by increasing cyclin abundance. In particular, the spatial patterning of a meristem requires more than unrestricted entry into S phase.

The timing of a response is essential to causal interpretation. If the amount of CYCD3 transcript rises shortly after cytokinin treatment, and DNA synthesis increases later, the temporal order is compatible with an upstream regulatory role. An increase in transcript alone does not prove that the protein is active or sufficient. Measuring protein abundance, testing loss-of-function mutants and examining DNA replication in the same tissue provide stronger evidence. If a treatment increases both CYCD3 and DNA synthesis but also changes sugar availability, one must control the metabolic difference before attributing all of the effect to cytokinin.

DNA synthesis can be measured by incorporation of a labeled nucleoside analogue such as EdU. Cells incorporating the analogue during a labeling period are entering or occupying S phase. The fraction of labeled nuclei provides an indication of DNA replication activity, not necessarily the final number of daughter cells. Some plant cells replicate DNA without proceeding through ordinary mitosis, a process associated with endoreduplication. Therefore, increased EdU incorporation should be complemented by measures of mitotic activity, cell counts and developmental context if the question specifically concerns proliferation. This distinction prevents a common error in interpreting cell cycle experiments.

A mitotic index is the proportion of observed cells in mitosis at a particular time. If 40 of 400 scored cells are in mitosis, the mitotic index is 10 percent. If a cytokinin-treated culture has 72 mitotic cells out of 400, the corresponding value is 18 percent. That result could reflect increased entry into mitosis, but it could also reflect a longer average duration of mitosis. A drug that arrests cells in mitosis might raise the index while reducing production of daughter cells. Thus a single image or index is insufficient to establish the rate of completed cell divisions. A time-course measurement of total viable cell numbers and phase-specific markers provides stronger support.

A similarly useful calculation concerns organ dimensions. Imagine two comparable young leaves with areas of 24 and 36 square millimetres. If mean projected epidermal cell area is 0.002 square millimetres in each sample, an approximate epidermal cell count would be 12,000 and 18,000 respectively, assuming uniform cell size and negligible non-cellular area in the measurement. The larger leaf in this hypothetical example has approximately 50 percent more epidermal cells. If instead the cell count is the same but mean area is larger, an expansion-based interpretation is more plausible. Neither estimate provides a direct measure of internal tissue layers, and the assumptions must be stated. Examinations frequently ask students to distinguish growth caused by proliferation from growth caused by expansion.

Cytokinin and auxin can jointly support cell proliferation in cultured tissues, but neither hormone substitutes for basic metabolic resources. Cells need carbon and nitrogen for nucleotide synthesis, proteins and cell wall production. A strongly activated signaling pathway cannot indefinitely maintain division if ATP production or nitrogen supply becomes limiting. This explains why hormone dose alone is not a sufficient predictor of culture growth. In an intact plant, hormonal regulation helps coordinate proliferation with nutrient conditions rather than abolishing the dependence on those conditions.

The ability to influence the cell cycle must be distinguished from the ability to maintain an organ-forming stem cell niche. A proliferating population can lose the pattern of spatial organization needed to generate a functional shoot. Conversely, a slowly dividing cell in the central region of a meristem can be indispensable to long-term growth because it retains developmental identity and supplies descendants. Cytokinin is involved in both proliferation and meristem regulation, but the latter includes interactions with positional transcription factors and neighboring cells. The next sections explain how those interactions produce the distinct behavior of shoot and root meristems.

10. Shoot apical meristem maintenance and shoot organ initiation

The shoot apical meristem is a small, organized population of cells at the growing apex. Its central region maintains stem cell identity, while descendants displaced toward surrounding regions contribute to new organs. The stem cells must be maintained over time even as other cells leave the meristem. If every stem cell were to differentiate immediately, the apex would lose its capacity for repeated organ production. If all cells remained indefinitely in an undifferentiated state, normal leaves and flowers would not form. A functioning meristem therefore requires coordination of renewal, division and differentiation.

Cytokinin signaling contributes to this balance. The importance of local activation is illustrated by the rice LOG mutant described earlier. A defect in the release of active cytokinin can compromise continued meristem function even though the plant has a vascular system and other potential sites of cytokinin production. A localized enzyme can help establish a hormone response domain in the apex. The consequence is not simply an increase in the average number of mitotic cells throughout the plant; it is a change in the regulatory conditions that support ongoing activity of a particular developmental region.

A central regulator in the Arabidopsis shoot apical meristem is WUSCHEL, abbreviated WUS. WUS encodes a homeodomain transcription factor associated with the organizing center, a region below the stem cell population. WUS activity helps maintain stem cell identity in neighboring cells. This is a positional arrangement: the cells producing a key regulatory factor are not necessarily identical to the cells whose identity is maintained by it. A signal that modifies WUS expression or the sensitivity of the organizing region can thus alter the developmental organization of the apex.

Cytokinin signaling and WUS expression are functionally connected. In suitable meristem contexts, positive type-B ARR transcriptional regulators support WUS expression. This provides a route from perception of cytokinin through the AHK–AHP–ARR relay to a gene involved in specifying a stem cell niche. The exact relationship depends on developmental stage and other regulatory inputs, so a simple arrow from cytokinin to WUS should be interpreted as a positive regulatory influence rather than a universal statement that cytokinin alone is sufficient for WUS expression in every cell.

The connection also operates in the opposite direction. WUS can repress several type-A ARR genes that normally limit cytokinin responses. In a region where WUS activity is high, reduced expression of those negative regulators can enhance sensitivity to cytokinin. This is an example of a positive regulatory relationship embedded within a broader network. A small increase in cytokinin output can favor WUS activity, and WUS-mediated suppression of inhibitors can reinforce local responsiveness. The presence of positive reinforcement makes spatial boundaries and balancing signals particularly important; otherwise the domain of activity could expand inappropriately.

One balancing system involves CLAVATA signaling. CLV3, a peptide associated with stem cells, contributes to signaling that restricts the WUS expression domain. WUS supports the stem cell population, which in turn supplies a signal limiting WUS. This negative regulatory relationship helps maintain a stable meristem organization. It is helpful to draw the cytokinin–WUS relationship and the WUS–CLV3 feedback on the same diagram, using different arrow symbols for activation and inhibition. The resulting network explains why a mutation in cytokinin signaling need not produce precisely the same phenotype as a mutation in CLV signaling, even though both can affect the shoot apex.

When analyzing a meristem mutant, distinguish meristem size, stem cell identity and production of lateral organs. An enlarged apex can result from altered differentiation or a failure to restrict stem cell identity, rather than an increase in the rate of cell division alone. A small apex could reflect reduced proliferation, premature differentiation or loss of a stem cell population. Meristem termination is a more specific phenotype in which the capacity for continued production of shoot tissues is lost. Histological measurements, cell identity markers and developmental time courses are needed to separate these outcomes.

Local auxin distribution provides another important layer of regulation. In the peripheral zone of the shoot apex, patterns of auxin accumulation and transport contribute to positioning new organ primordia. Cytokinin helps maintain the competence and activity of meristematic regions, but auxin maxima are important positional signals for particular organ initiation events. The hormones may interact positively or antagonistically depending on the process and location. A model that says cytokinin creates shoots while auxin creates roots cannot explain the need for auxin in the initiation of leaves from the shoot apex. That slogan describes a restricted tissue culture tendency, not a general map of hormone functions.

Cytokinin also contributes to the formation and activity of axillary meristems. An axillary meristem arises in association with a leaf axil and may produce an axillary bud. During the initiation of these meristems, cytokinin-responsive type-B ARRs can support WUS expression. Subsequent bud outgrowth is influenced by nutritional conditions, auxin-dependent apical dominance, strigolactones and local cytokinin action. Meristem initiation and bud outgrowth are related but distinct processes: a bud may already exist yet remain dormant or grow only slowly. A treatment that releases existing buds is not necessarily a treatment that creates new axillary meristems.

An experiment on branching should therefore begin with a precise developmental readout. Count the number of axillary sites containing meristems or buds before treatment, then measure the length or activity of those buds over time. If cytokinin application increases the length of already established buds without increasing their number, the result supports a role in outgrowth rather than initial formation. If the treatment affects the number of newly initiated meristems, a different developmental mechanism is implicated. The same whole-plant appearance of increased branching could otherwise conceal different underlying events.

The role of cytokinin in shoot development extends to young leaves and vascular tissues. Developing leaves initially contain proliferating cells that later undergo expansion and differentiation. Cytokinin availability can affect the timing and extent of proliferation, while interactions with auxin and other regulators contribute to patterning. In vascular development, cytokinin signaling participates in cell division and differentiation decisions, and local inhibitory mechanisms such as AHP6 help establish distinct response domains. For this chapter, the important principle is that spatially restricted cytokinin output can influence cell fate. Detailed vascular anatomy and organ patterning belong to the developmental biology chapters.

These mechanisms explain why constitutive increases in cytokinin are not invariably beneficial for shoot growth. A properly functioning apex needs the appropriate magnitude and localization of the response. Excessive or mistimed activation can disturb the normal balance between maintenance and differentiation. An optimal physiological outcome is therefore not equivalent to the maximum possible receptor signal. When evaluating a proposed agricultural manipulation of cytokinin, consider the targeted tissue, developmental stage, and effects on other organs instead of assuming that more hormone necessarily produces a larger or more productive plant.

11. Root meristems: cytokinin and the division–differentiation boundary

The root apical meristem provides one of the clearest examples of why cytokinin cannot be described as a universally positive regulator of proliferation. At the root tip, stem cells and their descendants contribute to different tissue layers. Cells produced near the tip pass through a region of active division, then enter zones where division ceases or becomes less frequent and elongation becomes prominent. Differentiation ultimately establishes specialized cells involved in absorption, support, conduction and other functions. A root that cannot make this transition would be unable to build functional mature tissues.

The size of the root meristem depends on both production of cells and their departure from the dividing population. A small proliferative region does not necessarily mean that mitosis has been directly blocked in every cell. If the rate at which cells commit to differentiation increases, the population remaining in the meristem may become smaller even when some cells continue dividing normally. Conversely, delaying differentiation can expand the meristem without necessarily increasing the intrinsic division rate of each cell. Cytokinin helps regulate this boundary, especially through interactions with auxin in the transition zone.

In a well-studied Arabidopsis mechanism, cytokinin promotes expression of the gene SHY2, also called IAA3, through type-B response regulators including ARR1. SHY2 encodes an Aux/IAA-family protein involved in limiting auxin-responsive transcription. Increased SHY2 activity reduces expression of particular PIN auxin transport facilitators, thereby influencing the distribution of auxin. The altered auxin response and transport environment supports a transition toward differentiation. This pathway links cytokinin perception to the spatial relationship between dividing and differentiating cells rather than requiring cytokinin to act as a direct inhibitor of DNA polymerase or mitosis.

Auxin can oppose aspects of this mechanism by promoting the degradation of Aux/IAA proteins through the TIR1/AFB pathway. If auxin signaling accelerates SHY2 removal, repression of relevant auxin-responsive genes can be relieved. This provides a point at which cytokinin and auxin converge on a regulatory protein. The abundance of SHY2 depends on both its production and its removal. Cytokinin can increase production, whereas auxin can favor removal. A change in either hormonal pathway can therefore alter the same developmental boundary. The model demonstrates the usefulness of analyzing synthesis and degradation of regulatory proteins in the same way as metabolic homeostasis.

The 2008 study by Dello Ioio and colleagues combined genetic and molecular evidence to connect ARR1-dependent SHY2 regulation with PIN expression and root meristem size. A particularly informative approach is to alter a component upstream of SHY2 and determine whether its effect still occurs when SHY2 function is compromised. If overexpression of ARR1 reduces meristem size in a normal genetic background but fails to produce the same reduction in a suitable shy2 loss-of-function background, SHY2 is implicated as a required downstream mediator of that response. This type of genetic reasoning is called epistasis analysis. The result supports pathway order in the particular process examined, not necessarily the conclusion that every target of ARR1 is controlled by SHY2.

A stabilized Aux/IAA repressor illustrates the complementary prediction. Normally, auxin can accelerate removal of such repressors. If a mutation makes SHY2 resistant to degradation, repression can persist even when auxin is supplied. The developmental effect may resemble a condition in which the balance is shifted toward differentiation or reduced auxin responsiveness. Increasing cytokinin and stabilizing SHY2 do not have identical effects on all genes, but they can converge on aspects of the root meristem transition. If an exam asks whether exogenous auxin must rescue a stabilized repressor, the correct reasoning is that the mutation can impair the very degradation mechanism through which auxin normally acts.

A simple model of meristem population dynamics helps connect the molecular pathway with measurable tissue organization. Let M be the number of cells in a defined proliferative region. Let P denote the number of net additional cells added per hour by division, and let E denote the number of cells leaving that region per hour through developmental transition. Then the net change in the population is dM/dt = P − E. This accounting equation does not specify the molecular regulation of either term. Its value is that it separates two processes that can both affect the observed size of a meristem.

Suppose a hypothetical root meristem initially contains 100 cells in a defined tissue file or scoring domain. At a particular developmental stage, division adds 12 net cells per hour and differentiation removes 12 cells per hour. The population is stable. After a cytokinin treatment, assume for illustration that division remains at 12 net cells per hour while exit increases to 16 per hour. The region initially loses four cells per hour. If the rates remained constant for three hours, the region would contain 88 cells. In a real plant the rates may change as the region changes size, so the calculation is an initial approximation rather than a prediction of indefinite linear decline.

The same logic clarifies the effect of reducing cytokinin by manipulating CKX. Some cytokinin-deficient plants display enhanced root growth together with impaired shoot growth. A reduction in cytokinin-dependent differentiation signals can favor the maintenance of a larger proliferative region in particular contexts. This is consistent with the opposite roles documented in shoot and root meristems. Yet it would be incorrect to extrapolate that removing all cytokinin is always advantageous for the root. Cytokinin also contributes to vascular differentiation, interactions with nutrients and other essential developmental processes. Genetic manipulations must therefore be interpreted by tissue and degree of perturbation.

Root length is another measurement that requires caution. It reflects the rate of cell production, the extent of elongation, the duration of growth and the behavior of multiple root tissues. A long primary root can result from increased cell production, longer mature cells or both. A treatment might increase the number of meristematic cells while reducing cell elongation, producing little net increase in root length. Conversely, an increase in elongation can produce a longer root without increasing meristem size. When evaluating a figure showing root length alone, identify what it supports directly and what would require additional measurements.

Cytokinin also influences lateral root development. Local cytokinin signaling can limit aspects of lateral root initiation and spacing, in part through effects on auxin distribution and developmental competence. The primary root meristem and lateral root primordia have different regulatory contexts. A treatment that increases primary root length need not increase lateral root number, and the reverse can also occur. The density of lateral roots per unit length is different from their absolute number. If a longer root contains the same number of lateral roots, their density decreases even though initiation count is unchanged. Such normalization is often important in quantitative examination questions.

Comparing shoot and root responses reveals a general lesson. Cytokinin does not possess a single inherent instruction that applies equally to all cell populations. In shoots, it can support maintenance and proliferation in meristematic contexts; in roots, it can help establish a boundary at which cells differentiate. The shared molecular signaling pathway produces different downstream outcomes because the available targets, other hormones and developmental organization differ. A successful answer to an integrated plant physiology question explains those differences causally rather than treating them as exceptions to a memorized rule.

12. Hormonal control of regeneration and organ formation in culture

Tissue culture allows developmental competence to be examined outside the normal organization of an intact plant. An explant is a piece of plant tissue used to initiate a culture. Depending on its origin, it may contain dividing meristematic cells, differentiated cells or cells capable of changing their developmental program after wounding. A culture medium supplies inorganic nutrients, a carbon source in many systems, and other necessary components. Auxins and cytokinins can then be varied as experimental inputs. The developmental outcomes reveal how the tissue interprets signals rather than simply which nutrient is present.

The classical auxin–cytokinin relationship can be represented as a two-dimensional treatment matrix. One dimension is the concentration of auxin, and the other is the concentration of cytokinin. In a responsive culture system, relatively high auxin compared with cytokinin often favors rooting, while relatively high cytokinin favors shooting. Intermediate combinations may support continued proliferation. It is more informative to vary both hormones independently than to compare a single treatment with an untreated control. A factorial design can identify whether an effect of cytokinin depends on the auxin concentration.

Consider two hypothetical culture media with equal molar ratios: the first contains 0.1 micromolar auxin and 0.1 micromolar cytokinin, whereas the second contains 10 micromolar of each. Both have a cytokinin-to-auxin ratio of one, but the absolute concentrations differ by a factor of one hundred. Receptor occupancy, metabolic turnover and possible inhibitory effects may differ substantially. A ratio is therefore an incomplete descriptor of the hormonal environment. Moreover, comparing mass concentrations of different molecules without considering molecular mass can obscure molar differences. A valid experiment reports the identities and concentrations of the compounds, the developmental source of the explant, and the culture conditions.

Many regeneration systems involve successive stages rather than one permanent medium. An explant may first be placed on a medium that supports acquisition of regeneration competence and formation of proliferative tissue. Transfer to a shoot-inducing medium with a different hormone balance can promote establishment of a new shoot meristem. The shoots may then be transferred to conditions that favor root formation. The changes in hormone composition interact with the developmental history of the culture. A tissue exposed to the same final medium without an earlier induction period may not produce the same result. The sequence is therefore as relevant as the final ratio.

In Arabidopsis shoot regeneration, activation of WUS expression is an important step in establishing a shoot stem cell niche. Cytokinin signaling through type-B ARRs can contribute to this activation in competent tissues. The relationship provides a mechanistic bridge between the old tissue culture observations and modern developmental genetics. A shoot-inducing treatment does not merely accelerate random proliferation. Under appropriate conditions, it helps establish a regulatory domain that can organize a new meristem. Local cell identity, auxin distribution and the previous culture stage contribute to whether such a domain develops.

A tissue culture experiment needs a distinction between the fraction of explants that regenerate and the number of shoots formed by each responding explant. Suppose 30 of 50 explants in a control treatment regenerate, producing 90 shoots in total, while 40 of 50 cytokinin-treated explants regenerate, producing 100 shoots. The regeneration frequency increases from 60 to 80 percent, but mean shoots per responding explant falls from 3 to 2.5. Mean shoots per initial explant increases from 1.8 to 2.0. Each calculation answers a different question. The treatment appears to increase the likelihood of regeneration without increasing the mean multiplicity among successful explants.

The interpretation also depends on biological replication. Fifty tissue pieces cut from one parent plant do not necessarily provide the same independence as explants obtained from several parent plants under independently repeated culture conditions. Differences among batches, genotypes or developmental stages may influence the outcome. A meaningful comparison includes replicate cultures and records the distribution of responses, rather than reporting only the largest number of shoots in one vessel. A control without cytokinin, a control for the solvent used to apply the hormone and an appropriate auxin comparison help identify the causal variable.

If a cytokinin receptor mutant fails to generate shoots on a cytokinin-rich medium while wild-type tissue succeeds, the result supports a role for the receptor in regeneration under those conditions. It would be premature to conclude that cytokinin has no other function or that the mutant can never regenerate. The tissue could respond to another developmental route or retain residual signaling through redundant receptors. Examination of early transcriptional markers, explant viability and WUS activation can help determine whether the mutant fails to acquire competence, initiate a meristem or sustain subsequent growth. Each possibility has a different mechanistic implication.

The same conceptual framework explains why a plant regenerated through tissue culture need not resemble every other culture derived from the same species. Developmental competence, genetic variation and culture history can influence morphology. Prolonged culture may introduce additional variation, so successful shoot formation is not in itself proof of genetic fidelity or normal subsequent physiology. At the examination level, the central result remains the experimental demonstration that hormone balance influences developmental fate. The practical application is regeneration and propagation, but the scientific explanation requires receptors, gene expression, positional organization and appropriate cellular resources.

13. Nutrient-dependent cytokinin signaling and whole-plant coordination

A plant must allocate resources between roots that explore the soil and shoots that intercept light. If one region of the root system encounters abundant nitrate, continued shoot growth may become more favorable because the plant can acquire nitrogen needed for proteins and photosynthetic machinery. If nutrient acquisition is poor, allocating more resources to new leaves may be costly. Hormonal communication helps link local nutrient perception with growth in distant organs. Cytokinin participates in this coordination because its biosynthesis and transport can respond to nitrate availability.

Nitrate has two distinguishable roles in this context. It is a nitrogen source that can ultimately contribute to amino acid and nucleotide synthesis, and it can act as a regulatory signal that changes gene expression. The molecular events by which nitrate is reduced and assimilated belong to nitrogen metabolism; cytokinin is not an enzyme of nitrate reduction. Its role is to modify developmental and metabolic responses to the nutrient environment. A plant supplied with cytokinin but no usable nitrogen cannot indefinitely make new nitrogen-containing biomass. A hormone can convey information about availability or influence allocation, but cannot substitute for the missing element.

In Arabidopsis, nitrate supply can stimulate expression of particular IPT genes, notably IPT3 in relevant root contexts, and alter the formation of cytokinin species. Trans-zeatin-related compounds can move to the shoot through the vascular system, with ABCG14 contributing to their efficient distribution. The shoot then interprets the hormonal input using its cytokinin signaling pathway. Responses can include changes in growth, meristem function and nitrogen-related metabolism. The complete response is not a single linear pathway acting in isolation: other nutrient signals, carbon status, auxin and additional regulators contribute.

A classic way to examine systemic coordination is a split-root experiment. Roots of a plant grow into separate compartments so that one side can receive a nutrient treatment while the other remains under a different condition. The shoot is shared between both root compartments. Changes in the untreated side after supplying nitrate to the other side suggest that the response may involve information transmitted through the plant rather than only direct contact with the nutrient. The design is especially informative when paired with cytokinin measurements, transport mutants and transcriptional markers. It should not be interpreted as proof of cytokinin action solely because distant responses occur; mobile metabolites, peptides and other signals can also participate.

Suppose a hypothetical experiment measures xylem trans-zeatin-type compounds and a cytokinin-response reporter in shoots after nitrate resupply. Wild type shows increases in both measures. An abcg14 mutant shows accumulation of relevant compounds in roots, reduced delivery to the shoot and a weaker shoot reporter response. A root IPT-deficient line shows reduced cytokinin production following nitrate treatment. These observations would support distinguishable roles for biosynthesis and transport. To strengthen the inference, a researcher could compare shoot responses after direct application of active cytokinin, or use reciprocal grafting to identify the tissue in which the transport defect is important.

A failure of direct cytokinin application to restore a particular nitrate response would not necessarily refute the entire model. The response might require actual nitrogen assimilation in addition to hormonal signaling. A supplied cytokinin could restore an early reporter signal while failing to restore long-term growth in a nitrogen-starved plant. This distinction between an informational output and a resource-dependent output is central to physiological reasoning. If the applied hormone restores transcription of a cytokinin-responsive gene, it indicates that at least part of the downstream signaling machinery remains competent; it does not establish that the plant has sufficient nutrients for sustained biomass production.

Cytokinin effects can differ between nitrate and other nutrient conditions, among plant species and depending on developmental stage. Phosphate limitation and interactions with other signaling systems may modify the root architecture response. Rather than memorize a universal statement that nutrient abundance always increases every cytokinin form, identify which nutrient was altered, the sampled tissue, the molecular form measured and the developmental phenotype. Nutrient availability and cytokinin concentration are distinct variables even when their values often change together.

Another quantitative distinction involves allocation versus overall growth. Consider a hypothetical plant with dry root mass of 0.40 grams and shoot mass of 0.80 grams before treatment. Its root-to-shoot mass ratio is 0.50. Following a nutrient and hormone response, root mass becomes 0.50 grams and shoot mass becomes 1.50 grams. The ratio falls to approximately 0.33 even though the root has grown. A lower root-to-shoot ratio therefore does not imply that root biomass has decreased. Both absolute growth and relative allocation should be reported. In this example, shoot biomass has increased proportionally more than root biomass, which is consistent with a shift toward shoot allocation but does not by itself establish the molecular cause.

The physiological importance of cytokinin is clearest when the plant is considered as an integrated system. A local nutrient signal can induce metabolic changes in roots, vascular transport can convey relevant chemical information, and a distant meristem can alter its growth in response. The feedback from a growing shoot can in turn change demand for nitrogen and carbon allocation to roots. Such interactions prevent a simple one-way interpretation of root-to-shoot signaling. The cytokinin pathway is part of a coordinated control network that must operate within the limits of the plant's metabolic resources.

14. Delaying leaf senescence: preserving function rather than simply increasing division

Leaf senescence is an organized developmental process during which the leaf changes its metabolism and remobilizes resources. The reduction in chlorophyll associated with many senescing leaves reflects changes in chloroplast structure and pigment metabolism. Proteins and other macromolecules may be degraded so that their constituents can be reused in developing tissues. Although senescence eventually contributes to the loss of leaf function, it is not equivalent to an instantaneous passive death caused by damage. The plant can regulate its timing in response to age, developmental demands and environmental conditions.

Cytokinin commonly delays leaf senescence in experimental contexts. Application of an active cytokinin to a detached leaf or leaf region can help retain chlorophyll for longer relative to an untreated control. Such an effect cannot be explained solely by the production of many new mesophyll cells. Most mature photosynthetic cells are not actively cycling in the same way as meristematic cells. The hormone can alter gene expression and metabolic regulation in those cells, thereby influencing the timing of functional decline. This illustrates how the same class of molecule can regulate both cell division in growing tissue and maintenance in differentiated tissue.

A classical detached-leaf assay needs appropriate controls because detachment itself alters water supply, nutrient exchange and stress signals. Leaves should be matched for developmental age, position and initial chlorophyll content, and maintained under the same light and environmental conditions. One group receives cytokinin and another receives the corresponding solvent or treatment medium without cytokinin. Measurements can include chlorophyll content, photosynthetic performance and visible tissue deterioration over time. If a treated leaf remains green longer, the immediate evidence supports delayed pigment loss. A claim that the leaf remains fully functional requires additional physiological evidence.

The distinction between appearance and function is important. A leaf may retain pigment without maintaining normal carbon assimilation if other parts of its photosynthetic machinery are damaged. Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements, gas exchange measurements and protein abundance can complement visual scoring. The maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II, often represented by Fv/Fm under appropriate dark-adapted conditions, can provide information about photosynthetic apparatus performance. However, no single fluorescence parameter measures all aspects of carbon assimilation, and stomatal limitations or other metabolic effects can reduce assimilation even when some fluorescence measures remain favorable.

A landmark experiment by Gan and Amasino in 1995 used regulated expression of a cytokinin biosynthesis gene in tobacco to delay senescence. The design linked IPT expression to a senescence-associated promoter, commonly described as SAG12-driven IPT expression. As senescence-associated transcription began, cytokinin production increased and could suppress the progression of senescence. The key innovation was feedback through the developmental program itself. A promoter associated with senescence was used to stimulate a hormone that delays that process, creating a regulatory system different from constitutive production throughout the plant.

This experimental arrangement helps distinguish temporal targeting from excessive hormone supply. If IPT were expressed strongly in all tissues throughout development, the plant could experience inappropriate hormone activity in roots, meristems and developing organs. Inducing cytokinin production preferentially when senescence begins reduces some of those potential conflicts. The strategy illustrates a broader principle of plant hormone manipulation: the timing and spatial distribution of a signal can be as important as its total amount. An intervention aimed at maintaining older leaves should not automatically alter every stage of root and shoot development.

The feedback loop can be interpreted as a form of negative regulation of the initiating process. Senescence-associated gene expression increases IPT production, IPT increases cytokinin synthesis, and cytokinin delays senescence, which can reduce the initiating promoter activity. It is not a perpetual positive amplification loop in which senescence and hormone production always increase together. The precise dynamics depend on promoter regulation, hormone metabolism and tissue response, but the conceptual circuit explains why a senescence-inducible construct is different from an always-active promoter. An examination question may ask which element serves as the sensor of developmental state and which element changes hormone supply.

An illustrative chlorophyll dataset makes the distinction between relative retention and absolute differences explicit. Suppose comparable leaves initially contain 2.0 milligrams chlorophyll per gram fresh mass. After five days, untreated leaves contain 0.6 milligrams per gram and treated leaves contain 1.4 milligrams per gram. The untreated group retains 30 percent of its initial value and the treated group retains 70 percent. The difference in retention is 40 percentage points, while the treated endpoint is approximately 2.33 times the untreated endpoint. These are distinct numerical descriptions. The calculation assumes comparable sampling and that fresh-mass changes do not distort concentration comparisons. Total chlorophyll per leaf or dry-mass normalization may be helpful if water content changes substantially.

Delaying senescence can have potential agricultural benefits, but prolonged greenness is not an unconditional indicator of greater yield. Older leaves can supply fixed carbon for growing organs, yet nutrient remobilization from those leaves may also be important during reproductive development. Extending leaf lifespan may alter this balance. The outcome depends on the crop, environmental conditions and timing of the intervention. An experiment should measure actual photosynthetic performance, resource allocation and reproductive output rather than infer yield directly from leaf color. This reasoning links cytokinin physiology with the broader question of tradeoffs in whole-plant growth.

Cytokinin-associated senescence control and meristem maintenance therefore share a regulatory foundation but differ in their cellular outputs. In a meristem, cytokinin can contribute to stem cell identity and production of descendants; in an older leaf, it can delay changes associated with functional decline. Both responses require an appropriate signaling system and developmental context. Neither is adequately described by the name cytokinin alone.

15. Integrating biochemical, genetic and physiological evidence

The strongest explanation of a hormone phenotype combines evidence from multiple levels. A molecular assay identifies which compounds or proteins are present. A signaling reporter indicates whether a particular response pathway is active. An organ-level measurement reveals a developmental or physiological outcome. The three observations are related but not interchangeable. If a mutant has small shoots, that is a phenotype. If it has low trans-zeatin in shoots, that is a chemical observation. If it has reduced reporter activity after cytokinin treatment, that is evidence about signaling output. The causal chain is strengthened when perturbations and rescue experiments link these observations.

Consider four hypothetical mutants with reduced shoot development. One has reduced adenylate IPT activity, another lacks a major local LOG activation function, a third has impaired ABCG14-dependent distribution, and a fourth has deficient AHK-mediated perception. Applying the same active cytokinin to all four does not guarantee identical responses. An IPT-deficient plant may respond if downstream signaling remains intact. A LOG-deficient region may respond to a free base that bypasses the activation reaction, whereas application of a ribotide need not be equally effective. A transport mutant may respond to direct shoot delivery despite poor delivery from roots. A strongly receptor-deficient tissue may show little response even when sufficient active hormone reaches it.

Distinguishing these mutants requires more than assigning a single label based on appearance. In the IPT-deficient line, analysis of precursor and product pools may reveal a shortage of newly formed cytokinin nucleotides. In the LOG-deficient line, the relation between precursor and active-base abundance may shift. In the transport mutant, the root and shoot distributions are informative. In the receptor mutant, chemical levels can be normal or elevated because defective feedback and altered metabolism may affect the pool. The response reporter and ligand-binding or phosphorelay assays can further distinguish perception from downstream transmission. No one measurement resolves every case.

The concept of epistasis is particularly useful when a molecular pathway has a defined order. Suppose a cytokinin treatment increases WUS expression in a responsive shoot regeneration system. A mutant lacking key type-B ARRs fails to show that induction. If a suitably controlled downstream manipulation of WUS restores a meristem-related outcome in that background, it would support the view that WUS acts downstream for that outcome. It would not prove that every cytokinin-dependent effect is mediated by WUS, since the hormone also affects cell cycle genes, nutrient responses and leaf senescence. Pathway order is established for a specified phenotype, not for the entire organism at once.

A reliable conclusion should match the strength of the evidence. A correlation between cytokinin abundance and shoot size is compatible with a growth-regulatory role, but a common upstream factor such as nitrate supply could influence both. A treatment that changes cytokinin availability while holding nitrate constant gives more direct evidence. A genetic manipulation of a biosynthetic or catabolic enzyme can provide another line of support. Restoration by an appropriate active ligand, together with evidence that downstream signaling is intact, further strengthens the causal interpretation. Multiple independent approaches reduce the chance that the observed effect is due to an unrelated feature of one treatment.

When studying the hormone as a whole, keep a clear causal sequence in mind: a metabolic source supplies precursors; chemical transformations generate active ligands; transport and degradation shape the distribution; receptors initiate phosphorylation; response regulators alter gene expression; local developmental and metabolic context determines the physiological consequence. Feedback can act at several stages, and other hormones can change the interpretation of the signal. The chain is a useful model for reasoning, not a rigid assertion that every molecule follows the same route or that every response occurs in the same cell. This is why the same cytokinin treatment can have very different effects in a callus, a root transition zone, a shoot apex and an aging leaf.

16. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Cytokinin always stimulates division

A tissue can respond by differentiating or delaying senescence. The name emphasizes discovery, not a universal effect. In a root-meristem question ask whether cell exit or cell entry changed.

2. All cytokinin comes from roots

Shoots also synthesize and activate cytokinin locally. Root-to-shoot delivery is memorable but does not exclude local LOG activity. Distinguish source and activation site.

3. Kinetin is the principal endogenous cytokinin

Kinetin was important in discovery and is used experimentally; natural iP and zeatin are key physiological forms. A question about discovery differs from one about endogenous ligand identity.

4. Cis and trans zeatin are the same ligand in every context

Geometry affects recognition and metabolism. Similar formulas cause confusion; identify which isomer and receptor are specified.

5. Every adenine derivative activates cytokinin receptors

The appropriate N6 side chain and molecular form matter. Adenine, nucleotides and active cytokinin are distinct; check the chemical state.

6. IPT and LOG are interchangeable

IPT introduces a side chain; LOG releases free base from monophosphate. Both increase potential supply but a precursor-versus-free-base rescue separates them.

7. All conjugation is irreversible breakdown

O-glucosides can in some contexts be hydrolyzed. The term inactive describes current receptor activity, not permanent chemical destruction.

8. CKX cleaves all cytokinin types equally

Substrate preferences differ; DHZ and many aromatics are relatively resistant. Compare the side-chain chemistry rather than treating all hormones as equivalent.

9. Xylem concentration equals delivery

Delivery is concentration times flow. Concentration is easy to observe, but flux determines quantity per time. Calculate both if flow changes.

10. ABCG14 is the only cytokinin transporter

It contributes to specific systemic distribution; several proteins and pathways influence local and systemic transport. A transport phenotype does not establish exclusivity.

11. AHK is a typical animal receptor tyrosine kinase

Canonical cytokinin perception uses hybrid His kinase machinery. Membrane localization alone does not identify the phosphorylation chemistry.

12. The hormone itself is transferred from AHK to ARR

The phosphoryl group, supplied by ATP, is transferred. Ligand binding triggers the relay. Label the arrows with the entity that actually moves.

13. Every AHP is a positive relay protein

AHP6 lacks the conserved histidine and can inhibit signaling. A similar name is not proof of biochemical function.

14. All ARR proteins activate cytokinin responses

Type-B ARRs commonly activate transcription; type-A ARRs commonly provide negative feedback. Class membership is essential to predict mutant output.

15. A reporter is a direct concentration sensor

TCSn and ARR promoter reporters record transcriptional output. Receptor abundance and feedback can uncouple fluorescence from concentration.

16. High cytokinin-to-auxin ratio guarantees shoots

This is a context-specific culture tendency. Absolute dose, competence and treatment history matter. Compare both concentrations and explant conditions.

17. Greener leaves necessarily have higher yield

Pigment retention does not establish carbon fixation or reproductive allocation. Use functional measures and actual yield evidence.

18. EdU labeling proves completed cell division

EdU records DNA synthesis, including possible endoreduplication. Use cell counts and mitotic/cytokinetic measures to assess completed proliferation.

Part B: foundational and mechanistic understanding

1. Which structural description best identifies most natural isoprenoid cytokinins?

  1. N6-substituted adenines
  2. Carotenoid cleavage products
  3. Indole carboxylic acids
  4. Sterol derivatives
Answer and explanation

A — N6-substituted adenines. iP and zeatin have a modified adenine N6 side chain. ABA and strigolactones are carotenoid-derived, IAA is an indole auxin, and brassinosteroids are sterol-derived.

2. Which two molecules differ in geometry around a side-chain double bond?

  1. iP and iP ribotide
  2. cis-zeatin and trans-zeatin
  3. Zeatin and its O-glucoside
  4. Adenine and adenosine
Answer and explanation

B — cis-zeatin and trans-zeatin. The cis/trans pair has the same connectivity but different double-bond geometry. The other pairs differ in sugar, phosphate or chemical substitution.

3. What is the principal function of adenylate IPT?

  1. Degrade zeatin
  2. Transfer phosphate to ARR
  3. Add an isoprenoid group to an adenine nucleotide
  4. Remove phosphoribose from cytokinin ribotide
Answer and explanation

C — Add an isoprenoid group to an adenine nucleotide. IPT initiates de novo isoprenoid cytokinin synthesis. CKX cleaves susceptible side chains; AHP transfers phosphate; LOG activates monophosphate precursors.

4. Which enzyme converts iP-type nucleotides into tZ-type nucleotides?

  1. LOG
  2. CKX
  3. AHP
  4. CYP735A
Answer and explanation

D — CYP735A. CYP735A hydroxylates the side chain. LOG releases a free base, CKX degrades susceptible substrates and AHP transfers phosphate.

5. What is the immediate product of LOG activity on tZRMP?

  1. Free trans-zeatin
  2. Trans-zeatin O-glucoside
  3. Adenine
  4. Phosphorylated ARR
Answer and explanation

A — Free trans-zeatin. LOG removes the phosphoribose group from the cytokinin riboside monophosphate, producing the free cytokinin base. Glycosylation, CKX cleavage and phosphorelay are distinct processes.

6. Which statement best distinguishes a reversible O-glucoside from a CKX degradation product?

  1. Both are necessarily active ligands
  2. O-glucoside may release intact hormone on hydrolysis
  3. CKX creates an O-glucoside
  4. Neither undergoes enzymatic conversion
Answer and explanation

B — O-glucoside may release intact hormone on hydrolysis. Certain O-glucosides conserve the cytokinin scaffold and can be hydrolyzed; CKX destroys the susceptible side chain. Neither should be assumed to be an active ligand.

7. Which type of compound is often relatively resistant to typical CKX side-chain cleavage?

  1. Free iP
  2. Free trans-zeatin
  3. Dihydrozeatin
  4. Every cytokinin nucleotide equally
Answer and explanation

C — Dihydrozeatin. The saturated side chain of DHZ confers resistance to many CKX enzymes; iP and tZ have susceptible unsaturated side chains. Substrate preference is isoform-dependent.

8. Which vascular association is generally appropriate in model plants?

  1. tZ-type compounds travel only through phloem
  2. iP is synthesized exclusively in roots
  3. All cytokinins move only in xylem
  4. tZ-type root-to-shoot signals often travel in xylem
Answer and explanation

D — tZ-type root-to-shoot signals often travel in xylem. Xylem commonly carries root-derived tZ-related compounds; iP-related compounds are often associated with phloem. These are tendencies, not exclusive routes.

9. Which protein contributes importantly to root-to-shoot cytokinin distribution in Arabidopsis?

  1. ABCG14
  2. DELLA
  3. TIR1
  4. PHYB
Answer and explanation

A — ABCG14. ABCG14 contributes to vascular cytokinin transport. DELLA is a GA repressor, TIR1 an auxin receptor component and PHYB a photoreceptor.

10. Which proteins are canonical Arabidopsis cytokinin receptors?

  1. AUX1 and PIN1
  2. AHK2, AHK3 and AHK4
  3. ARR5 and ARR7
  4. GID1 and BRI1
Answer and explanation

B — AHK2, AHK3 and AHK4. AHK2/3/4 are hybrid histidine kinase receptors. AUX1/PIN participate in auxin transport; ARR5/7 are response regulators; GID1 and BRI1 recognize other hormones.

11. Which order describes the canonical cytokinin phosphorelay residues?

  1. Ser–Thr–Tyr–Ser
  2. Asp–Asp–His–His
  3. His–Asp–His–Asp
  4. Tyr–His–Ser–Asp
Answer and explanation

C — His–Asp–His–Asp. The AHK kinase His transfers to AHK receiver Asp, then AHP His, then ARR Asp. The other orders do not describe this relay.

12. Which function characterizes type-B ARRs?

  1. Cytokinin synthesis
  2. Cleavage of cytokinin side chains
  3. Vascular water conduction
  4. Transcriptional activation of cytokinin-responsive genes
Answer and explanation

D — Transcriptional activation of cytokinin-responsive genes. Type-B ARRs are response-regulator transcription factors; IPT and CKX act in metabolism, and xylem conduits carry water.

13. Which class commonly mediates negative feedback after cytokinin induction?

  1. Type-A ARRs
  2. Type-B ARRs as a class
  3. PIN proteins as cytokinin receptors
  4. LOG enzymes as phosphatases
Answer and explanation

A — Type-A ARRs. Many type-A ARRs are induced by cytokinin and restrain signaling. Type-B ARRs usually promote transcriptional output; PIN and LOG have distinct functions.

14. Why is AHP6 called a pseudo-phosphotransfer protein?

  1. It is a glycosyltransferase
  2. It lacks the canonical phosphoaccepting histidine
  3. It is an auxin receptor
  4. It contains no amino acids
Answer and explanation

B — It lacks the canonical phosphoaccepting histidine. AHP6 lacks the conserved His needed for the ordinary relay and can inhibit signaling. It is still a protein and is not an auxin receptor or metabolic glycosyltransferase.

15. Which protein family links cytokinin to G1/S regulation?

  1. Rubisco
  2. Aquaporins
  3. D-type cyclins, including CYCD3
  4. Light-harvesting chlorophyll proteins
Answer and explanation

C — D-type cyclins, including CYCD3. CYCD3 induction contributes to the control of cell cycle entry. The other proteins have metabolic, water transport or light-harvesting roles.

16. What is WUSCHEL's central role in the shoot apex?

  1. Oxidative degradation of cytokinin
  2. Nitrate reduction
  3. Xylem loading of tZ riboside
  4. Maintenance of shoot stem cell identity through organizing-center activity
Answer and explanation

D — Maintenance of shoot stem cell identity through organizing-center activity. WUS is a developmental transcription factor, not a cytokinin metabolic enzyme or transporter. It supports a stem cell niche.

17. Which gene is induced by ARR1 in the classic Arabidopsis root differentiation model?

  1. SHY2/IAA3
  2. RBCS
  3. GID1
  4. ACS
Answer and explanation

A — SHY2/IAA3. ARR1 promotes SHY2, which influences auxin response and PIN expression. The others encode unrelated photosynthetic or other hormone components.

18. In a competent classic tobacco culture, a relatively high cytokinin-to-auxin balance commonly favors what?

  1. Only root initiation
  2. Shoot formation
  3. An inevitable arrest of all proliferation
  4. No developmental change under any conditions
Answer and explanation

B — Shoot formation. A cytokinin-rich balance can promote shoots; auxin-rich conditions often favor roots. Absolute dose, tissue and history prevent universal predictions.

19. Which treatment commonly stimulates root cytokinin production in Arabidopsis?

  1. Complete absence of all energy sources
  2. Direct removal of all receptors
  3. Nitrate resupply
  4. Conversion of every cytokinin to adenine
Answer and explanation

C — Nitrate resupply. Nitrate can induce IPT-dependent synthesis, contributing to systemic nutrient communication. Receptor loss or complete energy deprivation does not represent this induction mechanism.

20. What does the senescence-associated SAG12::IPT strategy accomplish?

  1. Constitutive degradation of all chlorophyll
  2. Inhibition of IPT synthesis
  3. Direct enzymatic synthesis of chlorophyll by IPT
  4. Senescence-linked cytokinin production that can delay further senescence
Answer and explanation

D — Senescence-linked cytokinin production that can delay further senescence. The promoter responds to senescence and drives cytokinin biosynthesis; the resulting hormone can delay the process. IPT is not a chlorophyll enzyme.

Part C: experimental, quantitative and integrated reasoning

21. A mutant has high root tZ-related pools, low shoot pools and small shoots; direct tZ supplied to the shoot improves growth. Which defect is best supported?

  1. AHK receptors cannot bind tZ
  2. Root-to-shoot distribution is impaired
  3. All cytokinin synthesis is absent
  4. Shoot WUS is permanently absent
Answer and explanation

B — Root-to-shoot distribution is impaired. The source retains hormone while the destination is deficient, and shoot application bypasses delivery. Receptor failure or permanent downstream loss would make rescue less likely; high root pools contradict total biosynthetic absence.

22. A LOG-deficient tissue is supplied with free tZ or tZRMP separately. Only free tZ restores a reporter under the stated conditions. What is the best inference?

  1. LOG is needed for efficient precursor activation
  2. LOG is a DNA-binding ARR
  3. Free tZ must be converted into tZRMP before perception
  4. LOG is the xylem water channel
Answer and explanation

A — LOG is needed for efficient precursor activation. Free tZ bypasses the impaired activation step. A precursor requires conversion, so its weaker response supports the LOG model. The result does not identify LOG as receptor, transcription factor or channel.

23. In a simple steady-state model, production is 18 nM h⁻¹ and removal coefficient 0.6 h⁻¹. A treatment halves the removal coefficient. What steady-state value follows if input is unchanged?

  1. 15 nM
  2. 30 nM
  3. 60 nM
  4. 10.8 nM
Answer and explanation

C — 60 nM. The model gives C=S/k. Initially 18/0.6=30 nM; after treatment 18/0.3=60 nM. The other values confuse initial and final values or multiply rates.

24. Xylem concentration rises from 20 to 40 nM while flow falls from 0.5 to 0.125 mL h⁻¹. What happens to delivery?

  1. It doubles
  2. It is unchanged
  3. It increases fourfold
  4. It falls to half
Answer and explanation

D — It falls to half. Initial delivery is 20×0.0005=0.010 nmol/h; final is 40×0.000125=0.005 nmol/h. A rise in concentration does not imply a rise in flux.

25. A receptor binds ligand with hypothetical Kd=5 nM. At 15 nM free ligand, what is predicted occupancy?

  1. 75%
  2. 25%
  3. 50%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

A — 75%. Occupancy is 15/(5+15)=0.75. This simple binding model does not establish the percentage of maximal organ growth.

26. A type-A arr multiple mutant displays normal early reporter induction but prolonged high output after cytokinin. What is the best explanation?

  1. All IPT activity is absent
  2. Loss of feedback inhibition
  3. AHP proteins now transport nitrate
  4. Type-B ARR transcription factors are necessarily missing
Answer and explanation

B — Loss of feedback inhibition. The preserved early response suggests intact positive signaling; prolonged activity is consistent with weaker negative feedback. Loss of biosynthesis, phosphotransfer or type-B activity would not explain the pattern as directly.

27. An AHK mutant retains ligand binding and autophosphorylation but lacks the receiver-domain phosphoacceptor Asp. What should be impaired most directly?

  1. CKX cleavage of hormone
  2. Synthesis of DMAPP
  3. Transfer from receptor kinase through receiver domain to AHP
  4. The presence of all type-A genes in DNA
Answer and explanation

C — Transfer from receptor kinase through receiver domain to AHP. The receptor's Asp is a required intermediate in the canonical relay. Upstream binding and kinase activation can persist; the mutation does not delete genes or directly alter cytokinin synthesis.

28. An AHP protein contains a substitution at its conserved histidine and cannot receive phosphate. Which early downstream event is likely reduced?

  1. IPT side-chain transfer itself
  2. O-glucoside hydrolysis itself
  3. ATP production in all cells
  4. Phosphorylation of cytokinin-responsive ARRs through that AHP
Answer and explanation

D — Phosphorylation of cytokinin-responsive ARRs through that AHP. Loss of the AHP phosphoacceptor interrupts the transfer to ARRs. Other AHPs can compensate; the mutation does not directly block metabolic steps.

29. A shoot apex contains high free cytokinin but weak TCSn reporter output after receptor disruption. What is justified?

  1. High hormone concentration does not ensure high signaling output
  2. TCSn directly measures chemical cytokinin concentration
  3. CKX must be absent
  4. The reporter proves total cytokinin is low
Answer and explanation

A — High hormone concentration does not ensure high signaling output. A reporter measures transcriptional output downstream of perception. A receptor lesion can uncouple concentration from response; CKX status cannot be inferred from the reporter alone.

30. A mutant expressing constitutive CYCD3 shows proliferation without exogenous cytokinin in a culture assay but fails to form normal shoots. Which conclusion fits?

  1. CYCD3 must be the cytokinin receptor
  2. Cell-cycle entry and meristem organization are separable
  3. All cytokinin effects are mediated by CYCD3
  4. Cytokinin cannot affect cell division
Answer and explanation

B — Cell-cycle entry and meristem organization are separable. A downstream cyclin can bypass some requirements for cell cycle entry without restoring positional regulators such as WUS. The other options overstate or contradict the evidence.

31. ARR1 overexpression shrinks the root meristem in wild type but not in a shy2 loss-of-function background. What does this support?

  1. SHY2 acts upstream of all AHK receptors
  2. ARR1 has no developmental function
  3. SHY2 is required downstream for this ARR1-dependent phenotype
  4. SHY2 is a cytokinin transporter
Answer and explanation

C — SHY2 is required downstream for this ARR1-dependent phenotype. Loss of the proposed downstream mediator suppresses the upstream perturbation's phenotype. The experiment supports dependency for this response, not every ARR1 target.

32. A root meristem adds 15 net cells/h and loses 15 cells/h to differentiation. A cytokinin treatment changes only exit to 20 cells/h. What is the immediate change?

  1. +5 cells/h
  2. −20 cells/h
  3. 0 cells/h
  4. −5 cells/h
Answer and explanation

D — −5 cells/h. The population balance is production minus exit: 15−20=−5 cells/h. Reduced meristem size can reflect faster differentiation even without reduced division.

33. A cytokinin-treated leaf contains 30% more area but unchanged total epidermal cell count. What is most directly supported?

  1. Greater mean projected cell area
  2. Increased cell count
  3. All cells entered mitosis
  4. WUS is necessarily overexpressed
Answer and explanation

A — Greater mean projected cell area. Area divided by cell number estimates mean projected area under comparable sampling. No evidence establishes greater proliferation or the identity of a particular regulator.

34. Two media contain auxin:cytokinin at 1:1, but doses are 0.05 and 5 μM respectively. Which prediction is warranted?

  1. They must induce identical organ numbers
  2. Equal ratios do not ensure equal responses
  3. The high-dose medium must always produce roots
  4. The low-dose medium cannot support any signaling
Answer and explanation

B — Equal ratios do not ensure equal responses. Absolute ligand concentration affects occupancy, metabolism and toxicity; a ratio alone does not determine outcome. Tissue competence also matters.

35. Thirty of 40 explants regenerate under A, producing 90 shoots. Twenty of 40 regenerate under B, producing 100 shoots. Which comparison is correct?

  1. A has higher mean shoots per responding explant
  2. B has higher regeneration frequency
  3. A has higher regeneration frequency but B has more shoots per responding explant
  4. Both have equal regeneration frequency
Answer and explanation

C — A has higher regeneration frequency but B has more shoots per responding explant. A: 75% and 3 shoots/responding explant. B: 50% and 5 shoots/responding explant. Frequency and multiplicity measure different outcomes.

36. A CKX-overexpressing line responds strongly to a resistant aromatic cytokinin but weakly to a susceptible isoprenoid ligand. Which explanation fits best?

  1. The receptor is absent in both treatments
  2. The aromatic compound is necessarily an auxin
  3. CKX phosphorylates ARR directly
  4. Differential hormone degradation can alter effective dose
Answer and explanation

D — Differential hormone degradation can alter effective dose. CKX specificity changes ligand persistence; the strong response to one cytokinin suggests some signaling competence. Receptor preferences may also contribute, so measure active pools.

37. A nitrate-starved plant receives active cytokinin. A shoot reporter rises, but biomass does not increase substantially. What is the best interpretation?

  1. Signaling can be restored without replacing missing nitrogen resources
  2. Cytokinin is a nitrogen fertilizer at normal signaling dose
  3. The reporter is invalid solely because biomass does not increase
  4. All nitrate assimilation enzymes must be normal
Answer and explanation

A — Signaling can be restored without replacing missing nitrogen resources. The reporter demonstrates a regulatory output, not sufficient nitrogen substrate for new biomass. Long-term growth requires resources and additional processes.

38. Leaves retain 1.2 mg chlorophyll/g after treatment, compared with 0.4 in controls; both began at 2.0. What are the retention fractions?

  1. 30% versus 10%
  2. 60% versus 20%
  3. 120% versus 40%
  4. 80% versus 40%
Answer and explanation

B — 60% versus 20%. Divide endpoint by initial concentration: 1.2/2.0=60%, 0.4/2.0=20%. Pigment retention alone does not prove retained photosynthetic function.

39. If SAG12-driven IPT expression delays senescence, what feedback relation can follow?

  1. Senescence continually accelerates itself
  2. IPT becomes a cytokinin receptor
  3. Delayed senescence can reduce the initiating promoter activity
  4. The construct necessarily expresses at maximum in every root
Answer and explanation

C — Delayed senescence can reduce the initiating promoter activity. The promoter is linked to a developmental state; hormone production suppresses that state, potentially reducing promoter activity. It is not a constitutive expression system.

40. A wild-type rootstock rescues much of a mutant shoot phenotype, but cytokinin was not measured. Which claim is strongest?

  1. ABCG14 is conclusively the only causal gene
  2. A mobile cytokinin molecule is conclusively identified
  3. No root-derived signal is involved
  4. Rootstock function influences the shoot phenotype; the mobile signal's identity remains to be tested
Answer and explanation

D — Rootstock function influences the shoot phenotype; the mobile signal's identity remains to be tested. Grafting demonstrates a systemic influence but does not uniquely identify the compound or transporter. Water, nutrient and additional signaling differences must be considered.

41. A treatment doubles the EdU-labeled fraction without increasing completed cell divisions. Which interpretation remains possible?

  1. Increased DNA synthesis including endoreduplication or delayed progression
  2. Every labeled cell has completed cytokinesis
  3. AHP6 must be overexpressed
  4. DNA synthesis has stopped
Answer and explanation

A — Increased DNA synthesis including endoreduplication or delayed progression. EdU detects DNA replication, not completed division. Endoreduplication and phase-duration changes can raise labeling without adding daughter cells.

42. A treatment increases primary root length from 5 to 8 cm while lateral root number remains 20. How does lateral root density change?

  1. Increases from 2.5 to 4 per cm
  2. Decreases from 4 to 2.5 per cm
  3. Remains 4 per cm
  4. Increases to 20 per cm
Answer and explanation

B — Decreases from 4 to 2.5 per cm. Density equals number divided by length. 20/5=4 and 20/8=2.5. Absolute initiation count is unchanged, so density cannot establish fewer initiation events.

43. A tissue has 100 pmol total cytokinin with 5 pmol free base; another has 60 pmol total and 15 pmol free base. If volume, access and receptor properties match, which is more likely to have greater immediate ligand input?

  1. The first because total is greater
  2. Both must be identical
  3. The second because free base is greater
  4. Neither can contain an active ligand
Answer and explanation

C — The second because free base is greater. For the stated matching conditions, the second has three times the free-base amount. The total pool may include weakly active or inactive species.

44. A researcher measures only shoot cytokinin after nitrate treatment and finds a decrease. Which additional measurements best distinguish reduced production from altered delivery?

  1. Only chlorophyll color
  2. Only flower number
  3. Only root length
  4. Root and shoot cytokinin forms, sap flux and a tracer or genetic transport test
Answer and explanation

D — Root and shoot cytokinin forms, sap flux and a tracer or genetic transport test. The additional measures separate source pools from transport and target availability. Phenotypes alone cannot distinguish those mechanisms.

45. A type-B arr mutant has poor WUS induction. Forced WUS expression restores a shoot stem-cell marker but not leaf longevity. What is supported?

  1. WUS is downstream for the rescued meristem output, not all cytokinin responses
  2. WUS is the universal CKX enzyme
  3. WUS is necessary for every cytokinin action without exception
  4. Cytokinin signaling is unrelated to WUS
Answer and explanation

A — WUS is downstream for the rescued meristem output, not all cytokinin responses. Selective rescue is evidence of a downstream role in a specified developmental context. The failure to restore another response reinforces pathway branching.

Assertion–reason and SET-style applications

46. Assertion: cytokinin can reduce Arabidopsis root meristem size. Reason: cytokinin can promote differentiation through ARR1–SHY2 regulation.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A — Both true; reason explains assertion. Faster exit from the dividing population can reduce meristem size. The stated mechanism explains the observation in the relevant context.

47. Assertion: a high total cytokinin pool always means a high free-base concentration. Reason: cytokinin can occur as ribotides and glycosylated forms.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D — Assertion false; reason true. Total includes chemically diverse species, so the free fraction can vary independently. The reason is true and explains why the assertion fails.

48. A student wants to distinguish delayed pigment loss from maintenance of photosynthesis. Which comparison is best?

  1. Measure color only
  2. Measure pigment plus gas exchange and appropriate fluorescence parameters
  3. Measure root hair number only
  4. Measure total DNA only
Answer and explanation

B — Measure pigment plus gas exchange and appropriate fluorescence parameters. Combining pigment measurements with physiological photosynthesis measures is more informative than color alone. Root hairs and DNA do not test leaf function directly.

49. A culture is treated with cytokinin dissolved in a small amount of solvent. Which negative control is essential?

  1. A culture lacking all mineral nutrients
  2. A culture of unrelated species with different age
  3. Matched medium with the same solvent but no added cytokinin
  4. A culture without any water
Answer and explanation

C — Matched medium with the same solvent but no added cytokinin. The solvent-only control separates cytokinin action from the vehicle. Other groups introduce additional variables.

50. A plant has 0.4 g dry root mass and 0.8 g dry shoot mass before treatment; afterward root and shoot masses are 0.6 and 1.8 g. What is the correct allocation interpretation?

  1. Root mass decreased
  2. Root-to-shoot ratio increased
  3. Shoot mass decreased
  4. Both increased, but root-to-shoot ratio fell from 0.5 to 0.33
Answer and explanation

D — Both increased, but root-to-shoot ratio fell from 0.5 to 0.33. The ratio falls because the shoot grows proportionally more. Absolute root growth does not contradict a lower relative allocation.
