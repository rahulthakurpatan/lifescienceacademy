1. Why movement is essential even in a stationary cell

A living cell can remain attached to a tissue throughout its lifetime and nevertheless be constantly moving on the inside. Vesicles leave the Golgi apparatus, damaged components travel towards degradative compartments, and mitochondria redistribute when the demand for ATP changes. A white blood cell changes its outline and crawls towards a chemical signal. A respiratory epithelial cell remains fixed in its tissue but beats its cilia to move a layer of mucus. These observations raise one central question: how does a cell turn molecular events into organized mechanical displacement?

Small dissolved molecules can move by thermal diffusion. Their random movements are useful over short distances, and no molecular motor is required merely to allow them to spread. But a large vesicle in crowded cytoplasm must reach a particular destination, often many micrometres away. Diffusion has no intrinsic preference for that destination. Similarly, a cell must do mechanical work against resistance to change its shape or move surrounding liquid. Molecular motors and dynamic cytoskeletal polymers provide complementary solutions to these problems.

Three filament systems were introduced in the preceding chapter. Actin filaments are polar polymers, microtubules are polar tubes constructed from tubulin, and intermediate filaments form generally nonpolar structures that resist mechanical stress. Actin and microtubules provide directional tracks for motor proteins. Intermediate filaments provide resilience during deformation but do not serve as conventional ATP-powered walking-motor tracks. Their mechanical function remains vital; a filament does not need to support motor walking to be useful.

Distinguish three forms of motion before proceeding. A motor may move a cargo along a relatively stationary track. A motor attached to a fixed structure may instead move the track itself. Finally, growth of a filament can exert force against a barrier without a walking motor at its growing end. In all three cases an object changes position, but the underlying mechanisms and experimental predictions are different. A moving fluorescent spot alone does not identify the source of force.

Transport also differs from membrane fusion. A vesicle that has reached its destination still requires the correct tethering and fusion machinery to deliver its contents. Motors can improve the speed and spatial organization of trafficking but cannot replace the specificity of membrane recognition. This distinction links the preceding chapters on organelles and membrane sorting with the mechanical principles developed here.

2. Track polarity and the reference frame

Motor direction depends on the asymmetric molecular arrangement of the track. In actin, subunits are assembled in a common orientation, producing a plus or barbed end and a minus or pointed end. Under many assembly conditions the barbed end grows more rapidly. These names refer to structural differences, not electrical charges. Classical microscopy of myosin-decorated actin revealed arrowhead patterns whose tips point towards the pointed end. Such structural markers allow polarity to be established independently of motor motion.

Microtubules are assembled from alpha-beta tubulin heterodimers aligned head-to-tail into protofilaments. A typical cytoplasmic microtubule has thirteen protofilaments arranged into a hollow cylinder. Beta-tubulin faces the plus end in the idealized lattice, whereas alpha-tubulin faces the minus end. The repeated asymmetric structure provides a directional reference to a motor domain. In many animal cells, microtubules are arranged radially with minus ends near a centrosome and plus ends pointing outwards. Other arrangements occur in differentiated epithelia, dendrites and plant cells, so the geographical direction of movement cannot be inferred from motor identity alone.

Imagine two microtubules oriented in opposite directions. The same conventional plus-end kinesin moves towards opposite ends of the cell when it switches from one track to the other. No change in its ATPase or intrinsic polarity preference is necessary. An examination question may deliberately reverse the orientation shown in a diagram. First identify the plus and minus ends; only then decide whether movement is towards the nucleus, cell cortex, axon terminal or another landmark.

The polarity of a track does not require that it be growing. A stabilized microtubule can support motor walking, and relatively stable actin can support myosin movement. Tubulin GTP hydrolysis affects microtubule assembly and dynamics; ATP hydrolysis by kinesin or dynein drives their motor cycles. Similarly, ATP associated with an actin subunit is not the same ATP molecule hydrolyzed by myosin. Confusing the nucleotide cycle of a polymer with that of its associated motor obscures the origin of the mechanical energy.

A motor usually prefers a direction under physiological conditions, but its movement is not a rigid sequence of irreversible steps. Thermal fluctuations, opposing loads, obstacles and detachment can interrupt or reverse individual steps. Directionality is a bias in the coupled mechanochemical cycle. To interpret experiments it is equally important to specify whether the motor or the filament is moving relative to the laboratory surface. An anchored plus-end-directed motor tends to displace a freely moving track in the opposite direction.

3. Mechanochemical coupling: how ATP creates movement

A motor is a flexible enzyme rather than a rigid mechanical machine. Its nucleotide-binding site communicates with regions that attach to a filament and regions that generate mechanical displacement. ATP binding, hydrolysis, phosphate release and ADP release change the motor's conformational preferences and affinity for its track. Because the track is polar, these changes can favor attachment at a different binding site and bias the next displacement. Different motor families implement this principle in different ways; a universal sequence of identical power strokes would be an oversimplification.

At thermal equilibrium a protein can fluctuate between conformations but cannot continuously extract directed mechanical work from those fluctuations alone. The cell maintains ATP and its hydrolysis products away from equilibrium by metabolism. The free-energy decrease associated with ATP hydrolysis can therefore drive a repeated cycle of preferential binding, displacement and release. Some of the available free energy is dissipated. A polar track supplies directional information but does not supply energy merely by being asymmetric.

A useful motor must attach strongly at some stages and release at others. Permanent attachment prevents repeated stepping, while uncontrolled detachment allows the cargo to escape. Processivity describes the ability to perform successive steps before dissociating. The duty ratio describes the fraction of a cycle spent strongly associated with the track under a defined set of conditions. These concepts are related but not identical. A two-headed cargo motor often coordinates the states of its two heads to maintain attachment. Many low-duty-ratio motors can instead cooperate in an ensemble, so the collective assembly remains effective despite brief attachment by each individual head.

Load influences kinetic transitions. An opposing force often slows the net velocity of a transport motor. At stall, the average velocity is zero under the measurement conditions but the motor may still exert force. It may also continue microscopic transitions and ATP turnover depending on the system. Zero net translational displacement means zero net external translational work during that interval, not necessarily the absence of chemical activity. The observed stall force is therefore a kinetic and mechanical property, not simply the maximum force allowed by thermodynamics.

The external work of a step against a constant opposing load is the product of force and distance along its direction. The chemical energy consumed per cycle sets an upper bound on this work for a sustained cycle. The actual relationship depends on the number of ATP molecules consumed, motor efficiency, microscopic stepping and load. Later calculations use a conventional one-ATP-per-step model for kinesin-1; it should not be generalized to all motors without additional evidence.

4. Myosin: force and movement on actin

Myosins possess a motor domain that binds actin and hydrolyzes ATP, a neck or lever-arm region, and a tail that participates in cargo binding, membrane association or filament formation. Light chains often stabilize or regulate the lever arm. A small change in the motor domain can be amplified into movement of the lever arm. The architecture explains how related motor domains can perform very different cellular jobs when connected to different tails.

The conventional myosin-II cycle begins, for teaching purposes, with a nucleotide-free motor head strongly bound to actin. Binding ATP decreases its actin affinity and promotes release. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and phosphate primes the motor in a higher-energy conformation. The primed head reattaches to an actin site, and strong binding with phosphate release is coupled to force generation and a change in lever-arm orientation, represented as the power stroke. ADP release returns the motor to a strongly bound state. The timing of microscopic intermediates depends on conditions, but the sequence establishes the essential causal relations between nucleotide state, binding and force.

ATP binding thus does not maintain conventional myosin in its rigor attachment. Instead, it allows detachment. When ATP becomes unavailable and motors finish cycling in strongly bound nucleotide-free states, actin and myosin can remain locked together. Rigor is therefore not active rapid contraction. Continuous force production through repeated cross-bridge cycling requires fresh ATP and appropriate regulatory conditions.

Myosin II has two motor heads and a tail that permits assembly into bipolar filaments. The heads at opposite ends of an assembly engage oppositely oriented actin filaments. The heads generally walk towards barbed ends relative to actin; if the barbed ends face outward, the relative motion slides actin filaments towards one another. Filaments need not become shorter for the distance between their attachments to decrease. This principle explains actin stress fibers, contractile cortical structures and the cytokinetic ring in nonmuscle cells. Detailed sarcomere physiology is reserved for the animal physiology unit.

Myosin I often interacts with membranes and cortical actin and is involved in local membrane deformation or tension control. Myosin V is adapted for processive movement of selected cargoes. Its long lever arms and coordinated heads allow representative myosin Va molecules to advance approximately 36 nm per center-of-mass step along actin in appropriate assays. This is not a universal step length for the myosin family. Myosin VI is a notable exception to the usual plus-end-directed movement of myosins; it moves towards the pointed end and participates in specialized cellular trafficking.

Regulation connects myosin activity to cellular demand. In nonmuscle myosin II, regulatory light-chain phosphorylation generally increases motor activity and favors formation of contractile filaments. Calcium–calmodulin-dependent kinase and Rho-associated signaling can participate in this regulation in different contexts. Restricting activation to a particular cortical region allows force to be generated locally rather than across the entire cell. A mutation affecting myosin assembly can reduce contractility even if individual isolated heads still hydrolyze ATP. A mutation affecting the motor domain can produce the reverse pattern: structurally assembled filaments with defective force generation.

A useful comparison is between contraction and cargo transport. A single myosin-II head may remain strongly attached for a relatively small fraction of its cycle, but many heads in a filament act together. A processive myosin V needs to retain its cargo's association with a track over multiple steps. Both are ATP-dependent, yet the kinetics suitable for collective force production differ from those suitable for a long uninterrupted cargo run.

5. Kinesin: stepping along microtubules

Conventional kinesin-1 is typically a dimer with two globular motor domains, short neck linkers, a coiled-coil stalk and a cargo-binding region. Accessory chains and adaptor proteins can contribute to cargo recognition. Each head recognizes the microtubule lattice and possesses a nucleotide-binding site. The neck linker helps bias movement toward a new position on the track, while the tail transmits the displacement to a cargo. Thus the motor domain defines the mechanism of motion and the cargo-binding apparatus helps define the biological destination.

In the simplified hand-over-hand cycle, one head is bound to the microtubule while the partner is positioned to search for a forward site. ATP-dependent neck-linker rearrangement biases the partner head toward the plus end. The new head establishes contact and releases ADP; the trailing head passes through hydrolysis and product-release transitions and detaches as the cycle proceeds. The heads exchange roles. Their coordinated kinetics reduce the probability that both detach together. This explains why a conventional kinesin can walk for many steps before releasing its cargo from the track.

Under typical low-load conditions, conventional kinesin-1 advances about 8 nm per net step, corresponding to the repeat distance of alpha-beta tubulin heterodimers along a protofilament. In the hand-over-hand picture the advancing individual head moves approximately 16 nm past its partner; the midpoint advances approximately 8 nm. A student who uses the individual head displacement instead of the cargo displacement will underestimate the number of cycles by a factor of two. The 8 nm step is also not the same as the approximately 25 nm outer diameter of a typical microtubule.

Run length describes how far the motor typically travels before dissociation, not how fast it walks. A cargo with two or more motors may remain attached longer or support greater load, yet it does not necessarily travel at two or three times the velocity of a single motor. Velocity depends on kinetic transitions, opposing force and coordination. Distinguishing speed from processivity is essential when a mutation reduces the duration or frequency of successful cargo runs but leaves their instantaneous velocity unchanged.

The kinesin superfamily includes different functional specializations. Kinesin-1, kinesin-2 and kinesin-3 include prominent plus-end cargo motors, whereas many kinesin-14 members move towards minus ends. Kinesin-13 proteins are important microtubule depolymerizing proteins rather than typical long-distance cargo walkers. Kinesin-5 can engage two microtubules and contribute to relative sliding in the mitotic spindle. Detailed chromosome movement belongs to cell division. Here these examples demonstrate that a family name does not uniquely determine function or direction.

Motor recruitment and activation are also regulated. Some kinesins are autoinhibited when no appropriate cargo is present. Cargo-associated adaptors can relieve this inhibition and support transport. A tail defect might therefore prevent vesicle movement while leaving the purified motor domain fully competent to walk. The distinction between catalytic function and coupling to cargo makes such mutants particularly informative in experimental reasoning.

6. Dynein: minus-end movement and force generation

Dynein is an ATP-dependent microtubule motor with a structure distinct from kinesin. A dynein heavy chain contains a prominent ring of AAA+ ATPase modules, a force-transmitting linker, a stalk ending in a microtubule-binding domain, and a tail that associates with other subunits or anchoring structures. Nucleotide-dependent conformational changes in the ring affect the position of the linker and the affinity of the microtubule-binding tip. The motor can thereby generate displacement through a cycle of binding, release and conformational change without using the kinesin neck-linker architecture.

Cytoplasmic dynein-1 normally moves towards the minus ends of microtubules. In a radial animal cell this can carry endosomes and other organelles towards the central cytoplasm and helps maintain the position of the Golgi apparatus. If its tail is connected to a stationary cortex rather than a mobile cargo, dynein can pull on the microtubule array itself. The resulting mechanical effect depends on what is attached to what, even though the motor's polarity preference is unchanged.

Efficient processive movement by mammalian cytoplasmic dynein-1 often depends on dynactin and a cargo-specific activating adaptor. Dynactin is not a second ATP-powered walking motor. It helps arrange the dynein complex into an active configuration and provides an interface for transport-associated factors. A cell with functional dynein and intact microtubules may therefore show impaired directional transport if a necessary adaptor is absent. Conversely, increasing the abundance of dynein alone may not rescue a defect in cargo recruitment.

Dynein can exhibit variable step sizes, lateral steps and backward steps, and its heads need not alternate in the strictly idealized fashion used for conventional kinesin. Consequently, an experimentally measured net displacement cannot be converted to ATP consumption using a universal 8 nm per ATP rule for dynein. The appropriate coupling ratio must be established or specified.

Two additional dynein categories are vital for understanding cilia. Axonemal dyneins are anchored to microtubule doublets, where they generate relative sliding that is converted into bending. Cytoplasmic dynein-2 participates in the return of intraflagellar transport material towards the ciliary base. Although both use ATP and microtubule tracks, their functions differ. A mutation impairing dynein-2 can disrupt assembly or maintenance of the cilium without directly eliminating the dynein arms that produce the beat.

A simplified radial transport experiment illustrates the causal distinction. If dynein-1 is selectively impaired while plus-end motors and tracks remain functional, inward runs may fall and cargo may redistribute towards the periphery. The result is consistent with loss of minus-end transport, but a steady-state distribution also reflects anchoring, organelle maturation and regulation of other motors. Live movement trajectories are more informative than a single image of cargo localization.

7. Motor recruitment, organelle positioning and track switching

A vesicle must first be formed at a donor membrane. Its coat and membrane proteins support selection of cargo and budding. After release, transport-associated proteins can recruit cytoskeletal motors to carry it through crowded cytoplasm. Rab proteins and their effectors often help organize this recruitment. At its destination, tethering and fusion machinery establish a specific membrane interaction. These are consecutive but distinct tasks: a motor cannot automatically select a correct target membrane merely by reaching its vicinity.

Microtubules often support long-range movements between central and peripheral regions of animal cells. Near the cortex, local actin networks can support delivery by myosins. A secretory vesicle may therefore move outward on a kinesin and then switch to an actin-based system before docking and fusion. This is one common organizational strategy, not an obligatory itinerary for all vesicles. In other contexts, diffusion and capture or repeated exchanges between tracks contribute to delivery. The arrangement depends on cell architecture and cargo-specific interactions.

Cargoes can carry motors of opposing directional preference at the same time. Time-lapse recordings may reveal outward runs, pauses and inward runs. A tug-of-war model imagines motors exerting opposing forces on the cargo; this can explain some behaviors. In other situations, regulatory proteins coordinate motor activation and prevent continuous unproductive opposition. A movement trace alone cannot prove that a cargo's reversal was caused by a mechanical tug-of-war. Measuring motor occupancy, catalytic activity and the effects of selective perturbations helps discriminate competing explanations.

Axonal microtubules are predominantly plus-end-out, so conventional kinesins commonly move cargo anterogradely from the cell body and cytoplasmic dynein moves cargo retrogradely. Dendritic microtubule polarity can be mixed. In epithelial cells, noncentrosomal arrays may have distinctive orientation. These examples reinforce the reference-frame rule: plus-end and minus-end are molecular directions, whereas anterograde and retrograde describe transport relative to an anatomical arrangement.

A steady-state organelle distribution reflects delivery, departure, local formation and anchoring. If lysosomes accumulate near a nucleus after a treatment, the observation is compatible with reduced outward transport, increased inward transport or altered retention. An imaging assay measuring runs in each direction can distinguish several of these possibilities. Measuring cargo velocity alone is insufficient if the main change is the proportion of runs initiated. The cell uses motors not simply to maximize speed but to maintain the correct spatial distribution of organelles under changing demands.

Cytoskeletal transport also supports cell polarity. Local delivery of membrane proteins and organelles helps maintain specialized regions of a cell. Anchored motors exert forces on filament arrays and influence the positions of cellular structures. The same general machinery can therefore both deliver selected cargoes and help establish the organization of the tracks that support delivery.

8. Experimental evidence: motors versus tracks

Microscopy reveals organelles near cytoskeletal filaments, but proximity does not establish what generates motion. The squid giant axon supplied a classical experimental system because its axoplasm could be separated into components while preserving structures capable of movement. In 1985 Vale, Reese, Sheetz and colleagues investigated microtubule-dependent organelle transport. Single microtubules supported bidirectional movement. Fractionation and reconstruction revealed soluble motor activity, and identification of kinesin established a protein-based mechanism of directed movement. Separating proteins with opposite direction preferences showed that the microtubule need not reverse its structural polarity to support transport in both directions.

Consider the logic of a purified motility experiment. Microtubules are immobilized on a surface and motor-coated beads are observed after ATP is provided. Directed runs along the tracks, absent in suitable controls lacking motor or ATP, support the conclusion that the motor–track system is sufficient for translocation under those conditions. Marking the microtubule ends independently allows the direction preference to be determined. Beads without motor help exclude bulk flow, while a catalytic mutant helps distinguish attachment from ATP-coupled movement.

Reverse the assay geometry by attaching motors to a surface while leaving the microtubules free to move. A plus-end-directed kinesin attempts to walk towards the plus end, but because the kinesin is fixed, the microtubule glides in the opposite direction relative to the surface. Its minus end leads. This gliding assay does not establish that the motor has switched its intrinsic polarity. It shows that the measured motion depends on which object is held fixed.

A purified system can demonstrate sufficiency under defined conditions, but it cannot by itself establish the complete set of physiological cargoes or regulatory pathways in a living cell. A loss-of-function mutant may demonstrate necessity for a particular cellular behavior, but secondary effects may complicate interpretation. Combining direct motor activity with loss-of-function and rescue experiments gives a stronger causal argument. A tail mutant that still moves in a purified assay but fails to transport vesicles in cells suggests a problem in attachment or regulation rather than ATP hydrolysis itself.

Modern fluorescence imaging can measure individual cargo trajectories, run velocities and pauses. A calibrated optical trap can impose a force and record the displacement of a motor-associated bead. Electron microscopy reveals filament arrangements and axonemal structures but does not directly measure motion. Different methods answer different questions, so structural evidence must be connected to functional tests before drawing mechanistic conclusions.

9. Quantitative foundations of motor-driven transport

For unbiased diffusion in one dimension, the mean squared displacement of an ensemble is 2Dt, where D is the diffusion coefficient and t is time. In isotropic three-dimensional free diffusion it is 6Dt. These equations describe an average of squared displacements, not a guaranteed first-arrival time. Crowded cellular environments may depart from ideal free diffusion. Nevertheless, the comparison provides a useful physical baseline for understanding the advantages of directed movement.

For a motor moving steadily along a straight track with speed v, distance L is covered in time L/v. Setting the root-mean-square one-dimensional diffusive displacement equal to L gives the characteristic diffusion time L²/(2D). Doubling L doubles the motor travel time but quadruples the diffusive timescale. For an illustrative cargo with D = 0.05 µm²/s and L = 10 µm, diffusion has a characteristic timescale of 100/(2 × 0.05) = 1000 s. A motor at 1 µm/s covers the distance in 10 s. This hundredfold difference illustrates why active transport can be valuable for large cargoes over long distances; it is not a universal numerical property of cells.

If a motor takes n equal steps of length d, its net distance in the idealized forward-only case is nd. If its mean stepping rate is f, its travel time is n/f, giving v = fd. A conventional kinesin-1 moving 2.4 µm in 8 nm steps covers 2400 nm and requires 300 net steps. At 100 steps/s its velocity is 800 nm/s = 0.8 µm/s and the trip takes 3 s. Assuming one ATP per forward cycle, the ideal consumption is 300 ATP molecules. Actual ATP use can be higher if the motor pauses, reverses, cycles without progress, or operates together with additional motors. The simple displacement relation estimates a specified idealized case, not a measured cellular ATP budget.

Mechanical work against a constant opposing force F is W = Fd. One piconewton is 10⁻¹² N and one nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m, so one pN·nm equals 10⁻²¹ J. A motor taking an 8 nm step against 5 pN performs 40 pN·nm, or 4.0 × 10⁻²⁰ J, of external work. Assume ATP hydrolysis provides a free-energy magnitude of 50 kJ/mol in the given environment. Dividing 50,000 J/mol by Avogadro's constant, 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, gives 8.30 × 10⁻²⁰ J, or approximately 83 pN·nm, per ATP.

Under a one-ATP-per-step assumption, the fraction of chemical free energy captured as external work in this example is 40/83 ≈ 48%. The ideal energy-limited force would be 83/8 ≈ 10.4 pN. This is an upper bound under the assumed coupling and not a prediction of the actual stall force, which depends on kinetics and dissipation. The available free energy of ATP depends on concentrations and conditions, so the standard-state value must not be treated as an invariant physiological energy.

Power is work per unit time. For steady displacement against a constant load it is P = Fv. A motor traveling at 0.4 µm/s against 5 pN performs work at 5 × 400 = 2000 pN·nm/s = 2 × 10⁻¹⁸ W. At stall its average velocity is zero, so its net external translational power is zero despite the presence of an opposing force. This is not the same as claiming no ATP turnover.

Finally, a cargo that moves 4 µm outward and 3 µm inward over a total of 10 s travels a total distance of 7 µm but is displaced only 1 µm outward. Its average speed is 0.7 µm/s while its signed mean velocity is +0.1 µm/s if outward is positive. A treatment may decrease mean net velocity by increasing pauses or reversals without changing the speed of individual runs. These measurements reveal different aspects of motor function and should not be substituted for one another.

10. Actin polymerization, adhesion and cell migration

A migrating cell faces a problem that is mechanically different from carrying a vesicle. It must deform its boundary, couple force to its surroundings, displace the main cell body and release attachments that would otherwise restrain it. A useful sequence is polarization, protrusion, attachment, translocation and rear release. These events overlap in time and feed back on one another; the sequence is an explanatory framework rather than a clock in which one stage must finish before the next begins.

Polarization establishes a front and rear. External chemical gradients, substrate contact and internal asymmetry can bias the activity of cortical actin regulators. Small GTPases of the Rho family help organize this process. Rac is commonly associated with broad lamellipodial protrusion, Cdc42 with cell polarity and filopodial organization, and RhoA with actomyosin contractility. These characteristic associations are useful for interpreting perturbations, but they do not describe mutually exclusive pathways in every cell. A cell must spatially regulate these activities so that protrusion does not occur equally in every direction.

In a lamellipodium, a network of actin filaments forms close to the leading membrane. The Arp2/3 complex nucleates branches on existing filaments, typically at an angle of approximately 70 degrees. Nucleation-promoting proteins activate Arp2/3 locally, and many growing barbed ends face towards the membrane. Continuous addition of actin subunits creates a collective protrusive force. Capping proteins limit elongation at individual ends, and severing and disassembly recycle older filaments. A broad network rather than a single long filament distributes force across the leading edge.

Filopodia are thinner protrusions supported by bundles of actin filaments, usually with the barbed ends towards the tip. Formins and other elongation factors can support long unbranched filaments, and bundling proteins help align them. Filopodia allow a cell to explore its surroundings and make contacts. Both types of protrusion can coexist; their distinguishing features are the organization of the actin filaments and their contribution to cell behavior rather than an absolute requirement that only one molecular pathway generate each structure.

How can a tiny actin monomer exert force on a membrane? Thermal fluctuations produce momentary gaps between a filament tip and the membrane. When a sufficiently large gap appears, a subunit can enter the growing filament. The extended filament makes backward displacement of the membrane relative to the tip less favorable. Repeated events bias movement of the boundary outward. This is the basic Brownian-ratchet model of polymerization-driven force. The free-energy conditions favoring addition of subunits support this process; fluctuations by themselves cannot drive sustained movement against a load at equilibrium. An opposing load makes suitable gaps less frequent, tending to slow the growth-driven protrusion.

Actin turnover enables sustained protrusion. ATP-actin is incorporated into filaments, ATP hydrolysis follows assembly, and older regions become enriched in ADP-actin. Cofilin can promote severing and turnover of older filaments in relevant contexts; profilin participates in monomer handling and recycling. Treadmilling refers to a condition where net assembly at one end balances net disassembly at the other, leaving filament length approximately constant. An entire lamellipodial network can also branch, grow, sever and flow. Treadmilling and myosin walking are distinct mechanisms, and observing the motion of actin markers does not by itself establish either one.

An actin network pushing a membrane experiences a reaction force. If it is not effectively connected to the environment, actin can flow backwards relative to the substrate while the protrusion advances only slowly. In adherent cells, integrins bind extracellular matrix and associate with intracellular proteins such as talin and vinculin, creating mechanical connections to actin. These adhesions function as clutches that can transmit force to the substrate. A weakly coupled network may show substantial retrograde flow with little traction, whereas stronger coupling can convert the same cytoskeletal activity into more effective displacement. The balance depends on the strength and turnover of adhesions.

Nonmuscle myosin II generates tension in actin networks, helps organize stress fibers, contributes to movement of the cell body and assists retraction of the rear. Rear attachments must be dismantled or remodeled during movement. Very weak adhesion can provide insufficient traction, but excessive adhesion can hinder detachment. Therefore migration speed need not increase monotonically with adhesion strength. A drug that selectively inhibits myosin II may reduce rear retraction and force even if actin polymerization at the front continues.

The classic sequence describes many cells on adhesive surfaces, but cells do not all use identical strategies. Some rapidly migrating immune cells in confined environments can rely more on cortical shape changes, friction and weak adhesion than on large stable focal adhesions. Bleb-based movement uses local separation or rupture of the cortex and pressure-driven expansion of the membrane, followed by rebuilding of the cortex. These cases are important limitations of a single lamellipodial model. Different cells may use different combinations of actin assembly, cortical contraction and environmental coupling.

Chemotaxis is biased migration in relation to a chemical gradient, rather than simply faster movement in response to a chemical. A uniform stimulant might increase speed without providing directional information. A gradient can bias where protrusion and traction occur. A cell with defective motor execution might sense a gradient normally yet fail to crawl effectively; a sensing-defective cell might move at normal speed but show no preferred direction. Measuring both speed and directional bias allows those possibilities to be distinguished.

11. Cytoplasmic streaming and other integrated movements

In large plant cells a central vacuole occupies much of the cell volume. Cytoplasm is often confined to a peripheral layer and strands crossing the vacuole. Under a microscope, particles and organelles can be seen moving in organized streams. In many plant cells class XI myosins move on polarized actin tracks and drive organelle-associated movements. Through mechanical coupling to neighboring material and fluid drag, active transport can contribute to bulk cytoplasmic streaming. The vacuole influences the geometry of the cytoplasmic layer but is not itself a molecular motor.

Large internodal cells of characean algae are particularly striking examples of streaming. Neighboring bands of cytoplasm can flow in opposite directions because the underlying actin bundles have different orientations. Other plant cells exhibit circulating streams determined by their cytoskeletal organization and shape. These examples show why a common plus-end motor can produce different anatomical directions of flow without changing its direction preference on actin. By improving mixing and distribution, streaming can assist material exchange in cells where diffusion alone would be slow.

Distinguish a motor-bound particle from a passive tracer. A vesicle connected to myosin can travel directly along an actin filament; a dissolved fluorescent tracer may move with the cytoplasmic flow generated by many such active movements. The tracer does not have to bind a motor. If an actin perturbation stops both organelle movement and tracer displacement, the result can be explained by disruption of the upstream force-generating system. It does not prove direct actin binding by every displaced substance.

Streaming also differs from phloem translocation, which involves long-distance transport in conducting tissues and additional physiological mechanisms. Nor does cytoplasmic streaming imply ciliary motility. Many higher-plant vegetative cells lack motile cilia but possess extensive actin-based movement. Some algal and plant reproductive cells do contain motile flagella, which operate through a microtubule-based mechanism.

A phagocytic cell provides another integrative example. Actin-rich protrusions help engulf a particle, after which microtubule motors can move the newly formed phagosome during maturation. A dividing animal cell uses an actomyosin ring for cytokinesis, whereas spindle microtubules and their associated motors participate in chromosome organization and separation. These examples use the same force-generating principles at different stages of a cellular process. Detailed division mechanisms will be addressed in the cell-cycle subunit.

The important conclusion is that cytoskeletal motility cannot be reduced to locomotion. Internal redistribution, mixing, local deformation and organelle positioning are all forms of useful movement. The next mechanism shows how an organized array of microtubules can transform the movement of microscopic motor domains into a coordinated wave extending over the length of a cell projection.

In interpreting observations outside the classic characean system, distinguish visible organelle transport from measurement of bulk fluid velocity. The extent to which active organelle motion drives bulk cytoplasmic flow in diverse plant cells is not equally established for all cell types. A soluble tracer or other independent fluid-motion measurement is preferable when testing a specific claim about bulk flow.

12. Cilia and eukaryotic flagella: structure from base to tip

A cilium is a membrane-covered extension containing a specialized microtubule scaffold, the axoneme. The word flagellum is generally used for a relatively long motile projection, such as the tail of a sperm or the motile appendage of an alga. Typical motile eukaryotic cilia and flagella share a closely related underlying structure. Their lengths, numbers and patterns of beating can differ, but these differences do not make them fundamentally different motor systems.

A typical motile axoneme displays the familiar 9 + 2 arrangement. Nine outer microtubule doublets encircle two separate central microtubules. Each outer doublet contains a complete A tubule, typically with thirteen protofilaments, and an incomplete B tubule, typically with about ten protofilaments, sharing part of the wall of the A tubule. The central pair consists of two singlets. There are thus eighteen constituent tubules in the outer doublets and two central singlets, for twenty constituent tubules, but not twenty independent complete microtubules. An illustration with nine isolated peripheral microtubules omits the defining doublet architecture.

Axonemal dynein arms extend from the A tubule of one doublet towards the B tubule of an adjacent doublet. Outer arms are especially important for generating force and influencing beat frequency; inner arms are strongly involved in waveform and regulation. Their roles overlap, and exact consequences of losing a particular arm depend on the organism and defect. Interdoublet links associated with the nexin–dynein regulatory complex help constrain sliding and organize motor activity. Radial spokes project inward from outer doublets towards the central-pair apparatus. These structures participate in the coordination of bending. Neither the spokes nor the central pair are ATP-powered walking motors.

The surrounding ciliary membrane is continuous with the plasma membrane but contains a distinctive collection of proteins and lipids. Selective trafficking and a transition-zone barrier help maintain the compartment's identity. Axonemal microtubules are generally oriented with their minus ends towards the base and plus ends towards the tip. This ordered polarity is crucial both for directional transport along the cilium and for the relative sliding that creates the beat.

At the base, a basal body organizes and anchors the axoneme. It is structurally related to a centriole and typically contains nine triplet microtubules arranged in a cylinder, with no central pair. The A and B tubules continue into the doublet region, whereas the C tubules terminate in the transition region. In a typical 9 + 2 cilium, the central pair begins distal to the basal body. Therefore a basal-body cross-section is not a 9 + 2 axoneme. The triplets provide a foundational organization for the doublet-based shaft.

Accessory structures may appear in specialized flagella. Mammalian sperm have supporting structures such as outer dense fibers and a fibrous sheath in regions outside the axoneme; mitochondria are concentrated in the midpiece. These modify mechanical behavior and support energy supply but do not substitute for axonemal dynein and microtubules. When identifying a sperm flagellum in a cross-section, the axoneme should be distinguished from surrounding accessory material.

Many primary cilia possess nine peripheral doublets and no central pair, the 9 + 0 arrangement. They are usually nonmotile sensory or signaling organelles and commonly lack the full motor apparatus required for active bending. However, the rule that every 9 + 0 cilium is immotile is false. Vertebrate embryonic nodal cilia are a standard counterexample: many have 9 + 0 organization with active dynein and rotate to produce fluid flow associated with left-right patterning. Conversely, a cilium with apparently intact 9 + 2 geometry can fail to beat if its arms or regulatory apparatus are defective. Numerical architecture alone cannot establish motility.

13. Dynein-driven sliding and the conversion to bending

If the microtubules within a cilium simply grew and shortened independently, the axoneme would not behave as a coordinated bending structure. The core mechanical intermediate is relative sliding between neighboring doublets. Dynein supplies longitudinal force; the architecture of the axoneme converts the resulting shear into curvature. This is analogous in a limited sense to actomyosin filament sliding, but the tracks, motors and structural arrangement differ.

Take two adjacent doublets with their plus ends at the tip and minus ends at the base. An axonemal dynein arm is anchored by its tail to the A tubule of one doublet and transiently engages the neighboring B tubule through its motor domain. Its motor domain tends to move towards the minus end of the B tubule. If the A-containing doublet is held stationary, the reaction force tends to displace the neighboring B-containing doublet towards its plus end relative to A. If B is held fixed instead, the anchored A doublet tends to move basally. These descriptions refer to the same relative sliding in different reference frames. A correct answer should specify which doublet is treated as fixed.

Why does the axoneme bend instead of separating into individually sliding rods? Doublets share a common basal attachment and are linked along their lengths. Interdoublet linkages resist unrestricted displacement, while the microtubules themselves resist bending. Dynein-generated shear can therefore be accommodated by changes in the curvature of the bundle. The outer side of a bend follows a longer path than the inner side. A relatively small difference in longitudinal position between filaments can create a considerable angular bend.

A simplified geometrical model makes this connection quantitative. Take two filaments separated by distance a, forming concentric arcs of radii R and R + a through an angle θ in radians. The inner arc has length Rθ; the outer arc has length (R + a)θ. Subtracting yields the relative arc-length difference Δs = aθ. For an illustrative separation of 0.18 µm and a bend through 90 degrees, θ = π/2, so Δs = 0.18 × 1.571 ≈ 0.283 µm. This is a two-filament idealization of sliding geometry, not a detailed calculation for all doublets in a real axoneme. The effective separation depends on the bending plane, the position of the doublets and elastic constraints.

Continuous activation of one set of dynein arms might maintain a bend or distort the axoneme rather than producing a rhythmic waveform. Beating requires coordinated changes in dynein activity at different locations along the shaft and on different sides of it. When the spatial pattern of active motors changes, the curvature also changes. The central apparatus, radial spokes and nexin–dynein regulatory complex participate in controlling the pattern, while mechanical feedback helps coordinate force production. A switch between two opposing sets of motors illustrates the basic principle but is not a complete universal model of waveform generation.

Classical observations and reactivation experiments established the sliding model. Satir's observations of differences in doublet positions in bent cilia supported relative sliding. In experiments by Summers and Gibbons, isolated flagellar axonemes exposed to ATP after limited proteolytic treatment could exhibit doublet sliding or extrusion. Demembranated axonemes can be reactivated under suitable ATP-containing conditions; weakening interdoublet constraints while preserving the motors can permit sliding instead of coordinated bending. Such an experiment would be uninformative if treatment also destroyed dynein, so motor integrity and appropriate untreated controls are essential.

These findings distinguish three necessary aspects of the mechanism. First, ATP-dependent force generation can occur within the axoneme. Second, neighboring doublets slide relative to each other. Third, structural constraints and regulation are needed to transform that sliding into a controlled beat. ATP and dynein are not by themselves sufficient to guarantee a normal waveform. A defect in radial spokes, interdoublet linkages or basal anchoring may alter bending despite retention of the gross doublet arrangement.

This distinction predicts different outcomes of perturbations. A preparation missing dynein activity cannot generate ordinary active sliding simply because the cross-section looks normal. If dynein remains active but interdoublet constraints are lost, sliding may occur without a useful bend. If motor activity and constraints remain but temporal coordination is impaired, the waveform may be abnormal. Identifying the level at which a failure occurs is more informative than describing every defect as ciliary immobility.

14. Beating, wave propagation and fluid movement

A motile axoneme generates a time-dependent pattern of curvature. Many short motile cilia produce an effective stroke in a relatively extended configuration, pushing surrounding liquid preferentially in one direction. During recovery, the cilium bends and returns by a different path, reducing the reverse displacement of fluid. This asymmetry is important because viscous effects often dominate inertia at the small length and velocity scales of a cilium.

At low Reynolds number, in an ideal Newtonian fluid, a motion that exactly retraces its configurations in reverse is ineffective at producing net movement over a complete reciprocal cycle. A nonreciprocal beat can instead drive directional flow. Cilia therefore depend on more than how rapidly their motors generate force. The timing and geometry of the stroke determine whether useful fluid displacement results. This is the physical reason why a cilium may beat visibly but contribute little net transport when its waveform is severely abnormal.

Long flagella frequently generate traveling bending waves rather than a clearly separated effective and recovery stroke. A sperm flagellum propagates a bending pattern along its length, producing thrust through interaction with liquid. The two flagella of Chlamydomonas often execute a breaststroke-like pattern during forward swimming and change their beating in other behaviors. Eukaryotic flagella are generally flexible beating structures rather than rigid rotating helices like typical bacterial flagella. Some specialized eukaryotic cilia nevertheless rotate, so motion must be described in relation to the organism and architecture.

In a field of motile cilia, neighboring appendages may beat with phase differences. A propagating pattern of those phases is called a metachronal wave. It is not a physical migration of cilia between cells. Hydrodynamic coupling and the organization of basal bodies and cell polarity can contribute to coordinated behavior. Individually functional cilia can generate inefficient tissue-level transport if they are poorly aligned, because their directed forces can partially cancel.

In airway epithelium, cilia move mucus containing trapped particles towards the pharynx. In the oviduct, beating cilia contribute to material transport together with other tissue mechanisms. In Paramecium, cilia propel the organism and can sweep food into the oral region. These functions demonstrate how one axonemal mechanism can move the surrounding fluid, a suspended particle or an entire cell depending on its mechanical context.

Beat frequency f is the number of complete cycles divided by time and is measured in hertz. The period is T = 1/f. A cilium completing twelve cycles in 0.8 s has f = 15 Hz and T ≈ 0.067 s. Frequency alone does not determine fluid velocity; stroke amplitude, waveform, orientation, coordination and fluid properties matter. If two tissues have the same beat frequency but very different clearance rates, one must examine those other factors rather than assume that the frequency measurement is incorrect.

A traveling bending pattern has a phase speed distinct from the swimming speed of the cell. If a periodic wave with wavelength λ advances by one wavelength in one period, its phase speed is c = λ/T = λf. For λ = 18 µm and f = 12 Hz, c = 216 µm/s. The swimmer may translate at a much smaller speed. Its actual velocity depends on the geometry and hydrodynamic interaction of the flagellum and cell body. The computed phase speed cannot be substituted for swimming speed without additional information.

15. Ciliary assembly, intraflagellar transport and sensory function

Most proteins needed to build and maintain an axoneme are synthesized in the cell body. A basal body provides an organizing foundation, but it does not synthesize the components of the shaft. Transport into and out of the cilium is consequently essential. The transition zone restricts the exchange of many components with the rest of the cell, while specialized transport machinery helps maintain the characteristic ciliary compartment.

Intraflagellar transport, commonly abbreviated IFT, consists of movement of protein complexes and associated cargo along the axonemal microtubules, generally in the space between the doublets and the ciliary membrane. Kinesin-2 family motors typically drive anterograde movement from the base towards the plus ends at the tip. Cytoplasmic dynein-2 drives retrograde movement towards the base. IFT complexes can carry assembly material towards the tip and return components for recycling. They should not be confused with the dynein arms anchored to the doublets that produce bending.

At the ciliary tip, assembly and turnover contribute to maintenance of length. If tip-directed IFT is impaired, materials required for growth may not arrive efficiently and the cilium can be short or absent. If retrograde IFT is defective, IFT particles and associated materials may accumulate near the tip, producing an abnormal or swollen cilium. Exact phenotypes differ by organism and mutation. A static short cilium alone is insufficient to identify the affected motor. Directly measuring the direction of IFT particles and comparing assembly and return transport gives stronger evidence.

A primary cilium can require ATP-powered motors even though it ordinarily does not beat. Many primary cilia contain receptors and signaling components important for cellular sensing and developmental regulation. These structures still need to be assembled, supplied and maintained. Absence of functional axonemal bending machinery does not imply absence of all microtubule motors from the organelle. It is possible to have active IFT along a nonmotile primary cilium.

In many vertebrate cells a mature mother centriole can dock near the cell membrane and function as a basal body for a primary cilium. Cilium assembly and resorption are coordinated with cell state, including the cell cycle. The basal body has an organizing and anchoring function rather than a walking-motor function. Detailed centriole duplication and cell-cycle control will be addressed in the division subunit.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia provides an example of defective motile cilia. Mutations affecting dynein arms, their assembly or associated regulatory machinery may impair mucus clearance, sperm motility and, in some affected individuals, the establishment of left-right body asymmetry. Not all patients show reversal of organ arrangement and not all genetic defects cause the same visible ultrastructural change. The scientifically important connection is between a particular structural or regulatory defect and a specific loss of function. This disorder should not be confused with all diseases of primary sensory cilia; many of those involve different defects in signaling or compartment maintenance.

16. Why bacterial flagella are fundamentally different

The term flagellum describes a motility appendage in very different organisms. The common name does not establish a common architecture. Eukaryotic motile flagella contain a membrane-covered microtubule axoneme with ATP-dependent dynein arms. Typical bacterial flagella instead contain an external helical filament composed principally of flagellin, a hook and a rotary basal apparatus embedded in the cell envelope. They have neither a 9 + 2 microtubule axoneme nor axonemal dynein.

Many bacterial flagellar motors use the electrochemical gradient of protons across the cytoplasmic membrane, while some use a sodium-ion gradient. Ion movement through stator complexes is coupled to torque on a rotor; the hook transfers rotation to the external helical filament. ATP may be needed for flagellar protein export and other bacterial processes, but repeated rotation of a typical bacterial flagellar motor is directly powered by an ion motive force rather than an ATPase in the external filament. Dissipation of the relevant ion gradient can stop the rotation even when ATP remains available.

In peritrichously flagellated bacteria such as Escherichia coli, coordination of flagellar rotation can produce a bundle associated with smooth swimming, while changes in rotation can disrupt the bundle and reorient the cell. This is a representative example, not a universal description of all bacterial motility. The detailed signaling pathway of bacterial chemotaxis belongs elsewhere in the curriculum. Here the example establishes the mechanical difference between a rotary helical filament and a flexible bending axoneme.

Archaea possess another distinct rotary structure, the archaellum, which is ATP-powered and structurally different from the bacterial flagellum. It is also not a eukaryotic microtubule-based axoneme. Therefore the statement that every prokaryotic flagellum-like structure is powered by an ion gradient is incorrect. Specify the organism when interpreting experimental evidence.

The structures give contrasting reactivation predictions. A demembranated eukaryotic axoneme can often be reactivated by providing ATP under appropriate conditions. A bacterial rotary motor normally requires a functional energy-transducing membrane and an appropriate ion gradient. Simply adding ATP to an isolated bacterial external flagellin filament will not make it rotate. These predictions follow from the different organization of force-generating machinery.

17. Interpreting mutations, inhibitors and motility phenotypes

Perturbation experiments become informative when four layers are distinguished: the cytoskeletal track, the motor, the motor–cargo connection and the energy supply. Nocodazole can disrupt many cytoplasmic microtubules, reducing long-range motor-dependent transport. This does not establish direct inhibition of kinesin ATPase. Paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules and suppresses dynamic instability under suitable conditions; a stabilized microtubule can still support walking. A decline in microtubule dynamics and a decline in motor catalytic activity are different phenomena. Both drugs may have indirect effects on organization, and the concentration and treatment duration influence the outcome.

Latrunculin sequesters actin monomers and can reduce assembly, while cytochalasins interfere with barbed-end elongation. Jasplakinolide can stabilize actin filaments and alter assembly. Such treatments often disturb protrusion and streaming because those processes depend on dynamic actin. Blebbistatin inhibits myosin-II activity and can impair tension without directly abolishing actin polymerization. If protrusion persists but traction and rear retraction decline, the data support the distinct contribution of myosin-II contractility. None of these compounds should be assumed perfectly specific at every dose or in every cell.

ATP depletion is a broad perturbation. It can reduce motor activity, compromise ion pumps and influence signaling and metabolism. If all movement stops after depletion, an energy-dependent process is implicated but the motor identity remains uncertain. A more discriminating experiment preserves the track, selectively alters a candidate motor, and tests whether a functional copy rescues the motion. A catalytic mutant with intact cargo attachment can test the importance of mechanical cycling; a tail mutant with normal purified walking but defective cellular transport can implicate attachment or recruitment.

Consider hypothetical cargo data from an animal cell with independently verified radial microtubule polarity. In a control, eighty outward and seventy inward runs occur in a defined observation interval. After a mutation, outward runs drop to twelve while inward runs remain at sixty-five. The intact track orientation and selectively reduced outward movements support a defect in plus-end transport. If the remaining outward runs retain their usual speed, a reduction in successful initiation or recruitment may be more likely than slowing every motor. If the running speed also falls, a kinetic defect or additional mechanical load may contribute. These interpretations generate testable hypotheses rather than a unique diagnosis from a single data set.

A ciliary defect also requires appropriate measurements. A normal doublet arrangement with absent outer dynein arms and reduced frequency suggests a failure of the beating machinery. An apparently intact array with abnormal waveform may suggest a regulatory defect, though normal morphology does not prove normal motor activity. Normal individual beats with disordered orientations can account for low tissue-level transport. IFT material that accumulates near the tip despite retained outward runs points towards a different level of function, retrograde turnover.

To compare an experimental result with a model, distinguish direct observation and inference. In a cell-migration assay the direct observations might be leading-edge protrusion, substrate deformation and rear position. A fall in substrate deformation is evidence of altered traction; it does not by itself prove that a specific myosin gene is mutated. Likewise, a structural image of a cilium demonstrates the visible arrangement of components but does not measure motor activity. Independent functional assays, appropriate controls and rescue experiments make the explanation stronger.

A final general lesson follows: organized motility is not merely the presence of ATPase proteins and filaments. It is the outcome of coupling energy conversion to a mechanical arrangement and regulating that arrangement in space and time. Exam questions become easier when the measured variable is linked explicitly to the molecular mechanism rather than to an isolated keyword.

18. Integrated comparison and transition to the next subunit

A motor protein converts chemical free energy into force by cycling through binding and conformational states on a polar filament. Myosins use actin; kinesins and dyneins generally use microtubules. Their characteristic direction preferences have important exceptions, and the cell-level direction always depends on the orientation of the track. Cargo motion and track sliding are opposite descriptions when the attachment geometry is reversed.

Actin assembly can also generate force independently of a walking motor at the filament tip. In crawling cells, polymerization pushes the boundary, adhesions transmit traction, and myosin-II tension helps move the cell body. In large plant cells, actin-associated motor transport can produce bulk cytoplasmic streaming. Intermediate filaments support the integrity of mechanically stressed cells without providing a conventional directional walking track.

Axonemal dynein converts ATP hydrolysis into relative sliding of neighboring microtubule doublets. Basal anchoring and interdoublet connections convert sliding into curvature. Coordinated motor activation turns repeated changes in curvature into a waveform, and the waveform interacting with fluid produces propulsion or transport. IFT is a separate motor-dependent system of assembly and maintenance. The same numerical arrangement of tubules cannot establish whether a cilium is motile, and the bacterial flagellar mechanism is fundamentally different.

For an unfamiliar exam problem, identify the object that moves and its reference frame, determine the track and polarity, identify the motor and energy source, and then distinguish changes in speed, direction, initiation, processivity, force, frequency and waveform. Finally ask whether the observation demonstrates a direct mechanism, is consistent with a hypothesis, or leaves competing explanations open. This approach transfers to problems involving both intracellular organelles and whole-cell behavior.

The organelle subunit now brings together spatial compartments, energy supply, transport and the dynamic cytoskeletal systems that organize them. The next subunit examines the genes and chromosomes that carry the information for cellular organization. Later, the cell-division subunit returns to cytoskeletal mechanics through chromosome segregation and cytokinesis.

19. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Question 1

A conventional kinesin-1 walks on a microtubule whose plus end faces the nucleus. Which direction is favored?

A. Towards nucleus; B. Away from nucleus; C. Toward actin pointed end; D. Determined by nuclear electrical potential

Answer and explanation

A. Direction depends on track polarity, not position. B assumes a radial plus-end-out array, C uses the wrong track and D confuses polarity with electrical charge.

Question 2

ATP binds nucleotide-free myosin II strongly attached to actin. Which event is favored?

A. Stronger rigor binding; B. Detachment; C. Tubulin hydrolysis; D. Actin depolymerization

Answer and explanation

B. ATP binding lowers myosin affinity for actin. A reverses the relation; C involves microtubules; D is not the direct motor transition.

Question 3

Which is a prominent exception to plus-end-directed myosin movement?

A. Myosin V; B. Myosin II; C. Myosin VI; D. Kinesin-1

Answer and explanation

C. Myosin VI moves towards the pointed end. A and B usually move towards the barbed end, while D acts on microtubules.

Question 4

Which directly promotes branched actin nucleation in lamellipodia?

A. Arp2/3; B. Dynein-2; C. Flagellin; D. Dynactin

Answer and explanation

A. Arp2/3 nucleates branches on existing actin. B drives retrograde IFT; C forms a bacterial flagellar filament; D supports cytoplasmic dynein.

Question 5

A typical basal body contains which arrangement?

A. Nine triplets; B. Nine doublets and two central singlets; C. Nine actin bundles; D. Thirteen intermediate filaments

Answer and explanation

A. B is typical motile axonemal structure. C and D describe unrelated cytoskeletal elements.

Question 6

Retrograde IFT is typically driven by which motor?

A. Kinesin-2; B. Myosin V; C. Cytoplasmic dynein-2; D. Axonemal outer-arm dynein

Answer and explanation

C. Dynein-2 returns IFT cargo to the base. A drives outward IFT, B uses actin, and D generates axonemal sliding.

Question 7

Which statement about 9 + 0 cilia is correct?

A. Always immotile; B. Always motile; C. Commonly sensory but motile exceptions exist; D. Lack all microtubules

Answer and explanation

C. Nodal cilia are a major motile exception. A and B overgeneralize, while D contradicts the nine doublets.

Question 8

What directly drives a typical proton-dependent bacterial flagellar motor?

A. Dynein ATPase; B. H⁺ electrochemical gradient; C. Actomyosin; D. Tubulin assembly

Answer and explanation

B. The membrane ion gradient drives rotary torque. The other mechanisms are not components of the typical bacterial flagellar motor.

Question 9

Assertion: A motor can exert force at stall. Reason: Force and net velocity are distinct physical quantities.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but reason unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Stall means zero average velocity under load, not zero force. External net translational power can vanish while force remains.

Question 10

Assertion: All 9 + 0 cilia are immotile. Reason: Some embryonic nodal cilia possess motility-generating machinery despite 9 + 0 architecture.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but reason unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

D. Nodal cilia contradict the universal claim. Motor and regulatory apparatus must also be assessed.

Question 11

Assertion: Actin growth can push a cell boundary without a motor at the growing tip. Reason: Insertion of subunits into transient gaps can bias membrane displacement.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. This is the Brownian-ratchet principle. It does not imply that contractility is irrelevant to whole-cell migration.

Question 12

Which best describes dynactin?

A. Independent minus-end ATPase motor; B. Complex supporting dynein-mediated transport with adaptors; C. Bacterial rotary stator; D. Actin nucleator identical to Arp2/3

Answer and explanation

B. Dynactin helps assemble and activate dynein transport complexes. A incorrectly assigns walking ATPase activity to dynactin; C and D concern unrelated systems.

20. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Question 1

A kinesin cargo advances 4.8 µm with 8 nm net steps and no backsteps, at 0.6 µm/s. Find cycles and duration.

A. 60, 8 s; B. 600, 8 s; C. 600, 0.8 s; D. 4800, 6 s

Answer and explanation

B. 4800 nm / 8 nm = 600 steps; 4.8 / 0.6 = 8 s. A misses a factor of ten; C miscalculates time; D treats each nanometre as a step.

Question 2

A motor advances 8 nm against 4 pN, using 80 pN·nm chemical energy per cycle. External work fraction?

A. 20%; B. 32%; C. 40%; D. 80%

Answer and explanation

C. W = 4 × 8 = 32 pN·nm; 32/80 = 0.4. B mistakes numerical work for percentage; A and D do not use the energy ratio.

Question 3

For one-dimensional free diffusion with D = 0.02 µm²/s, compare RMS timescales for 2 µm and 8 µm.

A. 100 and 400 s; B. 100 and 1600 s; C. 50 and 200 s; D. 4 and 64 s

Answer and explanation

B. t = L²/(2D); 4/0.04 = 100 and 64/0.04 = 1600 s. A and C assume incorrect scaling; D omits D. These are RMS timescales, not guaranteed arrival times.

Question 4

Beads move towards plus ends along fixed microtubules. The same motors are fixed on glass, leaving microtubules free. Which end leads gliding?

A. Plus; B. Minus; C. Both; D. No movement possible

Answer and explanation

B. Reversing the fixed object reverses observed relative motion without changing the motor polarity. A confuses reference frames; C ignores directional motion; D is unsupported.

Question 5

An adaptor mutation reduces outward runs from 100 to 15 per minute but the residual run speed and track orientation remain normal. Best hypothesis?

A. All motors step more slowly; B. Reduced recruitment or initiation of outward runs; C. Tracks reverse polarity; D. Complete ATP depletion

Answer and explanation

B. The frequency of successful runs changes rather than speed. A conflicts with residual speed; C with the orientation data; D would broadly affect ATP-dependent processes.

Question 6

A cargo travels +6 µm in 3 s, pauses 2 s, then travels −4 µm in 5 s. Average speed and signed mean velocity?

A. 0.2 and 1 µm/s; B. 1 and +0.2 µm/s; C. 2 and −0.8 µm/s; D. 10 and +2 µm/s

Answer and explanation

B. Total time 10 s, distance 10 µm, displacement +2 µm. A swaps the quantities; C gives individual running velocities; D omits division by time.

Question 7

An isolated axoneme is treated to weaken interdoublet links without destroying dynein, then receives ATP. Which result supports the sliding model?

A. Doublet extrusion instead of coordinated bending; B. Tubulin changes into actin; C. Bacterial rotation; D. ATP synthesis by tubules

Answer and explanation

A. Sliding can occur without normal constraints, supporting the proposed intermediate. B–D are unrelated. A control must establish that dynein remains functional.

Question 8

Two ideal parallel curved filaments are separated by 0.12 µm and bend through 60 degrees. Estimate relative arc-length difference.

A. 7.2 µm; B. 0.063 µm; C. 0.126 µm; D. 0.72 µm

Answer and explanation

C. θ = π/3 radians; Δs = 0.12 × 1.047 ≈ 0.126 µm. A uses degrees directly; B halves the angle; D is an erroneous conversion.

Question 9

A cilium completes 30 cycles in 2.5 s; a mutant completes 20 in 2.5 s. Frequencies and percentage reduction?

A. 12 and 8 Hz, 33.3%; B. 12 and 8 Hz, 50%; C. 75 and 50 Hz, 33.3%; D. 8 and 12 Hz, 50%

Answer and explanation

A. 30/2.5 = 12 and 20/2.5 = 8 Hz; reduction 4/12 ≈ 33.3%. B uses the mutant baseline, C multiplies, and D reverses the groups.

Question 10

IFT particles accumulate at the tip despite retained tip-directed runs and visibly normal axonemal arms. Which defect is most directly suggested?

A. Dynein-2 retrograde IFT; B. Complete absence of kinesin-2; C. Loss of all track polarity; D. Myosin-II contraction

Answer and explanation

A. Material arrives but does not efficiently return. B contradicts outward runs; C would impair both directions; D is not IFT. Direct retrograde imaging would test the hypothesis.

Question 11

Actin polymerization and protrusion continue in a treated fibroblast, but traction falls and the rear fails to retract. Which interpretation fits selective myosin-II inhibition?

A. Protrusion alone guarantees migration; B. Contractility contributes separately to integrated migration; C. Axonemal dynein drives the rear; D. Actin becomes nonpolar

Answer and explanation

B. Actin growth and contractility are separable mechanical processes. A contradicts observations; C invokes an unrelated motor; D confuses activity with structure.

Question 12

A cell has normal speed in uniform attractant but no directional bias in a gradient. Which process is preferentially impaired?

A. All motor ATPases; B. Directional sensing or polarization; C. All actin assembly; D. All adhesion

Answer and explanation

B. Motility persists but directional information is not translated into bias. A, C and D are incompatible with generally retained movement in the described assay.

Question 13

A flagellar bending wave has wavelength 15 µm and frequency 20 Hz. Which conclusion follows?

A. Phase speed 300 µm/s; swimming speed needs more information; B. Swimming speed must be 300 µm/s; C. Phase speed 0.75 µm/s; D. Frequency equals swimming speed

Answer and explanation

A. c = λf = 300 µm/s. B assumes wave and cell speed are equal; C divides incompatible quantities; D confuses units.

Question 14

Cilia beat at normal frequency but are oriented randomly and mucus transport declines. Which explanation is best?

A. Frequency alone determines flow; B. Loss of coordination reduces net fluid displacement; C. All dynein arms absent; D. Bacterial flagella replace cilia

Answer and explanation

B. Forces in different directions may partially cancel. A ignores orientation and waveform; C conflicts with retained beats; D is unsupported.

Question 15

A kinesin tail mutation prevents vesicle binding, but its purified motor retains normal walking speed. What is predicted?

A. Purified walking is absent; B. Cellular cargo movement may fail despite intact motor chemistry; C. Actin must depolymerize; D. Dynein reverses direction

Answer and explanation

B. Cargo attachment and catalytic walking are distinct. A contradicts the observation; C and D do not follow.

Question 16

A myosin carries vesicles in opposite anatomical directions on neighboring actin bundles. Which observation supports unchanged motor polarity?

A. Opposite barbed-end orientations; B. Same polarity and replacement of ATP with GTP; C. Both bundles nonpolar; D. No motors on vesicles

Answer and explanation
