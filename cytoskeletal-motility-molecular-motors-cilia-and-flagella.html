1. Why movement is essential even in a stationary cell

A living cell can remain attached to a tissue throughout its lifetime and nevertheless be constantly moving on the inside. Vesicles leave the Golgi apparatus, damaged components travel towards degradative compartments, and mitochondria redistribute when the demand for ATP changes. A white blood cell changes its outline and crawls towards a chemical signal. A respiratory epithelial cell remains fixed in its tissue but beats its cilia to move a layer of mucus. These observations raise one central question: how does a cell turn molecular events into organized mechanical displacement?

Small dissolved molecules can move by thermal diffusion. Their random movements are useful over short distances, and no molecular motor is required merely to allow them to spread. But a large vesicle in crowded cytoplasm must reach a particular destination, often many micrometres away. Diffusion has no intrinsic preference for that destination. Similarly, a cell must do mechanical work against resistance to change its shape or move surrounding liquid. Molecular motors and dynamic cytoskeletal polymers provide complementary solutions to these problems.

Three filament systems were introduced in the preceding chapter. Actin filaments are polar polymers, microtubules are polar tubes constructed from tubulin, and intermediate filaments form generally nonpolar structures that resist mechanical stress. Actin and microtubules provide directional tracks for motor proteins. Intermediate filaments provide resilience during deformation but do not serve as conventional ATP-powered walking-motor tracks. Their mechanical function remains vital; a filament does not need to support motor walking to be useful.

Distinguish three forms of motion before proceeding. A motor may move a cargo along a relatively stationary track. A motor attached to a fixed structure may instead move the track itself. Finally, growth of a filament can exert force against a barrier without a walking motor at its growing end. In all three cases an object changes position, but the underlying mechanisms and experimental predictions are different. A moving fluorescent spot alone does not identify the source of force.

Transport also differs from membrane fusion. A vesicle that has reached its destination still requires the correct tethering and fusion machinery to deliver its contents. Motors can improve the speed and spatial organization of trafficking but cannot replace the specificity of membrane recognition. This distinction links the preceding chapters on organelles and membrane sorting with the mechanical principles developed here.

2. Track polarity and the reference frame

Motor direction depends on the asymmetric molecular arrangement of the track. In actin, subunits are assembled in a common orientation, producing a plus or barbed end and a minus or pointed end. Under many assembly conditions the barbed end grows more rapidly. These names refer to structural differences, not electrical charges. Classical microscopy of myosin-decorated actin revealed arrowhead patterns whose tips point towards the pointed end. Such structural markers allow polarity to be established independently of motor motion.

Microtubules are assembled from alpha-beta tubulin heterodimers aligned head-to-tail into protofilaments. A typical cytoplasmic microtubule has thirteen protofilaments arranged into a hollow cylinder. Beta-tubulin faces the plus end in the idealized lattice, whereas alpha-tubulin faces the minus end. The repeated asymmetric structure provides a directional reference to a motor domain. In many animal cells, microtubules are arranged radially with minus ends near a centrosome and plus ends pointing outwards. Other arrangements occur in differentiated epithelia, dendrites and plant cells, so the geographical direction of movement cannot be inferred from motor identity alone.

Imagine two microtubules oriented in opposite directions. The same conventional plus-end kinesin moves towards opposite ends of the cell when it switches from one track to the other. No change in its ATPase or intrinsic polarity preference is necessary. An examination question may deliberately reverse the orientation shown in a diagram. First identify the plus and minus ends; only then decide whether movement is towards the nucleus, cell cortex, axon terminal or another landmark.

The polarity of a track does not require that it be growing. A stabilized microtubule can support motor walking, and relatively stable actin can support myosin movement. Tubulin GTP hydrolysis affects microtubule assembly and dynamics; ATP hydrolysis by kinesin or dynein drives their motor cycles. Similarly, ATP associated with an actin subunit is not the same ATP molecule hydrolyzed by myosin. Confusing the nucleotide cycle of a polymer with that of its associated motor obscures the origin of the mechanical energy.

A motor usually prefers a direction under physiological conditions, but its movement is not a rigid sequence of irreversible steps. Thermal fluctuations, opposing loads, obstacles and detachment can interrupt or reverse individual steps. Directionality is a bias in the coupled mechanochemical cycle. To interpret experiments it is equally important to specify whether the motor or the filament is moving relative to the laboratory surface. An anchored plus-end-directed motor tends to displace a freely moving track in the opposite direction.

3. Mechanochemical coupling: how ATP creates movement

A motor is a flexible enzyme rather than a rigid mechanical machine. Its nucleotide-binding site communicates with regions that attach to a filament and regions that generate mechanical displacement. ATP binding, hydrolysis, phosphate release and ADP release change the motor's conformational preferences and affinity for its track. Because the track is polar, these changes can favor attachment at a different binding site and bias the next displacement. Different motor families implement this principle in different ways; a universal sequence of identical power strokes would be an oversimplification.

At thermal equilibrium a protein can fluctuate between conformations but cannot continuously extract directed mechanical work from those fluctuations alone. The cell maintains ATP and its hydrolysis products away from equilibrium by metabolism. The free-energy decrease associated with ATP hydrolysis can therefore drive a repeated cycle of preferential binding, displacement and release. Some of the available free energy is dissipated. A polar track supplies directional information but does not supply energy merely by being asymmetric.

A useful motor must attach strongly at some stages and release at others. Permanent attachment prevents repeated stepping, while uncontrolled detachment allows the cargo to escape. Processivity describes the ability to perform successive steps before dissociating. The duty ratio describes the fraction of a cycle spent strongly associated with the track under a defined set of conditions. These concepts are related but not identical. A two-headed cargo motor often coordinates the states of its two heads to maintain attachment. Many low-duty-ratio motors can instead cooperate in an ensemble, so the collective assembly remains effective despite brief attachment by each individual head.

Load influences kinetic transitions. An opposing force often slows the net velocity of a transport motor. At stall, the average velocity is zero under the measurement conditions but the motor may still exert force. It may also continue microscopic transitions and ATP turnover depending on the system. Zero net translational displacement means zero net external translational work during that interval, not necessarily the absence of chemical activity. The observed stall force is therefore a kinetic and mechanical property, not simply the maximum force allowed by thermodynamics.

The external work of a step against a constant opposing load is the product of force and distance along its direction. The chemical energy consumed per cycle sets an upper bound on this work for a sustained cycle. The actual relationship depends on the number of ATP molecules consumed, motor efficiency, microscopic stepping and load. Later calculations use a conventional one-ATP-per-step model for kinesin-1; it should not be generalized to all motors without additional evidence.

4. Myosin: force and movement on actin

Myosins possess a motor domain that binds actin and hydrolyzes ATP, a neck or lever-arm region, and a tail that participates in cargo binding, membrane association or filament formation. Light chains often stabilize or regulate the lever arm. A small change in the motor domain can be amplified into movement of the lever arm. The architecture explains how related motor domains can perform very different cellular jobs when connected to different tails.

The conventional myosin-II cycle begins, for teaching purposes, with a nucleotide-free motor head strongly bound to actin. Binding ATP decreases its actin affinity and promotes release. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and phosphate primes the motor in a higher-energy conformation. The primed head reattaches to an actin site, and strong binding with phosphate release is coupled to force generation and a change in lever-arm orientation, represented as the power stroke. ADP release returns the motor to a strongly bound state. The timing of microscopic intermediates depends on conditions, but the sequence establishes the essential causal relations between nucleotide state, binding and force.

ATP binding thus does not maintain conventional myosin in its rigor attachment. Instead, it allows detachment. When ATP becomes unavailable and motors finish cycling in strongly bound nucleotide-free states, actin and myosin can remain locked together. Rigor is therefore not active rapid contraction. Continuous force production through repeated cross-bridge cycling requires fresh ATP and appropriate regulatory conditions.

Myosin II has two motor heads and a tail that permits assembly into bipolar filaments. The heads at opposite ends of an assembly engage oppositely oriented actin filaments. The heads generally walk towards barbed ends relative to actin; if the barbed ends face outward, the relative motion slides actin filaments towards one another. Filaments need not become shorter for the distance between their attachments to decrease. This principle explains actin stress fibers, contractile cortical structures and the cytokinetic ring in nonmuscle cells. Detailed sarcomere physiology is reserved for the animal physiology unit.

Myosin I often interacts with membranes and cortical actin and is involved in local membrane deformation or tension control. Myosin V is adapted for processive movement of selected cargoes. Its long lever arms and coordinated heads allow representative myosin Va molecules to advance approximately 36 nm per center-of-mass step along actin in appropriate assays. This is not a universal step length for the myosin family. Myosin VI is a notable exception to the usual plus-end-directed movement of myosins; it moves towards the pointed end and participates in specialized cellular trafficking.

Regulation connects myosin activity to cellular demand. In nonmuscle myosin II, regulatory light-chain phosphorylation generally increases motor activity and favors formation of contractile filaments. Calcium–calmodulin-dependent kinase and Rho-associated signaling can participate in this regulation in different contexts. Restricting activation to a particular cortical region allows force to be generated locally rather than across the entire cell. A mutation affecting myosin assembly can reduce contractility even if individual isolated heads still hydrolyze ATP. A mutation affecting the motor domain can produce the reverse pattern: structurally assembled filaments with defective force generation.

A useful comparison is between contraction and cargo transport. A single myosin-II head may remain strongly attached for a relatively small fraction of its cycle, but many heads in a filament act together. A processive myosin V needs to retain its cargo's association with a track over multiple steps. Both are ATP-dependent, yet the kinetics suitable for collective force production differ from those suitable for a long uninterrupted cargo run.

5. Kinesin: stepping along microtubules

Conventional kinesin-1 is typically a dimer with two globular motor domains, short neck linkers, a coiled-coil stalk and a cargo-binding region. Accessory chains and adaptor proteins can contribute to cargo recognition. Each head recognizes the microtubule lattice and possesses a nucleotide-binding site. The neck linker helps bias movement toward a new position on the track, while the tail transmits the displacement to a cargo. Thus the motor domain defines the mechanism of motion and the cargo-binding apparatus helps define the biological destination.

In the simplified hand-over-hand cycle, one head is bound to the microtubule while the partner is positioned to search for a forward site. ATP-dependent neck-linker rearrangement biases the partner head toward the plus end. The new head establishes contact and releases ADP; the trailing head passes through hydrolysis and product-release transitions and detaches as the cycle proceeds. The heads exchange roles. Their coordinated kinetics reduce the probability that both detach together. This explains why a conventional kinesin can walk for many steps before releasing its cargo from the track.

Under typical low-load conditions, conventional kinesin-1 advances about 8 nm per net step, corresponding to the repeat distance of alpha-beta tubulin heterodimers along a protofilament. In the hand-over-hand picture the advancing individual head moves approximately 16 nm past its partner; the midpoint advances approximately 8 nm. A student who uses the individual head displacement instead of the cargo displacement will underestimate the number of cycles by a factor of two. The 8 nm step is also not the same as the approximately 25 nm outer diameter of a typical microtubule.

Run length describes how far the motor typically travels before dissociation, not how fast it walks. A cargo with two or more motors may remain attached longer or support greater load, yet it does not necessarily travel at two or three times the velocity of a single motor. Velocity depends on kinetic transitions, opposing force and coordination. Distinguishing speed from processivity is essential when a mutation reduces the duration or frequency of successful cargo runs but leaves their instantaneous velocity unchanged.

The kinesin superfamily includes different functional specializations. Kinesin-1, kinesin-2 and kinesin-3 include prominent plus-end cargo motors, whereas many kinesin-14 members move towards minus ends. Kinesin-13 proteins are important microtubule depolymerizing proteins rather than typical long-distance cargo walkers. Kinesin-5 can engage two microtubules and contribute to relative sliding in the mitotic spindle. Detailed chromosome movement belongs to cell division. Here these examples demonstrate that a family name does not uniquely determine function or direction.

Motor recruitment and activation are also regulated. Some kinesins are autoinhibited when no appropriate cargo is present. Cargo-associated adaptors can relieve this inhibition and support transport. A tail defect might therefore prevent vesicle movement while leaving the purified motor domain fully competent to walk. The distinction between catalytic function and coupling to cargo makes such mutants particularly informative in experimental reasoning.

6. Dynein: minus-end movement and force generation

Dynein is an ATP-dependent microtubule motor with a structure distinct from kinesin. A dynein heavy chain contains a prominent ring of AAA+ ATPase modules, a force-transmitting linker, a stalk ending in a microtubule-binding domain, and a tail that associates with other subunits or anchoring structures. Nucleotide-dependent conformational changes in the ring affect the position of the linker and the affinity of the microtubule-binding tip. The motor can thereby generate displacement through a cycle of binding, release and conformational change without using the kinesin neck-linker architecture.

Cytoplasmic dynein-1 normally moves towards the minus ends of microtubules. In a radial animal cell this can carry endosomes and other organelles towards the central cytoplasm and helps maintain the position of the Golgi apparatus. If its tail is connected to a stationary cortex rather than a mobile cargo, dynein can pull on the microtubule array itself. The resulting mechanical effect depends on what is attached to what, even though the motor's polarity preference is unchanged.

Efficient processive movement by mammalian cytoplasmic dynein-1 often depends on dynactin and a cargo-specific activating adaptor. Dynactin is not a second ATP-powered walking motor. It helps arrange the dynein complex into an active configuration and provides an interface for transport-associated factors. A cell with functional dynein and intact microtubules may therefore show impaired directional transport if a necessary adaptor is absent. Conversely, increasing the abundance of dynein alone may not rescue a defect in cargo recruitment.

Dynein can exhibit variable step sizes, lateral steps and backward steps, and its heads need not alternate in the strictly idealized fashion used for conventional kinesin. Consequently, an experimentally measured net displacement cannot be converted to ATP consumption using a universal 8 nm per ATP rule for dynein. The appropriate coupling ratio must be established or specified.

Two additional dynein categories are vital for understanding cilia. Axonemal dyneins are anchored to microtubule doublets, where they generate relative sliding that is converted into bending. Cytoplasmic dynein-2 participates in the return of intraflagellar transport material towards the ciliary base. Although both use ATP and microtubule tracks, their functions differ. A mutation impairing dynein-2 can disrupt assembly or maintenance of the cilium without directly eliminating the dynein arms that produce the beat.

A simplified radial transport experiment illustrates the causal distinction. If dynein-1 is selectively impaired while plus-end motors and tracks remain functional, inward runs may fall and cargo may redistribute towards the periphery. The result is consistent with loss of minus-end transport, but a steady-state distribution also reflects anchoring, organelle maturation and regulation of other motors. Live movement trajectories are more informative than a single image of cargo localization.

7. Motor recruitment, organelle positioning and track switching

A vesicle must first be formed at a donor membrane. Its coat and membrane proteins support selection of cargo and budding. After release, transport-associated proteins can recruit cytoskeletal motors to carry it through crowded cytoplasm. Rab proteins and their effectors often help organize this recruitment. At its destination, tethering and fusion machinery establish a specific membrane interaction. These are consecutive but distinct tasks: a motor cannot automatically select a correct target membrane merely by reaching its vicinity.

Microtubules often support long-range movements between central and peripheral regions of animal cells. Near the cortex, local actin networks can support delivery by myosins. A secretory vesicle may therefore move outward on a kinesin and then switch to an actin-based system before docking and fusion. This is one common organizational strategy, not an obligatory itinerary for all vesicles. In other contexts, diffusion and capture or repeated exchanges between tracks contribute to delivery. The arrangement depends on cell architecture and cargo-specific interactions.

Cargoes can carry motors of opposing directional preference at the same time. Time-lapse recordings may reveal outward runs, pauses and inward runs. A tug-of-war model imagines motors exerting opposing forces on the cargo; this can explain some behaviors. In other situations, regulatory proteins coordinate motor activation and prevent continuous unproductive opposition. A movement trace alone cannot prove that a cargo's reversal was caused by a mechanical tug-of-war. Measuring motor occupancy, catalytic activity and the effects of selective perturbations helps discriminate competing explanations.

Axonal microtubules are predominantly plus-end-out, so conventional kinesins commonly move cargo anterogradely from the cell body and cytoplasmic dynein moves cargo retrogradely. Dendritic microtubule polarity can be mixed. In epithelial cells, noncentrosomal arrays may have distinctive orientation. These examples reinforce the reference-frame rule: plus-end and minus-end are molecular directions, whereas anterograde and retrograde describe transport relative to an anatomical arrangement.

A steady-state organelle distribution reflects delivery, departure, local formation and anchoring. If lysosomes accumulate near a nucleus after a treatment, the observation is compatible with reduced outward transport, increased inward transport or altered retention. An imaging assay measuring runs in each direction can distinguish several of these possibilities. Measuring cargo velocity alone is insufficient if the main change is the proportion of runs initiated. The cell uses motors not simply to maximize speed but to maintain the correct spatial distribution of organelles under changing demands.

Cytoskeletal transport also supports cell polarity. Local delivery of membrane proteins and organelles helps maintain specialized regions of a cell. Anchored motors exert forces on filament arrays and influence the positions of cellular structures. The same general machinery can therefore both deliver selected cargoes and help establish the organization of the tracks that support delivery.

8. Experimental evidence: motors versus tracks

Microscopy reveals organelles near cytoskeletal filaments, but proximity does not establish what generates motion. The squid giant axon supplied a classical experimental system because its axoplasm could be separated into components while preserving structures capable of movement. In 1985 Vale, Reese, Sheetz and colleagues investigated microtubule-dependent organelle transport. Single microtubules supported bidirectional movement. Fractionation and reconstruction revealed soluble motor activity, and identification of kinesin established a protein-based mechanism of directed movement. Separating proteins with opposite direction preferences showed that the microtubule need not reverse its structural polarity to support transport in both directions.

Consider the logic of a purified motility experiment. Microtubules are immobilized on a surface and motor-coated beads are observed after ATP is provided. Directed runs along the tracks, absent in suitable controls lacking motor or ATP, support the conclusion that the motor–track system is sufficient for translocation under those conditions. Marking the microtubule ends independently allows the direction preference to be determined. Beads without motor help exclude bulk flow, while a catalytic mutant helps distinguish attachment from ATP-coupled movement.

Reverse the assay geometry by attaching motors to a surface while leaving the microtubules free to move. A plus-end-directed kinesin attempts to walk towards the plus end, but because the kinesin is fixed, the microtubule glides in the opposite direction relative to the surface. Its minus end leads. This gliding assay does not establish that the motor has switched its intrinsic polarity. It shows that the measured motion depends on which object is held fixed.

A purified system can demonstrate sufficiency under defined conditions, but it cannot by itself establish the complete set of physiological cargoes or regulatory pathways in a living cell. A loss-of-function mutant may demonstrate necessity for a particular cellular behavior, but secondary effects may complicate interpretation. Combining direct motor activity with loss-of-function and rescue experiments gives a stronger causal argument. A tail mutant that still moves in a purified assay but fails to transport vesicles in cells suggests a problem in attachment or regulation rather than ATP hydrolysis itself.

Modern fluorescence imaging can measure individual cargo trajectories, run velocities and pauses. A calibrated optical trap can impose a force and record the displacement of a motor-associated bead. Electron microscopy reveals filament arrangements and axonemal structures but does not directly measure motion. Different methods answer different questions, so structural evidence must be connected to functional tests before drawing mechanistic conclusions.

9. Quantitative foundations of motor-driven transport

For unbiased diffusion in one dimension, the mean squared displacement of an ensemble is 2Dt, where D is the diffusion coefficient and t is time. In isotropic three-dimensional free diffusion it is 6Dt. These equations describe an average of squared displacements, not a guaranteed first-arrival time. Crowded cellular environments may depart from ideal free diffusion. Nevertheless, the comparison provides a useful physical baseline for understanding the advantages of directed movement.

For a motor moving steadily along a straight track with speed v, distance L is covered in time L/v. Setting the root-mean-square one-dimensional diffusive displacement equal to L gives the characteristic diffusion time L²/(2D). Doubling L doubles the motor travel time but quadruples the diffusive timescale. For an illustrative cargo with D = 0.05 µm²/s and L = 10 µm, diffusion has a characteristic timescale of 100/(2 × 0.05) = 1000 s. A motor at 1 µm/s covers the distance in 10 s. This hundredfold difference illustrates why active transport can be valuable for large cargoes over long distances; it is not a universal numerical property of cells.

If a motor takes n equal steps of length d, its net distance in the idealized forward-only case is nd. If its mean stepping rate is f, its travel time is n/f, giving v = fd. A conventional kinesin-1 moving 2.4 µm in 8 nm steps covers 2400 nm and requires 300 net steps. At 100 steps/s its velocity is 800 nm/s = 0.8 µm/s and the trip takes 3 s. Assuming one ATP per forward cycle, the ideal consumption is 300 ATP molecules. Actual ATP use can be higher if the motor pauses, reverses, cycles without progress, or operates together with additional motors. The simple displacement relation estimates a specified idealized case, not a measured cellular ATP budget.

Mechanical work against a constant opposing force F is W = Fd. One piconewton is 10⁻¹² N and one nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m, so one pN·nm equals 10⁻²¹ J. A motor taking an 8 nm step against 5 pN performs 40 pN·nm, or 4.0 × 10⁻²⁰ J, of external work. Assume ATP hydrolysis provides a free-energy magnitude of 50 kJ/mol in the given environment. Dividing 50,000 J/mol by Avogadro's constant, 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, gives 8.30 × 10⁻²⁰ J, or approximately 83 pN·nm, per ATP.

Under a one-ATP-per-step assumption, the fraction of chemical free energy captured as external work in this example is 40/83 ≈ 48%. The ideal energy-limited force would be 83/8 ≈ 10.4 pN. This is an upper bound under the assumed coupling and not a prediction of the actual stall force, which depends on kinetics and dissipation. The available free energy of ATP depends on concentrations and conditions, so the standard-state value must not be treated as an invariant physiological energy.

Power is work per unit time. For steady displacement against a constant load it is P = Fv. A motor traveling at 0.4 µm/s against 5 pN performs work at 5 × 400 = 2000 pN·nm/s = 2 × 10⁻¹⁸ W. At stall its average velocity is zero, so its net external translational power is zero despite the presence of an opposing force. This is not the same as claiming no ATP turnover.

Finally, a cargo that moves 4 µm outward and 3 µm inward over a total of 10 s travels a total distance of 7 µm but is displaced only 1 µm outward. Its average speed is 0.7 µm/s while its signed mean velocity is +0.1 µm/s if outward is positive. A treatment may decrease mean net velocity by increasing pauses or reversals without changing the speed of individual runs. These measurements reveal different aspects of motor function and should not be substituted for one another.
