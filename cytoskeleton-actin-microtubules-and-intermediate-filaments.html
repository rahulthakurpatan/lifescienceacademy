1. A cell is organized, not merely enclosed

A thin plasma membrane separates the cell from its surroundings, but it cannot by itself explain the shape of a neuron, the resistance of a skin cell to pulling or the position of the Golgi apparatus near a nucleus. A membrane is a flexible two-dimensional fluid. If a large animal cell contained only membrane and freely diffusing proteins, its contents would have no reliable spatial arrangement. Moreover, a cell must change shape without tearing apart. It must solve the apparently contradictory problems of maintaining a structure and repeatedly rebuilding that structure.

The solution is a collection of protein polymers distributed through the cytoplasm and, in some cases, along the inner face of the nuclear envelope. Together with the proteins that connect, regulate and move along them, these polymers form the cytoskeleton. The word skeleton may suggest a rigid, permanent framework, but that impression is misleading. Most actin filaments and many microtubules exchange their molecular subunits even when the overall shape of the cell looks unchanged. Intermediate filaments usually turn over more slowly, yet they too can be reorganized. The living cell is therefore closer to a continuously maintained network than to a building made of immovable beams.

The organelles studied earlier make this problem concrete. A mitochondrion must be positioned where energy is required; a secretory vesicle must travel towards a selected membrane; a chloroplast must function within an organized plant cytoplasm. Chemical targeting signals specify the destination of a cargo, whereas cytoskeletal organization determines many of the physical routes and positions that make targeted delivery possible. The two explanations are complementary. Filaments do not replace membrane recognition machinery, and a sorting signal alone cannot move a large cargo across a long cellular distance.

Three major systems are recognized in typical animal cells: actin filaments (microfilaments), microtubules and intermediate filaments. Their typical diameters are about 7, 25 and 10 nm respectively. These are not three thicknesses of a single protein. Each is assembled from different molecular building blocks, has a different architecture and specializes in different kinds of cellular work. Plants and fungi also have extensive actin and microtubule systems, although they lack the conventional animal-type cytoplasmic intermediate filament network. The animal cell is a useful starting point, not a universal blueprint.

To understand any filament, ask four questions. What is its subunit? How do the subunits assemble into a long structure? What determines when the structure grows, shrinks or changes position? Finally, how does that behavior solve a biological problem? These questions lead naturally from protein structure and chemistry to cell shape, mechanical stability, polarity and organelle organization.

2. From soluble proteins to mechanically useful polymers

A polymer is a structure made of repeated units. In the cytoskeleton the building blocks are already complete polypeptides or protein complexes, not amino acids being joined by new peptide bonds. Polymerization ordinarily involves reversible, noncovalent contacts between neighboring subunits. A large number of individually weak contacts can create a stable filament, while the loss of contacts at an exposed end allows regulated disassembly. The same pool of protein can thus be redistributed much faster than it could be replaced by protein synthesis and degradation.

When a subunit joins a filament, it sacrifices some freedom of movement in solution but gains favorable interactions with its neighbors. Assembly is favored when the combined chemical conditions, including the amount of assembly-competent subunit, make the assembled state more favorable. At low concentration the probability of maintaining the necessary contacts is reduced. At higher concentration encounters become more frequent, and an existing filament can elongate. The free concentration of competent subunits is consequently more informative than the total concentration of cytoskeletal protein in the cell.

The first few subunits often form an unstable cluster because there are fewer stabilizing contacts than in a mature filament. This creates a nucleation barrier. Once a sufficiently stable nucleus exists, additional subunits attach much more readily. A reaction starting with only free subunits can show an initial lag followed by a faster elongation phase and finally a plateau in net polymer mass. Adding preformed fragments or a nucleator reduces the lag without necessarily increasing the final polymer mass. This distinction is essential when interpreting a kinetic graph: a faster start need not mean a different final equilibrium or steady state.

The geometry of the contact surface determines whether a polymer has polarity. If asymmetric subunits are all aligned in the same orientation, one end exposes a different molecular surface from the other end. The polymer has a structural plus end and minus end. These names do not refer to electrical charges. They identify molecular orientation; the two ends may also have different association and dissociation rates. Actin and microtubules are polar because of their aligned subunits. The antiparallel assembly of intermediate filament tetramers largely removes this directional asymmetry.

Mechanical function also emerges from geometry. A thick hollow tube is relatively resistant to bending because much of its material lies away from its central axis. A thinner filament bends more readily and can be arranged into tight bundles or networks. A rope-like filament made of many interacting proteins can tolerate tension and deformation. No single material is optimal for all three jobs. The cytoskeleton distributes mechanical tasks among networks and then connects them so that a force applied at the membrane can be communicated to the cell interior.

There is a second distinction between a polymer's structure and the energy-consuming processes that maintain it. ATP-bound actin and GTP-bound tubulin are important assembly-competent forms. Hydrolysis after incorporation changes the stability and behavior of the polymer. The hydrolysis energy is not a requirement for every individual association event; association can occur through favorable binding. Instead nucleotide hydrolysis and exchange drive a cycle that enables the population of filaments to remain dynamic and out of thermodynamic equilibrium. Intermediate filament assembly lacks an equivalent intrinsic ATP/GTP hydrolysis cycle, although ATP-dependent kinases and other regulators still control these filaments.

3. Actin architecture: from G-actin to F-actin

Actin is an abundant globular protein of approximately 42 kDa. The soluble monomer is called G-actin. It has a cleft that binds ATP or ADP together with a divalent cation, commonly Mg²⁺ under cellular conditions. The protein has a particular three-dimensional orientation, with molecular surfaces that fit differently against the next subunit. When monomers assemble head-to-tail, they form filamentous actin, or F-actin. The mature filament can be described as two intertwined helical strands or as a single helical chain of subunits; these are alternative ways of describing the same polymer architecture.

The typical filament diameter is approximately 7 nm. Actin subunits along the filament are displaced axially by about 2.75 nm per subunit on average; because of the helical arrangement, consecutive subunits alternate around the filament axis. A filament is not a hollow tube like a microtubule. Its relatively small diameter allows it to follow curved membrane surfaces and pack into bundles that support microvilli and other thin protrusions.

Because every subunit has the same molecular orientation, the two ends differ. They are commonly called the barbed (plus) and pointed (minus) ends. These names arose from the arrowhead pattern observed when myosin fragments decorate actin filaments in electron microscopy. The pattern provided a visible indicator of structural polarity long before fluorescent imaging could follow individual ends. At physiological conditions an uncapped barbed end usually adds actin more rapidly than a pointed end, but either end can grow or shrink depending on concentration and nucleotide state.

The actin family includes different isoforms. Muscle α-actins are important in contractile structures; β- and γ-cytoplasmic actins are widespread in nonmuscle animal cells. The general assembly principles apply to both, yet the structures produced depend strongly on associated proteins and cellular location. A bundle inside a microvillus is not chemically a different kind of filament from one in a fibroblast stress fiber. Its organization and partners differ. This is one reason that studying actin alone in solution cannot explain the full behavior of an intact cell.

When ATP-actin is incorporated, polymerization stimulates ATP hydrolysis. Phosphate release often occurs after hydrolysis, so a filament can contain regions enriched in ATP-actin, ADP–Pi-actin and older ADP-actin. These nucleotide states influence the strength of contacts and the binding of regulatory proteins. In a growing filament newly added subunits tend to be closer to the barbed end, whereas many older ADP-rich subunits lie further behind. This is a statistical age pattern rather than a perfectly sharp chemical boundary. Hydrolysis and phosphate release are separate molecular events.

An actin filament is thus both a mechanical object and a record of recent assembly. Its nucleotide-dependent properties allow accessory proteins to discriminate between recently formed and older regions. This principle helps explain how the same cell can preserve a long-lived microvillus while rapidly replacing short-lived cortical networks in a neighboring region.

4. Critical concentration and the logic of actin treadmilling

The simplest useful model separates addition from loss at one end of a filament. Let C be the free concentration of assembly-competent actin, kon the association rate constant at a specified end and koff the effective dissociation rate constant at that end. An association event requires a collision with a free monomer, so the number of additions per second is proportional to C. Dissociation does not require a free monomer, so its rate can be approximated by a constant for an exposed end of a given nucleotide composition.

The net rate r is additions minus losses. If C is expressed in μM, kon has units μM⁻¹ s⁻¹, and koff has units s⁻¹. The net result has units subunits per second at a single filament end. A positive result means elongation and a negative result means net shortening. At the concentration where the two rates exactly balance, the end has zero net length change even though association and dissociation can both occur. Setting r = 0 and solving defines the end-specific critical concentration Cc.

r = konC − koff; at r = 0, Cc = koff/kon. Therefore r = kon(C − Cc).

For an uncapped ATP-actin filament under commonly cited in vitro conditions, the apparent critical concentration of the barbed end is roughly 0.1 μM and that of the pointed end is roughly 0.6 μM. These values are not universal constants: nucleotide state, salt, accessory proteins and measurement conditions affect them. They are useful as a standard example of two ends having different effective kinetic behavior in a nucleotide-hydrolyzing system.

Three concentration regimes now follow directly. Below both critical concentrations, an exposed filament tends to lose subunits at both ends. Above both values, it can add at both ends. Between the two values, the barbed end has net addition while the pointed end has net loss. In this intermediate regime the filament can exchange subunits directionally. If the addition rate at one end exactly equals the loss rate at the other, its total length remains constant while actin subunits flow through the polymer in a process called treadmilling. If the rates are unequal, directional exchange can coexist with net elongation or net shortening; merely lying between the critical concentrations does not guarantee constant length.

The distinction is important because no monomer needs to crawl through the filament interior. An individual labeled subunit that joins at a fixed growing end becomes part of the lattice. Relative to the moving boundaries it becomes progressively older and may eventually leave at the shortening end. The whole filament need not translate through space. Nor does treadmilling mean that one whole actin filament repeatedly vanishes and reappears; it is a flux of subunits through a polymer that can retain a relatively steady length.

The apparent difference between end-specific critical concentrations relies on a driven cycle with hydrolysis and nucleotide-state changes. At true equilibrium with identical chemical states, detailed balance constrains net flux. A sustained directional cycle cannot occur indefinitely without an energy source. ATP hydrolysis and recharging of released actin support non-equilibrium turnover. In a cell the effective kinetics are further modified by end-binding proteins and monomer-binding proteins, so the simple linear model is a starting point rather than a complete prediction.

Consider an illustrative set of rate constants: barbed-end kon = 10 μM⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 1 s⁻¹; pointed-end kon = 2 μM⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 1.2 s⁻¹. The critical concentrations are 0.1 and 0.6 μM. At C = 0.20 μM, r+ = 10 × 0.20 − 1 = 1.0 subunit s⁻¹; r− = 2 × 0.20 − 1.2 = −0.8 subunit s⁻¹. The whole filament therefore gains 0.2 subunit s⁻¹ on average. Using the 2.75 nm axial increment, its length increases by about 0.55 nm per second, even though its two ends show opposing behavior.

To find the steady-length concentration in this idealized two-end model, require r+ + r− = 0. Substitution gives 12C − 2.2 = 0, or C = 0.1833 μM. The barbed end then adds approximately 0.833 subunit s⁻¹ and the pointed end loses approximately 0.833 subunit s⁻¹. That is the particular concentration for steady-length treadmilling in this model, rather than every concentration between 0.1 and 0.6 μM. All rates are ensemble averages; individual addition and loss events are discrete and stochastic.

This kind of calculation also clarifies why capping one end changes the problem. If a barbed-end capping protein eliminates exchange there while the pointed end remains accessible at 0.20 μM, the only modeled rate is −0.8 subunit s⁻¹. The filament shortens unless other processes intervene. If instead the pointed end is capped, the exposed barbed end elongates under the same assumptions. An exam question may provide the same free actin concentration in both situations; the different result follows from the different end boundary conditions.

5. Actin-binding proteins turn polymerization into architecture

The cytoplasm contains many actin monomers, but cells do not allow all of them to polymerize indiscriminately. They regulate the availability of monomers, the number of growing ends, the rate of elongation and the arrangement of completed filaments. Understanding these control points makes it possible to predict what happens when a particular accessory protein is lost.

Profilin binds G-actin and promotes nucleotide exchange, favoring the supply of ATP-actin. It can deliver monomers to growing barbed ends, especially in association with formins. Its effect depends on which partners are present; it is not simply an unconditional activator of all actin assembly. Thymosin β4, in contrast, can bind and sequester a reservoir of monomers in animal cells, reducing the pool directly available for ordinary filament growth. The distinction between total monomer and free assembly-competent monomer explains how an apparently abundant cellular actin pool can coexist with tightly localized assembly.

A cell also controls where new filaments begin. Formins can nucleate relatively unbranched actin filaments and remain associated with their growing barbed ends while permitting addition. Their action supports structures in which long filaments are useful, including many contractile bundles. The Arp2/3 complex, activated by nucleation-promoting factors, typically binds to the side of a pre-existing filament and initiates a daughter filament at about 70°. Repeated branching creates a dense network. A branched network and an unbranched bundle may use the same subunits, but they distribute force very differently.

The angle and location of branching matter. A dense array with many barbed ends directed towards a membrane can distribute polymerization-induced pushing across a broad front. A parallel bundle focuses structural support along a narrow projection. A cell does not need an entirely new set of proteins to switch between these geometries; changes in the local activity of nucleators, elongators and cross-linkers can reorganize the same actin system. More detailed protrusion and movement mechanisms are considered in the next chapter.

Capping proteins bind filament ends and block exchange. Barbed-end capping limits continued elongation and can make monomers available to other growing ends. Stabilizing minus-end caps are important in particular long-lived structures. Severing proteins alter the number of ends by splitting filaments. Gelsolin, for example, can sever actin and cap the newly formed barbed end, with regulation by calcium and phosphoinositides. Severing does not automatically cause only disassembly: newly generated ends may permit growth or shrinkage depending on whether they are capped and on the free monomer concentration.

Cofilin preferentially interacts with older ADP-actin regions and promotes filament turnover, in part by changing filament properties and facilitating severing at boundaries between decorated and undecorated regions. The released monomers can exchange ADP for ATP, often aided by profilin, and re-enter assembly. Thus a network may rapidly renew itself without continuously synthesizing new actin. Stabilizing proteins such as tropomyosins can protect selected filaments and influence which other proteins bind. This creates regional differences in filament lifetime.

Imagine a cell in which nucleation is severely inhibited but the total actin concentration remains normal. Existing filaments may still elongate, yet the cell has fewer sites at which new networks can arise. In a different cell, excess sequestration of monomers reduces growth even if nucleation sites remain present. A third perturbation that removes capping may produce overly long filaments and deplete the local monomer pool. These are different failures, not interchangeable examples of 'actin dysfunction'. A strong answer identifies the control point and follows its consequences to network organization.

6. Actin networks at the cell surface and in the cytoplasm

The cell cortex is a layer of cytoskeletal material beneath the plasma membrane, particularly rich in actin in animal cells. The membrane itself resists stretching of its lipid bilayer but bends easily and cannot maintain a complex shape unaided. A connected cortical actin network supplies shear resistance and distributes mechanical stress over a larger area. It also provides a substrate for regulated contractile tension and for anchoring many membrane-associated proteins.

The cortical network is not an impermeable shell. It contains spaces through which cytoplasmic molecules and many membrane trafficking events can occur. Local remodeling may be required for a vesicle to approach or depart from the membrane. The cortical mesh can also restrict the lateral movement of some membrane proteins through direct or indirect attachment. This builds upon the idea of a fluid membrane: the lipid bilayer can remain fluid even when underlying structures constrain the trajectories of selected proteins.

A particularly instructive example is the erythrocyte. A mature mammalian red blood cell lacks the ordinary organelle complement and does not contain the extensive microtubule network found in a typical nucleated cell. Instead, its membrane is supported by a spectrin-based network connected through short actin filaments and other junctional proteins. Ankyrin helps attach spectrin to membrane proteins such as band 3; protein 4.1R contributes to junctional stability and membrane connections. This elastic network lets the red cell deform through narrow passages and recover its shape. Disrupting the attachments can weaken membrane stability even when the lipid bilayer itself has not changed its chemical composition.

Microvilli illustrate a different solution. These narrow projections increase surface area and contain parallel actin bundles whose barbed ends are generally directed towards their tips. Bundling proteins such as fimbrin (plastin) and villin help maintain the core in appropriate cells; membrane linkers couple the bundle to the surrounding membrane. The basal region connects to an underlying terminal web. An intestinal epithelial cell can therefore create a highly organized apical surface without using a microtubule-based axoneme. Microvilli should not be confused with cilia, which have microtubule-based cores and are studied in the motility chapter.

Other actin arrays include stress fibers, in which actin bundles, cross-linking proteins and nonmuscle myosin II support tension and adhesion. α-Actinin can space neighboring actin filaments so that myosin has room to act. In contrast, closely packed parallel bundles made by proteins such as fascin or fimbrin are useful for narrow protrusions and generally have less room for myosin II assemblies. The architecture of the bundle is therefore as important as the identity of the individual filament.

At the end of animal cell division, actin and myosin form a contractile ring that constricts the cell cortex. Its location and timing depend on signals linked to the division apparatus. The ring demonstrates that actin organization is reversible: a cell can build a new circular structure in a specific region and later dismantle it. The complete regulation of chromosome separation and cytokinesis belongs to the cell-cycle chapter; here the ring is an example of actin-based structural remodeling.

Actin-rich regions are also involved in the changing shape of endocytic membranes, in local anchoring and in the organization of the cell periphery. In plants, long actin arrays help organize the cytoplasm and support organelle movement, even though a cell wall and turgor provide much of the gross mechanical support. A rigid external wall does not eliminate the need to organize the living interior.

7. Microtubule architecture: the polarized hollow tube

Microtubules are usually the largest-diameter of the three cytoskeletal filaments. A standard cytoplasmic microtubule has an outer diameter of approximately 25 nm, with a hollow lumen. Its subunit is not an individual tubulin polypeptide but a stable heterodimer of α-tubulin and β-tubulin, each about 50 kDa. The αβ pair acts as the repeating unit during ordinary assembly. The two proteins have related folds yet occupy distinct positions and have distinct nucleotide-binding behavior.

Heterodimers align head-to-tail to form a protofilament. Parallel protofilaments associate laterally to create a closed tube, typically with 13 protofilaments in cytoplasmic microtubules. Each successive heterodimer contributes about 8 nm to the axial length of one protofilament. Most cellular microtubules have 13 protofilaments, but variations occur in specialized structures and in vitro; thirteen is the standard exam model, not a universal geometric necessity. The doublet and triplet microtubules of cilia and centrioles are specialized assemblies, not independent ordinary hollow tubes of identical completeness.

The common orientation of heterodimers creates polarity. At a typical plus end the terminal exposed subunit is β-tubulin, while at the minus end α-tubulin is exposed. Both ends can undergo subunit exchange if accessible, but their rates and regulation differ. In many animal interphase cells the minus end is attached to a microtubule organizing region, while the plus end extends into the cytoplasm and explores peripheral space. A minus end can therefore appear stable not because all minus ends are intrinsically permanent but because anchoring or capping suppresses its dynamics.

Each αβ heterodimer binds two GTP molecules in the GTP-loaded form. The nucleotide associated with α-tubulin is nonexchangeable in the intact dimer under ordinary conditions and is not the nucleotide hydrolyzed during normal polymer dynamics. The β-tubulin site is exchangeable in the free dimer. After the dimer enters the microtubule, GTP at this β site can be hydrolyzed, influencing the stability of the assembled lattice. Confusing the two nucleotide sites obscures the mechanism of dynamic instability.

The hollow tubular geometry makes microtubules relatively stiff. They can span long distances, resist bending more effectively than an isolated actin filament and provide directional tracks for intracellular transport. But stiffness is not invulnerability: long microtubules may buckle under compression, and their lattice can disassemble extremely rapidly under appropriate conditions. The cell supports and regulates the tubes using surrounding networks and microtubule-associated proteins.

The length of a microtubule can also be related to its molecular composition. A 4 μm long ideal 13-protofilament microtubule has length 4000 nm. Each protofilament contains about 4000/8 = 500 αβ dimers. Multiplying by 13 gives approximately 6500 dimers, or 13,000 individual tubulin polypeptides. This calculation assumes full-length protofilaments and neglects tapering ends and lattice defects. It is important to multiply by the number of protofilaments only after calculating dimers per protofilament; counting 4 nm per heterodimer would incorrectly double the result.

8. GTP caps and dynamic instability

Microtubule elongation begins when GTP-loaded tubulin dimers add to a competent end. Favorable longitudinal and lateral contacts hold the dimers within the wall. The growing end is usually enriched in relatively recently incorporated GTP-containing tubulin and related stabilizing nucleotide states. This stabilizing region is commonly called the GTP cap. The term is a useful mechanistic model; the real end is not necessarily a flat ring of identical nucleotide states.

After incorporation, GTP hydrolysis at β-tubulin produces GDP and inorganic phosphate. GDP-tubulin constrained within the wall can remain in the comparatively straight lattice even though it favors a more curved conformation outside that context. The surrounding lateral contacts help hold it in place. This creates a stored structural tendency to curve, which becomes especially important at an unstable end.

As long as addition and the end structure maintain an adequate stabilizing region, a microtubule can grow. If the cap becomes too small or unstable, contacts near the end can fail. Protofilaments may peel outward and GDP-tubulin is released rapidly. The abrupt transition from growth to rapid shortening is called catastrophe. A switch from shortening back to growth is rescue. Rescue can occur when a shrinking end encounters a stabilizing lattice region or other factors that allow a growing end to be re-established. The molecular details can vary; rescue is a defined observable transition, not proof of one universal rescue mechanism.

This behavior was established especially clearly by classic work of Mitchison and Kirschner in 1984. Instead of assuming that a population of microtubules all changes length together, their experiments demonstrated how growing and shrinking filaments can coexist under the same solution conditions. One filament may be growing while another is shrinking rapidly because each end has its own history of nucleotide states and stabilizing contacts. Modern time-lapse imaging of individual fluorescent microtubules makes the same logic especially intuitive: a length-versus-time trace contains alternating slopes with identifiable transitions.

Dynamic instability must not be confused with treadmilling. Treadmilling concerns opposing net subunit fluxes at the two ends of a polar filament; dynamic instability concerns a single end switching between growth and shrinkage. Both depend on nucleotide-driven dynamics and may coexist in some conditions, but the characteristic observations are different. A microtubule whose minus end is anchored can still show dynamic instability at its plus end; simultaneous plus-end addition and minus-end loss are not required.

The rate of tubulin addition depends on the free tubulin supply and the end configuration. Under otherwise comparable conditions, a reduction in available GTP-tubulin can slow elongation, giving hydrolysis and end rearrangements more opportunity to destabilize the growing region. The catastrophe frequency may consequently increase. Conversely, increasing available tubulin can often favor longer growth episodes, but the result in living cells also depends on regulators, mechanical interactions and stabilization at particular sites. It is safer to reason from cap formation and loss than to memorize a universal monotonic drug-response rule.

Why would a cell invest energy in a structure that repeatedly disassembles? A permanent microtubule array would be poor at adapting to changing cell geometry. A dynamic array can explore space with its growing ends. When a growing microtubule meets a suitable anchoring or stabilizing region at the cortex, it can persist there longer than neighboring filaments. Others disappear and return their subunits to the soluble pool. Local differences in stability can gradually establish a directional array even when the cell initially nucleates many microtubules in different directions. This search-and-capture principle is a bridge to organelle organization and the mitotic spindle, without requiring the detailed cell-cycle mechanisms here.

A stable subset of microtubules can coexist with a highly dynamic population. In a long cellular extension, preserving some tracks for transport is useful; near a remodeling cell edge, rapid exploration is useful. Nucleotide turnover, end binding, lattice stabilization, anchoring and severing provide separate ways to tune the lifetime and spatial pattern of a microtubule. One should not infer that every microtubule in a cell has the same lifetime simply because every tube is made of αβ-tubulin.

9. Quantitative reasoning about microtubule growth and shrinkage

A single-filament time series provides more information than a photograph of an entire cytoskeleton. Imagine measuring the distance between a fixed microtubule minus end and its plus end at successive times. An increasing distance indicates net growth; a decreasing distance indicates net shrinkage. The slope of length against time gives the net velocity. This velocity is not the speed of a motor traveling along the microtubule. It is the rate at which the boundary of the polymer itself changes position.

For a constant growth episode, velocity v = ΔL/Δt. When length is measured in μm and time in minutes, v is expressed in μm min⁻¹. A decreasing length produces a negative signed velocity; shrinkage speed is often reported as the positive magnitude of that velocity. Using a consistent sign convention prevents mistakes when combining growth and shortening phases. The total change over successive episodes is the algebraic sum of their individual changes.

Consider an illustrative trace: at t = 0 the length is 2 μm. At 3 min it reaches 5 μm, at 4 min it has shortened to 2 μm, and at 7 min it has grown to 5 μm again. The first growth velocity is (5 − 2)/3 = 1 μm min⁻¹. Shrinkage occurs at signed velocity (2 − 5)/1 = −3 μm min⁻¹. The second growth velocity is (5 − 2)/3 = 1 μm min⁻¹. A catastrophe occurs at 3 min and a rescue at 4 min. The total length increases by 3 μm over seven minutes, despite a 3 μm loss during the intermediate episode. The net average velocity is 3/7 ≈ 0.429 μm min⁻¹; it would be wrong to average the three phase velocities without weighting by their durations.

Transition frequency is normally defined using the time spent in the relevant starting state. In the example, there is one catastrophe over six total minutes of growth, giving an observed catastrophe frequency of 1/6 ≈ 0.167 min⁻¹. There is one rescue over one minute of shortening, giving an observed rescue frequency of 1 min⁻¹. These estimates use a single short trace and are not reliable population parameters. A real experiment requires many events, sufficient imaging duration and a consistent definition of paused states and transitions.

A second illustrative experiment measures many filaments. In control cells there are 24 catastrophes during 120 total minutes of observed growth, and 12 rescues during 30 minutes of shortening. Under treatment X there are 40 catastrophes during 100 minutes of growth and six rescues during 40 minutes of shortening. The catastrophe frequency increases from 0.20 to 0.40 min⁻¹, while rescue frequency decreases from 0.40 to 0.15 min⁻¹. The pattern predicts shorter persistence of growing filaments and fewer switches back to growth, although the final length distribution also depends on growth and shortening velocities, nucleation and stabilization. These data alone cannot identify the molecular drug target.

To connect the measured velocity with molecular turnover, consider a 13-protofilament microtubule elongating at 0.48 μm min⁻¹. Convert the velocity to 480 nm min⁻¹. Dividing by 8 nm per dimer axial repeat gives 60 dimers min⁻¹ per protofilament. Multiplying by 13 gives a net incorporation of approximately 780 dimers min⁻¹ across the wall, or 13 dimers s⁻¹. This is a net rate, not the gross number of association events; simultaneous losses could be occurring. It also assumes a uniform 13-protofilament cylindrical model.

The same logic allows an exam question to distinguish a polymerization defect from a motor defect. If the length trace and end transition frequencies are normal but cargo movement along otherwise intact tracks is reduced, the primary problem may lie in the motor or cargo attachment rather than in microtubule assembly. Conversely, abnormal length dynamics with an intact motor protein can still disrupt cargo distribution because the tracks themselves are no longer properly organized. Correct interpretation requires matching the measured quantity to the underlying process.

10. Centrosomes, microtubule-organizing centers and nucleation

A cell faces a spatial problem: even if tubulin is abundant, randomly initiating filaments throughout the cytoplasm would not reliably establish a coordinated array. A microtubule-organizing center (MTOC) is a region where microtubules are nucleated, anchored, or organized. In many animal cells the major interphase MTOC is the centrosome, usually located near the nucleus. Its position helps establish an approximately radial network with many minus ends near the center and plus ends directed towards the periphery.

The typical animal centrosome contains a pair of centrioles surrounded by pericentriolar material (PCM). Each centriole is a cylindrical structure with nine microtubule triplets arranged around its circumference. The two centrioles are often oriented approximately at right angles. The triplets consist of an A tubule and incomplete B and C tubules that share parts of their walls. A centriole has no central pair of microtubules; the familiar 9+2 arrangement belongs to many motile ciliary axonemes, not centrioles.

A centriole is not simply the point at which every cytoplasmic microtubule directly grows from its own wall. Much nucleation occurs in the PCM surrounding the centrioles. A key component is γ-tubulin, which is distinct from the αβ dimers making up the microtubule lattice. γ-Tubulin ring complexes (γ-TuRCs) provide a template that helps organize the first αβ dimers into a suitable arrangement, lowering the nucleation barrier. The nucleating template is associated with the minus end; the newly formed plus end can extend away from the organizing site.

This separation between a nucleation site and a polymer lattice explains a useful experimental distinction. A cell lacking functional γ-tubulin recruitment can have abundant soluble αβ-tubulin yet nucleate fewer microtubules at centrosomes. By contrast, a cell with normal γ-TuRC function but defective αβ-tubulin polymerization might form initial assemblies that fail to elongate or persist. Both perturbations can reduce the number of visible microtubules, but an assay measuring new filament emergence and individual elongation rates can separate them.

The centrosome is duplicated in coordination with the animal cell cycle and contributes to organizing the division apparatus. The detailed duplication cycle, spindle mechanics and chromosome segregation belong to the later cell-cycle subunit. For the present purpose, note that a change in the number, distribution or activity of MTOCs can reorganize the entire orientation of the microtubule network.

Centrosomes are not the only MTOCs. Differentiated cells can maintain noncentrosomal arrays, for example at cortical sites, the nuclear envelope or the Golgi apparatus. Many higher plant cells lack conventional centriole-containing centrosomes; nevertheless they organize abundant microtubules at dispersed sites, including sites associated with existing microtubules and the cell cortex. The fission yeast spindle pole body is another example of a microtubule-organizing structure distinct from a typical animal centrosome. The function of organizing and nucleating microtubules is therefore more general than the presence of a centriole.

After depolymerizing a microtubule array, researchers can observe where microtubules first reappear during recovery. Rapid appearance near a centrosome supports localized nucleation or anchoring there. It does not prove that all microtubules in the original cell had been nucleated exclusively at that location. Some filaments may arise at other sites or detach and be reorganized. A good interpretation describes what the recovery observation establishes and what remains open.

11. Microtubule-associated proteins and specialized cellular arrays

Microtubules need accessory proteins for the same general reason that actin does: the cell must determine where a filament starts, how long it persists and how it interacts with its surroundings. Microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs) include lattice-binding stabilizers, proteins that recognize growing ends, destabilizing proteins, severing enzymes and factors that anchor or connect the array to other structures.

Classical neuronal MAPs illustrate stabilization and spacing. Tau is particularly associated with axonal microtubules, while MAP2 is enriched in dendrites. Their interactions influence microtubule organization, spacing and stability, but expression patterns and functions are not absolute in every neuron or developmental stage. A loss of a stabilizing MAP can reduce the lifetime of selected microtubules even if tubulin synthesis is unchanged. A change in phosphorylation can modify the affinity of some MAPs for the lattice, so signaling can regulate organization without changing the total amount of tubulin.

The EB family of plus-end tracking proteins recognizes the characteristic structural and nucleotide-state region near growing microtubule ends. Fluorescent EB proteins often form moving comet-like signals. A comet trajectory reports the direction and rate of a growing end's advance, not the movement of a motor-bound vesicle. Not every plus-end tracking protein directly recognizes the lattice in the same manner: some are recruited through interactions with EB proteins or other factors. In an exam, an EB signal is best interpreted as a marker of actively growing ends under suitable conditions rather than as a marker of all microtubule mass.

Stathmin can bind soluble tubulin and influence microtubule destabilization. By reducing the concentration of assembly-competent dimers, a regulator can alter the rate of cap renewal and the balance of microtubule growth. Katanin and related severing proteins act on the lattice and generate new ends. As with actin severing, the outcome depends on the fate of those ends: fragments may disassemble, be stabilized, or contribute to amplification of a network. Therefore an increase in severing is not automatically equivalent to a complete loss of polymer mass.

Microtubules organize the cytoplasm because they provide relatively stiff polarized tracks and because their ends and lattices can interact with selected cellular structures. In many animal cells the radial microtubule array helps maintain the Golgi apparatus near the centrosomal region, distributes endoplasmic reticulum tubules and influences the position of mitochondria, endosomes and other organelles. Positioning depends on anchoring and the balance of motor-driven forces, not merely on passive adhesion to any nearby tube. The detailed kinesin and dynein cycles are reserved for the next node.

A neuron demonstrates why the orientation of the array matters. Most axonal microtubules in vertebrates are oriented with plus ends towards the distal axon, while dendritic arrays often contain mixed orientations. Thus a movement direction defined relative to a filament end cannot always be translated into a movement direction relative to the entire cell without knowing the local array orientation. This principle also applies to epithelial cells whose arrays may become nonradial during differentiation.

In plants, cortical microtubules lie close to the inner surface of the plasma membrane and help guide the trajectories of cellulose synthase complexes, influencing the orientation of cellulose microfibril deposition in the wall. An organized wall resists extension more strongly along certain directions than others. Microtubule alignment can thus influence the direction of cell expansion through wall organization. The cytoskeleton does not itself synthesize cellulose, and wall deposition is also subject to other regulatory mechanisms. The example links intracellular architecture to the physical properties of an external cell wall.

12. Intermediate filaments: an apolar, tensile network

Actin and microtubules are made from globular subunits that use nucleotide-dependent cycles. Intermediate filaments are instead built from a diverse family of proteins whose shared structural feature is a central α-helical rod domain. The name intermediate refers to their usual diameter of about 10 nm, intermediate between the diameters of actin filaments and microtubules. Their subunit proteins vary widely in molecular mass, head and tail regions and tissue expression.

Two compatible polypeptides associate through their rod domains to make a parallel coiled-coil dimer. A coiled coil is formed when two α-helices wrap around each other and interact through regularly arranged side chains. The dimer has a directional organization because the two chains have their amino ends on the same side. Two dimers then associate in an antiparallel, staggered manner to form a tetramer. This arrangement places opposite orientations together, removing the persistent plus-versus-minus molecular identity characteristic of actin and microtubules.

Tetramers associate laterally into larger short assemblies, often called unit-length filaments, which join longitudinally and compact into mature filaments. This is the modern conceptual description of many cytoplasmic intermediate filament systems. Older textbook diagrams describe protofilaments and protofibrils as intermediate stages; they convey the same general idea of multiple intertwined protein assemblies, but the exact subunit counts and detailed packing should not be assumed identical for every filament type. Nuclear lamins share the coiled-coil design but have distinct assembly behavior, so their network should not simply be drawn as a conventional cylindrical cytoplasmic filament.

The antiparallel tetramer is the crucial exam-level structural feature. Since the mature cytoplasmic intermediate filament is effectively apolar, it does not provide the same directional substrate for kinesin or dynein as a microtubule, or for myosin as actin. It can nevertheless be positioned and reorganized through cross-linking and indirect interactions with motors acting on other filament systems. The absence of intrinsic polarity does not imply an absence of cellular organization.

Intermediate filament assembly does not require an intrinsic ATP or GTP binding and hydrolysis cycle analogous to actin or tubulin. This helps explain why its regulation does not ordinarily produce classical actin treadmilling or microtubule dynamic instability. However, intermediate filament proteins can be modified by kinases, which consume ATP, and filaments can exchange subunits, move, sever or remodel. Thus 'does not use ATP directly for polymerization' must not be turned into 'never uses energy in the cell'.

Their mechanical value lies in their ability to resist tension and tolerate substantial deformation. A filament that is pulled can redistribute strain through its many interacting subunits. A network of such filaments across the cytoplasm can help transmit tensile loads from cell attachments and protect delicate structures against deformation. This is distinct from the relatively stiff microtubule acting as a compression-resistant beam or an actin network whose tension is actively controlled by myosin. All three filament systems can bear mechanical loads, but they differ in how they do so.

An important consequence is that removing intermediate filaments may not immediately abolish the shape of a cell under gentle culture conditions. The defect may become apparent during stretching, repeated deformation or mechanical stress. The correct experiment should therefore test mechanical resilience, not just inspect an unstressed cell. This relationship between molecular architecture and the conditions under which a phenotype appears is a recurring theme in cytoskeletal biology.

13. Intermediate filament families and their tissue-specific functions

Unlike actin and tubulin, the identity of an intermediate filament protein can give strong information about the tissue in which it occurs. In epithelia, keratins form an extensive network. Acidic type I keratins generally pair with basic or neutral type II keratins to form heterodimers and ultimately filaments. Different epithelial cells express different keratin combinations, suited to their specialized environments. The filament network spans from regions near the nucleus to cell junctions and contributes to the mechanical continuity of epithelial sheets.

A mutation in keratin 5 or keratin 14 can produce epidermolysis bullosa simplex, in which basal epidermal cells become mechanically fragile and blistering can result from relatively minor stress. The essential lesson is structural, not clinical: impairment of a load-bearing intracellular filament network can make a tissue vulnerable even when its cells still contain their normal membrane lipids and many other proteins. Keratin molecules are not extracellular collagen; they bear forces inside the epithelial cells.

Vimentin is characteristic of many mesenchymal cells, including fibroblasts, and contributes to mechanical organization and organelle positioning. Desmin is prominent in muscle, where it helps connect and align the contractile apparatus and related cellular structures. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is associated particularly with astrocytes. Neurofilament proteins are important in neurons, especially long axons, where they contribute to axonal caliber and mechanical organization. These expression associations are useful markers but are not exclusive rules: co-expression and changes during differentiation occur.

Nuclear lamins are the major intermediate-filament-related proteins of the nuclear lamina. They form a network associated with the inner nuclear membrane, connect with nuclear-envelope proteins and contribute to nuclear shape and mechanical integrity. A- and B-type lamins differ in their regulation and association with the envelope. During open mitosis, phosphorylation of lamins contributes to lamina disassembly; dephosphorylation after chromosome segregation helps re-establish nuclear architecture. Some B-type lamins remain associated with membrane structures differently from A-type lamins. The key concept is reversible regulation, not permanent insolubility. Not all eukaryotes undergo open mitosis.

The nuclear lamina makes clear why classifying intermediate filaments exclusively as cytoplasmic would be incomplete. Both the cell surface and nucleus need mechanical protection. Through membrane-associated connections, changes in cytoplasmic forces can influence nuclear shape. This connection is useful when considering cell polarity and mechanical stress, while the detailed effects on chromatin and gene regulation belong in later chapters.

Intermediate filament proteins are often used as cellular identity markers by immunostaining. A keratin-positive population may support an epithelial identity, while a vimentin-positive population may support mesenchymal characteristics. Marker interpretation should consider morphology, additional markers and context, since a single marker can be expressed in multiple cell states. In a question presenting two cells with different filament proteins, the safest conclusion concerns probable tissue specialization, not a unique diagnosis from one marker.

14. Linking the cytoskeleton to membranes, adhesions and the nucleus

A filament has little value in supporting a membrane if there is no connection between them. Many membrane proteins and adapter proteins therefore create a physical bridge between the cytoskeletal network and another cellular or extracellular structure. This is particularly important in multicellular tissues, where forces must pass from one cell to another without rupturing their plasma membranes.

At an adherens junction, classical cadherins mediate calcium-dependent cell-cell adhesion. Their cytoplasmic regions associate with catenin-containing complexes that couple the adhesion to actin. In epithelial cells a belt-like arrangement of actin can run around the cell near the apical region. Changes in tension in such networks can affect the shape of neighboring cells because the junction couples them mechanically. The extracellular adhesion molecule and the intracellular load-bearing filament are distinct components of the connection.

At a focal adhesion, integrins mediate attachment to extracellular matrix components. Adapter proteins including talin and vinculin connect the cytoplasmic region of integrins to the actin network in a regulated manner. Actin-generated tension can be transmitted across the membrane into the matrix. This arrangement helps a cell spread, maintain a particular shape and respond to mechanical conditions. If the integrin-binding ability or the actin linkage fails, the membrane may still be physically intact while the cell loses its effective adhesion and shape.

Desmosomes use specialized cadherins, desmogleins and desmocollins, together with cytoplasmic plaque proteins such as desmoplakin, to connect intermediate filament networks of adjacent cells mechanically. The filaments do not extend directly across the extracellular space; the junction transmits force through its membrane and extracellular adhesion components. Hemidesmosomes instead connect epithelial cells to the underlying basement membrane, notably through α6β4 integrin-containing complexes linked through proteins such as plectin to keratin filaments. Distinguishing desmosomes from hemidesmosomes requires identifying whether the adhesion is cell-cell or cell-matrix, not merely noting that both use intermediate filaments.

Actin can also connect to the plasma membrane through proteins such as the ERM family (ezrin, radixin and moesin). Such connections are especially useful in cortical structures and membrane projections. Spectrin-based assemblies, described earlier for erythrocytes, provide another way of linking membranes to cytoskeletal elements. These distinct adapters let a cell choose different mechanical properties in different regions of the same membrane.

Networks also communicate across the cytoplasm. Plectin and other cytolinker proteins help connect intermediate filaments to other cytoskeletal structures. Across the nuclear envelope, SUN and KASH-domain-containing proteins form LINC complexes that physically couple the nuclear interior to cytoplasmic cytoskeletal systems. For a cell under mechanical load, forces can therefore be transmitted from extracellular matrix to adhesion sites, through cytoskeletal networks, and towards the nucleus. This is a mechanical connection rather than a claim that every external force directly controls a specific gene.

Consider an epithelial sheet exposed to repeated stretching. If actin-linked junctions fail, regulation of cell shape and cortical tension may be impaired. If keratin-linked desmosomes fail, the sheet can lose its resistance to tensile stress. If cell-matrix attachments fail, the sheet may detach from its underlying support. All three phenotypes involve cell adhesion or shape, but the disrupted molecular bridge differs. Comparing the attachment location and associated filament allows a student to discriminate between superficially similar defects.

15. Cell shape, polarity, organelle position and remodeling

Cell shape is a result of many interacting factors: membrane mechanics, volume, cortical tension, adhesion, extracellular structures and internal cytoskeletal architecture. It is therefore not useful to search for a single filament that 'gives the cell its shape' in all circumstances. Each network makes a different contribution, and the balance changes with the cell's function.

A cell that attaches and spreads on a surface can generate a wide peripheral actin network and organized contractile bundles. Microtubules extend into its interior, helping establish the position of organelles and allowing large structures to be distributed across the spreading cytoplasm. Intermediate filaments help distribute stresses between the nucleus, cytoplasm and adhesive regions. Removing one system may change the behavior of the others because their mechanical and biochemical connections are interdependent.

Cell polarity means that different regions of a cell have distinct organization and functions. An epithelial cell has an apical surface facing a lumen and a basal surface attached to a supporting matrix. The apical membrane may contain abundant microvilli, whereas the basal region contains specialized adhesions. Polarized sorting machinery places different membrane proteins at the two surfaces, and polarized cytoskeletal arrays help maintain the appropriate delivery routes and membrane structures. The cytoskeleton is one contributor to polarity, not the only source of positional information.

A migrating cell develops a front and a rear, even though both regions contain the same basic kinds of cytoskeletal proteins. Local actin nucleation can build a network at a protrusive region, whereas other actin structures support contraction and attachment. Microtubule growth and selective stabilization can orient intracellular delivery toward particular sites. Intermediate filaments maintain mechanical continuity as the cell changes shape. These structural principles prepare the learner for the molecular motility mechanisms in the next chapter.

Organelle distribution is another manifestation of polarity. The position of the Golgi in many cultured animal cells often depends on an intact microtubule array and motor-mediated organization. If microtubules are depolymerized, Golgi material can become dispersed into peripheral structures; after recovery, microtubule-dependent organization can restore a more centralized pattern. The result is not necessarily destruction of the Golgi enzymes or a complete shutdown of all secretion. It is a defect in spatial organization whose effects depend on the cell and perturbation.

Actin can help position organelles near the cell cortex or organize their local access to membrane sites. Intermediate filaments influence the organization of the nucleus and some organelles through mechanical and linker relationships. Plant cells use both actin and microtubules even in the presence of a large central vacuole, turgor pressure and a rigid wall. Cytoplasmic organization is especially important in the narrow living cytoplasmic regions surrounding a vacuole.

Remodeling involves coordinated regulation, not random breakdown. Small GTPases such as Rho-family members help couple signals to actin organization. RhoA commonly promotes contractile structures and formin-associated assembly; Rac commonly promotes branched protrusive networks, and Cdc42 contributes to polarity and filopodia-related organization. These are canonical associations, not rigid one-to-one rules. The downstream pathways and precise effects are context dependent. Their central relevance here is that the cell can translate a local chemical signal into a local physical change in filament architecture.

Some microtubules become more stable through interactions with the cortex or lattice-associated regulators. Some intermediate filament networks reorganize following phosphorylation. A cell entering division changes all three networks: microtubules form a new organization, nuclear lamina components can disassemble in open mitosis, and actin reorganizes to support rounding and cytokinesis. This illustrates a central principle of cell biology: a striking change in cellular architecture can arise from redistribution of existing protein subunits, regulated assembly and altered connections without requiring that the cell synthesize all of its structural proteins anew.

16. Mechanical reasoning: bending, tension and the importance of geometry

The different geometries of the three cytoskeletal polymers explain why they complement one another. Resistance to bending depends not only on the material but also on how the material is distributed across the cross-section. A tube can place much of its material far from its central axis, making bending relatively difficult for a given amount of material. This helps explain the considerable flexural stiffness of microtubules, although real filament stiffness also depends on molecular contacts and lattice structure.

A useful physical descriptor is persistence length, the length scale over which a filament tends to retain its directional orientation against thermal bending. A filament much shorter than its persistence length tends to appear relatively straight; a filament much longer can explore a broad range of curved shapes. Typical in vitro orders of magnitude are millimeters for microtubules, roughly tens of micrometers for actin and much shorter, often around a micrometer, for intermediate filaments. Actual values depend on filament type, experimental conditions, length and associated proteins. A high persistence length describes bending stiffness, not necessarily tensile strength or resistance to fracture.

For an ideal thermally fluctuating filament in the simple worm-like chain model, persistence length Lp is related to bending rigidity κ by Lp = κ/(kBT). Here kB is Boltzmann's constant, T is absolute temperature and κ has units of energy multiplied by length. This expression shows that for two filaments under the same temperature, the ratio of persistence lengths equals the ratio of bending rigidities. If a representative microtubule has Lp = 1000 μm and an actin filament has Lp = 10 μm, the former is about 100 times as resistant to thermal bending in this idealized comparison. The ratio does not imply that the microtubule tolerates 100 times the pulling force.

An individual slender filament can buckle under compression. For an ideal uniform rod with hinged ends, the Euler estimate of the critical compressive force is Fcrit = π²κ/L², where L is the unsupported length and κ is the bending rigidity. This expression is introduced only as a scaling model. Real microtubules are coupled to other cytoskeletal elements, may have different end constraints, and are not perfect homogeneous rods. Nevertheless the inverse-square dependence offers a powerful qualitative insight: with the same bending rigidity and boundary conditions, doubling unsupported length reduces the critical load to one quarter.

Suppose a supported microtubule segment has an idealized critical force of 8 pN at 2 μm unsupported length. At 4 μm, all else equal, the predicted critical force is 8 × (2/4)² = 2 pN. The calculation does not predict a universal buckling force for all cellular microtubules. It demonstrates why lateral support, cross-linking and shorter unsupported lengths matter even for a relatively stiff polymer. A cell can increase effective resistance to compression by organizing a network, not merely by increasing tubulin expression.

In contrast, closely packed actin bundles acquire a larger collective resistance to bending than isolated actin filaments. How much this increases depends on the coupling between neighboring filaments: a loose bundle can allow filaments to slide relative to one another, while a strongly cross-linked bundle behaves more like a single thicker element. Intermediate filament networks are particularly useful in accommodating tensile deformation, in which structures are stretched rather than bent. Their capacity to redistribute mechanical load helps preserve tissue integrity under repeated strain.

The lesson from these physical models is not to assign an absolute mechanical role to each system. Actin can push, resist compression over short distances and carry tension; microtubules can experience pulling forces as well as compression; intermediate filaments can resist bending and interact with other networks. The value of the comparison is to understand the dominant structural advantages and why a living cell uses a composite cytoskeleton.

17. How cytoskeletal structure and turnover are studied

Early electron microscopy distinguished filaments by their sizes and shapes. The hollow microtubule wall, thin actin filaments and intermediate-sized fibrous structures could be recognized, especially when specimen preparation preserved their organization. However, a fixed image captures a moment in a cell's life. It cannot by itself show whether a filament grows, shrinks or exchanges subunits. Preparation can also distort or preferentially preserve certain structures, so a single observation should be interpreted with the limitations of the method in mind.

Purification and reconstitution made it possible to separate the intrinsic assembly properties of cytoskeletal proteins from the influence of intact cells. In vitro experiments using actin or tubulin measured how polymer mass changed with subunit concentration, time, nucleotide state and the presence of accessory proteins. The lag phase could be compared with reactions containing seeds, and free versus polymerized protein could be estimated by separation methods such as sedimentation under suitable conditions. Reconstitution shows that an observed property can arise from the tested components under the chosen conditions, but it does not establish that the same components are the sole regulators in the cell.

Fluorescence microscopy allows the three networks to be identified in cells. Fluorescent phalloidin is widely used to label F-actin, particularly in fixed and permeabilized samples. It binds and stabilizes filamentous actin, so it is not a neutral label for unrestricted live-cell dynamics. Antibodies against tubulin or specific intermediate filament proteins can show where those proteins are distributed. Fluorescent protein fusions to actin, tubulin or associated markers can permit time-lapse imaging in suitable systems, but expression level and labeling method can perturb the structures being observed.

Photobleaching provides a way of asking whether a fluorescent population is replaced or moved. In fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), a small region is bleached and recovery is measured. Recovery can result from exchange of labeled subunits, movement of labeled polymers, diffusion of soluble protein or a combination of these effects. A rapid recovery does not automatically prove rapid polymerization. A proper comparison considers the marker, the geometry of the bleached region, the unbleached control and the contribution from whole-filament motion.

For a simple illustrative experiment, suppose fluorescence intensities after background correction are 100 units before bleaching, 20 units immediately after bleaching and 80 units at the recovery plateau. If bleaching elsewhere is negligible or corrected, the recoverable fraction is (80 − 20)/(100 − 20) = 60/80 = 0.75, or 75%. The remaining 25% is operationally unrecovered during the observation period. It does not prove that exactly one quarter of the molecules are permanently immobilized. A slowly exchanging population could recover with a longer observation time. In cytoskeletal biology the observed signal must always be interpreted against the timescale of the experiment.

A more direct test of microtubule dynamics uses time-lapse observation of a single end, as in the earlier quantitative example. A test of actin polarity may use end-specific growth measurements or the classic decoration pattern from myosin fragments. Intermediate filament mechanics can be examined by comparing the deformation or survival of otherwise comparable cells under mechanical stress. Each approach addresses a different property: molecular orientation, turnover, or force-bearing function. No single staining image establishes all three.

A strong perturbation experiment includes an untreated or solvent-treated control, an appropriate time course and an independent readout of the predicted effect. For example, if a drug reduces F-actin staining, the phenotype could reflect loss of filaments, altered accessibility of the stain or cell damage. Measuring both total actin and the polymerized fraction, observing cell viability and using a second perturbation or a recovery condition helps narrow the explanation. The experimental aim is to connect a change in a molecular mechanism to a corresponding cellular consequence, not simply to produce an attractive microscopy image.

18. Drug perturbations reveal why dynamic assembly matters

Cytoskeletal drugs are valuable experimental tools because they change defined aspects of filament organization. Their effects provide causal evidence when interpreted with appropriate controls. A drug that disrupts a network may also affect other processes indirectly, so a phenotype does not always identify a direct target. Concentration, exposure time and cell type matter. In examination questions, distinguish the primary molecular action from later cellular consequences.

Cytochalasins interact with actin filament barbed ends and inhibit normal elongation, with effects that vary among compounds and conditions. When barbed-end addition is blocked while loss continues elsewhere, many actin structures shorten or become disorganized. The resulting defects may include altered cortical shape, impaired protrusions and defective cytokinesis. This does not mean that cytochalasin dissolves every actin molecule directly; it disrupts the balance of assembly and disassembly.

Latrunculins bind actin monomers and reduce the concentration of monomers available for filament assembly. This acts at a different point in the system than barbed-end capping. In the simple critical concentration model, a reduction in free competent actin can cause net loss from accessible ends. Both latrunculin and cytochalasin can lead to a decline in F-actin under many conditions, but their immediate molecular effects differ. This distinction is useful if an experiment provides a monomer pool measurement or an end-specific growth assay.

Phalloidin binds F-actin and stabilizes the polymer against depolymerization. In an appropriate experimental context, excessive stabilization can impair remodeling just as destabilization can. A cell needs to dismantle old structures and create new ones at specific times. Stabilization alone therefore does not necessarily improve actin function. Fluorescent phalloidin is particularly common as an actin stain in fixed cells because it binds polymerized actin selectively; access to intact living cells can be limited.

Colchicine binds tubulin and inhibits normal microtubule assembly. Nocodazole also disrupts the microtubule system, often allowing a useful reversible depolymerization-and-recovery experiment. Vinca alkaloids can suppress microtubule dynamics and, depending on concentration and conditions, destabilize normal microtubule assembly. These agents should be distinguished from drugs acting on actin. Many dynamic microtubules disappear during treatment, whereas relatively stable subsets can persist under particular conditions.

Paclitaxel (Taxol) binds microtubules and stabilizes the polymer, suppressing normal dynamics. It may increase the amount of polymer under some conditions, but excessive stabilization interferes with processes that require controlled turnover. A dividing cell needs microtubules not merely to exist but to change their organization and interact dynamically with other structures. Both destabilizing and stabilizing microtubule agents can therefore disrupt division, by opposite initial effects on polymer behavior.

Consider cells treated with two unknown substances. Treatment A reduces the polymerized tubulin fraction and eliminates many peripheral microtubules. Treatment B retains prominent microtubules but greatly reduces the frequency of growth and shortening transitions. Both cause division defects. The observation suggests A has a destabilizing effect and B a stabilizing or dynamics-suppressing effect; it does not prove that A is specifically colchicine or B specifically paclitaxel without biochemical evidence. This is exactly the type of distinction a Part C question often tests.

There is no equally universal classical acute inhibitor of all intermediate filament polymers comparable to the standard actin and microtubule tools. Their composition is heterogeneous. Mutations, changes in expression, altered phosphorylation or disruption of connecting proteins can instead help demonstrate their function. Mechanical stress tests can reveal the consequences. A general cellular stress response after drug treatment should not be assigned to intermediate filament disruption merely because the cell changes shape.

19. An integrated comparison and examination reasoning

The structural distinctions can now be used to infer biological function. If a question describes an ATP-binding globular monomer forming a polarized filament of about 7 nm, the appropriate identification is actin. A 25 nm hollow polymer formed from αβ heterodimers is a microtubule. A 10 nm rope-like filament built from antiparallel coiled-coil assemblies points to an intermediate filament. This identification becomes more valuable when a question asks what will happen if the structure is disrupted rather than merely requesting its name.

For actin, the mechanistic chain is monomer orientation → polar filament → nucleotide-dependent end kinetics → accessory-protein regulation → local bundles or networks. For microtubules, it is αβ orientation → hollow polar tube → β-tubulin GTP hydrolysis and end dynamics → controlled nucleation and stabilization → long-range organization. For intermediate filaments, it is coiled-coil dimer → antiparallel tetramer → apolar assembly → tensile resilience and tissue-specific anchoring. These chains connect molecular structure to cellular phenotype.

One should not equate 'dynamic' with 'weak' or 'stable' with 'nonfunctional'. Rapidly exchanging filaments can form reliable networks if assembly and disassembly are balanced in the correct region. Stable structures are important where a cell requires sustained mechanical support or transport tracks. Intermediate filaments can remodel without classical nucleotide-driven end dynamics. Microtubules can be selectively stabilized. Actin can persist in a specialized bundle or turn over rapidly in a cortical network. The diversity is a result of both intrinsic polymer properties and regulatory proteins.

Nor should the eukaryotic three-filament framework be imposed on every organism. Bacteria possess structural proteins with evolutionary or structural relationships to eukaryotic cytoskeletal systems, including tubulin-related FtsZ and actin-related MreB. Their functions and architectures are not identical to the conventional eukaryotic filaments. Bacterial division and physiology receive their own treatment later in Unit 2. A question about a bacterial Z ring is not a question about a canonical 13-protofilament eukaryotic microtubule.

Finally, the next conceptual step is to consider how polarized structures interact with ATP-powered motor proteins and how organized movement emerges. Polarity provides the directional coordinate system. Accessory proteins and the arrangement of filaments provide the track geometry. Motor proteins provide active transport and sliding forces. The present chapter establishes the architecture and dynamics necessary to understand those processes without confusing the motion of a filament end with the movement of cargo along a filament.

B1. A filament is assembled from 42 kDa globular monomers into a ~7 nm polar polymer. Which is it?

  1. Actin
  2. Tubulin
  3. Vimentin
  4. Spectrin
Answer and explanation

A. Actin — Actin has the stated size and architecture. Tubulin forms αβ-dimer microtubules, vimentin forms intermediate filaments, and spectrin is a membrane-skeleton protein.

B2. Which actin end is normally the faster-growing end under standard uncapped ATP-actin conditions?

  1. Pointed
  2. Barbed
  3. Both must be equal
  4. Neither has polarity
Answer and explanation

B. Barbed — The barbed end generally has a higher association rate; the pointed end can still grow when concentration exceeds its critical value. Structural polarity does not mean electrical charge.

B3. Which directly lowers the available G-actin monomer pool by binding monomers?

  1. Paclitaxel
  2. Latrunculin
  3. Phalloidin
  4. Nocodazole
Answer and explanation

B. Latrunculin — Latrunculin sequesters G-actin. Paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules; phalloidin binds F-actin; nocodazole acts on tubulin/microtubule assembly.

B4. Which complex nucleates a branched actin daughter filament from an existing filament?

  1. γ-TuRC
  2. Arp2/3
  3. LINC
  4. Desmoplakin
Answer and explanation

B. Arp2/3 — Arp2/3 produces branched actin networks. γ-TuRC nucleates microtubules; LINC couples nuclear and cytoplasmic structures; desmoplakin links desmosomes to intermediate filaments.

B5. What is hydrolyzed during ordinary αβ-tubulin lattice dynamics?

  1. The nonexchangeable GTP on α-tubulin
  2. ATP bound to keratin
  3. The exchangeable GTP on β-tubulin
  4. GTP on both tubulins at every addition
Answer and explanation

C. The exchangeable GTP on β-tubulin — The β site participates in the exchange/hydrolysis cycle. α-tubulin retains its nonexchangeable GTP in the intact dimer under ordinary conditions. Keratin has no analogous cycle.

B6. A microtubule switches from growth to rapid shortening. The transition is called:

  1. Rescue
  2. Catastrophe
  3. Treadmilling
  4. Nucleation
Answer and explanation

B. Catastrophe — Catastrophe is the growth-to-shrinkage switch; rescue is the reverse. Treadmilling is opposing end flux, and nucleation initiates a polymer.

B7. Where does much centrosomal microtubule nucleation occur?

  1. Central pair of a centriole
  2. Pericentriolar material containing γ-TuRC
  3. Nucleolus
  4. Desmosomal plaque
Answer and explanation

B. Pericentriolar material containing γ-TuRC — The PCM recruits γ-TuRC. A centriole has nine triplets, not a central pair. Neither the nucleolus nor desmosome is the typical centrosomal nucleation site.

B8. What removes structural polarity during cytoplasmic IF assembly?

  1. ATP hydrolysis
  2. Head-to-tail tubulin stacking
  3. Antiparallel association of coiled-coil dimers
  4. Motor binding
Answer and explanation

C. Antiparallel association of coiled-coil dimers — Oppositely oriented dimers form a tetramer with no sustained directional orientation. ATP hydrolysis, tubulin stacking and motor binding do not explain IF apolarity.

B9. Which pair is matched correctly?

  1. Desmosome – microtubule
  2. Hemidesmosome – keratin
  3. Focal adhesion – neurofilament
  4. Microvillus – tubulin axoneme
Answer and explanation

B. Hemidesmosome – keratin — Hemidesmosomes connect keratin to basement membrane. Desmosomes also link IFs, focal adhesions link actin, and microvilli contain actin bundles.

B10. What is the most appropriate characteristic marker of many astrocytes?

  1. Desmin
  2. GFAP
  3. Keratin 14
  4. γ-tubulin exclusively
Answer and explanation

B. GFAP — GFAP is a characteristic astrocytic IF. Desmin is associated with muscle and keratin 14 with basal epithelial cells; γ-tubulin is not astrocyte-specific.

B11. A mature mammalian erythrocyte's membrane skeleton primarily uses:

  1. Microtubule triplets
  2. Spectrin and short actin junctions
  3. A nuclear lamina
  4. A 9+2 axoneme
Answer and explanation

B. Spectrin and short actin junctions — Spectrin and short actin junctions support deformability. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack a nucleus and conventional axonemal/centrosomal architecture.

B12. Which treatment is expected to stabilize microtubules while suppressing their normal dynamics?

  1. Cytochalasin
  2. Latrunculin
  3. Paclitaxel
  4. Phalloidin
Answer and explanation

C. Paclitaxel — Paclitaxel binds the microtubule lattice. The other listed agents target actin monomers or filaments.

21. Original CSIR-NET Part C and analytical practice

Use the mechanisms, units and assumptions developed in the chapter. All numerical observations in these questions are illustrative.

C1. The barbed end has kon=8 μM⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff=0.8 s⁻¹. The pointed end has kon=2 and koff=1.2 in the same units. At C=0.25 μM, what are the signed rates and net filament change?

  1. (+1.2, −0.7), net +0.5 subunit/s
  2. (+1.2, +0.7), net +1.9
  3. (−1.2, +0.7), net −0.5
  4. Both zero
Answer and explanation

A. (+1.2, −0.7), net +0.5 subunit/s — Barbed: 8×0.25−0.8=1.2. Pointed: 2×0.25−1.2=−0.7. Sum = +0.5. Positive and negative ends coexist with net growth, so the zero-length-change claim fails.

C2. Using the same rate constants as C1, which concentration gives steady-length treadmilling?

  1. 0.10 μM
  2. 0.20 μM
  3. 0.60 μM
  4. 1.00 μM
Answer and explanation

B. 0.20 μM — Set (8+2)C−(0.8+1.2)=0, so C=0.20 μM. Rates are +0.8 and −0.8 subunit/s. The end-specific critical concentrations 0.10 and 0.60 are not the steady-length concentration.

C3. A free actin concentration of 0.3 μM lies between Cc+ = 0.1 and Cc− = 0.6 μM. Barbed ends are then fully capped; pointed ends remain open. Predict the initial net behavior.

  1. Growth only at the capped end
  2. Net shortening
  3. Unchanged length necessarily
  4. Dynamic instability caused by GTP
Answer and explanation

B. Net shortening — The pointed end loses subunits because 0.3 is below its critical concentration. Capping blocks the opposing addition, so net shortening follows. Actin uses ATP-related kinetics rather than β-tubulin GTP cap dynamics.

C4. A reaction with actin seeds reaches the same plateau as an unseeded reaction but has a shorter lag. What is supported?

  1. Seeds lower the nucleation barrier
  2. Seeds change ATP into GTP
  3. Seeds guarantee no turnover at plateau
  4. Seeds increase final polymer mass
Answer and explanation

A. Seeds lower the nucleation barrier — The changed onset without a changed plateau is consistent with easier nucleation. It neither proves that turnover stops nor requires a changed final mass or nucleotide identity.

C5. An ideal 13-protofilament microtubule is 2.4 μm long. Estimate the number of αβ dimers, taking 8 nm per axial repeat.

  1. 300
  2. 3900
  3. 7800
  4. 31200
Answer and explanation

B. 3900 — 2400 nm / 8 nm = 300 dimers per protofilament. Multiply by 13 = 3900 dimers. 7800 counts individual polypeptides, not dimers.

C6. A microtubule grows 4 μm in 2 min, then shrinks 3 μm in 0.5 min. Which signed velocities and overall mean are correct?

  1. +2, −6, +0.4 μm/min
  2. +2, −6, −2 μm/min
  3. +8, −1.5, +6.5 μm/min
  4. −2, +6, +0.4 μm/min
Answer and explanation

A. +2, −6, +0.4 μm/min — Growth is +4/2=+2; shrinkage −3/0.5=−6. Net displacement +1 μm over 2.5 min gives +0.4. An unweighted mean of phase velocities is inappropriate.

C7. There are 30 catastrophes over 150 min of growth and 10 rescues over 50 min of shortening. What are the observed transition frequencies?

  1. 0.15 and 0.05 min⁻¹
  2. 0.20 and 0.20 min⁻¹
  3. 0.30 and 0.10 min⁻¹
  4. 1 and 1 min⁻¹
Answer and explanation

B. 0.20 and 0.20 min⁻¹ — Use the time in each starting state: 30/150=0.20 and 10/50=0.20 min⁻¹. Do not divide both counts by the combined observation time.

C8. A fluorescent EB marker forms moving comets, but vesicles stop traveling after motor inhibition. What is the strongest interpretation?

  1. All microtubules have disappeared
  2. Microtubule plus ends can still grow while cargo transport is impaired
  3. EB comets are vesicles
  4. The drug must target γ-tubulin
Answer and explanation

B. Microtubule plus ends can still grow while cargo transport is impaired — Growing-end markers report polymer boundary dynamics, not cargo transport. Motor inhibition can selectively impair vesicle motion even with detectable growing microtubules.

C9. Drug X decreases F-actin. Drug Y increases polymerized actin but impairs shape changes. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Only X can impair cytoskeletal function
  2. Y must be a tubulin inhibitor
  3. Both excessive destabilization and excessive stabilization can impair remodeling
  4. Actin is permanently static
Answer and explanation

C. Both excessive destabilization and excessive stabilization can impair remodeling — Dynamic reorganization requires balanced assembly and disassembly. The effects are consistent with distinct disruptions; the identity of either compound is not established by the phenotype alone.

C10. After microtubule depolymerization and washout, new tubes first appear around the centrosome. What is justified?

  1. All microtubules in every eukaryote originate from centrioles
  2. The centrosomal region is a major organizing/nucleating site in these cells
  3. Tubulin is made only inside centrioles
  4. All plus ends are anchored to the centrosome
Answer and explanation

B. The centrosomal region is a major organizing/nucleating site in these cells — Recovery supports a major localized MTOC. Noncentrosomal nucleation remains possible, and αβ tubulin is not synthesized inside centrioles. Typical centrosomal minus ends are proximal.

C11. A keratin mutation causes epithelial cells to rupture on stretching, although microtubule staining appears normal. Which mechanism is most directly implicated?

  1. Loss of tensile continuity in IF network
  2. Failure of β-tubulin GTP hydrolysis
  3. Loss of chloroplast ATP synthesis
  4. Increased microtubule nucleation
Answer and explanation

A. Loss of tensile continuity in IF network — Keratin networks transmit tensile forces through epithelial junctions. The phenotype is stress dependent and does not require a primary microtubule or metabolic defect.

C12. In an ideal buckling model, unsupported rod length triples at fixed bending rigidity. Critical compressive force changes by what factor?

  1. 3
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/9
  4. 9
Answer and explanation

C. 1/9 — Euler scaling Fcrit∝1/L² gives (1/3)²=1/9. This is a model for comparable boundary conditions, not a universal cellular load.

C13. A background-corrected FRAP signal is 120 before bleaching, 30 just after and 84 at plateau. What is the recovered fraction?

  1. 70%
  2. 45%
  3. 60%
  4. 75%
Answer and explanation

C. 60% — (84−30)/(120−30)=54/90=0.60. 84/120 ignores the residual fluorescence immediately after bleaching. The unrecovered fraction is operational over the observation time.

C14. A plant cell lacks a centriole-containing centrosome but has aligned cortical microtubules. Which inference is correct?

  1. The observed tubes cannot be microtubules
  2. Dispersed MTOCs can organize microtubules without typical centrosomes
  3. They must be intermediate filaments
  4. Cellulose itself polymerizes tubulin
Answer and explanation

B. Dispersed MTOCs can organize microtubules without typical centrosomes — Plant cortical arrays can be nucleated and organized without animal-style centrosomes. Filament identity is not determined by the presence of centrioles, and cellulose is a distinct extracellular wall polymer.

C15. Assertion: A conventional cytoplasmic intermediate filament does not provide a polarized track for kinesin. Reason: Antiparallel tetramer assembly removes overall structural polarity.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion — The antiparallel arrangement removes a persistent directional end identity, explaining why intermediate filaments are not conventional kinesin tracks. They can still be moved indirectly by motors acting on other filament systems.

C16. Assertion: Every actin concentration between the two end-specific critical concentrations produces constant filament length. Reason: Barbed-end net addition and pointed-end net loss can coexist in this range.

  1. Both true; reason explains
  2. Both true; reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion false; reason true — Opposing net fluxes do coexist, but length remains constant only when their magnitudes match. The assertion falsely generalizes steady-length treadmilling to the entire interval.

C17. Assertion: Microtubules with anchored minus ends may exhibit dynamic instability. Reason: Dynamic instability is the switch between growth and shortening at a filament end.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion — An anchored minus end does not prevent plus-end transitions. Dynamic instability does not require simultaneous loss from the opposite end.

C18. A cell's Golgi becomes dispersed after nocodazole, but the Golgi enzyme concentration remains approximately unchanged. What is the best conclusion?

  1. Golgi enzymes were destroyed
  2. Microtubule disruption altered organelle positioning without proving loss of enzyme abundance
  3. The Golgi must be a microtubule
  4. Nocodazole directly hydrolyzed all actin
Answer and explanation

B. Microtubule disruption altered organelle positioning without proving loss of enzyme abundance — The structural organization can depend on microtubules even when the organelle's protein abundance is unchanged. More assays would be needed to determine the extent of functional secretion defects.
