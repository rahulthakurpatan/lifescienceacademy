Why a genetic map needs a population

Two plants may differ dramatically in their tolerance of drought even though they belong to the same species. One maintains green leaves during a dry period, while another wilts early. The observation invites a genetic explanation, but looking at the two plants alone cannot reveal which of their thousands of DNA differences matter. They also may have experienced different soil moisture, developmental histories or nutrient supplies. A mapping population creates a deliberate experiment in inheritance. Its members carry different combinations of parental alleles, and their genotypes and phenotypes can be compared under a defined experimental design. The objective is to find chromosome regions whose inheritance predicts the trait, while keeping environmental explanations and random sampling in view.

A mapping population is not simply a large group of plants collected at random. In a classical biparental mapping design, each individual or line descends from two known parents through a specified series of crosses. The pedigree tells us which allele combinations were possible and how recombination could have generated them. A genetic marker is a heritable DNA difference that can be scored reliably. If parent P1 carries marker allele A and P2 carries marker allele a, their offspring can reveal which parental chromosome segments were transmitted. A marker can track inheritance without itself controlling the trait. The corresponding causal gene may be nearby, and the association between marker and trait becomes weaker when recombination separates them.

The logic of linkage mapping originated in the recognition that genes lying on the same chromosome do not always assort independently. Mendel's experiments demonstrated segregation of inherited factors; subsequent chromosome-based genetics explained why associations between factors could persist through meiosis. Sturtevant's early linkage maps converted differences in recombinant frequency into relative distances. Modern plant populations extend that same reasoning from a few visible traits to many molecular markers. The key development was not merely replacing a visible marker with a DNA assay; it was recognizing that the number, history and organization of meioses in a pedigree determine how much information those assays contain.

The term population has several meanings in genetics. Here it usually denotes an experimental set of related individuals or independently derived lines. It need not represent a naturally interbreeding species or meet the conditions of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. For example, a collection of doubled haploid barley lines is a population for mapping even though its members are homozygous and separately maintained. In contrast, a natural association panel uses historical recombination in many ancestors, with relatedness and population structure as major analytical concerns. Classical controlled crosses provide a simpler starting point because parental origins and expected segregation are specified.

A mapping experiment has at least two distinct outcomes. A linkage map describes the order and recombination-based separation of markers. A trait map associates an observed phenotype with inherited loci, for example a disease-resistance gene or a quantitative trait locus (QTL). The first can be constructed even if no target phenotype is available. The second requires measurements as well as genotypes. Neither automatically establishes a molecular mechanism. Mapping narrows candidate regions; subsequent evidence must test the responsible variant. This distinction explains why a population appropriate for locating a chromosome region may not be the best choice for validating a specific gene.

What the population actually measures

A useful starting distinction is between a locus, a chromosome interval and a statistical association. The locus may be an experimentally scored marker with two known DNA sequences, whereas the interval is a continuous piece of chromosome between two locations. The trait itself may arise from a gene that has not yet been identified. In a mapping experiment we can score a marker, reconstruct the inherited interval and ask whether trait values differ between the marker classes even without knowing the causal gene. For example, if plants inheriting the P1 allele at a marker have an average of eight more seeds per pod than plants inheriting the P2 allele, the difference is evidence for genetic variation somewhere in association with the marker. It remains possible that the marker tracks more than one functional variant in the same interval.

The essential comparison is within the descendants, rather than simply between the founders. Imagine that P1 is taller and flowers later than P2. Both traits may be controlled by the same gene, by two closely linked genes or by completely separate genes that happen to have different alleles in the parents. The two-parent comparison cannot distinguish these hypotheses. In a segregating family some individuals may combine tall stature with early flowering; such recombinant combinations establish that the parental association is not obligatory, although the separation may also be affected by environmental variation. The population transforms a single comparison of two whole genomes into many comparisons of different inherited chromosome combinations.

For a successful genetic experiment, both variation and a record of its transmission are required. An all-homozygous collection containing genetically identical plants is unsuitable for discovering a segregating locus because it has no allelic contrast. A collection of unrelated plants may have abundant variation but lacks the simple pedigree of an experimental family. A set of fixed lines derived independently from the same cross can contain variation between lines while maintaining uniformity within each line. Therefore the apparent contradiction that an inbred population can be genetically diverse is resolved by considering the level at which diversity is measured: heterozygosity within individuals is low, but founder ancestry differs among the independent lines.

Classical visible markers, such as a flower-color difference, can sometimes serve both as a phenotype and as an indicator of chromosomal inheritance. Molecular markers allow a greater separation of these roles. They can be scored in plants at a young developmental stage even when the target trait becomes visible much later. The breeder can then reconstruct inheritance independently of the visible phenotype. This distinction is crucial in understanding why marker genotype information is valuable even when environmental noise makes trait classification uncertain. It also explains why the experimental population should not be selected only on the final phenotype unless the analysis explicitly accounts for that sampling scheme.

Choosing informative founders

Begin by defining a biological question precisely. Suppose the target is flowering time. Do we seek a locus that controls days to first flower in a fixed photoperiod, or one that changes the response to day length? A large difference between two parents under one light regime does not necessarily imply a large difference under another. The trait definition determines the phenotype to be scored, the environmental controls and the kind of genetic variation required. Parents should differ at relevant alleles and possess sufficient marker polymorphisms for tracking the genome, but phenotypic contrast alone is not enough.

In a simple diploid cross, a marker is informative when the parents are homozygous for distinguishable alleles. P1 can be AA and P2 aa, making every genuine F1 offspring Aa at that marker. The A and a labels identify parental origins; they do not assert that A is dominant for the biological trait. A codominant marker distinguishes AA, Aa and aa; a dominant marker may distinguish only the presence or absence of a particular band. This difference becomes important in an F2, where heterozygotes form a large class. In a population of fixed homozygous lines, the lost ability to identify heterozygotes is less consequential, although missing calls and null alleles still matter.

Parental identity should be confirmed before producing a large population. Outcrossing, seed mixture, incorrect labeling and residual heterozygosity can create false assumptions about phase and inheritance. A small set of markers dispersed across the genome can test whether F1 plants carry the expected parental alleles. A marker that is monomorphic between founders is not informative for this biparental pedigree, even if it is highly variable in the species at large. Conversely, a pair of very divergent parents may supply many polymorphisms but exhibit poor fertility or distorted segregation, reducing effective sample size.

A plant breeder must distinguish a recurrent parent, whose genome is intended to be largely retained, from a donor parent, which supplies a desired allele or region. This vocabulary is most important for repeated backcrossing and near-isogenic lines. In ordinary F2 or recombinant inbred mapping, the two founders can be treated symmetrically under ideal transmission assumptions. The choice of maternal parent may nevertheless matter if plastid or mitochondrial variation, seed development, or a maternal effect influences the phenotype. A reciprocal cross reverses the maternal and paternal roles; comparing reciprocal progenies may help identify such effects.

Founder selection also sets the limits of inference. If two parents both carry the same functional allele at a gene controlling drought tolerance, their descendants cannot reveal a segregating effect of that allele. Another cross may detect it. A QTL detected between P1 and P2 is therefore a statement about genetic differences segregating in that pedigree and the environments studied. Failure to detect a locus does not show that the gene has no role in the species. This limitation is not cured by simply genotyping the same family at more markers.

From a crossing plan to a defensible pedigree

Before assigning parental labels, identify whether the species is normally self-pollinating, predominantly outcrossing, self-incompatible or vegetatively propagated. A scheme that is easy in one species may be biologically impractical in another. Self-pollinating species often permit repeated selfing with little additional effort. In self-incompatible species, F2-like segregation may be obtained by crossing equivalent heterozygous plants, if the parents and mating system permit it. In perennial species generation time itself may dominate the cost of developing an advanced population. These constraints are scientific properties of the experimental organism and are not simply operational inconveniences.

An ideal textbook biparental population starts with parents that are homozygous at the relevant loci. Many crop cultivars satisfy this approximation sufficiently for the intended analysis, but not all plant material does. If the supposed parent P1 is Aa rather than AA at a marker, and P2 is aa, not every F1 will be Aa. Pooling all F1 descendants without recording which individual produced them can create a mixture of families with different segregation probabilities. In such a case the marker can still be analyzed using the correct pedigree, but the simple 1:2:1 expectation for a single uniform F1 cannot be applied to the whole collection.

A cross may also have two potential biological directions. The nuclear marker genotypes of AA maternal × aa paternal and aa maternal × AA paternal are both Aa. The cytoplasmic inheritance, maternal tissues and early developmental environment may differ. In plants, a seed phenotype may be influenced by the maternal sporophyte, the diploid embryo or the usually triploid endosperm of angiosperms, depending on the trait. If the phenotype is recorded on a seed rather than on the later plant, specifying which generation and which tissue was measured becomes essential. This is why an apparent segregation anomaly can sometimes arise from assigning a maternal or endosperm phenotype to the embryo's genotype.

The question of marker informativeness must be separated from the question of genotyping precision. A perfectly reliable assay for a monomorphic locus reveals no founder ancestry. A polymorphic assay that frequently fails or scores heterozygotes as homozygotes can be misleading. An informative marker is therefore one that distinguishes the parental states in the relevant cross, can be called reproducibly and has an interpretable inheritance model. Verify heterozygote calls in the F1 as an early control when a codominant assay is being used.

A useful internal record for each plant includes its parent identities, cross direction, generation, unique sample identity and any known unusual feature such as delayed development or poor fertility. The genotype record must be associated with the same plant or line as the phenotype record. Suppose a student analyzes a perfect F2 marker table but accidentally pairs each marker genotype with the phenotype of the next row. The marginal genotype ratio may remain exactly 1:2:1, yet the association with the trait can be destroyed. Mendelian segregation and correct sample pairing are therefore independent quality checks.

The F1 and F2: observing segregation directly

Crossing AA with aa produces an F1 whose nuclear genotype at the marker is Aa. With homozygous parents the F1 individuals are ordinarily genetically uniform for segregating parental loci, apart from new mutations and exceptional inheritance. They are valuable for observing dominance and generating gametes but do not constitute an informative segregating population at those loci. To reveal combinations of alleles, self an F1 plant or intercross compatible equivalent F1 individuals. The resulting generation is F2.

At a single locus, fair meiosis produces A and a gametes with equal probability. Independent union of male and female gametes gives AA with probability one quarter, Aa with probability one half, and aa with probability one quarter. The two routes to Aa are A from the egg and a from pollen, or the reverse. Their probabilities add. Thus the genotype ratio is 1:2:1; it is not automatically the phenotype ratio. If A is completely dominant, AA and Aa share a phenotype and the two visible categories are expected in a 3:1 ratio. With incomplete dominance or a codominant marker, all three classes can be distinguished.

For a sample of 240 F2 plants, the expected genotype counts are 60 AA, 120 Aa and 60 aa. A result of 57, 126 and 57 is compatible with sampling variation. Pearson's chi-square statistic for these fixed probabilities is the sum of squared deviations divided by expected counts: 9/60 + 36/120 + 9/60 = 0.60, with two degrees of freedom under the usual large-sample assumptions. A low statistic is not evidence that genotypes were scored without errors; it simply means this particular deviation is not large relative to the null model. Genotyping errors can sometimes preserve apparent ratios while disrupting linkage.

At two linked loci the F1 may have phase AB/ab. Let r denote the fraction of recombinant gametes for the interval. The parental gamete probabilities are (1−r)/2 for AB and for ab; the recombinant probabilities are r/2 for Ab and aB. For r = 0.10, the gamete frequencies are 0.45, 0.45, 0.05 and 0.05. An F2 genotype AABB requires two AB gametes, with probability 0.2025 if both sexes have the same r and fertilization is random. An aabb plant likewise has probability 0.2025. The double heterozygote AaBb can arise from AB with ab or Ab with aB, giving 2(0.45×0.45) + 2(0.05×0.05) = 0.41. This last genotype does not directly tell us whether the two chromosomes have coupling or repulsion phase.

An F2 therefore contains information from two F1 gametes per individual, but unphased marker genotypes can obscure particular recombination events. Counting all individuals that look unlike their parents and dividing by population size is not a valid general two-point recombination estimator in an F2. The analyst must use probabilities for observable joint genotype classes, including alternative phase configurations. When male and female recombination rates differ, using a single common r is an additional assumption. The point of this example is not to memorize a special table but to reconstruct probabilities from the gametes that can produce each genotype.

The F2 has practical benefits. It is quick to create after a successful cross, provides both homozygous classes and heterozygotes, and permits the study of dominance. It can also yield many recombinants if sufficient individuals are raised. Its major weakness is that each heterozygous individual is genetically transient. Seeds from a heterozygous F2 segregate, so planting its descendants in a second season does not recreate its genotype exactly. Vegetatively propagated species may allow clonal replication; otherwise an F2:3 family design can estimate an F2 individual's genetic effects through progeny means, although within-family segregation must then be considered.

Constructing the full two-locus F2 distribution

The most reliable way to solve a two-locus F2 question is to start with four gametes and enumerate their unions. Write p = (1−r)/2 and q = r/2 for a coupling-phase F1, with equal recombination in the sexes. AB and ab each occur with probability p; Ab and aB each occur with probability q. The four possible double homozygotes have probabilities p² for AABB and aabb and q² for AAbb and aaBB. The four classes heterozygous at only one locus each occur with probability 2pq. The remaining double heterozygote occurs with probability 2p²+2q². These nine classes account for all possible unphased two-locus genotypes.

To check the distribution, add the four double homozygote probabilities, the four singly heterozygous probabilities and the double heterozygote probability. The sum is 2p²+2q²+8pq+2p²+2q² = 4(p+q)². Because p+q = 1/2, the sum is one. This elementary check detects an omitted class or a missing factor of two. If r = 0, q is zero. Only AABB, AaBb and aabb remain, each arising from parental gametes. If r = 1/2, p and q both equal one quarter. Each single-locus genotype ratio becomes independent of the other, and the joint probabilities become products of 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4.

Consider an illustrative F2 of 400 plants with r = 0.10. Expected AABB and aabb counts are each 81, the AAbb and aaBB counts each one, the four singly heterozygous classes each 18, and the AaBb class 164. The total is 81+81+1+1+72+164 = 400. A student who counts only AAbb and aaBB as recombinants would obtain 2/400 = 0.005, far below the true 0.10. Those rare double homozygotes require two matching recombinant gametes, whereas other genotype classes can be formed by one recombinant and one parental gamete. The visible genotype is not equivalent to a single scored gamete.

This same construction clarifies a repulsion-phase F1. Now Ab and aB are parental and have probabilities p; AB and ab are recombinant and have probabilities q. The four double homozygote probabilities are exchanged accordingly, while the double heterozygote probability remains 2p²+2q². A high frequency of AaBb therefore does not distinguish the two phases by itself. The frequencies of the appropriate double homozygotes and other classes, together with knowledge of the founders, reveal the relevant phase.

A dominant marker creates another layer of ambiguity. Suppose the A allele produces a visible band in AA and Aa, and the a allele produces no band in aa. With an ideal F2, band-present plants are expected to comprise three quarters of the population. The marker can still provide information, but AA and Aa cannot be separated by that assay alone. If an investigator simply calls every band-present plant AA, estimated heterozygosity and parental ancestry will be incorrect. Codominant marker scores are particularly useful in F2 designs because they preserve genotype classes needed for both linkage and dominance analysis.

If the question supplies a trait with partial penetrance rather than a perfect marker, treat the phenotype as conditional on genotype. The probability of a phenotypic category is obtained by adding each genotype's frequency multiplied by the probability of that category within the genotype. A phenotype ratio that differs from 3:1 can reflect penetrance or scoring conditions rather than abnormal chromosome segregation. Separate the genotype model from the phenotype model before interpreting a cross.

When genotype classes differ in phenotype

The presence of three marker classes makes an F2 especially useful for separating different patterns of inheritance. Imagine three groups of seedlings classified by a codominant marker, with mean leaf lengths of 12, 16 and 20 centimeters for aa, Aa and AA respectively. The heterozygote mean lies halfway between the homozygote means. If the marker itself is causal, or very tightly linked to a single causal locus under a suitable model, these means are consistent with an additive effect. Now suppose the heterozygote mean is 20 instead of 16. The pattern is consistent with complete dominance of the allele associated with the longer leaf. The genotype frequencies have not changed; the genotype-to-phenotype relationship has changed.

The distinction also matters when selecting an F2 population for mapping a recessive phenotype. In a hypothetical cross between a normal AA founder and recessive mutant aa founder, approximately one quarter of the F2 plants are aa under ordinary segregation. These mutant plants can be examined for marker ancestry. If a particular marker is extremely close to the recessive allele and the mutant allele came from P2, mutant plants will tend to carry the P2 marker allele. At a marker on another chromosome, P1 and P2 marker ancestry should be distributed independently of the mutant classification, under the simple assumptions. This is the foundation of comparing genotype information among plants selected by a trait, although a rigorous analysis must consider sample selection, marker–trait linkage and genotype uncertainty.

A marker linked to a causal allele need not produce genotype means exactly equal to the causal genotype means. Recombination can put a marker allele from one founder on the same chromosome as a causal allele from the other founder. In such a case each marker class contains a mixture of causal genotypes. The difference between marker-class phenotype means can be attenuated even if the causal locus has a strong effect. This reasoning explains why a weak marker association is not automatically evidence that the underlying gene has a weak biological effect. The strength of the observed association depends partly on how accurately the marker predicts the causal inheritance.

An F2 population also enables testing two-locus hypotheses when both markers segregate. For independent biallelic loci there are nine possible joint genotype classes. Some classes can be rare in a finite sample, especially if the loci are linked. To compare the phenotype of AABB with AAbb, for example, it is necessary to have enough individuals in both categories. A large total population does not necessarily supply enough observations for every rare genotype combination. A student should examine expected class counts before proposing a detailed comparison. This is particularly important when an exam question describes a recessive allele at one locus whose effect appears only in the presence of another specific allele.

These examples show why an F2 can provide richer direct information about heterozygous effects than a permanent fixed population, but may require large numbers to represent rare joint classes. A trial that begins with many plants may still lose information if scoring is restricted to survivors or to a subset of phenotypic extremes. The appropriate model depends on which plants were actually included and what was measured on them.

Backcross populations and transparent gamete scoring

A first backcross is produced by crossing the heterozygous F1 to one of its parental genotypes. With AA as recurrent parent, Aa × AA gives equal probabilities of AA and Aa, with no aa class. With aa as recurrent parent, Aa × aa gives equal probabilities of Aa and aa, with no AA class. The difference matters for interpreting a recessive trait: an aa phenotype is observable in the second design but may be absent from the first. The informative parent is the heterozygous F1, while the homozygous parent supplies a known allele to each offspring.

For two markers, the testcross AB/ab × ab/ab is particularly transparent because the tester always contributes ab. Each offspring reveals the F1 haplotype when markers are codominant. AB gives AaBb, ab gives aabb, Ab gives Aabb and aB gives aaBb. If the observed counts are 88, 92, 11 and 9 respectively, the recombinant fraction estimate is (11+9)/200 = 0.10. This estimates the meiotic recombinant fraction under correct phase, viable progeny, representative gamete recovery and reliable genotype scoring. An approximate sampling standard error is the square root of r(1−r)/N, or about 0.0212 for this example. The estimate is not exact merely because the ratio simplifies to a round number.

Repeated backcrossing has a different purpose. Suppose the donor supplies an allele that confers a desirable trait, but an established crop variety provides the favorable genomic background. Crossing the F1 repeatedly to the recurrent parent progressively increases its expected genomic contribution. For neutral, unselected nuclear loci with fair inheritance, the donor allele fraction is halved by each backcross. The F1 has an expected donor genomic proportion of one half, BC1 one quarter, BC2 one eighth and BC3 one sixteenth. After n backcrosses, the expected recurrent proportion is 1−(1/2)^(n+1). At BC5 this is 63/64, approximately 98.44 percent. This is a genome-wide expectation under the stated assumptions, not a guarantee for an individual plant or for a chromosome region retained by selection.

Selecting the donor allele preserves the region linked to it more often than unlinked donor regions. This persistence is called linkage drag when nearby undesirable donor variants accompany a desired allele. Recombination between the target and a neighboring locus can produce chromosomes that retain the desired allele but replace the unfavorable neighbor. The breeder may track markers on both sides of the target to identify such recombinants. Selection for recurrent parent alleles at unlinked markers can accelerate genome recovery, but such selected material no longer follows an unselected genome-wide expectation.

A single backcross can be economical for mapping a trait when one homozygous genotype is the necessary comparator. It cannot estimate the complete additive and dominance contrast at a locus because one homozygous class is absent. Repeated backcrossing is appropriate when testing or transferring a donor segment in a specific background, not when maintaining equal founder contributions throughout the genome. The exam distinction is between the population that maximizes observable segregation for a mapping question and the crossing scheme that progressively replaces a donor background.

Interpreting recurrent-background recovery at a locus

The usual backcross recurrence can be derived without memorizing percentages. Let Dn denote the expected fraction of donor alleles at an unselected nuclear locus after n backcrosses to the recurrent parent. The F1 starts with D0 = 1/2. Each offspring receives one chromosome copy from the recurrent parent, which has no donor allele at the distinguishing locus, and one from the selected preceding generation. Averaged over fair transmission, the expected donor proportion is half the previous proportion. Thus Dn = Dn−1/2. Iteration gives Dn = 2^(−n−1). The recurrent fraction is the complement.

Be careful about the difference between the allele fraction and the proportion of plants heterozygous for a marker. Suppose donor genotype AA and recurrent genotype aa. At BC1, half the plants are Aa and half are aa. Because an Aa plant has one donor allele among two copies, the donor allele frequency across BC1 is one quarter. Under further random backcrossing, the fraction of heterozygotes is one quarter at BC2 and one eighth at BC3, while the corresponding donor allele fractions are one eighth and one sixteenth. These two quantities differ by a factor of two because a donor homozygote is not produced by crossing to aa. In an exam, determine whether the denominator counts plants or allele copies.

The recurrence applies only to regions not affected by selection and other forces. If the donor A allele at a chosen locus must be retained, the selected BC1 individuals are Aa, not a random mixture of Aa and aa. Each additional backcross produces candidates, and the investigator deliberately retains those carrying A. The target locus therefore does not follow the unselected donor-frequency formula within the retained lineages. Nearby donor alleles may be enriched as well because the region is inherited with the selected A allele. A marker on a separate chromosome is less affected by selection on A, under ordinary independent assortment.

To illustrate linkage drag, let the donor carry AB and the recurrent parent ab. We wish to retain A but replace B with b because B has an undesirable effect. In an AB/ab heterozygote the desired recombinant gamete Ab has probability r/2, while all A-bearing gametes have total probability one half. Conditional on recovering an A-bearing gamete, the fraction also carrying b is therefore r. If r = 0.02, only about two percent of the retained A carriers are expected to carry the desired neighboring b allele in that informative meiosis. This is why a tight linkage may require screening many progeny even when the target allele itself is easily retained.

The role of two flanking markers is to identify whether the donor interval extends to the left or right of a target. One flanking recombinant may reduce linkage drag on one side without changing the other. Combining information from both sides can help narrow the retained segment over subsequent generations. Nevertheless, a flanking marker does not show that every intervening DNA base is recurrent or donor in origin, especially if multiple crossovers occurred. Marker-assisted background recovery is a probabilistic and measurement-based process, not an absolute guarantee of genetic identity.

Recombinant inbred lines: fixing a mosaic of ancestry

A recombinant inbred line is derived by repeated inbreeding from a segregating population, commonly through successive generations of self-fertilization in self-compatible plants. Starting with F2 individuals, maintain separate lineages and advance each lineage independently. Single seed descent is one conceptual approach: one seed is used to represent each lineage per generation, with provisions to avoid losing the entire collection. As generations pass, heterozygous segments segregate and are eventually fixed for one or the other parental allele. Different lines become homozygous for different mosaics of the founder chromosomes.

At a neutral locus that is heterozygous in the F1, one generation of selfing leaves half the offspring heterozygous. Once a lineage becomes AA or aa at that locus, further selfing retains that state in the absence of mutation, contamination or outcrossing. Therefore if H1 = 1, H2 = 1/2, H3 = 1/4, and in the Fn generation Hn = (1/2)^(n−1). The expected residual heterozygosity in F6 is 1/32, or 3.125 percent, and in F8 it is 1/128, or about 0.781 percent. Across many neutral independent lineages, the corresponding homozygote frequencies are each (1−Hn)/2. These formulas describe expected locus-level proportions, not a guarantee that every plant or every marker in an advanced line is homozygous.

Recombination can occur while a chromosome interval is still heterozygous in an advancing line. A crossover within a fully fixed interval cannot create a new combination of founder alleles because the homologs carry the same ancestry there. RIL development therefore accumulates useful recombinant breakpoints during successive generations, then preserves them in fixed lines. Lines generated from different independent segregants are the essential units. Taking fifty plants of a single RIL produces fifty replicates of the genotype, not fifty independently sampled recombination histories.

The fixed-line recombinant fraction R is not identical to the per-meiosis fraction r. For two-way RILs produced by selfing to fixation with standard assumptions, R = 2r/(1+2r). At r = 0.10, R = 0.20/1.20 = 0.1667. The inverse is r = R/[2(1−R)]. Thus 40 recombinant lines out of 200 imply R = 0.20 and r = 0.20/(1.60) = 0.125. At short intervals R approaches twice r, but the ratio diminishes as r increases. At independent assortment, both are 0.5. This is why calling every observed RIL breakpoint frequency a meiotic recombination fraction can inflate estimated genetic distance. Other mating designs, such as repeated sibling mating, require their own relationship.

The benefit of fixation is repeatability. A line may be genotyped once and grown across seasons, sites or treatments, with multiple plants per trial. Its genotype remains approximately the same, enabling an analysis of environmental effects and genotype by environment interaction. RILs generally lack the heterozygote class required to estimate within-locus dominance directly. Crosses among selected RILs can introduce heterozygotes for separate experiments, but the base panel alone cannot supply that contrast. Inbreeding depression, sterility or deliberate phenotypic selection during line development can change the genetic distribution, so inspect segregation and lineage survival rather than assuming every theoretical ratio is exact.

Why fixation and recombination accumulation are distinct

Consider a single marker in an F2 lineage. If the chosen F2 plant is AA, its selfed descendants are also AA under the ideal model. If it is Aa, the next generation contains AA, Aa and aa. A lineage initially Aa thus has a chance of fixing either allele in each generation. With no selection favoring one allele, symmetry ensures that the probability of eventual fixation for A equals that for a. Repeated selfing changes genotype frequencies dramatically but preserves the expected allele frequency across many independent lineages. This is the distinction between the reduction of heterozygosity and the replacement of one parental genome through backcrossing.

One can calculate the expected homozygote fraction at a finite generation. In F4, residual heterozygosity is (1/2)^3 = 1/8. The homozygous fraction is 7/8, divided equally between AA and aa in an ideal symmetric population. Thus the expected genotype proportions are 7/16 AA, 2/16 Aa and 7/16 aa. If a panel contains 160 independently advanced F4 lines, the expected counts are 70, 20 and 70. The same marker may be fixed in one lineage and segregating in another. For a panel with thousands of markers, a small per-marker heterozygosity rate can still correspond to substantial residual heterozygosity somewhere in the genome of individual lines.

The two-locus recombination expansion in a selfed RIL can be understood from the changing opportunities for a haplotype transition before fixation. A neutral heterozygous interval may segregate through several selfing generations. A recombinant haplotype formed early can become fixed in a lineage, disappear from it or remain paired with another haplotype. Counting each crossover in every ancestral meiosis as an independent observation would be incorrect because many ancestral haplotypes are not retained in the final line. The fixed-line parameter R summarizes the probability that the parental origin differs at the two loci in a final fixed chromosome; it is not a tally of all ancestral crossovers.

The standard relationship R = 2r/(1+2r) has two important checks. If no recombination is possible, r = 0, R must also be zero. If the loci assort independently, r = 1/2 and R = 1/2, because the fixed ancestry at one locus is independent of the fixed ancestry at the other. For small r, the denominator is close to one and R is approximately 2r. These boundary checks explain why a constant doubling is only a short-interval approximation rather than a universal conversion. For example, if r = 0.30, the correct R is 0.375, not 0.60; a two-locus recombinant fraction above one half would contradict the standard symmetric model.

A useful distinction is between genetic map expansion and actual sample size. Suppose one hundred F1-derived DHs and one hundred fixed selfed RILs are analyzed for a short interval with r = 0.02. The expected recombinant counts are 2 for the DHs and approximately 3.85 for the RILs under the standard model. Neither number is guaranteed. The RIL panel records more expected recombinant ancestry patterns per line, but a DH panel with five hundred independently recovered lines might contain more such patterns in total. Hence design comparisons should be made at the actual achievable number of independent lines, not at an imaginary equal sample size unless the question explicitly specifies equal sizes.

In practice, maintaining independent line identities is essential. If a seed from line A is accidentally propagated as line B, both lines may look valid individually but their genotypes are no longer independent. Including many duplicated lines can inflate apparent precision and reduce effective coverage of the original recombinant diversity. Likewise, repeatedly choosing the tallest individual within each segregating line can favor alleles associated with height, changing the expected neutral distribution. Deliberate selection may be useful for breeding, but the resulting population should be described accurately rather than treated as an unselected RIL panel.

A recurrence derivation for the fixed recombinant probability

The formula for selfed two-parent RILs can be derived by following the remaining heterozygous states until fixation. Let x be the eventual probability of fixing a recombinant haplotype when the starting diplotype is AB/ab, and let y be the corresponding probability starting from Ab/aB. By exchanging the parental and recombinant labels, y = 1−x under the same symmetric meiotic process. Consider a genotype heterozygous at only one of the two loci. Its alternative haplotypes differ at that locus alone, so one is parental and one recombinant relative to the original founders. Repeated selfing eventually fixes either one with equal probability, giving a probability of one half of fixing a recombinant haplotype from any such state.

From the coupling double heterozygote, the two parental gametes each occur with probability p = (1−r)/2 and the recombinant gametes each occur with probability q = r/2. Independent fertilization gives two already fixed parental diplotypes with combined probability 2p², which contribute zero to x. The two already fixed recombinant diplotypes have combined probability 2q² and contribute their full probability. The coupling double heterozygote reappears with probability 2p² and the repulsion double heterozygote occurs with probability 2q². The four singly heterozygous diplotypes together have probability 8pq. They each have a one-half eventual recombinant-fixation probability.

Combining these possibilities gives x = 2q² + 2p²x + 2q²(1−x) + 4pq. Regroup the terms to obtain x[1−2p²+2q²] = 4q²+4pq. Because p+q = 1/2, the right side becomes 4q(p+q) = 2q = r. The difference 2p²−2q² equals 2(p−q)(p+q) = p−q = (1−2r)/2. Therefore the bracket on the left is 1/2+r, and x = r/(1/2+r) = 2r/(1+2r). This is the fixed recombinant ancestry fraction R for the standard selfed two-founder design.

This derivation shows which assumptions matter. We used equivalent male and female gamete probabilities, independent fertilization, no selection, and symmetric eventual fixation of the alternative alleles. The result concerns the ancestry at two loci in a randomly chosen fully fixed line. It is not automatically correct for a finite partially inbred generation, selected lines or a different mating scheme. The recurrence is useful because it makes these conditions visible rather than treating the formula as a conversion factor without a biological foundation.

The formula also provides a practical check on genetic map calculations. Suppose a selfed RIL panel yields 60 recombinant ancestry patterns among 300 fixed lines. Then R = 0.20 and r = 0.125. Under the Haldane mapping model, which assumes a Poisson crossover process without interference, the corresponding distance is −(1/2)ln(1−2r) Morgans, or approximately 0.1438 Morgans (14.38 cM). Treating R directly as r would instead give about 25.54 cM. The two results differ because one correctly separates ancestral fixation from a single meiosis and the other does not. The mapping function is a model-dependent conversion, not a literal measurement of DNA length.

Doubled haploid lines: fixing one gametic genome

A haploid plant or cell contains one basic chromosome set rather than the pair typical of a diploid sporophyte. A doubled haploid is produced when the chromosomes of that haploid genome are doubled, generating a diploid with matching copies of the sampled genome. In many plants, a gametic cell can contribute to haploid development through suitable induction systems; other methods use chromosome elimination following a cross. The biological route varies by species and genotype. The essential genetic point is that doubling copies a previously sampled haploid genome rather than combining two independent gametes.

Starting with a heterozygous F1 at a marker, half of its ordinary gametes contain A and half contain a. Doubling an A genome yields AA, while doubling an a genome yields aa. Under fair transmission and unbiased recovery, the final population has an expected 1:1 genotype ratio and no Aa class. For a two-locus F1 with phase AB/ab and r = 0.08, expected haploid probabilities are 0.46 AB, 0.46 ab, 0.04 Ab and 0.04 aB. Doubling each produces the corresponding fixed diploid combinations AABB, aabb, AAbb and aaBB. A conventional F1-derived DH population reflects recombination in the meiosis that produced the sampled gametic genomes. Chromosome doubling does not itself increase the number of meiotic breakpoints.

This helps explain a common comparison with RILs. DHs provide immediate fixation once haploid production and chromosome doubling succeed, whereas RILs need successive generations of inbreeding. RILs generally record additional recombination accumulated during their development. DHs from an F1 have not undergone those additional segregating generations. However, DHs produced from a later-generation or advanced intercross plant can capture earlier recombination as well. Always identify the source generation before deciding how much recombination a DH population represents.

DH technology is not automatically the fastest practical option for every crop. Some genotypes respond poorly to haploid induction, regeneration or doubling. Haploid fertility can be limited, and genotype-dependent survival or culture response can distort the observed marker ratios. Verify chromosome number, genetic uniformity and parental origin where relevant. A haploid escape or a diploid arising from somatic tissue rather than a gametic cell may not be a true doubled haploid. This is a quality-control issue that matters for interpreting segregation, not a reason to assume the theoretical model has no value.

Like RILs, DH lines can be replicated in trials because they are fixed. They are useful when the question involves additive differences and reproducible genotypes, particularly in species with efficient DH production. Their absence of heterozygotes makes direct dominance estimation impossible in the base panel. They may also lack allelic diversity from other parents, as all conventional biparental designs do. The choice is an experimental trade-off among production time, recovery bias, desired resolution and the kinds of genetic effects that must be estimated.

Tracing information from haploid origin to the fixed line

In ordinary sexual reproduction a diploid offspring combines one genome from each of two gametes. If an F1 is heterozygous at a particular locus, its descendants can inherit two different alleles because the two gametes are sampled separately. In the doubled haploid scheme, the genome of one haploid precursor is represented twice. The difference in sampling is enough to explain why a DH panel does not show the F2 genotype distribution. Both designs may begin with the same F1, but their final chromosome pairs have different genealogies.

Consider an F1 carrying alleles A and a at a marker and another marker B and b on a different chromosome. Under independent assortment, haplotypes AB, Ab, aB and ab each have probability one quarter. Conventional F1-derived doubled haploids show four corresponding fixed two-locus genotypes in that same expected ratio. An F2 has nine possible unphased two-locus genotypes because it combines two gametes. The absence of Aa or Bb in a true doubled haploid is therefore a direct consequence of duplicating one set, not of artificial selection against every heterozygote.

If an apparent DH panel shows a large number of robust heterozygous calls, consider several alternatives before claiming failure of Mendelian segregation. Some assays may detect multiple closely related genomic copies rather than a single locus. Some samples may contain a mixture of plants or cells, and some regenerated diploid plants may have originated from somatic rather than gametic tissues. In a polyploid organism, the definition of homozygosity at homologous or homeologous loci requires additional care. A simple two-allele diploid model does not automatically cover a polyploid marker assay. The appropriate response is to verify the biological material and the genetic interpretation of the assay.

Genotype-dependent regeneration is an especially important limitation when comparing a DH population with the predicted gamete frequencies. Suppose the F1 produces equal A and a gametes, but the probability of recovering a scored line is 0.8 for A and 0.4 for a. Before normalization, expected recovered proportions are 0.5×0.8 = 0.4 and 0.5×0.4 = 0.2. Among recovered lines, the expected ratio is 2:1 rather than 1:1. Such distortion does not require unequal segregation during meiosis. It can arise because later recovery is conditional on genotype. This simple example illustrates why observed DH proportions can reflect both gamete production and the biological process used to create viable lines.

The practical value of DH technology depends on the stage at which a program needs uniform genetic material. A fixed line enables seed multiplication, repeated phenotyping and comparisons among environments. However, the time needed to develop enough independent lines depends on the species and the efficiency of the recovery process. If only a few founders produce large numbers of viable lines, the population may not represent all desired crosses. When comparing DH and RIL development, examine both generation time and diversity retained, rather than assuming that rapid chromosome doubling solves every mapping problem.

Near-isogenic lines: testing a selected interval

A near-isogenic line differs from a comparison line at a relatively restricted genomic region while sharing most of the remaining genetic background. NILs are often constructed by repeatedly backcrossing to a recurrent parent while selecting a donor allele or a marker-defined donor segment, followed by fixation. They can also arise from residual heterozygosity in an otherwise highly inbred line. The important point is the contrast: instead of allowing the whole genome to segregate freely, the investigator tries to isolate the effects of a limited interval.

Suppose an F2 mapping experiment suggests that a chromosome segment from a donor delays flowering. The donor parent also differs at other genes affecting growth, so comparing the original parents is not a strong test of the chromosome segment alone. A NIL carrying the donor segment in the recurrent parent background provides a closer comparison. If the NIL flowers later than the recurrent parent under a controlled environment, the evidence supports a contribution from genetic differences in the introgressed region in that background. It does not prove which DNA change is responsible. The interval may contain several causal genes, and residual donor segments elsewhere may contribute.

Fine mapping can be approached through a set of related lines with overlapping donor segments. Imagine three fixed lines whose donor intervals span markers M1–M4, M2–M4 and M3–M4. If the first two show the trait and the third does not, and if there is a single causal donor allele with stable penetrance and comparable background, the informative difference lies in the region retained by the second but absent from the third. Marker positions only delimit the interval between known breakpoints; they do not define exact crossover coordinates. A conflicting line may indicate additional loci, epistasis, scoring error or an incorrect assumption about the introgressed DNA.

NIL comparisons are especially useful for examining background dependence. A donor allele that affects drought tolerance in one recurrent background may have a smaller effect in another because other genes modify its function. A NIL pair in only one background cannot establish that a locus will have the same effect throughout a species. Likewise, a trait difference that disappears under a new environment does not automatically invalidate the original mapping. It may be a genuine genotype by environment interaction.

An NIL panel is not usually the first choice for unbiased genome-wide mapping of many unknown traits because large parts of the genome are intentionally invariant. Its strength is targeted validation, fine mapping and isolating genetic effects. RILs and DHs distribute alternative parental segments widely across lines; NILs concentrate the contrast. In exams, read carefully whether the question asks for initial discovery, quantitative assessment across environments, or validation of a particular interval. Those are different experimental objectives.

Controls for causal interpretation of an introgressed interval

The word near in near-isogenic line is scientifically important. Isogenic refers to genetic identity, but an NIL differs at least at its intended donor interval, and may differ at additional unrecognized intervals. When studying an NIL pair, background similarity is a controlled approximation that must be supported by marker evidence. A claim of exact identity outside a single gene requires a much stronger characterization than simply performing a specified number of backcrosses.

Suppose an initial mapping population identifies a drought-response interval containing two linked genes, X and Y. The donor alleles of X and Y are introduced together into a recurrent background. If the resulting NIL maintains leaf water content better than the recurrent parent, at least three genetic explanations remain: X may be causal, Y may be causal, or both may contribute. A separate line carrying the donor X region but recurrent Y region, together with another line carrying donor Y but recurrent X, provides a way to distinguish these explanations if suitable recombination and background matching are available. The phenotype of a line carrying both alleles can then help assess whether the effects are additive or interact.

The direction of the comparison also deserves attention. Introducing a donor allele into a recurrent background tests its consequence in that particular context. The reciprocal experiment, placing the recurrent allele or segment into the original donor background, may produce a different result if interacting loci differ. This is not necessarily a contradiction. It can reveal that the effect depends on the other alleles present. Consequently, NIL-based validation can be powerful without supporting a universal prediction across every crop genotype.

To distinguish a genuine region effect from a field artifact, grow the NIL and recurrent parent in comparable conditions with randomized replication. It is valuable to include more than one independently derived line carrying a similar donor interval when possible, because independent construction histories reduce the chance that an accidental background difference explains every observation. If two independent NILs with the target interval show similar trait changes and a corresponding noncarrier line does not, the evidence for the interval is strengthened. A single unreplicated contrast remains much weaker.

Residual heterozygosity within an advanced RIL can provide another source of near-isogenic comparisons. A nearly fixed line may retain one segregating interval. Selfing descendants of that line can produce alternative homozygotes for the interval while preserving most of the background. Such a design is useful because the competing genotypes arise from a closely shared pedigree. It requires verifying that the remaining regions are sufficiently similar and that the target interval actually segregates. As with backcross-derived NILs, the comparison resolves a region rather than automatically identifying a nucleotide.

The purpose of fine mapping is to progressively exclude regions that do not explain the phenotype. It requires accurate knowledge of the donor segments and enough informative lines with different breakpoints. If a line carries a donor segment and shows no phenotype, the result can exclude that segment only under the assumed genetic model, including adequate penetrance, correct background and suitable environment. If a donor phenotype depends on two interacting regions, absence of one region may suppress the effect of the other. A fine-mapping argument is therefore an explicit conditional inference, not merely a visual comparison of colored chromosome bars.

Advanced intercrosses and extra recombination

An advanced intercross population extends the period during which chromosomes from different founders meet in heterozygous individuals. Rather than immediately fixing F2 lineages through selfing, related progenies are intermated for additional generations, ideally maintaining many contributing individuals and avoiding deliberate selection. Repeated recombination can shorten the chromosome blocks that remain associated with a parental allele. Some programs then fix advanced intercross genomes as inbred or doubled haploid lines so that the resulting combinations can be reused.

Extra generations create opportunities rather than guarantees. Closely spaced loci recombine less often than distant ones, and a chromosome segment with suppressed crossing over can remain large even after several intercross generations. Small breeding populations can lose alleles by genetic drift, and repeated use of a few parents can create close relatedness. The number of generations alone is therefore an inadequate description of the information content. The number of independent founder contributions, actual crossover history, marker coverage and viability of the progeny must also be considered.

Consider a hypothetical interval with per-meiosis r = 0.01. If 200 independent informative gametes are sampled in a conventional design, the expected number carrying a recombinant marker combination is two. Extra intermating can produce more distinct ancestry breakpoints in the population, but their count is not obtained by multiplying two by the number of generations without qualification. Breakpoints can be inherited repeatedly by descendants, and a later crossover can obscure a previously recombinant arrangement when only two markers are scored. Mapping methods for advanced pedigrees must reflect the actual breeding history.

The same reasoning explains why multi-parent designs may help when biparental populations contain too little allelic diversity. Additional founders potentially bring more alleles into the experiment. A multi-parent advanced generation intercross is one example, but its specialized statistical analysis is outside the present chapter. More founders also complicate ancestry assignment and can reduce the representation of each individual allele unless the population is large. It is not sufficient to assume that a complex design automatically gives better results.

For present purposes, advanced intercrosses should be understood as a means of increasing useful recombination before fixation or analysis. Their advantage is especially relevant to narrowing a region whose associated markers were inherited together in an initial cross. Their practical disadvantages are additional generation time, pedigree management and potential drift or selection. A simple F2 of large size may be more appropriate when an inexpensive rapid localization is the main objective.

The trade-off between additional generations and independent information

An intercross differs from repeated selfing because a new offspring can inherit haplotypes sampled from different members of the breeding population. If multiple independently recombined chromosomes are brought together, subsequent meioses may produce combinations not present in either immediate parent. This repeated reshuffling can reduce the length of founder-ancestry blocks. Selfing, in contrast, steadily removes heterozygosity, eventually limiting opportunities to alter founder ancestry through recombination. The relevant contrast is thus the persistence of segregating ancestry within the breeding population.

A common mistake is to assume that the number of historical crossovers alone determines localization. Suppose several hundred descendants share a breakpoint that occurred in a single influential ancestor. All those plants can provide replicated phenotype information, but they do not represent hundreds of independent breakpoint discoveries. The pedigree must be considered because descendants are correlated. A large population with balanced contributions from many breeding individuals helps preserve genetic diversity and reduce excessive dependence on a few ancestral genomes.

There is a parallel trade-off with allele frequency. In a two-founder cross, each founder allele may begin with expected frequency one half at a neutral locus. If a small subset of plants contributes disproportionately to later generations, random allele loss can occur. Once a founder allele is lost from a closed breeding population, additional mating among the remaining plants cannot restore it under ordinary inheritance. More generations without adequate population maintenance can therefore reduce informativeness at some loci even while improving recombination at others.

Advanced intercrosses are particularly useful as context for understanding the differences among mapping designs, rather than as a requirement to memorize specialized software or exact map expansion formulas. For example, when an exam asks how to improve localization of a tightly linked region, additional intermating before fixation can be a reasonable option if the species, time and breeding population size permit it. A large testcross or targeted NIL recombination screen can also improve localization in specific circumstances. The choice depends on whether the investigator wants a genome-wide reusable resource or a narrow answer at one region.

Multi-parent designs add a separate variable: the number and identity of founders. An advanced intercross need not involve more than two founders, and a multi-parent population is not automatically advanced unless it has an appropriate breeding history. Distinguishing founder diversity from recombination history prevents a student from attributing all the properties of a complex design to a single term. More parental haplotypes may help detect additional functional alleles, but their effects must be interpreted within the design and the sampled population.

Evidence from the intermated maize population

A documented plant genetics example is the intermated B73 × Mo17 maize mapping resource described by Lee and colleagues in 2002. Its pedigree deliberately introduced several additional mating generations before deriving inbred lines. The purpose was to obtain more observable recombination and thus a finer genetic map from a controlled founder cross. This is a concrete biological example of the principle that the construction of a mapping population determines its information content. It also illustrates how stable genetic resources can be shared by later investigators.

The central hypothesis behind such an experiment is that delaying fixation permits additional ancestry junctions to arise and survive in the final lines. One appropriate comparison is between maps derived from conventional and intermated descendants of the same parental cross, rather than comparing unrelated founder pairs. Keeping the founders the same reduces the possibility that a difference in marker informativeness is simply due to different parental DNA variation. Comparisons must still account for differences in marker set, number of lines, scoring quality and the specific map model used. Expanded map length in such a population reflects its pedigree, not a physical increase in chromosome length.

A subsequent study of this maize resource published in Genetics in 2006 compared marker segregation among related generations and found changes in allele frequencies during the development of the lines. This is an important complement to the expected benefit of intermating: the idealized model of balanced founder representation must be tested against actual data. Changes can arise during the breeding history and can reduce information at particular loci even while recombination opportunities increase elsewhere. The observation itself does not identify every cause of distortion.

A student interpreting this example should separate the experimental outcome from a universal prediction. Additional intermating is capable of enhancing recombination-based resolution, but the exact improvement in one population cannot be assumed for every species, interval or number of lines. An interval with almost no recombination may remain difficult to resolve, and a marker panel may fail to capture some useful ancestry junctions. The maize studies demonstrate why designing, genotyping and evaluating the population are inseparable parts of the mapping process. They do not establish that the advanced design is superior under every budget or experimental objective.

A similar distinction appears in studies of pea recombinant inbred lines, where fixed-line recombination observations have been converted to meiotic fractions before calculating genetic map distances. The principle is consistent with the standard two-founder selfing model discussed above. A population is not merely a list of marker genotypes; the pedigree determines the interpretation of the genotype transitions. The most transferable examination lesson is to identify the relevant generation, the unit of sampling and the statistical meaning of the observed recombinant fraction before applying a mapping formula.

Population size, marker density and map resolution

Genetic map resolution depends on observing recombination that separates a region into smaller independently inherited pieces. If every individual carries either the entire P1 or entire P2 haplotype across a long region, all markers in that region have identical segregation patterns. No statistical comparison within the population can decide which of those markers is nearest the responsible variant. New markers may reveal that an already existing breakpoint lies within the region, but they cannot manufacture a breakpoint that never occurred.

Under a simple testcross or F1-derived DH model, suppose N independent informative gametes are scored for an interval with recombination fraction r. The expected number of recombinant observations is Nr. The probability of seeing no recombinants is (1−r)^N, and the probability of at least one is 1−(1−r)^N. For r = 0.005 and N = 200, the latter is about 0.633. To reach at least 95 percent probability of one or more recombinants, solve (1−r)^N ≤ 0.05. Taking natural logarithms gives N ≥ ln(0.05)/ln(0.995), or about 597.65; round upward to 598. Observing one recombinant does not guarantee reliable fine mapping; multiple informative breakpoints and reliable trait measurements are generally needed.

Marker density is the number of scored loci per unit of map or physical sequence. If markers occur every 20 cM, an existing crossover may only be located to a wide genetic interval between adjacent informative markers. Closer markers can refine that interval, provided they are polymorphic, correctly scored and physically ordered. But in a recombination-poor region, thousands of markers may co-segregate. Additional independently produced recombinants, rather than additional markers alone, are needed to separate them genetically. Conversely, if a cross contains many breakpoints but few markers, denser genotyping can recover information that would otherwise be missed.

Genetic distance and physical distance are related but not interchangeable. A centimorgan describes recombination-based separation; a megabase describes DNA length. Recombination rates vary along chromosomes, across species, between sexes and sometimes across genotypes. Centromeric and structurally rearranged regions can exhibit reduced recombination. A small genetic interval may therefore span a large physical region. The breeder should avoid converting cM to base pairs using a genome-wide constant without establishing whether it is appropriate for the region.

Mapping power is the ability to detect a genuine locus effect under a stated design, whereas resolution describes how narrowly it can be localized. Increasing the number of plants may improve both, but environmental noise mainly undermines power by obscuring phenotype differences. Denser markers primarily improve localization and the ability to represent inheritance accurately. The number of independent genotypes and the number of within-genotype replicates solve different problems. These distinctions provide the foundation for reasoning about quantitative trait mapping without prematurely introducing detailed statistical algorithms.

Reading a map as an experiment rather than a picture

Suppose five adjacent markers have physical coordinates 10, 11, 12, 15 and 20 megabases in an assembled reference genome. A recombinant line has P1 ancestry at the first three markers and P2 ancestry at the fourth and fifth. The simplest interpretation is that at least one ancestry transition lies between the markers at 12 and 15 megabases. The data do not locate the transition to a single base, and an even number of unobserved crossovers between typed markers may be hidden. If a new informative marker at 13 megabases has P1 ancestry, the simplest inferred transition interval narrows to 13–15 megabases. The improved localization comes from detecting an existing ancestry boundary, not from increasing recombination itself.

Now suppose a second line has the opposite ancestry transition between the same two markers. This is another potentially informative genome, but it is not necessarily an independent crossover if the two lines are descendants of the same recombinant individual. Knowing the population's breeding design helps determine which observations are independent. The number of genotyped samples is therefore not always the number of independently sampled meioses. A fixed line panel, a family of related advanced intercross descendants and a simple testcross each have different structures of dependence.

A marker-order discrepancy can arise for several reasons. The reference genome may differ structurally from the parents, the physical assembly may contain an error, the genetic map may be based on too few recombinants, or a particular marker may be incorrectly called. If a line appears to switch ancestry from P1 to P2 and immediately back to P1 at three markers that are physically very close, a double crossover is possible but a genotype error is also a plausible explanation. Rechecking the original genotype data is often more appropriate than accepting every isolated discordant call as an authentic double recombinant. The same principle applies when inferring fine-mapping breakpoints.

The expected probability of recovering a recombinant increases with independent sample size, but the estimate has uncertainty. At r = 0.01, N = 100 gives an expectation of one recombinant, yet the chance of no recombinant is about 0.366. In other words, roughly one third of experiments with these ideal conditions would observe zero even if recombination were possible. A zero observation is not proof of absolute linkage. Using the binomial model, if no recombinants occur in N observations, an approximate one-sided 95 percent upper bound on r for large N and small r is 3/N. For N = 100 this is roughly 0.03. The approximation follows from solving (1−r)^N = 0.05, which gives r = 1−0.05^(1/N), about 0.0295.

When comparing sample-size plans, distinguish the goal of detecting one breakpoint from the goal of estimating the recombination fraction precisely. A single recombinant can establish a minimal observed separation but gives a highly variable estimate of r. Multiple independent recombinants provide greater evidence, and different breakpoint positions help subdivide the region. For a phenotype that requires two loci jointly, a recombinant chromosome may be informative only when the appropriate partner genotype is present. These conditions are why practical fine-mapping populations are often larger than a simple calculation for the first recombinant might suggest.

A practical quantitative comparison of competing designs

Consider a hypothetical budget permitting either 240 independently derived DH lines or 160 independently derived fixed selfed RILs from the same cross. Suppose a narrow interval has meiotic r = 0.01. The expected recombinant count in the DH panel is 240×0.01 = 2.4. In the RIL panel the fixed recombinant probability is 0.02/1.02, approximately 0.01961, and the expected count is 160×0.01961 ≈ 3.14. Under ideal independence, the probability of at least one recombinant is about 0.910 for the DH panel and 0.958 for the RIL panel. This comparison suggests an advantage for the smaller RIL panel in this particular short interval, but not because the line count is irrelevant. The effective probability per line differs between designs.

Now imagine that the trait is measured so poorly in the RIL panel that individuals with contrasting causal genotypes cannot be distinguished reliably, whereas the DH panel has precise replicated phenotype measurements. The greater probability of an ancestry breakpoint does not ensure greater power for trait localization. A recombinant observation is most useful when the genotype and phenotype provide enough evidence to discriminate between alternative hypotheses. Thus a design can be genetically rich but phenotypically weak. Conversely, excellent replication cannot compensate for a total absence of recombination within an unresolved interval.

The investigator can also allocate resources differently within a population type. Suppose 600 experimental plots are available for fixed lines. One option is 200 lines measured in three independent plots each; another is 100 lines measured in six plots each. Both use the same number of plots, but the first samples twice as many independent fixed genotypes. The second gives more precise genotype means under independent plot errors. If the main difficulty is obtaining recombinants in a short interval, the first may be preferable. If the genetic effect is difficult to distinguish from environmental variation, the second may be useful. There is no exact optimal allocation without information about effect size, variance, pedigree and experimental costs.

The precision of a mean illustrates why replication yields diminishing gains. If independent plot errors have standard deviation σ, the standard error of a mean over k plots is σ/√k. Increasing k from one to four halves the error. Increasing k from four to sixteen halves it again but requires twelve additional plots per line rather than three. Increasing the number of line genotypes, however, supplies additional independent genetic observations rather than only reducing within-line noise. Experimental design requires balancing these benefits.

A further constraint arises when a marker class is rare. In an ideal fixed two-parent population, a neutral biallelic marker is expected to split the lines equally. If distortion leaves only ten percent of lines with a donor allele, a panel of 100 lines may have only around ten donor-class observations. Even if the entire panel is large enough to detect some associations, estimates for that specific marker may be imprecise. Replicating existing donor lines improves their phenotype means but does not create additional independent genomes carrying the donor allele. Increasing representation through an appropriate breeding design or targeted validation may be necessary.

Phenotyping, replication and environmental control

A perfect genetic map cannot rescue an unreliable phenotype. A plant's height, flowering time or disease-response score may reflect its inherited genotype, temperature, soil conditions, light exposure, developmental stage and measurement error. Quantitative traits commonly show overlapping distributions among genotype classes. Mapping relies on comparing groups of plants or lines that differ in inherited markers while reducing environmental variation and quantifying uncertainty.

Replication means observing the same genotype or experimental condition in independently allocated experimental units. For a fixed RIL or DH line, multiple plants can be grown in replicated plots, blocks, sites or seasons. In an F2 of seed-propagated plants, an individual is ordinarily unique and cannot be recreated from selfed seed when heterozygous. Clonal propagation may permit replication of that genotype in species amenable to it. Alternatively, progeny families can provide information about the parent, but the family may segregate and must not be treated as a set of genetically identical individuals.

Randomization protects against systematic association of genotypes with environmental gradients. If all P1-like lines are planted at one end of a field and P2-like lines at the other, an apparent marker effect could arise because the soil is wetter at the first end. Randomized complete blocks, when suitable, place every treatment or genotype once in each block so that local environmental effects can be accounted for. Large line panels may use other layouts, but the principle is the same: spatial and temporal differences should not consistently align with marker classes. Record environmental conditions and relevant covariates rather than assuming uniformity.

Suppose the phenotypic error variance for independent plants of a fixed genotype is 16 squared units. The error variance of a mean of four independent observations is 16/4 = 4, and the standard error of that mean is 2 units. Increasing to sixteen observations reduces the standard error to 1 unit. The benefit follows from the independence assumption. If plants share a plot and are exposed to the same localized stress, their errors may be correlated, and treating each plant as an independent field replicate exaggerates precision. Distinguish subsampling within a plot from independent replicate plots.

Genotype by environment interaction occurs when genetic differences have different phenotypic consequences across environments. If line A is taller than line B in one environment and shorter in another, their reaction norms cross. Even if the ranking is unchanged, a change in the magnitude of the difference can indicate interaction. Permanent lines support such comparisons because the same genotypes can be studied repeatedly. The resulting evidence helps determine whether a mapped effect is stable, conditional or relevant only under a particular treatment.

Phenotype and genotype records must be linked through stable identifiers. Seed mixture, swapped labels, inconsistent developmental stages and missing observations can all create misleading marker associations. Missing genotypes or phenotypes should be recorded explicitly, not substituted with the most convenient class. Before drawing conclusions, verify founder polymorphism, segregating marker calls, plot identities and the reproducibility of measurements. A mapping population is as much an experimental resource as a collection of chromosomes.

Recognizing distortion, confounding and experimental units

Segregation distortion is a departure from the expected genotype proportions for a specified mating design. It may reflect unequal gamete production, differential fertilization, embryo or seed viability, genotype-dependent recovery, selection during line development, scoring errors or accidental mixing of families. A deviation from 1:2:1 in an F2 or from 1:1 in a fixed biparental panel is therefore a signal to investigate, not a diagnosis of one molecular mechanism. Compare deviations at neighboring markers, consider stage-specific sampling and examine whether the parents and F1 were correctly characterized.

For example, a hypothetical F2 begins with expected genotype counts 50 AA, 100 Aa and 50 aa among 200 embryos. Suppose all AA and Aa individuals survive, but only half the aa individuals survive to the scoring stage. The observed surviving counts become 50, 100 and 25, totaling 175. The proportions are then 2/7, 4/7 and 1/7 rather than 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. The deviation can arise despite completely ordinary meiosis and fertilization. A comparison between genotypes before and after the period of mortality could help distinguish postzygotic selection from transmission distortion. In an exam, pay attention to the developmental stage at which the counts were recorded.

Phenotypic selection can create additional distortion. If a breeder keeps only the earliest-flowering half of an F2 to construct a mapping panel, alleles affecting flowering time will be represented disproportionately among the retained plants. This can be useful for particular targeted analyses, but the retained sample is not a random F2. For example, comparing marker frequencies to 1:2:1 without taking the selection scheme into account may identify loci associated with the selection trait rather than a violation of meiosis. Random selection of individuals for a general-purpose mapping population preserves the simple expected genotype model more closely.

The experimental unit is the smallest unit to which the treatment or environment is independently assigned. If all plants of a genotype grow together in a single pot, increasing the number of leaves measured from that pot provides more measurements of one experimental unit, not independent treatment replicates. A field plot containing several plants may be the experimental unit for a fertilizer treatment. Independent blocks or plots assigned separately provide a basis for estimating treatment and environmental variation. This distinction matters even if the marker data are flawless, because artificially treating repeated subsamples as independent can make a weak association appear more precise than it is.

Consider a panel of 80 RILs with four plants per line in each of three blocks. There are 960 measured plants, but only 80 independently derived line genotypes. If the plots contain four plants together, there are 240 line-by-block plots. The genetic sample size for many mapping questions is 80 lines, while the phenotyping precision is supported by repeated plots and subsamples. Neither the number 960 nor the number 240 can be substituted uncritically for the number of independently sampled recombinant genomes. The experimental question determines which kind of information each observation contributes.

The same design can be extended across two environments. Ideally the full line panel is represented in both. If one environment contains only P1-like marker genotypes and the other only P2-like genotypes, genotype and environment are confounded. No statistical adjustment can fully restore a comparison never observed. When a question asks whether a marker effect depends on environment, look for the same genetic groups measured in both environments, with enough independent replication to estimate uncertainty. A balanced or suitably connected design is part of the biological evidence.

Interpreting line means with an illustrative dataset

Consider six fixed lines evaluated in two environments, normal water supply and reduced water supply. Three lines carry the P1 allele at a candidate marker and three carry the P2 allele. Their hypothetical trait means under normal supply are 20, 22 and 24 for the first group and 19, 21 and 23 for the second. Under reduced water supply they are 17, 18 and 19 for the first group and 10, 11 and 12 for the second. The first group averages 22 under normal water and 18 under reduced water, while the second averages 21 and 11. The marker-class contrast is one unit in the first environment and seven units in the second.

The data illustrate a possible allele-associated response to water availability rather than simply an allele-associated difference in overall growth. The difference between the marker contrasts is six units. One could also calculate each group's response to stress: P1-associated lines decline by four units, while P2-associated lines decline by ten. These two descriptions are equivalent ways to express the interaction contrast. A proper inference would require replicate error estimates, appropriate experimental units and sufficient independently derived lines. With just six line means and no estimates of uncertainty, the numbers are illustrative, not evidence from a published experiment.

Even if the association is statistically convincing, the marker is not necessarily the causal variant. The three P1-associated lines may share a larger region of parental ancestry, and different linked genes in that region could produce the apparent response. The same line group may also share alleles at another locus by chance, especially in a small population. A larger independently derived panel and a verified genetic map help reduce these ambiguities. Targeted recombinant or near-isogenic comparisons would provide more specific evidence for the candidate interval.

Measurements must be made at biologically comparable stages. If the reduced-water group develops more slowly, measuring all plants at an identical chronological age may compare different developmental stages. Sometimes the developmental delay is itself the trait of interest; in other cases it confounds measurement of a different trait. Define the phenotype before conducting the experiment and report the measurement criterion. In a mapping question, the correct genetic conclusion depends not only on the observed segregation but also on what the phenotype actually represents.

The value of a permanent population is especially clear in this example. The same line can be observed in both environments even if a particular individual plant is not physically reused. That repeated genotype permits an environmental response to be estimated without confusing the response with a different randomly sampled nuclear genotype. Individual F2 plants could only provide a comparable direct contrast if true clonal replicates or another justified design were available. Replication of fixed lines thus helps answer a biological question that may be difficult to address using a single generation of segregating individuals.

From experimental question to population choice

For a simple qualitative locus with a reliable visible phenotype, a large F2 may provide rapid mapping and reveal whether heterozygotes resemble one parent or have an intermediate phenotype. A testcross or backcross is attractive when the chosen tester exposes the alleles contributed by the F1 and dominance contrasts beyond the available classes are unnecessary. These choices prioritize speed and interpretability rather than the long-term reuse of lines. If the question requires repeated assessment of the same genotypes in many environmental conditions, a fixed panel becomes more attractive.

The distinction between additive and dominance effects can be seen without sophisticated QTL statistics. For genotype means μAA, μAa and μaa, define the homozygote midpoint m = (μAA+μaa)/2, the additive contrast a = (μAA−μaa)/2, and the dominance deviation d = μAa−m. Suppose the means are 24, 21 and 12. Then m = 18, a = 6 and d = 3. The heterozygote exceeds the homozygote midpoint by three units but does not reach the higher homozygote. A fixed RIL or DH panel with only AA and aa can estimate a homozygote contrast but cannot directly estimate d. A single backcross supplies two genotype means, which is not enough to estimate all three quantities without additional assumptions or data.

For a polygenic trait with noisy measurements, RILs or DHs allow the same line genotypes to be assessed across replicated trials. RIL development may take additional generations but can capture more crossovers than an F1-derived DH design. DHs can shorten the time to fixation where production is efficient. A study aiming to test a specific donor region in a commercially useful genetic background is better suited to NILs or a targeted backcross-derived panel. Fine mapping within that region requires informative recombinants, adequate genotyping and a phenotype precise enough to distinguish the recombinant classes.

The choice of population also affects biological generalization. An allele's effect in a biparental cross is conditional on the other segregating alleles and the environments represented in the experiment. A marker that tags a favorable allele in one cross may not tag it in unrelated germplasm because linkage phase differs. Validation in a new cross or near-isogenic comparison is therefore useful even after a convincing initial association. A chromosome region may contain linked variants whose individual effects are difficult to separate; additional recombination and targeted comparisons can address that problem.

A strong examination answer links a recommendation to the constraint in the question. If the question requires dominance, preserve heterozygotes. If it requires replicated genotype means, favor fixed lines or genuine clones. If it asks for targeted genetic background control, consider NILs. If it asks for separating tightly linked markers, consider independent recombination events, not merely marker count. If production time is the limiting factor, compare a rapid F2 with species-specific DH feasibility. There is no universally optimal mapping population because each pedigree changes both the genetic information available and the resources needed to obtain it.

A worked planning example: a crop with uncertain inheritance

Imagine a self-compatible crop in which two true-breeding parents differ in seedling growth under low nitrogen. The research group wishes first to identify major genomic regions associated with growth and later determine whether effects persist in a normal nitrogen environment. It has resources for 300 plants in a preliminary greenhouse experiment but may develop a permanent panel for subsequent years. The scientific objectives can be separated into initial localization, inheritance characterization, reproducibility and causal follow-up.

A preliminary F2 can establish whether the phenotype shows a discrete segregation pattern or a continuous distribution and can associate marker genotypes with trait variation. Because the population includes heterozygotes, the group can compare the marker-class means and examine whether heterozygotes are intermediate, resemble one homozygote or exceed both. If the initial trait distribution is continuous, the group should not force it into a Mendelian 3:1 ratio without a defensible threshold and evidence. Continuous variation is consistent with environmental effects, multiple loci or both.

For repeated trials, a set of RILs descended from independently sampled segregants would permit stable line identities. The group could compare the same lines under normal and low nitrogen, preferably with randomized allocation and independently replicated plots. If the species has reliable doubled haploid production, DHs may provide a faster route to a fixed panel. The group should compare the number of lines realistically obtainable, the possibility of biased line recovery, the generation time and the desired genetic resolution. It should also recognize that neither fixed panel directly preserves dominance contrasts.

Suppose an association on one chromosome is supported in the F2 and again in the permanent panel. That agreement is useful, but the interval still contains several genes and may have different effects in different environments. A targeted near-isogenic comparison can test whether the donor region contributes to the trait in a chosen recurrent background. Overlapping donor segments or newly recovered recombinants may help refine the interval. Confirmation that a particular molecular variant causes the effect would require separate functional evidence appropriate to the biological question.

This staged design illustrates how different populations can complement one another. The F2 permits rapid initial inference and heterozygote comparisons; a fixed panel permits more precise repeated phenotyping; NILs provide more controlled comparisons at a selected region. An examination may ask for the best single population given a limited objective, but in an actual genetic study several successive designs may be appropriate. Their value comes from the specific information each produces, not from a ranking in which one type is universally superior.

Finally, the experimental record should state the parent genotypes, breeding procedure, number of independently derived individuals or lines, marker system, treatment allocation, phenotyping method, missing data and assumptions used in inference. These details let another student or researcher interpret the evidence correctly. A population is not just a genetic resource; it is a documented sampling process. In the following chapters, the same habit of linking observed classes to an explicitly defined transmission mechanism will also support reasoning about bacterial genetic mapping.

Misconceptions and examination traps

F1 plants form a segregating panel
Homozygous founders give uniform F1 heterozygotes; an F2 or another segregating design is needed.

Why it is tempting: F1 uniformity is easily confused with segregation because its parents differ strongly. Exam reasoning: If the question asks for a population showing marker segregation, trace the gametes and identify the first generation with alternative genotypes.

An F2 always has a 3:1 ratio
The genotype ratio is 1:2:1; 3:1 refers to complete dominance for the observed phenotype.

Why it is tempting: The familiar phenotypic Mendelian ratio is often memorized without its dominance assumption. Exam reasoning: If a question asks for marker genotypes or incomplete dominance, keep all three genotype classes separate.

Every backcross is a testcross
A testcross uses a suitable tester; backcrossing to a dominant homozygote can conceal a recessive phenotype.

Why it is tempting: The terms overlap in many textbook crosses but describe different purposes. Exam reasoning: Read the tester genotype and ask whether the unknown gamete can be recognized from the scored offspring.

A backcross gives three genotype classes
A first backcross to a homozygous parent gives two classes at a segregating locus.

Why it is tempting: Students may transfer the F2 Punnett square to a backcross. Exam reasoning: List the gametes of the homozygous parent first; it supplies only one allele at the locus.

Selfing halves the donor allele frequency
Selfing halves expected heterozygosity, not the neutral allele frequency across independent lines.

Why it is tempting: Both backcrossing and selfing reduce heterozygosity, but only one recurrent genome is repeatedly supplied in a backcross. Exam reasoning: Distinguish heterozygote proportion from allele frequency and write the appropriate recurrence.

RIL recombination fraction equals meiotic r
For selfed two-parent fixed lines R = 2r/(1+2r), under the standard assumptions.

Why it is tempting: A line's ancestry switch resembles a recombinant gamete, but it can reflect several generations. Exam reasoning: Identify the breeding scheme and invert the RIL relationship before applying a meiotic map function.

Every F8 RIL is completely homozygous
Residual expected heterozygosity is 1/128 per initially heterozygous locus under ideal selfing.

Why it is tempting: A high percentage of fixation can be mistaken for complete fixation. Exam reasoning: Calculate residual heterozygosity per locus and avoid assuming it is exactly zero.

Chromosome doubling adds a new meiosis
It duplicates the haploid genome; it does not introduce another meiotic crossover.

Why it is tempting: The word doubling can suggest an extra round of chromosome exchange. Exam reasoning: Trace one haploid genome into two matching chromosome sets; no extra meiotic segregation is implied.

DH lines can directly estimate dominance
Fixed lines lack heterozygotes; a separate cross or F2 is needed.

Why it is tempting: Students often equate genotype differences with all genetic effects. Exam reasoning: Ask whether a heterozygote mean is available; without it, the dominance deviation cannot be estimated directly.

NILs are perfectly identical outside one gene
Residual donor segments or multiple linked genes may remain.

Why it is tempting: The name is sometimes interpreted as absolute isogenicity. Exam reasoning: Check interval boundaries and residual background variation before making a causal inference.

Repeated backcrossing guarantees exact background recovery
The recurrence formula is an unselected expectation, not an individual's genome measurement.

Why it is tempting: A genome-wide average is easily confused with a deterministic outcome. Exam reasoning: Use the expected fraction only for appropriate unselected regions; identify any selected donor interval.

More markers guarantee more genetic resolution
Co-segregating markers cannot separate a region without informative recombination.

Why it is tempting: Genotyping resolution and recombination resolution are often conflated. Exam reasoning: Find an actual ancestry transition between the markers; if none exists, additional markers alone cannot separate their effects.

More plants from one RIL create more independent recombinant lines
They replicate the same genotype and do not supply independent ancestry breakpoints.

Why it is tempting: Many measured plants look like a large mapping sample. Exam reasoning: Count unique independently derived lines separately from phenotype replicates or subsamples.

A linked marker is the causal gene
Association localizes an interval; causal variants require additional evidence.

Why it is tempting: Perfect association is tempting to interpret as direct molecular causation. Exam reasoning: Identify the unresolved linked region and request independent genetic or functional evidence.

Different environments invalidate a QTL
A reproducible difference in allele effect across environments can be genotype by environment interaction.

Why it is tempting: A conditional effect can be mistaken for lack of reproducibility. Exam reasoning: Compare the same genotypes across environments, with replication, and test whether the contrast changes.

Part B foundations

  1. Which marker is informative between parents AA and aa?

    1. A monomorphic SNP
    2. A codominant A/a marker
    3. A marker scored only in unrelated species
    4. A nonheritable color measurement
    Answer and reasoning

    B. A codominant marker distinguishing A and a. Both parental alleles differ; the F1 and later genotype classes can be tracked. The monomorphic SNP cannot track founder ancestry; an assay in unrelated material is irrelevant; a nonheritable measurement cannot be a genetic marker.

  2. What single-locus genotype ratio is expected in an ideal F2?

    1. 3:1:0
    2. 1:1:1
    3. 1:2:1
    4. 1:0:1
    Answer and reasoning

    C. 1:2:1. Two independent A/a gametes produce AA, Aa and aa with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. 3:1 is a complete-dominance phenotype ratio, not three genotype classes; 1:1:1 and 1:0:1 do not follow random union of Aa gametes.

  3. Which genotype is absent from Aa × aa?

    1. AA
    2. Aa
    3. aa
    4. Both Aa and aa
    Answer and reasoning

    A. AA. The tester never contributes A. Aa and aa are each produced with probability one half; neither is absent.

  4. What is a recurrent parent?

    1. The individual first measured
    2. The parent that provides all new mutations
    3. The donor of the selected allele
    4. The parent repeatedly crossed into the lineage
    Answer and reasoning

    D. The parent used repeatedly in backcrossing. Its genetic background is progressively recovered. A donor supplies desired variation, while measurement order or mutation origin does not define recurrent-parent status.

  5. What is the expected residual heterozygosity at F5 under selfing?

    1. 1/4
    2. 1/16
    3. 1/32
    4. 0
    Answer and reasoning

    B. 1/16. Four rounds of selfing since F1 halve heterozygosity four times. F5 is four selfings after F1; 1/4 and 1/32 refer to different generations, while complete fixation is not guaranteed.

  6. How does an F1-derived DH obtain homozygosity?

    1. Two unrelated diploid genomes fuse
    2. A heterozygote is repeatedly outcrossed
    3. One haploid chromosome set is doubled
    4. A diploid loses all its chromosomes
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Doubling a haploid chromosome set. Both copies derive from the same gametic genome. Fusion of independent genomes or outcrossing can retain heterozygosity; chromosome loss is not duplication.

  7. Which population typically retains heterozygotes for direct dominance analysis?

    1. Fixed DH panel
    2. NIL pair homozygous at the target
    3. Fully fixed RIL panel
    4. F2
    Answer and reasoning

    D. F2. The two homozygotes and the heterozygote coexist. All three fixed resources normally lack the heterozygous class necessary to estimate a within-locus dominance deviation.

  8. Which design emphasizes a restricted donor segment in a common background?

    1. NIL
    2. Unselected F2
    3. Unrelated diversity panel
    4. Random F1 individuals from identical parents
    Answer and reasoning

    A. NIL. Background similarity improves targeted genetic comparison. The other resources do not specifically isolate a selected donor interval within an otherwise similar background.

  9. What does one centimorgan measure?

    1. Exactly one million base pairs
    2. A physical chromosome band
    3. A recombination-based map distance
    4. The number of marker alleles
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Genetic map distance. It is not a fixed number of DNA bases. Base-pair length, cytological position and allele number are different quantities; cM is a genetic-distance unit.

  10. Why can RILs be grown repeatedly?

    1. Every line has the same alleles as every other line
    2. The lines are largely homozygous within each line
    3. All seeds undergo new recombination between founder alleles
    4. The phenotype never changes across environments
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Their ancestry mosaics are largely fixed. Seeds from a fixed line maintain its genotype barring contamination or mutation. Distinct lines differ, fixed regions do not yield new founder combinations, and environmental effects can still alter phenotype.

  11. What parental characteristic is essential for an informative biparental marker?

    1. Identical founder alleles
    2. Only high species-wide SNP diversity
    3. A particular dominant phenotype
    4. Distinguishable founder marker alleles
    Answer and reasoning

    D. Distinguishable alleles at the marker. A highly variable marker in a species is not useful if the two chosen parents carry identical alleles. Species-wide diversity is irrelevant when both founders have the same state; a phenotype is not necessarily an informative marker.

  12. Which genotype represents a fixed DH from an Ab gamete?

    1. AAbb
    2. AaBb
    3. AB/ab
    4. aaBB
    Answer and reasoning

    A. AAbb. Each haploid allele is duplicated, so the genome contains two copies of A and b. A single Ab gamete duplicates A and b; the other genotypes do not represent that gamete.

  13. What is the expected allele frequency of A after many neutral selfing generations starting from Aa?

    1. 0
    2. 1
    3. 1/2
    4. 1/4
    Answer and reasoning

    C. 1/2 across independent lines. Selfing changes heterozygosity, not the expected allele frequency in the symmetric neutral model. Fixation can favor either allele equally across neutral independent lines; the loss of heterozygosity does not imply loss of A.

  14. Why is a codominant marker advantageous in F2 mapping?

    1. It measures the environment directly
    2. It distinguishes AA, Aa and aa
    3. It prevents crossing over
    4. It makes all loci independent
    Answer and reasoning

    B. It distinguishes both homozygotes and the heterozygote. A presence–absence marker can merge AA and Aa, losing genotype information. A marker measures genotype, not environment; it neither blocks crossing over nor changes chromosome linkage.

  15. Which population is produced by repeatedly mating to a founder?

    1. Repeated backcross
    2. F2 selfing only
    3. A conventional F1-derived DH
    4. An unrelated association panel
    Answer and reasoning

    A. A repeated backcross population. The recurrent genome is increasingly represented, except where selection or linkage alters recovery. Only repeated backcrossing systematically mates descendants to a founder to recover that founder's background.

  16. What does linkage drag mean?

    1. Loss of all donor alleles
    2. A marker on an unrelated chromosome
    3. A form of chromosome doubling
    4. Retention of linked unwanted donor variation
    Answer and reasoning

    D. Retention of unwanted donor alleles linked to a selected donor allele. A nearby variant can travel with the desired allele until recombination separates them. The linked donor region may persist with a selected allele; complete donor loss, unlinked markers and doubling are distinct.

  17. Why use multiple independent RILs?

    1. To make all lines genetically identical
    2. To sample independent recombinant histories
    3. To avoid measuring phenotypes
    4. To eliminate chromosome linkage
    Answer and reasoning

    B. To represent multiple recombinant ancestry histories. Many plants from one fixed line do not supply new independent recombination observations. Independent derivations provide genetic diversity; they neither remove all linkage nor replace phenotyping.

  18. Which process helps reduce ancestry-block length before fixation?

    1. Immediate fixation of one F1 gamete
    2. Repeated selection of one individual only
    3. Intermating before fixation
    4. Doubling an identical chromosome set
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Additional intermating in an advanced intercross. It can generate new recombinant haplotypes while segregation is maintained. Intermating creates additional opportunities for ancestry exchange; immediate fixation and repeated use of one lineage limit them.

  19. What does a NIL comparison primarily validate?

    1. A region effect in the tested genetic background
    2. The exact causal nucleotide in every case
    3. A universal effect in every species
    4. The complete absence of residual donor DNA
    Answer and reasoning

    A. An effect of the differing genomic interval in the tested background. It does not by itself identify a single causal variant. NIL comparisons support region-level inference, not universal effects, nucleotide identity or complete background identity.

  20. What is the purpose of randomization in a field trial?

    1. To ensure no mutation occurs
    2. To double the number of independent genotypes
    3. To eliminate all environmental variation
    4. To avoid systematic association of genotypes with environmental gradients
    Answer and reasoning

    D. To avoid systematic alignment of genotype with environmental variation. Random assignment protects genotype comparisons from spatial and other allocation biases. Randomization does not change genotype number or guarantee absence of mutation and noise; it protects against allocation bias.

Part C analytical practice

  1. In an ideal F2 of 320 plants, compare the expected number of heterozygotes before and after all aa plants die. Explain why the heterozygote proportion changes.

    Answer and reasoning

    160 before and after; proportion changes from 1/2 to 2/3. Initially AA:Aa:aa = 80:160:80. If all aa die and others survive, 240 plants remain. The absolute number of Aa is unchanged but 160/240=2/3; survival is not evidence of altered gametic segregation.

  2. A testcross yields parental counts 75 and 85 and recombinant counts 18 and 22. A second independent sample of 200 progeny yields 8 and 12 recombinants. Compute the two estimates and the pooled estimate, and identify one assumption of pooling.

    Answer and reasoning

    r1=0.20, r2=0.10, pooled r=0.15. The samples give 40 and 20 recombinant progeny; pooling gives 60/400. Pooling is meaningful as an estimate of a common parameter only if the two samples share the same recombination process, scoring and selection assumptions.

  3. For R = 0.25 in a fixed selfed RIL, estimate meiotic r.

    Answer and reasoning

    1/6, approximately 0.167. Invert R = 2r/(1+2r) to obtain r = R/[2(1−R)].

  4. Compare BC4 and BC5 with the same unselected recurrent-parent scheme. Calculate recurrent-genome fractions and the expected reduction in donor proportion. Would the selected donor locus follow these values?

    Answer and reasoning

    31/32 and 63/64; donor fraction halves; the selected locus does not follow this expectation. BC4 donor proportion is 1/32 and BC5 is 1/64. Retaining the desired donor allele conditions the pedigree at the selected locus, and linked regions can remain donor-enriched.

  5. Two independent fixed-line trials have error variance 36 per plot. Design A uses four plots per line and design B uses nine. Compare the standard errors of line means and explain which gives more independent recombinant genotypes if both use 360 plots.

    Answer and reasoning

    Design A: SE 3 and 90 lines; B: SE 2 and 40 lines. SE=√(36/k). With a fixed total plot budget A samples more genotypes, while B estimates each line mean more precisely. The best design depends on recombination and phenotype constraints.

  6. A NIL with donor M1–M4 and one with M2–M4 show the trait; one with M3–M4 does not. What follows?

    Answer and reasoning

    The informative region is retained in M2–M4 but absent in M3–M4. Under the single-locus, comparable-background assumption, the contrasting segment narrows the region.

  7. A marker is perfectly associated with a trait in 200 plants, but no recombinant separates it from five neighboring markers. Can it be called causal?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. The marker group is genetically unresolved in the sampled population.

  8. Genotype means AA=30, Aa=28, aa=10. Find a and d.

    Answer and reasoning

    a = 10, d = 8. The midpoint is 20; half the homozygote difference is 10 and the heterozygote deviates by 8.

  9. A breeder needs the same 150 genotypes in three seasons and dominance at one locus. Is a DH panel alone sufficient?

    Answer and reasoning

    No. The panel provides repeatable homozygotes but needs an additional heterozygous design for dominance.

  10. Compare two experiments on an interval with r=0.005. Experiment A scores 100 independent gametes, B scores 500. Compute expected recombinant counts and the probability of at least one in each.

    Answer and reasoning

    A: 0.5, about 0.394; B: 2.5, about 0.918. Expected counts are Nr; the probability is 1−(1−r)^N. A fractional expectation is a long-run average, not a possible observed count. B improves the chance of detecting a breakpoint but does not ensure fine mapping.

  11. A testcross has 12 recombinants among 150 scored progeny. A technician excludes 30 parental progeny because they are harder to phenotype. Compare the original and filtered r estimates and explain the bias.

    Answer and reasoning

    0.08 originally; 0.10 after excluding parental progeny. 12/150=0.08; 12/120=0.10. Selective removal of a genotype class changes the denominator and makes observed recombinant frequency unrepresentative.

  12. A selfed RIL population has R = 0.40. What r does the ideal model imply?

    Answer and reasoning

    1/3. r = 0.40/[2(0.60)] = 0.3333; the fixed-line fraction exceeds the meiotic fraction.

  13. Under ideal selfing, 160 independent F4 lines are examined. Predict AA, Aa and aa counts.

    Answer and reasoning

    70, 20, 70. H = 1/8. Each homozygote has probability 7/16, yielding 70; heterozygotes yield 20.

  14. Two founders differ in flowering time but carry the same SNP at M1; they differ at nearby M2. Every late-flowering F2 is associated with the P1 allele of M2. Which conclusions follow? I: M1 can track founder ancestry. II: M2 is informative. III: M2 is necessarily causal.

    Answer and reasoning

    Only II follows. M1 is monomorphic in this cross. M2 differentiates the founders and can track inheritance, but its association with flowering may reflect linkage to another causal site.

  15. An F1 AB/ab has r = 0.20. What is the probability of AAbb in its selfed F2?

    Answer and reasoning

    0.01. AAbb requires two Ab recombinant gametes, each with probability 0.10; the product is 0.01.

  16. An F1 yields equal A and a gametes but their DH recovery probabilities are 0.9 and 0.3. Predict the ratio among recovered lines.

    Answer and reasoning

    3 AA : 1 aa. Weighted frequencies are 0.45 and 0.15; normalize to 0.75 and 0.25.

  17. A recurrent parent is aa and the donor is AA. What fraction of unselected BC2 plants should be heterozygous?

    Answer and reasoning

    1/4. The Aa class begins at 1/2 in BC1 and halves in BC2; donor allele frequency is 1/8.

  18. A marker interval with r = 0.02 is sampled in 50 independent gametes. What is the probability of no recombinants?

    Answer and reasoning

    Approximately 0.364. Under the binomial model, P(0) = 0.98^50 ≈ 0.364.

  19. A trial has 100 RILs, three replicate plots each and two plants per plot. How many distinct line genotypes and plots are represented?

    Answer and reasoning

    100 line genotypes and 300 plots. The 600 plants are subsamples or plants within plots, not 600 independent recombinant genotypes.

  20. Two independently derived NILs carry the same donor interval. In replicated control conditions neither differs from the recurrent parent; under drought both show a reproducible advantage. State the most defensible inference and one remaining alternative.

    Answer and reasoning

    Evidence supports a drought-dependent interval effect in that background; linked variants or residual common donor ancestry remain alternatives. Agreement across independent NILs and treatments strengthens the region-level interpretation. It does not establish a particular nucleotide or universal effect.

  21. Two linked markers in an F2 have coupling phase and r = 0.20 in both sexes. Out of 500 progeny, predict the expected number of double heterozygotes and explain why their phase cannot be inferred from their marker scores.

    Answer and reasoning

    170 double heterozygotes. P(AB)=P(ab)=0.4 and P(Ab)=P(aB)=0.1. Two orders of each complementary pair give 2(0.4²)+2(0.1²)=0.34; 500×0.34=170. Both complementary gamete pairs produce AaBb, so the unphased class does not identify the inherited haplotypes.

  22. Two panels contain 240 F1-derived DHs and 160 fully fixed selfed RILs. For r = 0.01, which has more expected recombinant lines under ideal assumptions, and why might the other still be preferred?

    Answer and reasoning

    The RIL panel: about 3.14 versus 2.4. DH expectation is 240×0.01. RIL expectation is 160×(0.02/1.02). Faster DH production or better replicated phenotype data can outweigh the expected recombination advantage of the RILs.

  23. A BC1 plant carrying a desired donor allele A also carries donor B in coupling. Among 250 independent A-bearing offspring with r=0.02, how many are expected to carry b, and why is the count not guaranteed?

    Answer and reasoning

    Five. P(b given A)=r=0.02, giving 250×0.02=5. This is a binomial expectation; sampling variation and viability differences can change observed counts.

  24. A replicated line panel yields genotype-class means P1=22, P2=21 in environment E1 and P1=18, P2=11 in E2. Calculate the difference of contrasts and give an appropriate interpretation.

    Answer and reasoning

    Six trait units. The differences are 1 and 7; the contrast changes by 6. This is compatible with a marker-associated genotype-by-environment interaction, not proof of a causal gene or a statistically significant effect without error estimates.

  25. An experiment recovers no recombinants among 100 independent gametes. A student asserts the interval never recombines. What is an approximate one-sided 95% upper bound, and what does it show?

    Answer and reasoning

    About 0.0295. Solve (1−r)^100=0.05 to get r=1−0.05^(1/100). Observing no recombinant is consistent with some nonzero r values; the claim of absolute linkage is not justified.

SET and mixed practice

  1. Assertion: An F2 is useful for estimating dominance. Reason: It can contain both homozygotes and heterozygotes.

    Answer and reasoning

    Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. The heterozygote deviation from the homozygote midpoint needs all three genotype means.

  2. Assertion: A DH line can be repeatedly grown from seed. Reason: The two chromosome copies originate from one haploid genome.

    Answer and reasoning

    Both true, and the reason explains the assertion under the fixed-line model. The doubled genome is homozygous, so ordinary selfed propagation retains the marker genotype.

  3. Assertion: Every recombinant inbred line carries the same genome. Reason: Inbreeding reduces within-line heterozygosity.

    Answer and reasoning

    Assertion false; reason true. Different lineages fix different founder ancestry mosaics.

  4. A trait is measured on maternal seed coat, but markers are scored in the embryo. Why might genotype–phenotype assignment be misleading?

    Answer and reasoning

    The tissues can belong to different genetic generations. The maternal seed coat derives from maternal sporophytic tissue; embryo genotype is not always the relevant genotype for that trait.

  5. A marker is added between two co-segregating markers, but all three still have identical genotype patterns. Has linkage resolution improved?

    Answer and reasoning

    Not for distinguishing those markers in the sampled population. The added marker provides no observed ancestry separation.

  6. A breeder wants donor A but not linked B; F1 AB/ab has r = 0.04. Among A-bearing gametes, what fraction carry b?

    Answer and reasoning

    0.04. P(Ab)=0.02 and P(A)=0.50, giving conditional probability 0.04.

  7. Choose a population for repeated trials when DH production is impractical but the plant self-fertilizes.

    Answer and reasoning

    A recombinant inbred population. Repeated selfing can create fixed lineages suitable for replicated phenotyping.

  8. What is wrong with assuming every missing marker call is P1 ancestry?

    Answer and reasoning

    It introduces systematic genotype misclassification. Missing data indicate uncertainty, not evidence for one founder allele.

  9. Two candidate loci lie within an introgressed region and a NIL displays the donor phenotype. Which experiment can narrow the responsible region?

    Answer and reasoning

    Compare additional overlapping recombinant introgression lines with matched backgrounds. Their distinct breakpoints may separate the loci, though functional evidence is still required for causal identification.

  10. Assertion: Backcrossing to aa generates no AA offspring. Reason: The recurrent parent contributes a to every offspring.

    Answer and reasoning

    Both true and causally related. Even if the other parent contributes A, the resulting genotype is Aa.

Revision and mastery

Essential relationships

  • F2 genotype proportions: 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa, 1/4 aa.
  • BC to aa: 1/2 Aa, 1/2 aa.
  • Unselected recurrent genome after n backcrosses: 1−2−(n+1).
  • Residual heterozygosity after selfing to Fn: 2−(n−1).
  • Fixed selfed RIL: R=2r/(1+2r); r=R/[2(1−R)].
  • Probability of at least one recombinant from N independent informative gametes: 1−(1−r)N.

Mastery checklist

Distinguish rapid segregating populations from fixed line resources. Reconstruct genotype ratios from pedigree and gamete probabilities; identify whether a design preserves heterozygotes, captures a single or multiple generations of recombination, or isolates a donor region. Check that a claimed mapping resolution is supported by actual independent recombinant observations.

  • Derive F2 and backcross ratios.
  • Calculate residual heterozygosity and recurrent-parent recovery.
  • Convert fixed selfed RIL recombination fraction to meiotic r.
  • Compare fixed versus transient population designs.
  • Separate marker density, independent sample size, phenotype replication and causal validation.
