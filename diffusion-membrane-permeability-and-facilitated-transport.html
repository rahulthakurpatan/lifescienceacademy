1. Why a selective boundary must also exchange matter

A living cell has a chemical composition unlike the fluid that surrounds it. Its plasma membrane preserves that difference, but the same barrier must admit nutrients, release waste and permit the exchange of gases. If all substances passed without restriction, the distinct composition of the cytoplasm would be difficult to maintain. If none passed, metabolism would soon stop. Selective permeability solves this apparent contradiction: the lipid bilayer creates a barrier, while particular membrane proteins provide additional pathways for selected solutes.

Consider a red blood cell. Oxygen can enter or leave its membrane without binding a specialized transporter. Glucose, although also a relatively small molecule, crosses a pure lipid bilayer extremely slowly; a membrane carrier called GLUT1 provides efficient passage. Sodium ions are smaller than glucose but are even less able to cross the bare lipid bilayer. These three observations show why permeability cannot be explained by molecular size alone. Charge, interactions with water, solubility within the membrane and the existence of a protein pathway all matter.

The previous nodes established the amphipathic organization of membrane lipids, the dynamic bilayer and the topology of membrane proteins. This chapter turns those structural ideas into a physical account of exchange. We must separate three questions: what determines the direction of spontaneous movement, what determines the speed of movement, and what evidence identifies the pathway actually used? A concentration difference can provide a strong tendency to move while a membrane prevents movement from occurring at an appreciable rate. Conversely, an excellent transporter cannot generate net passive flux in the absence of a thermodynamic driving force.

We begin with random motion in water, derive the laws describing flux, then introduce the additional energetic barrier of membrane partitioning. After this, the behavior of finite-capacity carriers becomes understandable. Glucose transport provides a biological test of the principles. Osmosis, detailed ion-channel mechanisms and energy-coupled transport are treated in subsequent nodes; they appear here only where they clarify the boundaries of passive solute movement.

2. Brownian movement and the origin of net diffusion

A dissolved molecule is surrounded by other molecules that continually collide with it. Because the impacts are unequal at any particular instant, its direction and speed change irregularly. Brownian movement is the observable random motion of small suspended particles caused by molecular thermal agitation. Robert Brown described this movement in 1827, and Einstein's quantitative treatment in 1905 connected the observed fluctuations to the molecular nature of matter. The essential lesson is that individual diffusing particles need neither ATP hydrolysis nor a biological motor to move.

Imagine placing a drop of dye in still water. Initially the dye molecules are crowded into a small region. Each molecule moves randomly; no molecule detects the surrounding concentration and chooses to travel toward the dilute region. Draw an imaginary plane through the solution. A molecule near either side of the plane has some chance of crossing. Since there are more molecules on the concentrated side, more crossing events occur from concentrated to dilute solution than in the reverse direction. Net diffusion emerges from these unequal gross crossing rates, even when the random movements of individual molecules are unbiased.

If two equally sized compartments eventually contain equal concentrations of a permeable neutral solute, their gross crossing rates become equal. The net flux is zero, but molecules continue moving in both directions. This is dynamic equilibrium. To demonstrate it, label a tiny fraction of molecules on one side with an isotope while leaving the total solute concentrations essentially unchanged. The label spreads to the other compartment. Tracer movement therefore does not necessarily imply a net transfer of the total solute. This distinction is essential in interpreting membrane transport experiments.

The diffusion coefficient D measures the rate of statistical spread. For an unbiased one-dimensional random walk, mean-square displacement is ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt; for unrestricted isotropic diffusion in three dimensions, ⟨r²⟩ = 6Dt. Average signed displacement may be zero while average squared displacement increases. Because distance scales with the square root of time, diffusion is highly effective over short distances but becomes increasingly inefficient over long distances. For example, if D = 5 × 10−10 m²/s, the time corresponding to a one-dimensional root-mean-square distance of 10 μm is (10−5)²/(2D) = 0.1 s. For 100 μm the corresponding time is 10 s. These are statistical characteristic times, not predictions of the exact time an individual molecule first arrives.

For a roughly spherical particle in a dilute continuum liquid, the Stokes–Einstein expression D = kBT/(6πηr) connects D with thermal energy and viscous drag. Here kB is Boltzmann's constant, T is absolute temperature, η is dynamic viscosity and r is hydrodynamic radius. Larger particles or more viscous liquid generally reduce D. Higher absolute temperature tends to increase D if other conditions remain unchanged. The formula is an idealized description of a sphere in a fluid, not an exact microscopic model for diffusion through a lipid bilayer. It nevertheless demonstrates why D is a property of a particular solute in a particular medium, not a universal constant.

3. Concentration gradients and chemical potential

Concentration measures amount per volume, whereas a concentration gradient measures how concentration changes with position. A high concentration does not necessarily imply a gradient: an entire solution may be uniformly concentrated. A large difference over a short distance creates a steep gradient. For a neutral dilute solute in comparable solutions, net movement down this gradient is spontaneous because the chemical potential falls.

Chemical potential μ is the partial molar Gibbs free energy of a component. At fixed temperature and pressure, μ = μ° + RT ln a, where a is dimensionless activity, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature and μ° is the standard chemical potential. For a sufficiently dilute solution, activity is approximately concentration divided by a reference concentration. Transfer of one mole from compartment 1 to compartment 2 gives Δμ = RT ln(a2/a1) when the standard states are comparable. A negative Δμ means transfer is thermodynamically favorable. For example, at 310 K, transfer from 10 mM to 1 mM gives 8.314 × 310 × ln(0.1) = −5.94 kJ/mol. Reversing the concentrations changes the sign to +5.94 kJ/mol. This calculation establishes direction, not the rate of movement: a large negative Δμ cannot make an almost impermeable bilayer rapidly transmit a molecule.

Why is Fick's law expressed in terms of a concentration derivative rather than a logarithm? In an ideal dilute solution, dμ/dx = RT(dC/dx)/C. A local diffusive flux can be written J = −(DC/RT)dμ/dx, where the coefficient contains the mobility and the concentration of the moving species. Substituting the ideal chemical-potential expression cancels C and RT, leaving J = −D dC/dx. Chemical potential supplies the thermodynamic interpretation, while concentration gradients are convenient for calculating neutral dilute-solute diffusion.

For ions, electrical potential must also be considered. Transfer between comparable solutions has electrochemical free-energy change Δμ̃ = RT ln(a2/a1) + zF(ψ2 − ψ1), where z is signed ionic valence, F is the Faraday constant and ψ is electrical potential. A positive ion can move toward higher concentration if the electrical contribution is sufficiently favorable. Such transport can still be passive because the total electrochemical potential decreases. Detailed equilibrium and membrane-potential calculations belong to 2.A.09. The rest of this chapter assumes neutral solutes and comparable aqueous media unless indicated otherwise.

A final thermodynamic distinction prepares us for partitioning. At equilibrium the chemical potential of a permeable solute is equal in two phases, but its concentration need not be equal if its standard chemical potential differs between those phases. A lipid-soluble molecule may accumulate to a higher concentration within lipid than in the adjacent water without violating equilibrium. The ratio of these concentrations is a partition coefficient.

4. Fick's first law: from local gradients to membrane flux

Adolf Fick formulated a quantitative law of diffusion in 1855. Define the coordinate x to increase from a left reservoir to a right reservoir. Positive flux J means transfer in the +x direction. A signed amount dn crossing area A during time dt gives J = (1/A)dn/dt. Flux density is expressed as mol m−2 s−1; the total transfer rate dn/dt is expressed as mol s−1. A large membrane can have a greater total transfer rate than a small membrane even when their flux densities are identical.

Fick's first law in one dimension is J = −D dC/dx. D has dimensions m²/s, and dC/dx has dimensions mol/m⁴; the product is mol/(m² s), as required. The negative sign indicates that flux opposes the concentration gradient. If concentration falls with increasing x, dC/dx is negative, so J is positive. Reversing the concentration difference reverses the signed flux. The minus sign must never be discarded until the direction convention has been stated.

Now consider a uniform flat membrane of thickness L. Let Cm1 and Cm2 denote the concentrations within the membrane immediately at x = 0 and x = L. Assume a constant diffusion coefficient Dm, constant area, no reaction within the slab and a steady state. If the entering flux exceeded the leaving flux, solute would accumulate within the membrane. At steady state this cannot happen, so J is the same at every internal plane.

Rearrange the first law to dCm/dx = −J/Dm. Integrate between the boundaries. The left side gives Cm2 − Cm1, and the right side gives −JL/Dm. Rearrangement yields J = Dm(Cm1 − Cm2)/L. Multiply by A to obtain the total rate dn/dt = ADm(Cm1 − Cm2)/L. The steady concentration profile inside this homogeneous slab is linear. For a nonuniform membrane or an early transient situation, a linear profile cannot simply be assumed.

The model yields separate predictions. Doubling the concentration difference doubles flux provided D and thickness do not change. Doubling thickness halves flux if the membrane material and boundary concentrations remain the same. Doubling D doubles flux. Doubling the exposed area changes total rate, not flux density. These relationships are frequently mixed up in examination problems. They are conditional predictions of a physical model, not universal descriptions of every transport experiment. In particular, the number of carrier proteins may limit a protein-mediated flux and destroy the linear relationship.

Notice that the integrated equation contains concentrations within the diffusing medium, not bulk aqueous concentrations. A polar solute may be abundant in water but scarce in the lipid core. Substituting the water concentration directly into the membrane diffusion equation would ignore this difference. We will introduce the partition coefficient after considering time-dependent diffusion.

5. Time-dependent diffusion and conservation of solute

Fick's first law gives a local flux for a given concentration gradient, but a gradient usually changes as molecules redistribute. Consider a thin slice of a homogeneous medium. Its amount changes by the difference between entering and leaving fluxes. With no reaction and constant cross-sectional area this conservation condition is ∂C/∂t = −∂J/∂x. Substitute J = −D∂C/∂x to obtain Fick's second law, ∂C/∂t = D∂²C/∂x², for constant D. If D varies spatially, the correct form is ∂C/∂t = ∂/∂x(D∂C/∂x). The second law describes how an initially uneven concentration distribution becomes smoother over time.

The second derivative measures curvature. At a local concentration maximum the curvature is negative and concentration tends to fall, while a local minimum tends to rise. A linear profile has zero curvature even when its slope is nonzero, so a constant nonzero flux can coexist with no local accumulation. Such a steady state is sustained if reservoirs maintain different concentrations. A uniform profile also has zero curvature, but its gradient is zero. In that situation a neutral solute has no net diffusive flux. Steady state and equilibrium are therefore not interchangeable terms.

Take two finite well-mixed reservoirs with constant volumes V1 and V2, separated by a membrane with area A and constant permeability P. For a neutral solute with no reactions or other pathways, mass conservation gives V1dC1/dt = −AP(C1−C2) and V2dC2/dt = +AP(C1−C2). The gain on one side equals the loss on the other. Define ΔC = C1−C2. Subtracting the concentration equations gives dΔC/dt = −AP(1/V1 + 1/V2)ΔC.

For constant parameters the solution is ΔC(t) = ΔC(0)e−kt, where k = AP(1/V1 + 1/V2) has units s−1. The half-time for the difference is ln 2/k. This assumes the membrane flux adjusts rapidly compared with reservoir concentrations and the reservoirs remain well mixed. At equilibrium the common concentration is (V1C1,0 + V2C2,0)/(V1 + V2). With equal volumes of 1 mL and starting concentrations 8 and 2 mM, the final concentration is 5 mM. If k = 0.20 min−1, after 3.47 minutes the difference is halved to 3 mM; the individual concentrations are 6.5 and 3.5 mM. Halving both original concentrations would violate mass conservation.

This time-dependent behavior is a major source of experimental misinterpretation. Uptake often becomes slower over time merely because the concentration difference declines, even if the membrane has entirely nonsaturable permeability. A plateau in an amount-versus-time plot is not equivalent to a plateau in initial-rate-versus-substrate-concentration plot. The first can indicate approach to equilibrium; the second, under carefully controlled conditions, may indicate limited transporter capacity.

6. The solubility–diffusion model: partitioning, thickness and permeability

For a molecule to cross a protein-free bilayer it must enter the membrane from water, move across the hydrophobic interior and leave the membrane into water again. The tendency to enter lipid is not the same property as the speed of diffusion within lipid. A solute with very rapid molecular mobility inside a membrane may still cross very slowly if almost no molecules enter the membrane in the first place.

Define the dimensionless partition coefficient K = Cm/Cw at local membrane–water partition equilibrium, with both concentrations in the same units. Let C1 and C2 be aqueous concentrations directly adjacent to the two membrane faces. For a symmetric homogeneous membrane with the same partition behavior at both faces, Cm1 = KC1 and Cm2 = KC2. Substitution into the integrated first law yields J = (Dm/L)(KC1−KC2) = (KDm/L)(C1−C2).

We define membrane permeability P = KDm/L. Thus J = PΔC and the total transfer rate is APΔC. The units of P are m/s, or frequently cm/s. This does not mean that P is the physical speed of a molecule. It is an effective coefficient relating net flux to the aqueous concentration difference. The three parameters have different meanings: K expresses relative solubility, Dm expresses diffusion inside the membrane, and L expresses path length. If the thickness is doubled without changing D or K, P halves.

A numerical comparison illustrates the importance of partitioning. X and Y have the same D in a membrane, but K for X is 2 and K for Y is 0.002. X has a thousandfold higher P. If the smaller Y diffuses ten times faster than X within the membrane, Y still has only one hundredth of X's permeability. Thus a smaller solute need not pass more readily. Conversely, a highly lipophilic but exceptionally large molecule may move slowly within lipid, limiting its permeability despite a favorable K.

The solubility–diffusion model assumes dilute solute behavior, rapid partition equilibration at both surfaces, fixed area and thickness, constant effective D and K, no reaction or bulk solvent flow, and a sufficiently steady flux. A real lipid bilayer has polar headgroups, interfacial regions and a hydrophobic core, so the homogeneous slab is an approximation. Membrane composition and temperature may change both K and D. When different barriers must be crossed successively, effective resistances add: 1/Ptotal = Σ1/Pi, provided permeabilities are defined with compatible aqueous concentration references, common area and steady conditions. In parallel pathways, by contrast, fluxes add under the same endpoint driving difference.

The aqueous regions adjacent to a membrane can themselves create appreciable resistance. In an unstirred water layer, molecular diffusion rather than mixing must deliver solute to the membrane surface. If this layer is highly resistive, bulk concentrations differ from concentrations immediately at the membrane. A very permeable membrane can therefore exhibit a slow measured overall rate. Stirring or reducing the water-layer thickness can increase apparent permeability without altering the lipid itself. Always establish whether a given P is a property of the membrane alone or an effective property of a complete experimental system.

7. Worked calculations: permeability, rate and surface-to-volume ratio

Example 1. A neutral solute has K = 0.05 and Dm = 2 × 10−12 m²/s in a membrane 5 nm thick. Convert 5 nm to 5 × 10−9 m. P = (0.05)(2 × 10−12)/(5 × 10−9) = 2 × 10−5 m/s. The aqueous boundary concentrations are 4 and 1 mM; because 1 mM = 1 mol/m³, ΔC = 3 mol/m³. Flux is 6 × 10−5 mol/(m² s). For exposed area 200 μm², convert A = 200 × 10−12 = 2 × 10−10 m². The rate becomes 1.2 × 10−14 mol/s, or approximately 7.23 × 109 molecules/s using Avogadro's constant. The answer is an illustrative initial or fixed-boundary rate, not a prediction of indefinite uptake into a finite cell.

Example 2. A membrane with area 0.5 cm² transfers a neutral solute initially at 0.24 nmol/s under a concentration difference of 2 mM. Convert area to 5 × 10−5 m² and rate to 2.4 × 10−10 mol/s. Dividing rate by area gives flux 4.8 × 10−6 mol/(m² s). Then P = J/ΔC = 2.4 × 10−6 m/s, equivalent to 2.4 × 10−4 cm/s. This is an effective permeability of whatever pathways were operating under the assay conditions. It cannot automatically be assigned to the lipid phase unless the experiment isolates that pathway.

Example 3. Compare two membranes: B has half the K, twice the D and twice the thickness of A. KD is unchanged, but L doubles, so P is halved. If B also has twice the area, AP remains unchanged. Under equal concentration differences, the total transfer rates are the same although B has only half the flux density. This is why an experimental rate should be normalized to the correct exposed membrane area before comparing material permeability.

Cell geometry imposes further constraints. A spherical cell of radius r has area 4πr² and volume 4πr³/3; therefore A/V = 3/r. Under constant external concentration, constant volume and negligible intracellular reaction or mixing resistance, VdCin/dt = AP(Cout−Cin). The difference decays exponentially with characteristic time τ = V/(AP) = r/(3P). For radius 5 μm and P = 10−6 m/s, τ = 1.67 s and the half-time is 1.16 s. Doubling the radius doubles the membrane-limited characteristic time. That linear radius dependence must not be confused with the distance-squared scaling of unrestricted diffusion in the cell interior.

Real cells are not necessarily spheres and may have complex internal structures. Increasing membrane area through folds or microvilli can enhance exchange without proportionally increasing cellular volume. If a solute moves slowly within the cytoplasm, internal diffusion adds another limitation, and the well-mixed-cell formula fails. A complete biological model identifies the relevant resistance rather than assigning every delay to membrane permeability.

8. Molecular properties that govern simple diffusion

The interior of a phospholipid bilayer resembles a nonpolar solvent more than water. A neutral nonpolar molecule can often enter that region with relatively little energetic penalty. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are familiar examples. Many hydrophobic steroid molecules can also cross lipid membranes; their precise rates depend on molecular size, interactions, membrane composition and additional biological pathways. Simple diffusion does not mean that all solutes pass at equal rates: it means that crossing occurs through the lipid without obligatory binding to a specific transport protein.

Small polar solutes behave differently. Their favorable hydrogen bonds and other interactions with water must be disrupted when they enter the hydrocarbon region. Water, ethanol and urea have finite but variable bare-bilayer permeabilities. Water can cross lipid directly, but aquaporins increase water permeability in many membranes. Glucose, with several hydroxyl groups, is strongly hydrated and has a large energetic penalty for entering the lipid core. Efficient glucose passage consequently requires suitable carriers in many biological contexts.

Ions are particularly restricted. Sodium and potassium ions are surrounded by hydration shells in water, and transferring a charge into a low-dielectric hydrocarbon environment is highly unfavorable. A sodium ion being smaller than a glucose molecule does not make it more permeable to the bare bilayer. Membranes are excellent electrical insulators in large part because ionic penetration into their lipid interior is strongly disfavored. Suitable membrane proteins overcome part of this barrier by providing environments where polar or charged substrates can interact favorably with the pathway.

Lipid composition modifies permeability. More unsaturated chains can disrupt packing and increase fluidity, often facilitating passage of some neutral solutes. Longer or more ordered chains may reduce free volume and increase path length. Cholesterol modulates packing and often decreases passive leakage of small polar compounds in fluid phospholipid membranes. Its effects depend on lipid composition, temperature and phase. Lateral mobility of a phospholipid and transmembrane permeability of a dissolved solute are not identical physical quantities. A fluid membrane may still strongly exclude ions.

Weak acids and bases provide a powerful example of pH-dependent permeability. A weak acid HA ⇌ H+ + A− has neutral fraction 1/(1+10pH−pKa) from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. At pH = pKa half the acid is neutral; one pH unit above pKa only about 9.1% is neutral. If HA crosses lipid much more readily than A−, flux is governed mainly by the gradient in HA. Two buffered compartments with the same total acid concentration but different pH can therefore show net movement of HA. At equilibrium of the neutral species, the total acid concentration may be greater on the more alkaline side because ionization retains solute there. A weak base behaves oppositely: its neutral fraction increases as pH increases. These conclusions assume rapid acid–base equilibration, fixed pH and negligible charged-form transport. The phenomenon is often called pH partitioning or ion trapping.

A good examination strategy is to examine charge first, then polarity and hydration, then partitioning and diffusivity, and finally available protein pathways. Molecular mass by itself cannot establish a universal permeability ranking. Membrane permeability must be described for a named solute and under stated conditions.

9. Facilitated diffusion and the alternating-access mechanism

A cell needing glucose could increase the permeability of its entire lipid membrane, but that would compromise selectivity. Instead it can insert a protein that specifically recognizes glucose. Such a protein provides a lower free-energy barrier for passage while leaving the lipid barrier intact for many other substances. A passive carrier accelerates approach to equilibrium; it does not supply free energy to maintain net movement against the substrate's chemical or electrochemical potential.

The alternating-access model explains how the carrier provides this alternative route. Imagine a membrane-spanning protein with a substrate-binding pocket facing the external solution. A glucose molecule binds through interactions with complementary chemical groups. The carrier undergoes a conformational transition that closes access from outside, often forming an occluded state in which substrate is inaccessible from either compartment. A further transition opens the pocket toward the cytoplasm and substrate can dissociate. The empty carrier can return to the outward-facing state. Each step is reversible; the schematic cycle is a way to organize the molecular events, not a claim that every carrier has identical intermediate states or dwell times.

The binding pocket provides specificity. Hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors and steric constraints are arranged to accommodate a particular substrate geometry. An alternative stereoisomer may have similar molecular mass and lipid solubility but interact less favorably with the pocket. Some related molecules bind the same carrier and may be transported; others bind without completing the cycle. These differences explain selective transport and competitive inhibition. Specificity is not absolute: individual transporters may accept multiple related solutes, and different isoforms have different substrate ranges.

Alternating access differs from an open aqueous pore because the binding site is generally not freely accessible from both sides simultaneously. The carrier must perform a conformational cycle for transport. This imposes a finite turnover rate, so increasing substrate concentration cannot increase the rate without limit. A channel, when open, can permit many particles to traverse its pore without completing a whole alternating-access cycle for each one. The carrier's slower conformational transitions explain typical differences in per-protein throughput, not an absolute distinction in every possible rate measurement.

The conformational cycle is driven by thermal motion and substrate binding and release. If the chemical potential is greater outside than inside, there is a net bias toward inward transfer. If intracellular free substrate activity becomes greater, the reverse sequence produces net export. At equal chemical potentials the cycles balance. Even if the carrier has different apparent affinities on its two sides, the complete reversible cycle must obey thermodynamic constraints. Asymmetric binding alone cannot create a perpetual uphill flux. This is why describing GLUT as an importer is useful only in a particular physiological setting.

Metabolism often maintains the gradient driving a passive carrier. After glucose enters, hexokinase can phosphorylate it to glucose-6-phosphate, removing free glucose from the intracellular pool. Continued inward transport may therefore depend indirectly on cellular ATP even though GLUT does not directly hydrolyze ATP or couple glucose transport to another solute. The complete cellular network consumes energy, whereas the membrane-crossing event itself is passive. Distinguishing these two levels of explanation is essential.

10. Saturable carrier kinetics: deriving apparent Km and Vmax

At low substrate concentration many carriers have empty binding sites. Increasing substrate raises the frequency of productive binding and therefore raises the rate of movement. At high concentration the binding sites spend much of their time occupied or undergoing conformational transitions. Further increasing concentration has little effect because the transport cycle itself limits throughput. Finite transporter number and finite turnover produce a maximum initial transport rate.

A simple initial-uptake model captures this behavior. Write E + S ⇌ ES with association rate constant kon and dissociation rate constant koff. Approximate productive translocation and return by an effective step of rate kt. Assume external substrate is effectively constant during the measurement, the internal substrate concentration is negligible, the carrier-bound population is at quasi-steady state, and there is one effective binding site per independent carrier. The rate of change of bound carrier is d[ES]/dt ≈ kon[E][S] − (koff+kt)[ES] = 0. Conservation of carrier gives [E] = [E]T−[ES].

Substitution and rearrangement give kon[E]T[S] = [ES](kon[S]+koff+kt). Divide by kon and define Km = (koff+kt)/kon. The bound concentration becomes [ES] = [E]T[S]/(Km+[S]). The initial transport rate is v = kt[ES], or v = Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]) with Vmax = kt[E]T. This is a Michaelis–Menten-like expression, not a claim that a reversible carrier is a chemical enzyme or that its complete kinetic cycle is irreversible. The approximation is justified only for the stated initial uptake conditions.

Km has concentration units. Vmax has whatever units the transport rate is expressed in, such as nmol/min or mol/(m² s) for an area-normalized flux. At [S] = Km, v = Vmax/2. At [S] much smaller than Km, v ≈ (Vmax/Km)[S]; at [S] much larger than Km, v approaches Vmax. This explains why a carrier can appear linear over a narrow low-concentration range. A finite maximum is approached gradually rather than reached abruptly at Km.

A smaller apparent Km indicates half-maximal uptake at lower substrate concentration under the particular assay conditions. It is often described as greater apparent affinity. However, the equilibrium binding dissociation constant Kd = koff/kon differs from the kinetic Km in this model. Km approximates Kd when productive translocation is slow compared with dissociation. More realistic carrier cycles also produce different apparent parameters for zero-trans uptake, exit and equilibrium exchange. Do not assume a reported Km is an unchanging intrinsic binding constant.

Worked calculation: suppose Vmax = 120 nmol/min and Km = 3 mM. At 1 mM, v = 120 × 1/(3+1) = 30 nmol/min. At 3 mM it is 60, at 9 mM it is 90, and at 27 mM it is 108. Tripling concentration from 3 to 9 mM raises rate only 1.5-fold. Solving v/Vmax = 0.9 = S/(Km+S) gives S = 9Km. To approach a maximum requires progressively greater substrate concentration.

If the number of identical surface carriers doubles, Vmax doubles while Km remains unchanged in the simplified model. Increasing effective turnover can also increase Vmax, so an observed capacity change does not alone establish whether abundance or turnover changed. An independent measurement of surface transporter number is needed to distinguish them.

11. Substrate specificity, competition and inhibition

Consider an experiment in which labeled D-glucose enters cells through a carrier. Adding a structurally related unlabeled molecule may reduce the rate of labeled glucose entry if that molecule competes for binding. A reversible competitive inhibitor occupies sites that could otherwise bind glucose. Raising glucose concentration makes productive glucose binding more probable and can overcome ideal competition. Thus the apparent half-saturation concentration increases without a change in the limiting maximum rate.

For a simple reversible competitive model, α = 1 + [I]/Ki and v = Vmax[S]/(αKm+[S]). Here [I] is inhibitor concentration and Ki is the inhibition constant. The expression follows from partitioning free carrier between unoccupied and inhibitor-bound states in the ratio 1:[I]/Ki, assuming the inhibitor binds competitively and the simple kinetic approximation holds. The resulting apparent Km is αKm. If the competing compound is itself transported, more complex exchange kinetics can appear; the equation is an ideal initial-rate description, not a universal rule for every inhibitor.

For the preceding carrier with Vmax = 120 and Km = 3 mM, an inhibitor concentration of 4 mM with Ki = 2 mM gives α = 3 and apparent Km = 9 mM. At 3 mM glucose, uptake becomes 120 × 3/(9+3) = 30 nmol/min instead of 60. At 9 mM glucose it is 60 instead of 90. The same limiting rate of 120 is approached at sufficiently high substrate. In contrast, removal of half the identical functioning surface carriers would halve Vmax without changing Km.

Transport inhibition alone does not demonstrate the molecular mode of inhibition. A treatment could alter the bilayer, change the cellular gradient, damage the membrane or reduce metabolism rather than bind the transporter directly. ATP depletion can reduce sustained glucose accumulation by preventing metabolic trapping even when the GLUT protein itself is passive. Short initial-rate measurements, gradient measurements, membrane integrity controls and appropriate comparison preparations help separate direct from indirect effects.

Stereoselectivity is another clue. If D-glucose enters much faster than L-glucose despite comparable basic bulk physical properties, a stereoselective interaction is implicated. Because membrane lipids and proteins are themselves chiral, stereoselectivity alone is not a formal proof of a specific carrier. Combined evidence from competition, saturability, specific loss of function and restoration in reconstituted membranes gives a stronger inference. Scientific identification of the mechanism comes from converging observations, not a single characteristic graph.

12. Bidirectionality, exchange and simultaneous transport pathways

The initial-rate equation cannot be used indiscriminately for net flux across a membrane. Imagine free glucose at 5 mM on both sides. Substituting the external concentration alone into the one-way equation predicts positive inward uptake, yet passive net transfer must be zero at equilibrium. The missing term is reverse transport. Gross inward and outward rates can be nonzero and equal.

An illustrative symmetric reversible model is Jnet = Jmax(Cout−Cin)/(K+Cout+Cin). This is a phenomenological symmetric occupancy model, not a universal GLUT rate equation. It reduces to the initial-uptake hyperbola when Cin = 0, gives zero net movement at equal concentrations, and reverses sign when the concentrations are exchanged. If Jmax = 100 and K = 5 mM, 10 mM outside and zero inside gives 66.7 flux units inward. With 5 mM inside it gives 25, while 5 mM outside and 10 mM inside gives −25. With equal 10 mM concentrations, net flux is zero. Real GLUT kinetics may be asymmetric and can show trans-acceleration, in which substrate on the opposite side increases a measured unidirectional exchange rate. These observations are compatible with zero net flux at equilibrium.

The lipid route and a carrier route may coexist for the same solute. At negligible internal concentration, write Jtotal = PC + JmaxC/(Km+C), with compatible units. The lipid component increases linearly, while the carrier term approaches a maximum. At high external concentration, the total curve need not plateau because the lipid contribution continues rising. Conversely, a linear graph over a limited low-concentration range does not exclude a carrier, because the first part of a saturation curve is approximately linear.

The best analysis separates pathways with independent measurements. Compare lipid-only membranes with otherwise matched protein-containing membranes, or isolate a validated inhibitor-sensitive component. Subtract the linear contribution before fitting the carrier term. A remaining high-concentration slope can reflect a parallel nonsaturable route. It could also reflect experimental artifacts, such as inconsistent mixing or changing membrane properties, so the two-component model must be tested against controls.

Parallel routes share the same endpoints and contribute additive rates. Serial barriers instead constrain a single steady flux and their resistances add. An epithelial cell may combine an apical transport route with a basolateral route; solute must traverse both to move across the tissue. One step may be passive and the other actively coupled. Identifying the energetics of an individual step is different from classifying the whole epithelial process.

13. GLUT transporters: passive movement adapted to different tissues

Glucose is essential for cellular metabolism, yet it crosses lipid inefficiently because of its polar hydroxyl groups. Mammalian members of the GLUT family (SLC2) provide facilitated transport of glucose and related sugars. Typical glucose-transporting GLUT proteins have twelve transmembrane helices and use an alternating-access mechanism. They do not directly hydrolyze ATP or obligatorily cotransport sodium for glucose translocation. Differences in expression, kinetics, localization and substrate recognition allow the same broad mechanism to serve different tissues.

GLUT1 is widely expressed and particularly abundant in erythrocytes and blood–brain barrier endothelium. Erythrocytes lack mitochondria and rely on glycolysis to generate ATP, making continuous access to glucose especially important. GLUT1 also helps deliver glucose across the endothelial barrier supplying the brain. Correct transporter localization on membrane surfaces matters as much as transporter synthesis: a protein retained inside a cell cannot directly provide passage across its plasma membrane.

GLUT3 is prominent in neurons and generally has high apparent glucose affinity. A transporter that reaches substantial flux at relatively low glucose concentration supports the sustained fuel needs of neurons. However, high affinity does not make glucose move uphill, nor does it guarantee the greatest possible flux at every concentration. A low-affinity carrier with very high capacity can exceed the rate of a high-affinity low-capacity carrier when substrate is abundant. Both Vmax and Km are required to compare rates.

GLUT2 is conventionally described as a relatively low-affinity, high-capacity glucose transporter, expressed in liver and basolateral intestinal and renal epithelia. After a meal, free glucose entering hepatocytes can be phosphorylated and metabolized or stored. During fasting, hepatic glucose production and glucose-6-phosphatase activity can increase free intracellular glucose so net export through GLUT2 becomes favorable. The same passive transporter can support opposite net directions depending on the gradient. Rodent pancreatic beta cells prominently express GLUT2, while human beta cells rely substantially on GLUT1 and GLUT3. The familiar rodent example must not be generalized indiscriminately across species.

GLUT4 is particularly important in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. In the basal state much of it is present in intracellular membrane compartments. Insulin stimulates recruitment of GLUT4 to the plasma membrane, increasing the number of available pathways. Muscle contraction can also increase surface GLUT4 through partly distinct regulatory mechanisms. The simple kinetic prediction is a rise in Vmax if more identical carriers are available without changing their intrinsic properties. Insulin does not supply a motor that drives glucose uphill. Transport remains passive; glucose metabolism helps preserve the favorable chemical-potential difference. Detailed signaling and vesicular trafficking belong to later chapters.

GLUT5 primarily transports fructose and is important for intestinal fructose absorption. It is a useful reminder that not every GLUT member is a major glucose transporter. In intestinal epithelial cells, apical SGLT1 uses the sodium electrochemical gradient for glucose uptake, whereas basolateral GLUT2 allows downhill glucose release when intracellular free glucose activity exceeds that outside. This sequence connects secondary active transport with facilitated diffusion, but the two individual mechanisms must not be confused.

A final distinction concerns free glucose versus intracellular glucose-derived material. Hexokinase converts glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, which is not a normal substrate of the plasma membrane glucose GLUTs. This removes free glucose from the cytoplasmic pool and favors continuing entry. A radiolabel accumulation assay may count glucose and its phosphorylated products together. A suitable nonmetabolizable transported analog, such as 3-O-methylglucose, allows membrane exchange to be examined more independently of metabolism. By contrast, 2-deoxyglucose can be phosphorylated and retained, so its accumulation reflects both entry and trapping. This is an important experimental reason why measuring total internal label does not necessarily measure the thermodynamic driving force for GLUT.

14. Distinguishing passive carriers, channels and active transport

In standard cell biology, facilitated diffusion includes passive carriers and channels, although some teaching texts use the term more narrowly for carriers. Channels create selective hydrophilic pores across the membrane when open. Substrates traverse the pore down their electrochemical potential. Carriers instead bind substrate and change access to the binding site through a conformational cycle. This difference usually makes an open channel capable of much greater particle throughput per protein, but actual rates depend on the channel, substrate and conditions.

Channels can be selective and gated, so they should not be described as permanent nonselective holes. Their selectivity can depend on geometry, interactions and charge distribution within the pore. They may also display concentration-dependent nonlinear behavior or saturation due to ion occupancy or conductance limits. It is too simplistic to state that channels can never saturate. Carrier-mediated saturation commonly reflects finite substrate-binding and conformational turnover; channel behavior is characterized by pore conductance, selectivity and gating. Detailed ion-channel mechanisms are reserved for 2.A.07.

Both passive and active carriers can bind substrates specifically, undergo conformational changes, show saturation and exhibit competitive inhibition. Those properties reveal protein-mediated transport, not its energetic category. The decisive difference is whether net transport of a solute can occur against its own electrochemical potential through coupling to a favorable process. Primary active transport may use ATP hydrolysis directly; secondary active transport can use the gradient of a second solute. GLUT glucose uniport uses neither such coupling mechanism.

Influx and efflux describe directions relative to a cell; passive and active describe thermodynamic relationships. A passive transporter can support net uptake or release. A steady intracellular concentration also does not establish equilibrium. If glucose entry equals metabolic consumption, intracellular free glucose remains constant while a net flux persists. The cell maintains an energy-dissipating steady state. The transport step is nonetheless passive when glucose moves downhill through GLUT.

15. Experimental evidence and analysis of transport-rate data

A membrane transport experiment should separate crossing the membrane from subsequent cellular events. In a protein-free liposome, solute passage can be measured through radiolabel accumulation or another validated concentration readout. A proteoliposome prepared with the same lipid composition and a chosen carrier can then be tested under matching conditions. If the protein-containing vesicle shows a large additional uptake of a recognized substrate, this supports a protein-mediated pathway. The comparison must control vesicle size, exposed area, integrity, protein orientation and substrate gradients. A damaged membrane may allow many unrelated molecules to leak in and out, which is not evidence for specific transport.

Initial rates are especially useful. The assay begins with a known external concentration and negligible internal concentration. An early time interval is selected during which substrate concentrations have changed little. The slope of the amount-versus-time curve in that interval estimates the initial rate. Measuring only one late time point mixes entry, back transport, depletion of the gradient and metabolism. A fixed interval may be sufficiently early for a slow preparation but not for a fast one; compare equivalent initial conditions rather than assuming every sample has reached the same kinetic regime.

Consider synthetic data from a neutral sugar assay. At external concentrations 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 mM, a protein-free membrane gives fluxes 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 in common units. A membrane containing the transporter gives 22, 37.3, 58, 82.7 and 112. Subtracting the lipid background yields carrier rates of 20, 33.3, 50, 66.7 and 80. The differences correspond to Jcarrier = 100C/(4+C), apart from rounding. The carrier's maximum is 100 units and its half-saturation concentration is 4 mM. The overall curve has no clear horizontal plateau because the linear pathway is still increasing. Identifying and subtracting the background before parameter estimation is more informative than blindly fitting total flux to a single hyperbola.

A second experimental pattern illustrates the distinction between transport and retention. Suppose cells continue accumulating labeled 2-deoxyglucose, whereas a suitable nonmetabolizable analog approaches an equilibrium distribution. The first compound can be phosphorylated and retained, while the second can leave through a reversible carrier. The measurements need not imply different energetics at the membrane. Measuring free solute separately, testing an appropriate nonmetabolizable tracer and comparing isolated membranes help resolve the ambiguity.

Historically, labeled-sugar exchange studies in erythrocytes provided evidence for reversible carrier transport and substrate-dependent kinetics before atomic-scale structures of glucose transporters were available. Functional experiments established properties such as exchange and apparent affinity; later identification and expression of GLUT proteins and structural evidence connected those properties to specific membrane proteins. The evidence is strongest when kinetic, biochemical and structural observations support the same explanation. We do not need specialist laboratory protocols to understand the logic of those experiments.

For examination reasoning, identify the experimental variables before interpreting a graph. Is the horizontal axis time or substrate concentration? Are the measurements net flux, gross tracer entry or total retained label? Are the gradients held fixed, or do they decrease during the assay? Is the protein specifically manipulated, or does the treatment also change cell integrity or metabolism? A plateau, inhibitor response or linear slope is meaningful only in relation to these conditions.

16. Biological integration and the next transport question

Gas exchange gives a familiar example of simple diffusion. Oxygen consumption during aerobic metabolism can maintain low free oxygen concentration at a consuming site, while carbon dioxide production can favor movement away from that site. Both gases can traverse lipid membranes passively. Whole-tissue exchange also involves blood delivery, diffusion through extracellular regions and cell layers, and chemical or protein binding processes. The permeability of a single lipid membrane is not the same as the effective conductance of an entire respiratory surface.

Selective nutrient uptake reveals why transport proteins are essential. Cells can maintain a tight lipid barrier against many hydrophilic compounds while expressing carriers that permit essential nutrients to cross. Regulating the number or activity of these carriers changes nutrient exchange without destroying the membrane's barrier function. Maintaining concentration gradients by metabolism or by other energy-coupled transport systems allows passive routes to support continuing physiological flux.

Diffusion also constrains cellular organization. Small cells have high surface-to-volume ratios and short internal diffusion distances. Larger cells may need expanded exchange surfaces or mechanisms that reduce effective transport distances. These adaptations modify area, path length or the relevant boundary conditions in the diffusion equations. They do not repeal the physical laws.

The unifying approach is to determine first the thermodynamic direction, next the physical pathway, and finally a rate model suited to the experiment. In the next node the moving molecule is water. Its chemical potential is influenced by solute activity and pressure, which means that a simple comparison of total solute concentrations is not enough to predict cell-volume changes. That motivates the separate treatment of osmosis, tonicity and water potential.

17. Original CSIR-NET Part B and assertion–reason practice

1. A neutral permeant solute is at equal chemical potential on both sides. Which is true? A. All crossing stops. B. Gross crossing persists with zero net flux. C. Only influx persists. D. ATP is needed to maintain equilibrium.

B. Thermal motion persists; opposing crossing rates balance. A confuses equilibrium with immobility; C violates balance; D is unnecessary for passive equilibrium.

2. Which quantity has SI units m/s? A. D. B. J. C. P. D. K.

C. P = KD/L has m/s. D has m²/s, J mol/(m² s), K is dimensionless.

3. Which change doubles total simple diffusion rate but leaves flux density unchanged? A. Double area. B. Double ΔC. C. Double D. D. Halve L.

A. Total rate = AJ. B, C and D each alter J itself under the simple model.

4. Why does a small ion cross bare lipid more slowly than a larger neutral nonpolar molecule? A. No thermal motion. B. Charge and hydration create an energetic barrier. C. Diffusion requires ATP. D. D is equal for all solutes.

B. The low-dielectric lipid core poorly accommodates charge. A and C are false; D ignores molecule and medium dependence.

5. Passive GLUT can export glucose when: A. GLUT hydrolyzes ATP. B. Free glucose activity is higher inside. C. External glucose is phosphorylated. D. Km is zero.

B. Free glucose chemical potential determines direction. Neither direct ATP hydrolysis, external phosphorylation nor zero Km is necessary.

6. Doubling the number of identical surface carriers ideally changes: A. Km only. B. Vmax doubles. C. Vmax halves. D. Equilibrium ratio doubles.

B. Capacity increases while intrinsic kinetic parameters of unchanged carriers remain the same. Equilibrium is not changed.

7. Ideal reversible competition changes: A. Vmax only. B. Apparent Km increases. C. Both become zero. D. Gradient reverses.

B. More substrate is needed for half-maximal uptake. Maximum rate is unchanged in the ideal model; inhibitor binding does not necessarily change the gradient.

8. Which is the strongest controlled test of a specific protein's contribution? A. Compare unrelated tissues. B. Matched liposomes and proteoliposomes. C. A single late uptake value. D. Count total lipids.

B. The membrane background is matched while the chosen protein is introduced. A confounds composition, C mixes transport and retention, D does not measure exchange.

9. Which GLUT is associated especially with insulin-responsive uptake? A. GLUT1. B. GLUT2. C. GLUT4. D. GLUT5.

C. GLUT4 surface recruitment increases transport capacity in muscle and adipose tissue. GLUT1 provides basal transport, GLUT2 supports hepatic and epithelial exchange, GLUT5 mainly moves fructose.

10. Assertion: Passive carriers can be stereoselective. Reason: Substrate-binding pockets recognize three-dimensional chemical geometry.

Both are true; the reason explains the assertion. Molecular recognition changes kinetics without requiring energy coupling.

11. Assertion: Steady nonzero flux requires accumulation within a uniform slab. Reason: Steady-state flux is equal at every plane.

Assertion false, reason true. Equal entering and leaving fluxes prevent accumulation. Reservoirs can maintain a nonzero gradient.

12. Assertion: Any saturable membrane transport must be active. Reason: Uphill active transport needs a favorable coupled process.

Assertion false, reason true. Passive carriers also saturate, and saturation alone cannot classify transport energetics.

18. Original CSIR-NET Part C: numerical and experimental practice

1. K = 0.1, D = 3 × 10⁻¹² m²/s, L = 6 nm, ΔC = 2 mM and A = 100 μm². Find P, J and total rate.

L = 6 × 10⁻⁹ m; P = 5 × 10⁻⁵ m/s. ΔC = 2 mol/m³ gives J = 10⁻⁴ mol/(m² s). A = 10⁻¹⁰ m², so rate = 10⁻¹⁴ mol/s. The area conversion must be squared.

2. At 310 K, compare ideal neutral-solute transfer from 2 to 8 mM and from 8 to 2 mM.

RT ln 4 = +3.57 kJ/mol in the first direction and −3.57 kJ/mol in the reverse direction. Passive net movement favors the latter.

3. B has twice K, three times thickness and twice area of A; D and ΔC are equal. Compare flux and rate.

P_B/P_A = 2/3. Flux ratio is 2/3; total rate ratio is 4/3. B transfers more per second despite its lower permeability.

4. Vmax = 80 nmol/min and Km = 2 mM. Find substrate concentration for v = 60.

0.75 = S/(2+S). Rearrange to 1.5 = 0.25S, so S = 6 mM or three Km.

5. Vmax = 90, Km = 3 mM. A competitive inhibitor has concentration 6 mM and Ki = 3 mM. Calculate rate at 3 mM substrate.

α = 1+6/3 = 3, apparent Km = 9 mM. Rate = 90 × 3/12 = 22.5, half the uninhibited rate of 45; maximum remains 90.

6. Initial rates 20, 40 and 60 occur at 1, 3 and 9 mM. Estimate Vmax and Km assuming one hyperbolic carrier.

From 40=3V/(K+3) and 60=9V/(K+9), equate V: 40(K+3)/3=60(K+9)/9. K=3 mM, V=80; at 1 mM, 80/4=20, validating the third point.

7. Equal closed reservoirs start at 12 and 4 mM with k = 0.10 min⁻¹. Predict concentrations at 6.93 min.

This is one half-time, ln 2/k. Difference falls from 8 to 4 mM, mean remains 8 mM, so concentrations are 10 and 6 mM. Mass is conserved.

8. Spherical cells have radii 4 and 12 μm, same P and ΔC, negligible internal diffusion resistance. Compare initial rate per volume and equilibration time.

A/V=3/r. The smaller cell has three times the initial rate per volume. τ=r/(3P), so the larger has three times the characteristic time.

9. A weak acid with pKa 5 has 10 mM total concentration on both sides. Side A pH 5, side B pH 6. Only HA permeates appreciably. Predict net movement.

Neutral fractions are 1/2 and 1/11, giving 5 and 0.909 mM HA. Initial HA movement is A to B despite equal total acid. Assumes fixed pH and negligible A⁻ transport.

10. An intervention reduces Vmax from 100 to 50 but leaves Km = 4 mM. Does it prove that the number of surface carriers halved?

No. Halving identical carrier abundance could explain it, but reducing turnover might also do so. An independent surface abundance measurement is required.

11. Equal unlabeled sugar concentrations exist on both sides. A negligible tracer amount is introduced outside and enters the cell. Does this prove net uphill sugar transport?

No. Labeled molecules redistribute through exchange while gross inward and outward total sugar fluxes balance. The tracer does not establish net transfer.

12. Two barriers in series have P = 2 × 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁶ m/s, same area and compatible reference concentrations. Find overall P.

1/P = 5 × 10⁴ + 10⁶ = 1.05 × 10⁶ s/m; P ≈ 9.52 × 10⁻⁷ m/s. The higher resistance dominates. Adding P directly would model parallel paths.

13. At 4 mM, total flux 58 and lipid flux 8; at 16 mM, total 112 and lipid 32. Estimate Km and Jmax.

Carrier fluxes are 50 and 80. 50=4Jmax/(Km+4), 80=16Jmax/(Km+16). Equating Jmax: 12.5(Km+4)=5(Km+16), so Km=4 mM, Jmax=100.

14. Total intracellular glucose-derived label exceeds external free glucose concentration while uptake continues. Does this prove active glucose transport?

No. Phosphorylated glucose and other products can retain label. Free glucose activity determines GLUT driving force. Measure free glucose or use a suitable nonmetabolizable analog.

15. Uptake amount plateaus with time at each external concentration. No initial rates were measured. Does the result establish carrier saturation?

No. A closed system can approach equilibrium with entirely linear permeability. Compare initial rates over a range of substrate concentrations under equivalent conditions.

16. In the illustrative symmetric reversible model, Jmax=60, K=2 mM, outside=2 mM, inside=8 mM. Calculate signed net flux.

60(2−8)/(2+2+8)=−30 flux units. Negative indicates net outward transfer with inward defined positive. The one-way uptake equation is inapplicable.

19. Consolidation and common conceptual traps

The first question in a membrane transport problem is thermodynamic: can the solute move spontaneously in the specified direction? For a dilute neutral solute in comparable aqueous compartments, concentration differences provide the answer. For charged species, the electrical term must also be considered. The next question is kinetic: what is the resistance or capacity of the route? A homogeneous lipid slab has P = KD/L and J = PΔC. Area converts flux into total rate. A changing concentration difference requires conservation and time-dependent reasoning rather than indefinite extrapolation of the initial gradient.

For a carrier, distinguish gross initial uptake from net reversible flux. The simple hyperbolic equation is valid under defined initial conditions; it cannot predict net movement at equilibrium without a reverse contribution. Apparent Km is a kinetic half-saturation parameter, not always a binding constant. Competition can raise apparent Km, while changing the number of otherwise identical carriers changes Vmax. Neither high affinity nor a large number of passive carriers can drive sustained uphill transport.

Finally, relate the model to living cells. A glucose carrier can export or import depending on the free glucose gradient. Metabolism can preserve a downhill gradient without making the membrane carrier active. A steady concentration need not mean equilibrium. A tracer can cross without any net transfer of the total solute. A time plateau need not indicate carrier saturation, and a concentration-dependent total flux need not be purely carrier-mediated. These distinctions prepare the reader for the more complex interplay of solute and water transport in the next node.
