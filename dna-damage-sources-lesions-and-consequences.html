1. The difference between a stable genome and an indestructible molecule

Prerequisites

Review nucleotide chemistry and DNA conformation, DNA topology and stability, cell-cycle checkpoints and oxidative stress.

A cell inherits an arrangement of nucleotides, not an abstract string of symbols. The distinction matters because every nucleotide is a material object with bonds that can be broken or modified. A chromosome can preserve a sequence across many generations even though individual molecules within that chromosome undergo chemical reactions. Such persistence is a property of the biological system, rather than proof that DNA itself is chemically inert. Accurate copying, chemical protection, removal of damaged components and controlled cellular responses contribute different parts of that persistence. To understand why these activities are necessary, first imagine DNA in the absence of any copying error. Even a polymerase that always selected the correct incoming nucleotide would be insufficient to maintain the genome if the parental molecule were allowed to decay between successive divisions.

DNA normally consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. Each nucleotide contributes a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and a nitrogen-containing base. Adjacent sugars are connected by phosphodiester bonds, whereas each base attaches to its own sugar through an N-glycosidic bond. Bases face toward the interior of the double helix, where stacking and hydrogen bonding stabilize the association of the strands. Complementary pairing means that the two strands encode overlapping information. In a normal G:C base pair, the presence of guanine specifies cytosine on the opposing strand. If a chemical reaction removes one guanine without altering the other strand, the original information is not necessarily lost from the duplex. It remains represented by the cytosine, although it is no longer represented by an intact guanine on its own strand. This asymmetry gives cells an opportunity to restore information, provided that the complementary strand remains available and unaltered.

The bonds involved in these structures have distinct biological roles. A phosphodiester bond provides physical continuity along the strand; a glycosidic bond anchors a base at its position; noncovalent interactions position the bases relative to one another. A lesion may interfere with one role without immediately interfering with all the others. For instance, removal of a base can leave both phosphodiester bonds associated with the sugar intact. The sequence then has a missing chemical letter but the chain has not been severed. By contrast, a single-strand break interrupts the chain but can leave every neighboring base chemically unmodified. A covalent bridge connecting opposite strands introduces yet another difficulty: the strands may remain continuous, but their necessary separation becomes obstructed. These examples explain why chromosome damage cannot be adequately characterized using only the number of base pairs present.

The word lesion refers to a chemically or structurally abnormal feature in a DNA molecule, including a modified base, a missing base, an abnormal covalent bridge or a broken backbone. A mutation is a persistent change in genetic sequence or chromosome structure relative to the relevant starting state. The two concepts overlap in their consequences but are not interchangeable. If cytosine becomes uracil within DNA, the molecule contains an abnormal base at that site. If repair restores cytosine before that site is copied incorrectly, the original base sequence can be maintained. If replication instead produces a thymine-containing daughter duplex after the lesion has been copied, a C:G to T:A substitution may become established. Conversely, a fixed T:A pair can be chemically normal even though it differs from the inherited C:G pair. A chemical assay that detects uracil would not necessarily detect the fixed substitution.

A third term, mismatch, describes inappropriate pairing between opposing nucleotides. A G:T pair can arise when a polymerase inserts thymine opposite guanine, or when a chemically modified cytosine becomes thymine while remaining opposite its original partner. Both outcomes produce the same pairing arrangement, but their histories differ. A mechanism that simply removes the nucleotide on one specified strand works only if that strand has been identified appropriately. During replication, a mismatch can indicate an error in the new strand. During chemical deamination, the altered nucleotide can be on either strand. The geometric abnormality alone does not reveal the complete history of the molecule.

There is also an important distinction between a potentially mutagenic lesion and a blocking lesion. A potentially mutagenic lesion changes the information that an enzyme may extract from the template; a blocking lesion prevents the enzyme from continuing efficiently. These categories are not mutually exclusive. One altered base might pair readily with a wrong nucleotide while a bulky chemical adduct might almost completely inhibit an ordinary polymerase. The outcome depends on the enzyme, the lesion's position, the surrounding sequence and the availability of alternative processing mechanisms. Thus a compound that causes very extensive DNA damage may produce comparatively few recovered mutations if severely damaged cells fail to survive. Survival, the frequency of chemical lesions and the frequency of inherited mutations must be considered as distinct observations.

In a mature non-dividing cell, a lesion may never be encountered by a replication fork during the time of observation. Nevertheless, the cell uses genes to produce RNA, and an RNA polymerase may encounter that lesion. Failure of RNA production can impair the supply of a needed protein. In a rapidly dividing cell the same lesion may additionally obstruct chromosome duplication. This is why the effect of DNA damage depends on cell state and on the functions occurring at the damaged locus. A base change in an inactive region may have little immediate physiological effect while a blocking lesion in a heavily used essential gene may matter before there is any inherited mutation.

A beginner can therefore approach DNA damage through three successive questions. First, which chemical structure has changed? Second, what does that alteration do to the DNA molecule as a substrate for enzymes? Third, what outcome follows if the altered substrate persists? Each question concerns a different scale of explanation: chemistry, molecular function and biological consequence. The distinction becomes especially useful when comparing radiation, hydrolytic reactions and oxidative stress, because their sources may differ even when some of their molecular products overlap.

2. Why endogenous DNA damage is unavoidable

A chromosome exists in a hydrated, chemically active environment. Water is indispensable for enzymes, solute transport and the stability of cellular structures, but it can also participate in chemical reactions involving DNA. Cellular metabolism produces reactive molecules, including oxidants and naturally occurring methyl-group donors. Some interact with DNA without the deliberate involvement of a DNA-modifying enzyme. Temperature, pH, availability of metals and local molecular architecture affect the rates of individual processes. The important conclusion is not that every chromosome deteriorates rapidly under every condition, but that none of these reactions has a universally zero probability.

An apparently spontaneous DNA lesion may have a well-defined chemical origin. In this context, spontaneous means that the event can occur without a specifically applied external damaging treatment. Hydrolysis can proceed through reactions involving water; deamination can change an amino substituent on a base; intracellular oxidants can alter bases or sugars. These reactions have chemical activation barriers, so they occur at characteristic rates that depend on conditions. At ordinary cellular temperatures the reactions are slow at an individual site, but the number of susceptible sites is enormous. A small probability multiplied by billions of chemical targets can correspond to a substantial number of events in a population of molecules.

Tomas Lindahl's work established the biological importance of intrinsic DNA instability. Measurements of decay reactions in DNA and subsequent investigation of repair systems showed why the preservation of genetic information requires continuous molecular maintenance. His 1993 review synthesized evidence that the primary chemical structure of DNA is not permanently stable. The key reasoning is that a process can be infrequent at a single nucleotide yet impose a major cumulative burden when considered over an entire genome and across a lifetime. This reconciles the familiar observation of stable heredity with the experimentally demonstrable vulnerability of DNA.

A measurement made with purified DNA can reveal spontaneous chemical change without requiring cells to replicate. For example, if DNA is kept under a defined set of conditions and released bases are detected over time, the resulting increase indicates that nucleotide chemistry changes even when a DNA polymerase is absent. A suitable comparison requires samples that began with equivalent DNA, a consistent method of measuring the chemical product and control of conditions such as temperature and pH. A background measurement taken at the initial time helps distinguish pre-existing damage from new damage. Such an experiment establishes that chemical loss is possible and can be quantified. It does not by itself establish how many lesions accumulate in a living chromosome, where DNA-associated proteins and enzymes alter the situation.

The cellular abundance of a lesion is determined by formation and disappearance. Suppose lesions form continuously and are removed soon after they form. A measurement taken at one instant can find relatively few lesions even though many have formed and been removed during the preceding hours. In a different cell, the formation rate might be lower but the removal process defective, giving a higher instantaneous abundance. Without knowing the time dependence or the removal capacity, it is not legitimate to interpret an increased abundance as evidence that formation necessarily increased. Part C problems commonly test this distinction through time-course measurements, mutant comparisons or different recovery periods.

It is also necessary to distinguish endogenous source from intracellular site. An oxidant can originate inside the cell during metabolism, or an external stimulus can initiate intracellular oxidant production. In both cases the immediate DNA lesion may be chemically similar. Conversely, one source such as ionizing radiation can generate several classes of damage. A classification by origin therefore answers a different question from classification by lesion type. The first helps explain exposure and prevention; the second helps explain template behavior and repair requirements. Neither is sufficient on its own.

In bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts, genetic material resides close to biochemical processes that involve electron transfer. This makes redox chemistry relevant to genome stability, but proximity is not enough to predict actual damage rates. Protective systems, DNA packaging, local metal availability, metabolic conditions and repair capacity differ among compartments and organisms. A photosynthetic cell additionally experiences light-dependent reactions, while its nuclear DNA may also be affected by environmental ultraviolet exposure. These comparisons support a general biological principle: the sources and processing of DNA damage reflect the physical and biochemical environment in which a genome is maintained.

A normal cell also has intentionally produced DNA intermediates. During replication and repair, enzymes can transiently generate nicks or gaps. During specific biological processes, regulated DNA breaks serve important purposes. Their existence should not be confused with indiscriminate injury. A controlled intermediate is normally created at a defined stage and subsequently processed in a coordinated way. An accidental or persistent intermediate becomes hazardous when this coordination fails. Thus even the boundary between an ordinary intermediate and a pathological lesion can depend on context, timing and whether the appropriate downstream process is functioning.

3. Classifying DNA lesions by the molecular target

Classification begins with whether the base, sugar, phosphodiester chain or relationship between strands has changed. A modified base such as uracil can remain attached to an intact sugar and an unbroken chain. An abasic site has lost its base while retaining the sugar initially. Damage to the sugar can destabilize the backbone, and cleavage of the backbone produces a strand discontinuity. These classes are connected chemically: a base modification may destabilize a glycosidic bond, base loss may make the sugar vulnerable to further reaction, and subsequent sugar damage can generate a strand break. A sample can therefore contain both an initiating lesion and downstream products.

The double helix can also acquire covalent links not present in normal DNA. Adjacent pyrimidines on one strand can become linked after absorption of ultraviolet radiation. A bulky adduct can project away from its attachment point and interfere with the fit of the duplex or of an enzyme acting on it. An interstrand crosslink joins the two complementary strands and can prevent their local separation. A DNA–protein crosslink joins DNA to a protein, creating an obstacle whose properties differ from those of a small altered base. Although all can interfere with enzyme progression, the geometry and size of the obstacle are different and the processing required to remove it is not identical.

Structural classification should be combined with an understanding of the coding face of the base. Polymerases distinguish nucleotides through several features, including the geometry and chemical interactions of the nascent pair. A modification that changes the donor or acceptor pattern can create an alternative pairing preference without producing a large deformation in the free duplex. An apparently small lesion may thus be unusually mutagenic. Conversely, a modification outside the coding face may primarily alter stability or interactions with proteins rather than immediately specify a different partner. Inferring mutagenic risk from the size of a chemical group alone is therefore unreliable.

A final distinction is between local and clustered damage. One isolated altered base can often be processed while the opposing strand remains intact. Several lesions located near one another may compromise both strands of the same region. Removing two nearby lesions independently can then create intermediates whose combination is more disruptive than either original site. Local clustering is particularly relevant to the physical deposition of ionizing radiation energy. A large number of well-separated lesions and a small number of clustered lesions are not equivalent burdens, even when a simple assay reports the same total number of damaged bases.

These categories establish the language needed for the subsequent chemical examples. The first reactions to examine are hydrolysis and deamination, because they demonstrate particularly clearly that the molecules responsible for storing information can change in the absence of an external irradiation or deliberate mutagen exposure.

4. Hydrolysis, depurination and the formation of abasic sites

A nucleotide is held together by bonds that differ chemically from one another. To explain depurination, focus on the attachment of a purine base to the sugar. In a deoxyribonucleotide containing adenine or guanine, the purine nitrogen at position 9 connects to carbon 1′ of deoxyribose. Cleavage of this N-glycosidic bond can release the purine from the strand. The result is a site at which the sugar and its associated backbone remain but the nitrogenous base is absent. The name depurination specifies the loss of a purine; loss of a pyrimidine is called depyrimidination. The broader expression abasic site or AP site, short for apurinic/apyrimidinic site, includes either origin.

Water participates in hydrolysis, but the word hydrolysis does not mean that every bond in DNA is equally vulnerable. The chemical environment around the glycosidic bond influences its reaction rate. Protonation of appropriate positions on a purine can facilitate base departure, helping explain why acidic conditions accelerate depurination. Base loss can also be accelerated by prior chemical modification. The N7 alkylation of guanine, for instance, can increase its tendency to be released. The initial alkylation and subsequent base loss are separate chemical events. If a researcher detects only the final AP site, the observation may not identify which initial process created it.

In an intact double helix, the abasic sugar occupies the position where a base should be. The opposing base is not automatically lost. Consider a short region in which a guanine on the upper strand faces cytosine on the lower strand. Once guanine leaves, the lower strand still contains cytosine, and consequently the original information remains inferable. The damaged upper strand cannot itself provide guanine's ordinary instructions for nucleotide pairing. These two descriptions of the same molecule explain both the possibility of accurate restoration and the danger of copying the lesion before restoration has occurred.

The missing base affects more than hydrogen bonding. Normal base stacking involves interactions between adjacent aromatic bases, and removal of one base changes local stacking and the shape of the duplex. The extent of deformation depends on neighboring nucleotides and on how the remaining sugar and opposite base are positioned. An AP site therefore need not create a completely open region or a visible gap in a chromosome image. It can nevertheless be recognized through the chemical and structural properties of the damaged region. A polymerase arriving at the site has no normal base with which to form the expected incoming pair.

The chemistry of an abasic deoxyribose makes the site potentially unstable. The sugar can interconvert between cyclic forms and an open-chain aldehyde form. The latter can participate in further reactions, including strand-cleaving processes. In particular, elimination reactions associated with the abasic sugar can break the backbone. These processes are promoted under some chemical conditions and are distinct from simple release of the base. The conversion explains why AP sites can be detected indirectly by methods that cause cleavage at chemically labile positions. It also explains why a preparation procedure can change the lesion being measured: an assay for breaks performed after harsh conditions may count AP sites converted into breaks during treatment, not solely breaks that were already present in the cell.

The distinction has an instructive parallel in the repair process. A DNA glycosylase is an enzyme that removes a particular damaged or inappropriate base from DNA. Its immediate product is also an abasic site. Such a product is not equivalent to a completely repaired molecule; further steps are required to replace the missing information and restore the backbone. In the next repair chapter, the coordinated handling of this intermediate will become important. For the present chapter, remember that an AP site may be formed by spontaneous hydrolysis, destabilization of a previously modified nucleotide or a deliberate enzymatic reaction. The same detectable lesion can have several origins.

An ordinary high-fidelity polymerase generally extends a primer most efficiently when a properly positioned template base supports the incoming nucleotide. At an AP site, this requirement cannot be satisfied normally. Polymerase pausing is therefore a reasonable prediction, although the actual probability of arrest is enzyme-dependent. Some DNA polymerases can insert a nucleotide opposite an AP site despite the absence of ordinary coding information. In several systems there is a preference for adenine insertion, often described as the A-rule. This is not a universally compulsory outcome. The surrounding sequence and the polymerase affect both insertion and subsequent extension. A student should avoid treating an AP site as a fifth base that always has adenine as a complement.

Consider the original G:C pair again. If a nucleotide A is inserted opposite the abasic sugar and the resulting strand subsequently acts as a template, thymine can be incorporated opposite that adenine. A T:A pair could replace the original G:C pair in a descendant molecule. If a different nucleotide is inserted, a different sequence outcome may occur. If extension fails, the main consequence may instead be a replication block. If an abasic site is accurately processed before such copying, the original G:C pair can be restored. The original chemistry therefore creates a branching set of possible outcomes rather than a predetermined mutation.

The relationship between a DNA lesion and strand length also illustrates how experimental observations should be interpreted. A small DNA fragment containing one AP site might migrate similarly to an undamaged fragment in a neutral separation system because its backbone length has not changed. After conditions that cleave at the abasic sugar, two shorter fragments may appear. A probe detecting one specific end would visualize only the corresponding fragment. The experiment could locate the lesion approximately by fragment length, provided the starting molecule and cleavage specificity were known. However, a band of reduced length alone does not prove that the initial event was depurination; other chemically sensitive lesions or pre-existing breaks could produce related results.

The chief lesson is that an abasic site represents a loss of coding information from one strand and a chemically altered sugar, rather than a deletion of a nucleotide pair from the genome. In sequence terminology, a deletion means that a position has been omitted from the recovered sequence. Base loss does not necessarily reduce the length of the covalent chain, and repair can restore the original position. Equating depurination with a genetic deletion would therefore confuse a molecular lesion with one possible genetic consequence.

5. Deamination and the chemical conversion of base identity

Deamination removes an amino group from a molecule. In nucleic acids, hydrolytic deamination of particular bases replaces an exocyclic amino substituent with an oxygen-containing group. Because base identity depends partly on the arrangement of amino and carbonyl groups, this modification can change base-pairing behavior. A deaminated base is often still attached to deoxyribose and incorporated into an otherwise continuous DNA strand. Unlike an abasic site, it retains a chemical surface that may instruct a polymerase to insert a specific nucleotide, even though that instruction differs from the original genetic message.

Cytosine provides the simplest and most important example. Cytosine is a pyrimidine whose base-pairing face normally complements guanine. Hydrolytic conversion of cytosine yields uracil. Uracil normally occurs in RNA and has the same principal Watson–Crick pairing partner as thymine, namely adenine. When cytosine in DNA becomes uracil, the original G on the opposite strand remains present. The first molecular product is therefore a U:G mismatch, not immediately a T:A base pair. This starting point is crucial for tracing subsequent replication.

Suppose the damaged strand serves as a template during semiconservative replication. If uracil directs insertion of adenine, the newly synthesized strand contains A at that position. The opposite parental strand, still containing G, can direct insertion of C. The two products are not equivalent: one retains the original information, while the other contains a lesion opposite a newly synthesized base that carries the altered instruction. If the A-containing strand is copied with normal DNA nucleotides, T is incorporated opposite A. This can yield a chemically normal T:A pair in one descendant lineage. Relative to the original C:G pair, the substitution is a transition. Whether this lineage survives and how many descendants carry the substitution depend on additional biological events.

Because uracil can pair with adenine, it is possible to ask why cells use thymine in DNA rather than using uracil directly. An important functional advantage of using thymine is that uracil in DNA can be identified as inappropriate and removed, including uracil produced by deamination of cytosine. If uracil were a normal major DNA base, identifying deaminated cytosine as chemically abnormal would be more difficult. This is a useful functional argument, not experimental proof of a unique evolutionary sequence of events. Several biochemical and evolutionary constraints affect nucleic acid composition, and the argument should not be presented as a complete historical explanation.

Not every uracil detected in DNA arose from cytosine deamination. DNA polymerases may incorporate dUMP from dUTP when nucleotide pools or selection conditions permit. This initially yields U:A rather than U:G if insertion occurs opposite A. The pairing may transmit the same information as T:A, although the presence of uracil remains chemically inappropriate in typical cellular DNA. An assay that reports total uracil cannot discriminate the two routes without additional information about pairing, nucleotide pools or treatment conditions. This example reinforces the need to identify both the chemical identity and the molecular context of a lesion.

The deamination of 5-methylcytosine creates a more difficult recognition problem. In many eukaryotic genomes, cytosines in particular sequence contexts can carry a methyl group at carbon 5 as a normal epigenetic modification. Deamination of 5-methylcytosine generates thymine, which is itself a normal DNA base. The original complementary guanine remains, creating a T:G mismatch. Because thymine is not chemically foreign to DNA, recognition cannot rely simply on the detection of uracil. Instead the abnormal pairing and context are important. If the mismatch is copied before correct restoration, a C:G to T:A transition can become fixed.

Methylated CpG sites are consequently well-known contexts associated with elevated rates of C-to-T substitutions in many vertebrate genomes. The methylation pattern is important: the biochemical presence of 5-methylcytosine supplies a substrate for the reaction. The same sequence in a system where cytosine methylation is absent or rare need not have the same mutational susceptibility. Moreover, the presence of a methylated CpG does not guarantee that deamination will occur, that repair will fail or that a mutation will be recovered. Mutation patterns are statistical outcomes reflecting substrate availability, chemical rates and the success of processing.

Adenine can undergo deamination to form hypoxanthine. As a nucleoside, deoxyinosine contains hypoxanthine as its base. Hypoxanthine tends to pair with cytosine rather than following adenine's normal preference for thymine. If this altered pairing is copied and fixed, an A:T to G:C transition is a possible consequence. The reasoning is parallel to cytosine deamination but the specific base identities differ. An examination question that asks for the expected substitution should begin with the original A:T pair, change adenine into hypoxanthine and then trace which normal base is added opposite the altered base before inferring the eventual canonical duplex.

Guanine deamination can produce xanthine. The consequences of this lesion are less conveniently reduced to one universal substitution rule. Xanthine can interfere with normal template use, and its pairing behavior and mutagenic outcome depend on the system. It is enough at NET/SET level to distinguish the chemical product from hypoxanthine and uracil, and to understand why an altered purine may affect both base selection and polymerase progression. Memorizing a single compulsory genetic result for every deaminated base would ignore the enzyme dependence of lesion bypass.

External chemistry can also promote deamination. Classical discussions of mutagens include nitrous acid as an example of a chemical agent capable of deaminating bases. This does not create a new definition of deamination; it illustrates that a lesion class also found during endogenous chemistry can be promoted by an exogenous source. To interpret experiments involving induced deamination, a comparison between treated and untreated samples is needed because the control group may already contain lesions formed spontaneously. The difference between groups estimates an effect of the treatment under those conditions, not the total spontaneous burden.

The local structure of DNA modifies susceptibility to some deamination reactions. A base within a paired duplex has a different environment from the same base in transiently single-stranded DNA. Replication, transcription and local DNA breathing can expose bases in ways that alter chemical accessibility and stability. It would be incorrect to assume that each cytosine in a genome has exactly the same reaction probability. This becomes important in advanced questions where two regions have equal cytosine content but differ in the time spent single stranded. If all other conditions were equal, a difference in susceptibility would be biologically plausible; a measured difference alone, however, would not establish which structural feature was responsible unless alternative explanations were controlled.

6. Connecting hydrolysis and deamination without confusing their products

The two processes are sometimes described together because both can involve water and occur spontaneously. Their effects on information are different. Hydrolysis of the N-glycosidic bond removes a base altogether. Hydrolytic deamination alters the base while typically preserving its attachment to the sugar. A polymerase facing an AP site therefore lacks an ordinary templating base, whereas a polymerase facing uracil has a base capable of hydrogen bonding to adenine. The most informative diagram labels the attachment bond and the changed amino group separately rather than representing both processes with an identical symbol for damaged DNA.

A single chemical product also cannot specify the entire history of damage. An AP site could have resulted from a repair enzyme excising uracil produced by deamination. In that sequence of events, the lesion population evolves from a modified base to a missing base and then to additional intermediates during repair. If uracil is measured at one time and AP sites at another, the values should not automatically be added as if they always represented different original chemical events. A kinetic experiment must distinguish a flux of newly damaged sites from the instantaneous amounts of chemically distinguishable intermediates. This point links the lesion chemistry to the quantitative models later in the chapter.

An informative hypothetical comparison is a cell with normal uracil removal and a cell lacking the relevant glycosylase. After equal formation of uracil, the first cell may initially display a faster decrease in uracil and a transient increase in AP sites. The second may retain uracil for longer. This does not establish that the first cell suffers more initiating damage. Rather, the first cell may be converting the initial lesion to a subsequent processing intermediate more quickly. Whether the overall process preserves the genome depends on successful completion of the remaining steps. Such causal reasoning is more powerful than simply ranking samples by whichever damage marker happens to have been measured.

7. Reactive oxygen species and the chemistry of oxidative DNA damage

Cells that use oxygen obtain major energetic advantages from transferring electrons to oxygen during respiration. Complete reduction of molecular oxygen yields water. However, electron transfer is not perfectly confined to the ideal sequence of reactions, and oxygen can undergo partial reduction to produce chemically reactive intermediates. The term reactive oxygen species, or ROS, includes radical species such as superoxide and hydroxyl radical, as well as nonradical species such as hydrogen peroxide. Calling all ROS free radicals is incorrect. A radical contains an unpaired electron; hydrogen peroxide does not. Nevertheless, peroxide can participate in reactions that generate radicals and can contribute to oxidative injury.

Superoxide can undergo dismutation to yield oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. Superoxide dismutases catalyze this reaction, while catalases and peroxidases participate in peroxide removal. These defenses help explain why the presence of oxygen and normal respiration does not inevitably result in extensive DNA damage. The relevant chemical exposure depends on the balance between generation, conversion and removal of reactive species. A deficiency in one protective enzyme can change that balance, but different species and subcellular compartments have different pathways, so the consequences should be inferred from the particular system under study.

Hydrogen peroxide is especially important because it can react with reduced transition-metal ions. The simplified Fenton reaction is Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH. In this representation, ferrous iron supplies an electron and hydrogen peroxide is converted into hydroxide and a hydroxyl radical. Both charge and atom counts balance: the left side has charge +2 and the right side has +3 −1 = +2. A hydroxyl radical is highly reactive and can attack nearby organic molecules. Consequently, the location of accessible iron matters. A radical generated close to DNA can react before it diffuses very far, whereas a radical generated at a distant site may react with other abundant cellular molecules. Iron that is tightly sequestered in a protein does not necessarily behave like accessible, chemically reactive iron.

Oxidants can attack both the bases and deoxyribose of DNA. Oxidation of a base alters its electronic structure and possibly its pairing properties. Attack on the sugar can create chemically unstable sugar products that lead to backbone cleavage. In addition, products of oxidative reactions involving other cellular molecules can subsequently react with DNA. Lipid peroxidation, for example, generates reactive carbonyl compounds that can form adducts on nucleobases. Thus the immediate source of a DNA modification may be an oxidant itself or another reactive compound generated as a consequence of oxidative chemistry. An assay detecting oxidized bases would not capture every DNA consequence of oxidative stress.

Several kinds of oxidative base modification occur because the four normal bases have different chemical structures. Guanine is particularly susceptible to oxidation because it has a relatively low oxidation potential among the normal DNA bases. A prominent product is 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine, commonly abbreviated 8-oxoG. Formation of this lesion changes the chemical structure near carbon 8 of the purine. The nucleotide is not necessarily removed from the strand, and the backbone may remain intact. Instead the altered base presents a different range of conformations and pairing interactions. The lesion is important in genetics because it can yield a characteristic transversion if it is copied inaccurately.

Consider guanine initially paired with cytosine. Normal guanine usually adopts a conformation about its glycosidic bond that permits standard Watson–Crick pairing with cytosine. Oxidized guanine can still pair with cytosine in an appropriate conformation, so oxidation does not guarantee inaccurate copying. However, it can also adopt a syn conformation and form an alternative pair with adenine. The two conformations differ in the orientation of the base relative to the sugar, which changes which portion of the base faces the incoming nucleotide. This is a good example of molecular structure influencing biological information transfer without necessarily creating a large visible distortion in the duplex.

If an enzyme inserts adenine opposite 8-oxoG in a parental strand, the intermediate is an oxidized guanine paired with adenine. During subsequent copying, the adenine-containing strand can direct the incorporation of thymine. The descendant duplex then contains a T:A pair where the original duplex contained G:C. The substitution G:C to T:A is a transversion because guanine, a purine, has been replaced at that oriented position by thymine, a pyrimidine. The complementary description C to A is equally valid when the opposite strand is chosen as the reference. It is essential to state the reference orientation rather than treating these descriptions as two independent mutations.

This oxidative substitution pathway differs from cytosine deamination. In the latter, the chemical conversion of C to U causes A insertion opposite the modified pyrimidine and can ultimately yield a C:G to T:A transition. In the former, the altered purine can present a misleading pairing surface that causes insertion of A instead of C, potentially yielding a transversion. Both examples involve adenine in an intermediate, but they have different initial base identities and therefore different mutation classes. Working through the entire sequence is a more reliable method than memorizing that an altered base pairs with adenine.

Oxidation need not first occur in chromosomal DNA. A nucleotide precursor in the soluble nucleotide pool can also be oxidized before incorporation. Oxidized dGTP may be inserted opposite adenine under some circumstances, producing a different route to mutagenesis. This distinction is particularly important when comparing the bacterial MutT, MutM and MutY functions conceptually. MutT limits the incorporation of oxidized guanine nucleotides from the pool; MutM participates in removal of 8-oxoG from DNA; MutY can remove adenine incorrectly paired with 8-oxoG. A defect in one function need not produce the same mutation spectrum as a defect in another. Details of repair chemistry belong to the following node, but the difference between precursor damage and template damage must already be understood.

A classic biochemical study by Maki and Sekiguchi connected the MutT protein to hydrolysis of an oxidized guanine nucleotide precursor, helping demonstrate that control of the nucleotide pool contributes to replication accuracy. This line of evidence addresses an important question that cannot be settled merely by observing oxidized bases in DNA: did the damaging event take place before or after the nucleotide was incorporated? An enzyme that removes a damaged precursor prevents its entry into DNA; it does not directly restore an existing chromosomal lesion. In a conceptual experiment, compare an oxidized nucleotide pool with a normal one using an otherwise identical copying system. If depletion of oxidized precursors changes the recovered error frequency, the pool contributes to the result. To distinguish this explanation from template damage, the starting DNA should be characterized and kept equivalent.

Oxidized pyrimidines can also have strong effects on polymerase progression. Thymine glycol is a representative lesion formed by oxidation of thymine, involving modification of the double bond in its pyrimidine ring. The altered base geometry can disrupt ordinary stacking and replication. Unlike the convenient mutagenic model for 8-oxoG, a thymine glycol lesion often illustrates the importance of blocking rather than simple alternative pairing. Its biological behavior depends on stereochemistry, sequence and polymerase identity. An examination scenario showing a polymerase that stops near an oxidized pyrimidine need not imply that all oxidative lesions block replication in exactly the same way.

Oxidative attack on deoxyribose can lead to single-strand breaks and, where lesions cluster or replication encounters a damaged strand, more serious consequences. A sugar-derived break may contain chemically modified ends rather than the normal 3′ hydroxyl and 5′ phosphate expected for a simple ligation substrate. The difference is mechanistically important because an enzyme that joins strands cannot necessarily act on every chemically abnormal end. Detecting a break tells us that the physical continuity of the backbone is interrupted; identifying its terminal chemistry tells us more about how the interruption arose and what processing is required before continuity can be restored.

Oxidative lesions can be measured through chemically specific detection of modified nucleosides or indirectly through lesion-recognizing enzymes. If a measurement is based on oxidized guanine, care must be taken to avoid additional oxidation during sample processing. Otherwise an apparently high cellular lesion burden may partly reflect damage generated outside the cell. Good comparisons employ equivalent preparation and appropriate controls. Furthermore, the amount of 8-oxoG is not interchangeable with total ROS concentration. An instantaneous ROS measurement, a stable DNA modification and a downstream mutation are three different endpoints, each determined by a different set of processes.

8. Alkylation: a small chemical group can change the genetic message

Alkylation refers to the addition of an alkyl group to a molecule. Methylation and ethylation are examples involving groups with one and two carbon atoms respectively. DNA contains several nitrogen and oxygen atoms that can react with suitable electrophilic compounds. The exact attachment site matters because different atoms on the same base contribute differently to base pairing, structural stability and protein recognition. One must distinguish accidental alkylation damage from normal enzymatic methylation used in biological regulation. A methyl group on the C5 position of cytosine in a regulatory context is not automatically a DNA lesion, even though the modified base can subsequently undergo a damaging reaction.

The potential sources of unwanted alkylation include endogenous reactive molecules and external chemicals. S-adenosylmethionine is a biologically important methyl donor used by enzymes, and spontaneous unwanted methyl transfer is possible. External alkylating compounds can increase the formation of several adduct types. Since their reaction chemistry differs, the distribution of alkylated sites depends on the particular source. A model in which every alkylating agent produces the same ratio of lesions would be chemically unjustified. For a NET question, the important distinction is between representative attachment positions and their different molecular consequences.

O6-methylguanine is a particularly instructive lesion. The oxygen at position 6 contributes to the normal guanine pairing surface. Attachment of a methyl group at this position changes the hydrogen-bonding properties and can favor inappropriate pairing with thymine. Begin with G:C. If the modified guanine directs incorporation of thymine, the resulting intermediate contains O6-methylguanine opposite T. If that T-containing strand is copied, A can be inserted opposite T, ultimately yielding A:T instead of G:C. This is a transition, not a transversion. The reasoning differs from 8-oxoG, which can direct adenine insertion and hence yield G:C to T:A. Both are modified forms of guanine, yet the favored mutational outcomes differ because the modified chemical structures differ.

N7-methylguanine provides a useful contrast. Nitrogen 7 of guanine is not part of the standard Watson–Crick pairing face in the same way as O6. An N7 methyl adduct does not simply have the same miscoding behavior as an O6 methyl adduct. Instead, it can destabilize the bond connecting guanine to deoxyribose and thereby increase formation of abasic sites. Thus the position of the methyl group affects the lesion's likely fate. A chemical analysis that detects a large amount of N7-methylguanine does not demonstrate that it contributes proportionately more point mutations than the less abundant O6-methylguanine. The biological effect per lesion is an additional variable.

N3-methyladenine illustrates yet another consequence of alkylation. This lesion can impede ordinary DNA polymerase progression, creating a problem of template accessibility and efficient synthesis. A cell with many such blocking lesions may show a marked reduction in DNA synthesis even if few fixed substitutions are subsequently recovered. Other methylation products, such as N1-methyladenine and N3-methylcytosine, can occur in appropriate contexts and interfere with normal pairing. These examples reinforce that alkylation is a chemical category rather than a guarantee of a particular mutation type or one common repair response.

A biologically meaningful comparison between methylated bases requires separating formation from removal. A system in which O6-methylguanine persists while other lesions disappear may develop a mutation pattern influenced disproportionately by the persistent lesion. A DNA methyltransferase that directly reverses O6 alkylation, such as mammalian MGMT, can change that persistence without reducing initial adduct formation. The methyl group is transferred to the protein, restoring the guanine and inactivating that individual acceptor molecule. More detail belongs to the direct reversal chapter. The conceptual significance here is that the abundance of the active protective protein can limit how many lesions are removed by this stoichiometric mechanism.

Consider two cell lines exposed to the same alkylating treatment. Suppose a chemical assay at the first time point finds equal O6-methylguanine burdens. Later, line X contains less of the lesion than line Y. If the assay is adequately controlled and the starting populations are comparable, the result suggests a difference in lesion disappearance, not necessarily in initial susceptibility to alkylation. It still does not by itself identify the responsible pathway: dilution during cell division, differences in DNA recovery, selective death and actual repair may all affect the measurement. Additional evidence, such as measurements in non-dividing cells and a comparison of relevant protective protein activity, can strengthen the inference.

The consequences of alkylation extend beyond altered base pairing. Some chemical agents have more than one reactive group and can connect two biological targets, producing crosslinks. Other molecules form larger adducts that interfere with DNA conformation. These lesions are discussed separately because their geometry creates distinctive physical obstacles, but the categories can overlap chemically. A crosslink can arise from alkylating chemistry while behaving very differently from a single methyl adduct. The appropriate conceptual classification therefore includes both the chemical reaction that produced the lesion and the molecular structure that remains afterward.

9. Ultraviolet radiation: how photons alter neighboring pyrimidines

A DNA base contains electrons distributed within a conjugated molecular structure. When light of an appropriate wavelength is absorbed, its energy can promote the molecule to an excited electronic state. Most excited molecules return to lower-energy states without producing a persistent covalent modification, but certain molecular arrangements allow a photochemical reaction to compete with relaxation. In DNA, two adjacent pyrimidines on the same strand can be positioned close enough for their excited-state chemistry to generate a covalent linkage. This reaction converts the normal arrangement of two separately attached bases into an abnormal product spanning neighboring positions.

Ultraviolet radiation is conventionally divided into UVA, UVB and UVC regions. The approximate wavelength ranges are 315–400 nm, 280–315 nm and 100–280 nm, respectively, although definitions may vary slightly. DNA absorbs strongly near 260 nm, so photons in the UVC region are effective at producing direct photochemical lesions in many experimental settings. Solar exposure at the Earth's surface differs from an artificial UVC source because atmospheric absorption largely excludes solar UVC and limits part of the UVB spectrum. Both UVB and UVA remain biologically relevant. UVA can generate oxidative damage through photosensitized chemistry and also produce pyrimidine photoproducts. It is an oversimplification to assign all direct DNA photochemistry to UVB and all oxidative injury to UVA.

The energy of an individual photon is given by E = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant, c the speed of light and λ the wavelength in metres. This equation connects a physical property of radiation with the energy available to excite molecular systems. A shorter wavelength corresponds to greater photon energy. It does not follow that every higher-energy photon produces a DNA lesion or that the same energy dose at different wavelengths produces identical damage. Absorption probabilities, scattering, penetration, molecular environment and the yield of a particular reaction all influence the outcome. Comparing wavelengths therefore requires distinguishing photon energy from biological effectiveness.

A cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer, abbreviated CPD, is one of the major ultraviolet-induced DNA lesions. Two adjacent pyrimidine bases normally have double bonds between carbons 5 and 6 in their rings. In a cyclobutane photoproduct these bonds participate in formation of a four-membered ring linking the two bases. In a simple chemical diagram, carbon 5 of one base connects to carbon 5 of the other, and carbon 6 connects to carbon 6. The original sugar-phosphate backbone is not necessarily broken, and the pyrimidines retain their individual attachments to their respective sugars. The new linkage restricts motion of two adjacent residues and changes their local geometry.

The popular name thymine dimer describes one example, not the complete set of CPDs. Depending on the underlying sequence, adjacent T–T, T–C, C–T and C–C positions can participate in photoproduct formation. The yields are not identical at all these sequences. The surrounding bases, helical conformation and DNA-associated proteins influence how readily two pyrimidines react. The word adjacent matters: the classic CPD joins successive pyrimidines along the same DNA strand, not two complementary bases facing each other across the duplex. Although other less common photoproducts can involve different geometries under particular conditions, this same-strand model is the central NET/SET concept.

A second major class is the pyrimidine(6–4)pyrimidone photoproduct, commonly called a 6–4 photoproduct or 6–4PP. Here the chemical linkage involves carbon 6 of the 5′ pyrimidine and carbon 4 of the adjacent 3′ pyrimidine, with additional alteration of the participating base structures. Unlike the CPD, it does not contain the same four-membered cyclobutane ring connecting both pairs of carbon positions. A schematic that labels 5′ and 3′ positions helps prevent the error of drawing the 6–4 lesion as a second cyclobutane structure. The two classes have different geometries and can therefore differ in their effects on local DNA shape and recognition by proteins.

The 6–4 photoproduct generally distorts the helix more strongly than a CPD, but the extent of distortion depends on the sequence and conditions. This distinction helps explain why a lesion that is less abundant immediately after irradiation can nevertheless be efficiently recognized by a system that responds to altered helical geometry. The amount of a lesion at a later time reflects both its initial yield and its rate of removal. If the less abundant lesion is also removed more rapidly, its contribution to a late measurement may be especially small. This observation should not be mistaken for proof that the lesion was never formed.

Classical photochemical studies helped identify these covalent products. Beukers and Berends reported a thymine photoproduct in 1960, and subsequent work investigated its relevance to irradiated DNA and biological inactivation. Setlow's 1966 review described evidence connecting cyclobutane dimers to ultraviolet effects, including the ability of a light-dependent photoreactivating enzyme to split dimers. The significance of this approach is that a physically detectable chemical product could be connected to a reversible biological effect. If removal of a defined lesion restores biological function, the observation supports a causal contribution of that lesion, rather than merely showing an association between irradiation and loss of activity.

A simplified reconstruction of the logic uses matched irradiated samples. One sample receives conditions allowing photoreactivation in a system containing an appropriate photolyase; another is held under conditions that do not activate the enzyme. An unirradiated control indicates the baseline activity or lesion signal. A useful additional control lacks the relevant photolyase but receives the same light exposure, allowing the investigator to distinguish enzymatic photoreactivation from other effects of light. If the photolyase-competent sample loses the appropriate dimers and recovers biological function, while the other irradiated groups do not recover equivalently, the evidence supports a functional contribution of these dimers. This is an educational description of experimental logic, not a claim that all historical experiments followed exactly these group assignments.

Photolyases require light energy to reverse particular photoproducts in organisms that possess the relevant enzyme. The light used for photoreactivation should not be confused with the ultraviolet exposure responsible for forming the damage. Formation and reversal are different photochemical processes occurring in different molecular contexts. Many bacteria, plants and other organisms possess photolyases, whereas placental mammals do not possess the conventional CPD or 6–4 DNA photolyase repair systems. Mammalian cells instead depend on other pathways, notably nucleotide excision repair, to remove these lesions. It is therefore unsafe to infer that exposing any irradiated cell to visible light will directly reverse its pyrimidine dimers.

The link between ultraviolet photoproducts and mutation requires further reasoning. A high-fidelity polymerase may stall at a linked pair because the bases cannot be positioned independently in the active site as normal template residues. A specialized bypass polymerase may accommodate the abnormal geometry and allow synthesis to continue. Some bypass events can be relatively accurate, whereas others generate mismatches. Cytosine-containing dimers can also undergo deamination, altering the instructions subsequently read during copying. The final mutation spectrum therefore depends on the chemical identity of the initial photoproduct and the circumstances of its processing.

C-to-T substitutions at dipyrimidine sites and tandem CC-to-TT changes are characteristic ultraviolet-associated mutation patterns. A tandem substitution concerns two adjacent positions and is particularly informative in the context of covalent lesions spanning neighboring pyrimidines. However, the existence of such a pattern does not allow every individual mutation to be attributed uniquely to one original photoproduct. Different processes can contribute to the same final sequence outcome, and the observed pattern is influenced by lesion formation, repair, bypass and selection. The correct scientific claim is an association supported by mechanistic and experimental evidence, not absolute identification of the history of every affected nucleotide.

In multicellular organisms, ultraviolet radiation illustrates how external exposure can become an internal molecular problem. Surface tissues receive a different exposure from deeply situated tissues, and optical shielding modifies the fraction of radiation reaching DNA. Pigments and tissue organization may reduce exposure but do not replace DNA maintenance mechanisms. In plants, ultraviolet screening and light-dependent repair can contribute to protection under sunlight, while different microbial habitats impose different exposure histories. These comparisons should not be converted into a universal ranking of susceptibility based only on organism size or habitat. The ability to prevent, remove and tolerate damage matters along with the energy reaching the genome.

10. Ionizing radiation: direct energy deposition, water radiolysis and clustered lesions

Ionizing radiation differs from the ultraviolet photochemistry just discussed because it can transfer enough energy to remove electrons from atoms or molecules. X-rays, gamma rays and certain energetic particles are examples. Radiation may interact directly with DNA or deposit energy in neighboring molecules. The direct route involves changes initiated by energy deposition in DNA itself. The indirect route involves reactive products generated from the surrounding medium, particularly water. Both routes can occur in the same biological exposure and may contribute to overlapping lesion classes.

Water radiolysis involves a sequence of physical and chemical events that can yield reactive species including hydroxyl radicals, hydrated electrons and hydrogen atoms, with further reactions generating other products. The hydroxyl radical can react with bases or deoxyribose, producing modified bases and backbone injury. The indirect route explains why the surrounding environment affects radiation damage even when the DNA sequence remains identical. Molecules capable of competing for reactive intermediates, the distribution of water and the presence of oxygen influence the resulting chemical products. These effects are context dependent; direct DNA ionization is not eliminated simply because indirect chemistry is suppressed.

Oxygen can react with some DNA-derived radicals and promote formation of more persistent oxidized products. This provides a chemical explanation for the familiar oxygen effect in radiation biology: for some types of ionizing radiation and biological endpoints, damage can be more effective under oxygenated than oxygen-poor conditions. The effect is not a statement that molecular oxygen is required for every radiation-induced lesion. Direct energy deposition and radical chemistry can still produce injury in its absence. The magnitude of the oxygen effect depends on radiation type, energy deposition pattern and the endpoint being measured.

The physical absorbed dose is energy deposited per unit mass. The gray, Gy, is defined as one joule per kilogram. A dose is not a count of DNA breaks. Energy deposited in a cell can affect water, proteins, lipids and other molecules as well as DNA, and radiation energy is not distributed uniformly over molecular targets. Two radiation qualities can deliver equal absorbed doses while producing different spatial patterns of ionization. Therefore, equal gray values do not guarantee equal DNA damage complexity or equal survival.

Energy deposition along a particle track can produce several chemical changes within a short distance of one another. This spatial clustering is important because two individually manageable lesions can create a more serious substrate when they occur on opposing strands nearby. An oxidized base adjacent to a nick, for instance, may complicate processing because removal of the base or incision of the backbone can create a second discontinuity. Closely opposed breaks can disrupt both strands and produce a double-strand break. A clustered damage site is not defined merely by the total number of lesions in a nucleus; it concerns the positions of lesions relative to one another on the molecular scale.

This distinction can be illustrated without assuming any particular radiation dose or organism. Imagine two DNA molecules of equal length, each with four damaged sites. In the first molecule, all four sites are widely separated on the upper strand. In the second molecule, two sites lie opposite one another within a short segment, and the remaining two form another closely spaced pair. The total lesion count is the same, but the second arrangement can present greater risks for preserving a usable complementary template. The first arrangement might permit independent processing of each site, while the second could create overlapping intermediates. The key variable is damage architecture, not merely lesion abundance.

An ionizing radiation exposure can produce base modifications, AP sites, sugar damage, single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks and crosslinks. It is therefore incorrect to learn a one-to-one rule such as ionizing radiation produces only double-strand breaks, whereas ultraviolet radiation produces only dimers. These are useful associations with prominent lesion classes, but real exposures produce distributions. The characteristic distinction is the underlying energy-transfer mechanism and the pattern of chemical injury. Ultraviolet photoproducts primarily involve direct excitation of neighboring bases, whereas ionizing radiation can produce a wider variety of changes through ionization and secondary chemical reactions.

An experimental comparison must also distinguish initial injury from later outcomes. If radiation is applied to a population and DNA is measured immediately, the result estimates damage present at the earliest sampled stage, subject to assay limitations. If the same population is measured several hours later, some initial lesions may have disappeared, while other lesions may have been converted into repair intermediates or secondary breaks. An increase in break signal after the exposure does not automatically indicate that radiation energy continued to arrive. It can reflect downstream processing or replication encountering persistent lesions. Conversely, a reduction in signal can reflect repair, loss of severely injured cells or other changes in the sampled population.

The absence of one radiation-associated lesion does not establish absence of all damage. A sample may show relatively little evidence of a particular oxidized base but contain many strand discontinuities. Another may contain numerous chemical modifications but retain long physical DNA fragments. The methods chosen must correspond to the scientific question. To compare total genotoxic effects, researchers commonly combine endpoints rather than assuming that a single marker reports every lesion equally well. This general principle will become important when interpreting electrophoretic measurements and lesion-specific assays in later sections.

11. Single-strand breaks: loss of continuity within one DNA chain

A single-strand break, often abbreviated SSB, interrupts one of the two covalent polynucleotide chains in a duplex. The other chain remains continuous at the corresponding position. This distinction separates a single-strand break from an abasic site: a missing base does not necessarily interrupt the chain, whereas a break necessarily interrupts the linkage between successive nucleotides. It also separates a simple nick from a gap. A nick is a discontinuity between two adjacent nucleotides, while a gap contains one or more missing nucleotides. A gap necessarily has discontinuities at its boundaries, but its chemical and informational properties differ from those of a nick with no missing residues.

Complementary hydrogen bonding can hold the two sides of a nicked strand in position against the intact partner. A chromosome containing an SSB therefore need not fall into two separate double-stranded fragments. In an isolated linear duplex under conditions preserving base pairing, the nick may be relatively difficult to recognize by fragment length alone. In a denaturing environment, however, the interrupted strand separates into shorter pieces while the continuous complementary strand retains its length. The contrast between native and denaturing observations is a useful way to reason about the structure of the damaged DNA.

A covalently closed circular DNA molecule provides another instructive example. When both strands are covalently continuous and the duplex is topologically constrained, a molecule can retain supercoiling. A nick in one strand permits rotation around the remaining continuous strand and can relax superhelical stress. The resulting open circular form has a different conformation from the compact supercoiled molecule. Electrophoretic separation can reveal this conversion even though the number of base pairs remains essentially unchanged. The precise migration order depends on gel and DNA conditions, but in commonly used agarose gel conditions an open circular plasmid tends to migrate more slowly than the compact supercoiled form. A linearized plasmid of the same sequence is a separate structural form and often migrates between them. Comparing bands therefore requires knowledge of topology as well as molecular length.

One cannot assume that all strand breaks have ends suitable for immediate joining. Ordinary DNA ligases join appropriately juxtaposed 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate ends using a chemically activated intermediate. A break generated by sugar oxidation may instead contain a blocked 3′ terminus or another abnormal end structure. The problem is then not simply that the ends are spatially separated; their chemistry may be incompatible with ligation. Additional processing can be required before joining. The exact reactions differ with lesion type and are taught in the subsequent repair nodes. At this stage the principle is that a gel reporting broken DNA cannot distinguish a simple ligatable nick from every chemically complex break.

Single-strand discontinuities also arise during normal cellular reactions. On the lagging strand of a replication fork, DNA is synthesized as fragments that eventually become covalently linked. Temporary nicks are therefore expected intermediates. Topoisomerases use transient strand cleavage to regulate DNA topology; their normal catalytic cycles restore continuity. An enzyme-linked break becomes a problem when the reaction is interrupted before restoration. DNA repair similarly creates transient interruptions to remove damaged constituents and insert replacements. A sample undergoing active repair may contain a temporary excess of nicks even when the initiating lesions are being processed successfully.

The most important biological consequence of a persistent single-strand break may appear when a replication fork reaches it. Consider a replisome moving along a duplex toward an interruption in one parental template. As the parental strands separate, the continuity of one template can no longer support the same molecular arrangement. Depending on the strand affected and fork configuration, progression can generate a broken or collapsed replication structure, including a one-ended double-strand break. This phenomenon explains why a lesion that seemed structurally modest in non-replicating DNA may become severe during S phase. The replication machinery does not simply copy across a physical discontinuity as though it were an ordinary altered base.

An SSB on the template strand of an actively transcribed gene can also interfere with RNA production, particularly if the discontinuity has abnormal ends or creates a gap. The exact response depends on lesion location and the transcription system. An interruption elsewhere in the duplex may exert less immediate effect on that transcription event. A lesion's consequence cannot therefore be predicted solely from its classification: its strand identity and genomic position are relevant variables. This principle connects strand integrity to the functional use of DNA without requiring a detailed description of transcriptional regulation.

12. Double-strand breaks and loss of local template continuity

A double-strand break, or DSB, interrupts both polynucleotide chains within a local region so that duplex continuity is lost. The cuts need not occur exactly opposite each other. Closely staggered cuts can produce single-stranded extensions, and cuts may generate blunt ends or ends with complex chemical structures. A diagram representing two aligned straight breaks is useful for teaching the basic definition, but it should not imply that all actual DSBs possess identical ends. The spacing of the cuts and the chemistry of the termini influence how the broken molecule can subsequently be processed.

In a linear chromosome, a DSB separates the continuous duplex into two broken portions. The complementary strand that would normally supply local information is itself interrupted. This makes a DSB qualitatively different from an isolated base lesion in an otherwise intact duplex. A homologous copy, such as a sister chromatid after replication, may provide an alternative source of information. Alternatively, the broken ends may be joined directly if the cellular machinery can identify and process them. Neither restoration route can be assumed simply from the detection of a break. The availability of homologous DNA, cell-cycle state, end chemistry and competition among processing pathways affect the possible outcomes.

Some DSBs are generated directly, for example when local ionizing energy deposition damages both strands. Other DSBs arise secondarily. Two nearby single-strand breaks may together disrupt duplex continuity; processing nearby damaged bases can generate opposed incisions; a fork encountering a persistent nick can produce a one-ended break. In each case the measured DSB is a product of a different sequence of events. This distinction is essential when interpreting a time course after an external exposure. A delayed increase in DSBs may reflect processing of earlier lesions rather than new primary DSB formation by the original external source.

A conventional double-stranded restriction enzyme cut provides a clean conceptual comparator. The enzyme recognizes a particular sequence and cleaves defined phosphodiester bonds to generate products with characteristic ends. A radiation-induced break may occur at a less predictable position and may be accompanied by nearby damaged bases or abnormal sugar residues. Thus two samples can contain the same number of double-strand breaks and still differ substantially in how readily the breaks can be restored. Counting the breaks does not fully describe their molecular complexity.

When a chromosome is broken, end association becomes a problem. Joining the original partners appropriately can restore continuity, but inappropriate joining can connect DNA segments that were not previously adjacent. Loss of sequence during processing can produce a deletion; connection of unrelated chromosomal segments can produce a rearrangement. An exchange between broken chromosomes can lead to a translocation. Such outcomes are mutations at the chromosome level if the altered structure becomes established. The original DSB was a lesion, and the rearranged chromosome is one possible stable genetic result of processing it. The distinction is the same in principle as that between cytosine deamination and a fixed C-to-T substitution, even though the scale of the damage is much larger.

A break may also leave a chromosome fragment without the structures required for normal segregation. In eukaryotic cells, the centromere enables interaction with the chromosome segregation machinery. A fragment lacking a functional centromere may fail to segregate normally. Unprotected broken ends can also participate in abnormal chromosome interactions. This links the DNA integrity problem to the chromosome architecture and cell division concepts from Unit 2. A damaged chromosome need not produce only a local sequence error; it can alter the inheritance of much larger regions of genetic material.

The biological burden of a DSB cannot be reduced to a universal probability of lethality. Some organisms and cell states efficiently restore substantial amounts of broken DNA; others are more sensitive. Even in one cell, a break at an essential or structurally difficult locus may have different consequences from a break elsewhere. A cell may delay division, restore continuity, survive with a rearrangement, undergo permanent arrest or die. The observed outcome represents a response of the entire system, not a simple property of the broken phosphodiester bonds.

13. Bulky adducts, intrastrand crosslinks, interstrand crosslinks and DNA–protein crosslinks

A DNA adduct is a chemical group covalently attached to DNA as a result of a reaction. An adduct may be relatively small, like a methyl group, or sufficiently bulky to alter the shape and flexibility of the double helix. Some environmental compounds undergo metabolic activation to generate reactive derivatives that attach to nucleobases. A well-known example in discussions of carcinogenesis is the formation of bulky guanine adducts by reactive metabolites of certain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The parent compound and the ultimate DNA-reactive derivative should not be conflated: metabolic transformations can influence whether an exposure creates a DNA lesion at all. This provides a bridge between metabolism and genetic toxicity.

A bulky adduct can interfere with enzyme progression even when the backbone is intact and the original base remains present. To act on DNA, a polymerase must position the template within an active site and coordinate its movement relative to incoming nucleotides. An attached chemical group can make an otherwise ordinary base difficult to accommodate. The adduct can also disrupt pairing or stacking, changing how the local DNA segment is recognized. In some cases a bypass polymerase can copy across it with an altered probability of misincorporation. In other cases progression stops. Therefore, the structure of an adduct determines a range of possible consequences rather than providing a simple answer about which base will ultimately replace the original one.

An intrastrand crosslink joins two sites on the same strand. A cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer is a photochemical example; certain chemical agents create other types of intrastrand adducts. Cisplatin is a standard example from molecular biology: its DNA lesions include predominantly intrastrand platinum crosslinks, especially between adjacent purine residues, as well as less frequent interstrand crosslinks and other adducts. The important lesson is the geometry of linkage, not the clinical use of the compound. Adjacent guanines joined by a platinum-containing adduct differ chemically from adjacent thymines joined by a cyclobutane ring, even though both lesions restrict same-strand nucleotide positioning.

An interstrand crosslink joins the complementary chains to one another through an abnormal covalent linkage. The ordinary double helix is stabilized by noncovalent interactions between the strands, which can be disrupted locally to allow replication and transcription. An interstrand covalent bridge cannot be separated simply by unwinding the helix or breaking ordinary hydrogen bonds. This creates a direct mechanical obstacle to processes requiring strand separation. Importantly, such a molecule may retain a continuous sugar-phosphate backbone in both strands. A test measuring intact fragment length could therefore fail to reveal the very lesion that prevents the molecule from functioning as a template.

The reason interstrand crosslinks are difficult to process is that both templates are involved in the same chemical structure. Excision of one modified base alone may not suffice to separate the strands and restore normal information. Multiple coordinated reactions may be required to release the linkage and restore the affected region. The repair details belong to later chapters; here the structural explanation is sufficient to predict that an interstrand crosslink can block replication and transcription severely. A student who understands this physical constraint can infer the effects of an unfamiliar crosslinking agent even without memorizing a specific chemical name.

Crosslinks can also connect DNA to proteins. A DNA–protein crosslink may arise when a reactive chemical links a protein to DNA or when a normally transient covalent enzyme–DNA intermediate persists. Topoisomerases provide a useful example of the latter possibility. They normally create controlled cleavage intermediates in which the enzyme is covalently linked to DNA, followed by restoration of the strand. If that intermediate persists, the attached protein and the interrupted backbone can together form an obstacle. A large protein adduct differs from a small base adduct in both steric properties and the possible requirements for processing. This illustrates how enzyme malfunction and chemical injury can generate related structural problems.

The distinction between a trapped topoisomerase intermediate and a simple inhibitor of catalytic activity deserves attention. A compound that prevents an enzyme from performing its normal catalytic reaction need not necessarily increase the amount of the covalent cleavage intermediate. A compound that stabilizes such an intermediate can increase DNA-associated cleavage complexes even if the net physiological outcome is reduced topoisomerase function. Both may disrupt DNA metabolism, but their primary molecular effects differ. An experimental result showing accumulation of protein-linked breaks is more directly consistent with persistent cleavage complexes than with a nonspecific reduction in enzyme abundance alone.

The effect of an interstrand crosslink on DNA separation suggests a useful experimental prediction. If DNA strands would ordinarily separate under denaturing conditions, a region containing a covalent interstrand bridge may remain associated even when normal base pairing is disrupted. Similarly, crosslinking can reduce the migration of DNA in a break-sensitive assay because the damaged strands cannot move as freely. A reduced migration signal is not necessarily evidence of less damage; it can reflect a different lesion class that opposes migration. An experiment that introduces a controlled comparison of break formation and crosslink-associated retention can help distinguish these possibilities, provided the analysis remains sensitive to other causes of reduced migration.

The general relationship between lesion structure and cellular consequences can now be seen clearly. Small miscoding modifications change the information presented at a nucleotide position. Missing bases remove that information from one strand. Strand breaks disrupt physical continuity. Bulky adducts obstruct protein movement. Same-strand links restrict neighboring residues, and interstrand links obstruct strand separation itself. These categories are useful precisely because they lead to different mechanistic predictions even when they all fall under the general heading of DNA damage.

14. How DNA damage affects replication, transcription and cell physiology

To copy a chromosome, the replisome must expose single-stranded DNA and repeatedly present template nucleotides to polymerases. A chemical change can affect any of these requirements. An interstrand crosslink prevents normal local strand separation, whereas a small miscoding lesion may permit separation but alter nucleotide selection. A polymerase-blocking lesion can arrest synthesis while a helicase continues to unwind DNA for some distance. This uncoupling can produce exposed single-stranded regions that must be protected from inappropriate reactions. Thus a single chemical lesion can generate a larger structural disturbance through the activities of the replication machinery.

The choice between accurate synthesis and arrest depends on the substrate and the enzyme. A high-fidelity replicative polymerase has a constrained active site that helps reject unsuitable base pairs, but this precision also makes some damaged templates difficult to copy. A specialized translesion polymerase may have an active site that accommodates a damaged template more readily. Its activity can permit replication to resume, but the accuracy of bypass depends on both the enzyme and the lesion. Some bypass polymerases are relatively accurate for specific lesions, and their effects cannot be summarized as uniformly error prone. Crucially, synthesis past a lesion does not necessarily remove it from the parental strand. The distinction between tolerating damage and chemically restoring DNA will be developed in Node 3.A.10.

A stalled replication fork is not synonymous with a double-strand break. Stalling refers to an inability to continue replication efficiently at that time, and the underlying DNA structure may initially remain recoverable. If the obstacle is processed, synthesis may restart without losing substantial sequence. Persistent or improperly handled arrest can generate additional problems, including gaps, broken replication structures and incomplete chromosome duplication. This sequence explains why a treatment can produce rapid inhibition of DNA synthesis but only later produce a break-associated marker. The earlier lesion and the later break are mechanistically connected without being identical.

Replication stress also has causes other than chemical lesions. Limited nucleotide supply, difficult DNA sequences, torsional stress and conflicts between replication and other processes can impede fork progression. Inhibition of DNA synthesis alone is therefore insufficient evidence of DNA damage. For example, depletion of an essential nucleotide precursor can reduce incorporation of labeled nucleotide without producing a particular base adduct. Measuring the relevant precursor pools, examining lesion-specific endpoints and testing recovery can help discriminate these possibilities. This distinction becomes important in exam problems in which a drug decreases DNA synthesis but its direct target has not been established.

The eukaryotic DNA damage response provides a way to link physical substrate abnormalities to cell-cycle regulation. ATM and ATR are protein kinases commonly associated with responses to double-strand breaks and replication stress, respectively. At a DSB, the recognition and signaling machinery can activate ATM-dependent responses; at a stressed replication fork, accumulation of RPA-coated single-stranded DNA helps recruit and activate ATR signaling. These are useful principal associations rather than exclusive rules, because processing of DSBs can also create single-stranded DNA and the signaling systems interact. The kinases participate in signaling pathways that can restrain cell-cycle progression and help preserve genome integrity. They do not chemically reverse a thymine dimer merely by phosphorylating a target.

A checkpoint-mediated decrease in division can be protective. If a cell divides before chromosome duplication or repair has been completed, damaged structures may be segregated abnormally. Temporarily restraining progression can provide an opportunity to process lesions. A cell unable to activate a checkpoint might therefore appear to continue division more readily immediately after damage yet display greater chromosome instability later. Short-term proliferation and long-term reproductive viability are not equivalent endpoints. Conversely, persistent arrest is not proof that all lesions remain; the regulatory response can have a different time course from chemical repair.

Transcription creates a parallel requirement to read DNA, even in a cell that is not dividing. RNA polymerase moves along a template strand and synthesizes complementary RNA. A blocking lesion in the transcribed strand can interfere with elongation and reduce production of full-length transcripts. A lesion in the opposite strand may have a different direct effect, although a large deformation or an interstrand crosslink can obstruct both. The position of the lesion relative to promoter recognition and the transcribed region also matters. A lesion downstream of the transcription termination site may have little direct effect on a particular transcript, while the same lesion within an essential coding region can be physiologically important.

Some damaged bases can alter RNA nucleotide selection without creating a permanent DNA mutation. If an RNA polymerase reads an oxidized guanine incorrectly, an altered RNA molecule may be generated. Such transient errors can change a protein produced from that transcript, a phenomenon called transcriptional mutagenesis. This differs from a fixed DNA mutation because the altered RNA is not necessarily copied into the genome and usually has a limited lifetime. Multiple transcripts may nevertheless be affected while the DNA lesion persists. In a long-lived non-dividing cell, the physiological significance of lesions can therefore extend well beyond inherited mutations.

A cell with persistent DNA damage may experience altered gene expression, delayed proliferation, permanent arrest or cell death. In multicellular systems, apoptosis can eliminate a severely compromised cell, reducing the chance that an abnormal cell lineage will expand. Survival with a fixed mutation is a different outcome from death, and not every surviving mutation is harmful. Its effect depends on location, gene function, dominance and the biological environment. Cancer development is a useful example of the importance of genome stability, but a single lesion does not automatically cause cancer. Damage must be considered in the context of mutation fixation, cellular selection and multiple other biological requirements.

Comparisons across organisms highlight the distinction between resistance to DNA damage and resistance to cell death. A microorganism that survives substantial irradiation may do so through effective protection, restoration of genetic information, efficient protein maintenance or a combination of traits. Its survival does not necessarily imply that no lesions were formed. Likewise, plants exposed to sunlight can deploy protective pigments and repair systems without making their DNA intrinsically immune to UV photochemistry. An observed resistance phenotype is a system-level outcome. Identifying its cause requires measurements at appropriate molecular and cellular levels.

15. Quantifying lesion formation, disappearance and mutational probability

A useful first approximation treats lesion production as a collection of rare events. Suppose a DNA population contains N susceptible sites and each site has probability p of suffering a specified lesion during a defined interval. If the sites have equal probabilities, the expected number of affected sites is Np. For example, a hypothetical genome contains 2 × 10⁶ susceptible bases and the probability of modification of each base in an interval is 3 × 10⁻⁶. The expected number is 6 lesions per genome. An expectation of six does not mean that every genome has exactly six lesions. It is the average predicted across many equivalent genomes. Independence is not required to calculate the expectation by summing probabilities, but it is required for the simple independent-site probability distribution used below.

Counting units is essential. A double-stranded genome containing 3 × 10⁹ base pairs contains approximately 6 × 10⁹ individual nucleotide residues. If the probability is quoted per nucleotide rather than per base pair, the appropriate target count changes. For a lesion restricted to guanine, only the number of guanine residues should enter the formula. Suppose a hypothetical haploid duplex has 4 × 10⁶ base pairs and its GC content is 50%. The duplex contains 8 × 10⁶ nucleotide residues, of which 2 × 10⁶ are guanines and 2 × 10⁶ cytosines, because the number of G residues equals the number of C residues in a complete ordinary duplex. If the probability of a lesion per guanine is 2 × 10⁻⁶, the expected count is four, not sixteen. The latter would incorrectly treat every nucleotide as an equally susceptible guanine.

If events are rare and approximately independent, the number of lesions can often be approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean λ. The probability of observing zero events is e^(−λ), and the probability of at least one is 1 − e^(−λ). For a hypothetical DNA fragment with mean 0.5 blocking lesions, the probability of an undamaged fragment is e^(−0.5) ≈ 0.607, while the probability of at least one blocking lesion is about 0.393. For a mean of two lesions, the corresponding probabilities are about 0.135 and 0.865. Longer fragments tend to have a greater probability of encountering a lesion if the per-length rate is constant. However, clustered radiation damage, sequence-specific photochemistry and heterogeneous chromatin accessibility violate the simple uniform independent-event assumptions. The calculation is an illustrative model, not an exact description of every genome.

A second model considers changes in lesion burden with time. Let L represent the mean number of a particular lesion per genome and F represent a constant formation rate in lesions per genome per hour. If disappearance is approximately first order with rate constant k in h⁻¹, the change is dL/dt = F − kL. The first term supplies new lesions; the second removes a fraction of the existing burden per unit time. At steady state the mean burden is constant, so 0 = F − kL and L = F/k. If F = 80 lesions per genome per hour and k = 0.4 h⁻¹, the steady-state burden is 200 lesions per genome. Doubling the formation rate at fixed k doubles the burden. Halving k at fixed F also doubles the burden. A single measurement of 400 lesions cannot distinguish these two explanations.

When an external pulse of damage ends and new formation is negligible relative to the excess being studied, the model becomes dL/dt = −kL. Separating variables gives dL/L = −k dt. Integrating between the initial time and time t gives ln(L/L₀) = −kt, hence L = L₀e^(−kt). Here L₀ is the initial excess burden and t is in hours. The half-life follows by setting L/L₀ equal to 1/2, giving t½ = ln(2)/k. If a lesion burden falls from 120 to 30 per genome in four hours, the remaining fraction is 0.25. Under the model, k = −ln(0.25)/4 ≈ 0.347 h⁻¹ and the half-life is about two hours. The model assumes a constant fractional disappearance rate and no substantial ongoing excess production, dilution or selective cell loss. If these assumptions fail, the fitted constant cannot be identified uniquely as a repair rate.

The presence of a nonzero background changes the interpretation of recovery curves. Suppose an untreated population has a stable mean of 20 lesions and a treated population has 180 immediately after exposure. The initial excess is 160. If two hours later the treated population has 60, its excess is 40, so the excess has fallen to one quarter of the initial value. Assuming the background remains stable and excess removal follows first-order kinetics, the half-life is one hour. Calculating from the total values 60 and 180 without subtracting background would give a different answer. Background subtraction is justified only when the untreated comparison accurately represents the background under the relevant conditions.

The probability of mutation fixation can also be represented using a deliberately simplified conditional model. Suppose a cell experiences an average of 100 potentially miscoding lesions. If 90% are accurately repaired before copying, an average of ten persist to the next relevant stage. If 20% of persistent lesions direct incorrect nucleotide incorporation, an average of two miscoding events occur. If half of those lead to fixed substitutions in the surviving lineages being modeled, the expectation is one fixed substitution. These values are hypothetical, and the stages are conditional rather than independent alternatives: the probability of miscopying applies to lesions that persist, not to the whole original lesion population. Real outcomes depend on cell lineage, lesion position, repair after misincorporation, survival and selection. A low mutation count cannot by itself demonstrate a low lesion formation rate.

Radiation-related calculations require a different physical unit. Using h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, a photon of wavelength 260 nm has energy (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸)/(260 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 7.64 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or about 4.77 eV using 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A 365 nm photon has energy about 3.40 eV. The ratio is 365/260 ≈ 1.40, not the square of that ratio. The number of photochemical lesions cannot be calculated from photon energy alone, because the fraction absorbed and the probability of lesion formation after absorption are not specified. This is why simple physical calculations can clarify trends while leaving biological outcomes uncertain.

For ionizing radiation, a hypothetical sample of mass 0.002 kg absorbing 0.004 J receives an absorbed dose of 0.004/0.002 = 2 Gy. The calculation states energy per mass; it does not specify how many lesions were generated. If an exam independently provides a measured yield of lesions per genome per gray and assumes linearity over the given range, multiplication by dose can predict the expected lesion count. Without the measured yield or an appropriate model, conversion from gray directly to DSB number is unjustified. The physical and biological quantities have different dimensions and must not be substituted for one another.

16. How experimental measurements distinguish lesion chemistry and consequences

Direct chemical analysis can identify modified nucleosides using separation and detection methods. A lesion-specific signal is most informative when the identity of the detected compound is validated against a suitable standard. For example, detection of 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine can provide evidence of oxidized guanine residues, but the interpretation must account for possible oxidation during isolation and processing. An internal standard and matched preparation can improve comparability. Such a measurement does not directly tell whether a replication fork encountered the lesion or whether a mutation resulted. Those questions require separate measurements of replication or sequence outcomes.

Antibodies that recognize particular UV photoproducts can provide a different type of information. A CPD-specific signal measured shortly after irradiation can be compared with the signal after a recovery interval. Loss of signal is consistent with removal of the recognized photoproduct, provided that detection efficiency, DNA recovery and population composition remain comparable. The signal is not necessarily a linear count of all DNA damage types. A CPD antibody will not automatically measure 6–4 photoproducts, oxidative bases or strand breaks. Use of more than one validated lesion-specific reagent can distinguish different kinetic profiles after the same treatment.

Gel-based methods examine DNA movement through a matrix and can reveal fragment size or topology. With circular DNA, conversion from supercoiled to open circular or linear forms can report physical strand discontinuities under appropriate conditions. With large chromosomal DNA, methods adapted for separating large fragments can help assess loss of duplex continuity. In a denaturing gel, single-strand fragments can reveal interruptions that do not divide the intact duplex in a native gel. Each result must be interpreted relative to the molecular form expected under the experimental conditions. Migration speed alone does not uniquely identify a lesion.

The comet assay provides a cell-by-cell view of DNA migration after lysis and electrophoresis. DNA from individual nuclei forms a head region and, when sufficient DNA can migrate, a tail region. The standard alkaline assay is sensitive to strand discontinuities and alkali-labile sites; it does not identify each original lesion separately. A lesion-recognizing enzyme can convert some otherwise poorly detected base modifications into detectable breaks. If the enzyme-treated sample displays greater migration than a matched sample without the enzyme, the difference can support the presence of enzyme-sensitive lesions. It is not valid to equate that difference with a specific absolute lesion count without appropriate calibration and knowledge of enzyme specificity.

Neutral comet measurements should not be described as universally and exclusively specific to DSBs. The conditions and physical basis of migration matter. Similarly, DNA crosslinks may reduce migration by restricting the movement of strands, even when damage is greater. This makes interpretation of a reduced comet tail particularly sensitive to the experimental context. A meaningful comparison of crosslinking includes a break-inducing reference condition and a group with both effects so that inhibition of migration can be distinguished from an absence of strand breaks. Severe cell death can also produce extensively fragmented DNA, complicating the interpretation of a large migration signal as direct primary damage.

The phosphorylation of histone H2AX near DNA damage-associated sites provides another useful marker in eukaryotic cells. Gamma-H2AX foci can indicate activation of a response associated with DSBs and other relevant stresses. However, a focus is a region of signaling, not a direct visual image of one broken phosphodiester bond. Foci may overlap, persist after some molecular changes have been resolved, or arise in contexts including replication stress. It is therefore inappropriate to convert every focus count into an exact DSB count without validating the relationship in the system. Combining a signaling measurement with a physical or chemical damage assay gives a stronger basis for conclusions.

Measurements of DNA synthesis can show that genome duplication has slowed, while transcription measurements can show reduced production of RNA. Sequence analysis after growth can reveal substitutions and rearrangements that have become established. Colony formation or long-term proliferation can assess the ability of cells to produce descendants. These endpoints answer complementary questions. A chemical lesion might be abundant without an increase in surviving mutations if most damaged cells die; a mutation may persist after the chemical lesion has vanished; a checkpoint may reduce proliferation without direct inhibition of polymerase by a damaging compound. A well-designed experiment therefore matches each measurement to a specific hypothesis and avoids using a downstream proxy as though it measured every upstream event.

For an integrated hypothetical experiment, compare two genetically matched cell populations differing in one protective function. Measure a specific lesion at the earliest comparable time and again after recovery, monitor DNA synthesis, and examine survival and mutations among surviving descendants. Equal initial lesion measurements followed by different recovery kinetics suggest a difference in processing or persistence. A slower return of synthesis together with more persistent blocking lesions supports a mechanistic link but does not exclude a checkpoint contribution. A higher mutation frequency among survivors may reflect lesion bypass or selection rather than a greater initial formation rate. Independent biological replicates, matched untreated controls and appropriate normalization allow these alternatives to be investigated rather than assumed.

The next stage of studying genome stability is to ask how each substrate is recognized and restored. The chemical taxonomy developed here supplies the reason that different repair mechanisms exist: a misplaced chemical group can sometimes be reversed directly, a damaged base can be removed and replaced, a bulky lesion may require removal of a short region, and a broken duplex or covalent link between strands presents a different structural problem. These are not arbitrary categories to memorize. They follow from the physical structure of DNA and from the need to preserve its information while maintaining a usable molecular template.

18. Part B — Conceptual questions (original)

B1. Which event produces an AP site directly? (A) Breaking a hydrogen bond between paired bases (B) Joining two DNA ends (C) Hydrolyzing a base–sugar glycosidic bond (D) Replacing one complete base pair with another.

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. The base departs, but the sugar and backbone can initially remain. Disrupting pairing does not remove the base, ligation restores continuity, and exchanging complete base pairs describes a substitution.

B2. The first product of cytosine deamination in a G:C pair is: (A) G:U (B) G:A (C) a DSB. (D) A:T

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The complementary G is unchanged; T:A may appear only after miscopying and fixation. No backbone cleavage is required.

B3. Deamination of 5-methylcytosine yields: (A) xanthine. (B) uracil (C) hypoxanthine (D) thymine

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. The methyl group remains at C5. Unmethylated C gives U, A gives hypoxanthine, and G can yield xanthine.

B4. Hypoxanthine in DNA can arise from: (A) guanine oxidation (B) adenine deamination (C) thymine dimerization (D) cytosine methylation.

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. The product can favor pairing with C; oxidation of G may instead yield 8-oxoG.

B5. Which is a nonradical ROS? (A) Superoxide radical (B) A hydrated electron. (C) Hydrogen peroxide (D) Hydroxyl radical

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. H₂O₂ has no unpaired electron, although it can generate radicals in metal-mediated reactions. Hydroxyl and superoxide are radicals; a hydrated electron is not a nonradical oxygen species.

B6. A characteristic substitution arising from 8-oxoG in a G:C pair is: (A) deletion of every oxidized G. (B) G:C → A:T (C) A:T → G:C exclusively (D) G:C → T:A

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. A can be inserted opposite oxidized G and subsequently direct T insertion. G:C → A:T is a characteristic possibility for O6-methylG; the remaining choices are not compulsory consequences.

B7. Which alkylated base can favor inappropriate thymine incorporation? (A) N7-methylguanine exclusively (B) O6-methylguanine (C) 5-methylcytosine in normal pairing (D) thymine glycol.

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Its altered coding surface is important; N7 methylation instead destabilizes base attachment. The other choices do not describe this lesion.

B8. Which property best distinguishes an SSB from an AP site? (A) A missing phosphodiester continuity (B) number of purines (C) presence of chromatin. (D) presence of water

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. A strand discontinuity is necessary for an SSB but not for an AP site.

B9. The usual UV CPD links: (A) two proteins. (B) A and T across the duplex by a hydrogen bond (C) two adjacent pyrimidines of one strand (D) two distant phosphate groups

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. It is a covalent same-strand lesion; ordinary base pairing and the other listed linkages are not CPDs.

B10. A 6–4 photoproduct involves: (A) removal of every C6 atom (B) two phosphodiester cuts (C) normal purine pairing. (D) a C6–C4 linkage between successive pyrimidines

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Its geometry differs from the four-membered ring of a CPD.

B11. Which unit is used for absorbed ionizing radiation dose? (A) base pairs s⁻¹ (B) Gy, J kg⁻¹ (C) mutations per genome. (D) mol L⁻¹

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Absorbed dose is energy per mass; the other units describe different quantities.

B12. Which lesion most directly prevents strand separation through a covalent connection between complementary chains? (A) interstrand crosslink (B) a simple nick. (C) isolated U:G mismatch (D) N7-methylG

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The other lesions do not inherently join the two chains covalently.

B13. A nick in a covalently closed circular DNA molecule can produce: (A) obligatory loss of all bases (B) an RNA molecule (C) an interstrand crosslink. (D) an open circular relaxed form

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Strand discontinuity releases the topological constraint. It need not change molecule length or produce the other listed outcomes.

B14. A DNA ligase generally needs which termini to join a simple nick? (A) two 3′-phosphates only (B) two 5′-OH ends only. (C) juxtaposed 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate (D) two unmodified bases with no backbone

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Other end structures may require processing before ligation.

B15. A blocking lesion in the transcribed strand of a gene may directly cause: (A) impaired RNA polymerase elongation (B) loss of every transcript. (C) obligatory chromosome doubling (D) immediate Mendelian segregation

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The effect depends on locus, lesion and enzyme; Chromosome doubling, immediate segregation and universal loss of transcripts are unjustified generalizations.

B16. Which statement about lesion bypass is correct? (A) It always removes the lesion (B) It can permit synthesis despite a persistent lesion (C) It necessarily causes a mutation (D) It is identical to photoreactivation.

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Tolerance is distinct from removal; accuracy depends on the polymerase and substrate.

B17. MutT in E. coli primarily illustrates: (A) histone phosphorylation (B) pyrimidine dimer formation. (C) severing both DNA strands (D) sanitation of oxidized nucleotide precursors

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. It acts on oxidized nucleotide substrates before incorporation rather than directly repairing a chromosomal CPD.

B18. Which is a valid example of an endogenous damage source? (A) only inherited sequence variation. (B) only laboratory UVC lamps (C) reactive products of ordinary cellular metabolism (D) only external gamma radiation

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Endogenous chemistry occurs within the organism; Laboratory UVC and external gamma radiation are exogenous examples; inherited sequence variation is not a chemical source.

B19. Which is most directly consistent with the appearance of a fixed mutation? (A) a changed canonical base pair inherited after replication (B) an increased H₂O₂ concentration alone (C) one AP site in an isolated molecule (D) a transient mismatch that is accurately repaired.

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The other choices do not establish a stable sequence change.

B20. A standard alkaline comet assay primarily responds to: (A) exclusively CPDs without modification (B) strand breaks and alkali-labile sites (C) only protein concentration. (D) only point mutations

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. The assay is not a direct sequencing method or a universal lesion-specific measurement.

Assertion–reason (B21–B25)

Use A: both statements true, reason explains assertion; B: both true, reason does not explain assertion; C: assertion true, reason false; D: assertion false, reason true.

B21. Assertion: A base can be lost without immediate strand cleavage. Reason: The base–sugar bond is distinct from the phosphodiester backbone.

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The chemical independence of these linkages explains why AP sites need not be breaks.

B22. Assertion: 5-methylcytosine deamination is challenging to recognize. Reason: The product thymine is normally present in DNA.

Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The normal chemical identity of thymine complicates discrimination of T:G compared with U:G.

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. The assertion is true in the specified organisms, but placental mammals lack the conventional photolyase pathways.

Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Stalling precedes collapse in some situations but does not require it.

Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Both are true but the definition of a transition does not explain clustered energy deposition.

19. Part C — Analytical and quantitative questions (original)

All numerical datasets below are hypothetical teaching examples, not reported experimental values.

C1. A C:G pair becomes U:G. After copying of the U-containing strand with A insertion and copying of the A-containing daughter, which pair is fixed? Identify the mutation class.

Answer and explanation

Answer: T:A; transition at the original C position. The undamaged G-containing parental strand can still yield C:G. It is incorrect to treat the first U:G intermediate as an already fixed mutation.

C2. Two guanine adducts direct insertion of A and T, respectively. Starting from G:C, predict the canonical substitutions after copying of the newly inserted nucleotide.

Answer and explanation

Answer: A insertion directs T on subsequent copying, yielding T:A, a transversion. T insertion directs A, yielding A:T, a transition. This is why 8-oxoG and O6-methylG can have different mutation signatures despite both modifying G.

C3. A purified duplex retains full native length but yields a shorter labeled strand after alkali treatment. A specific assay detects AP sites before alkali. Which interpretation is best?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Alkali can convert AP sites to breaks; the short band need not reflect a pre-existing SSB. Native length alone cannot exclude other lesions; use matched untreated and cleavage-specific controls.

C4. A plasmid sample initially contains compact supercoiled DNA. After treatment, an open circular band increases, but the linear band does not. What change is most consistent?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Nicking of one strand relaxes covalently constrained circular DNA. This is not definitive proof of exclusively SSBs, but the unchanged linear band argues against extensive conversion to linear DSB products.

C5. Two populations contain 80 lesions/genome immediately after equal treatment. After two hours X has 20 and Y 60. If background is negligible, what is the most direct interpretation?

Answer and explanation

Answer: X has faster net disappearance (75% versus 25%). Initial formation appears similar at the sampled time; repair, dilution and selection need to be distinguished before attributing the difference solely to repair.

C6. A 5 × 10⁶ bp complete duplex has 40% GC. If probability of oxidation per G is 10⁻⁶, calculate expected G lesions.

Answer and explanation

Answer: 10⁷ nucleotide residues; 40% are G+C, half are G = 2 × 10⁶. Expected count = 2. Counting all GC residues would double the estimate.

C7. Independent blocking lesions occur with mean 0.8 per template. Estimate the proportion with no lesion and at least one.

Answer and explanation

Answer: e^(−0.8) ≈ 0.4493; at least one ≈ 0.5507. The Poisson assumption fails for strongly clustered or heterogeneous events.

C8. A pulse increases lesion burden from a baseline of 10 to 170. After 3 h the total is 30. Estimate excess half-life under first-order kinetics.

Answer and explanation

Answer: Initial excess 160, final excess 20, ratio 1/8 = three half-lives. Half-life 1 h. Using total values would incorrectly ignore background.

C9. Formation rate is 60 lesions/genome/hour and disappearance constant is 0.3 h⁻¹. A mutation halves the disappearance constant without changing formation. Determine the new steady-state burden.

Answer and explanation

Answer: Initial 60/0.3 = 200; new 60/0.15 = 400 lesions/genome. Increased steady-state burden does not demonstrate increased formation.

C10. A lesion falls from 200 to 50 in 4 h. Estimate k and predict the amount after a total of 6 h.

Answer and explanation

Answer: k = ln(4)/4 ≈ 0.347 h⁻¹; half-life 2 h, so after 6 h the amount is 25. Assumes no continuing formation and no dilution.

C11. A sample absorbs 0.012 J of ionizing radiation and has mass 0.004 kg. Compute dose. Can DSBs be counted from it alone?

Answer and explanation

Answer: 3 Gy. No: a measured or assumed lesion yield is required, and energy deposition affects more than DNA.

C12. Compare photon energies at 300 nm and 400 nm.

Answer and explanation

Answer: E₃₀₀/E₄₀₀ = 400/300 = 4/3. The 300 nm photon has about 33% more energy. This does not establish 33% more lesions because absorption and quantum yield differ.

C13. A population has 500 premutagenic lesions on average. 96% are removed before copying; 25% of the remaining lesions cause an error; 40% of errors become fixed. Calculate expected fixed substitutions.

Answer and explanation

Answer: 500 × 0.04 × 0.25 × 0.40 = 2. Each fraction applies to the previous stage; adding fractions is invalid.

C14. Two strains show equal initial CPD burden. A photolyase-containing strain loses CPD signal in visible light, but a matched photolyase-deficient strain does not. Which additional groups distinguish enzyme-dependent light reversal from ordinary recovery and establish baseline?

Answer and explanation

Answer: The enzyme-deficient irradiated light group is already an important control; add the competent irradiated dark group and unirradiated baseline. A light-dependent difference restricted to enzyme competence supports photoreactivation; it does not prove every UV lesion is a CPD.

C15. After equal exposure, one sample exhibits a higher comet tail than another. Addition of a crosslinking condition lowers the tail signal despite equal break-inducing treatment. Explain.

Answer and explanation

Answer: Crosslinks can constrain DNA migration. Lower migration need not mean fewer initiating breaks. Compare the break-only group, crosslink-only group and combined group, with viability and matched preparation controls.

C16. A mutant accumulates uracil after damage, while the wild type transiently shows increased AP sites. What molecular step may differ?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Removal of uracil by a glycosylase could be impaired in the mutant; the wild-type AP sites could be processing intermediates. The data do not prove repair completion; follow AP-site disappearance and mutation outcomes.

C17. A polymerase incorporates normally on unmodified DNA but stops at a bulky lesion. A second polymerase extends across the lesion without reducing its chemically measured abundance. Which distinction is demonstrated?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Bypass/tolerance versus lesion removal. Extension across a damaged template does not chemically reverse the adduct. Sequencing of products is needed to assess accuracy.

C18. Four hypothetical lesions are either all far apart on one strand or grouped as two closely opposed pairs. Which arrangement is more likely to produce DSB intermediates during processing and why?

Answer and explanation

Answer: The opposed pairs, because adjacent processing on both strands can compromise duplex continuity. Counts alone do not capture spatial complexity. Outcome still depends on separation, processing timing and chemistry.

C19. Match the primary molecular obstacle: I AP site; II interstrand crosslink; III SSB; IV O6-methylG. Options: a misleading pairing surface; b missing base; c covalent obstruction to strand separation; d interruption of one backbone.

Answer and explanation

Answer: I–b, II–c, III–d, IV–a. This is classification by immediate structure, not by final mutation outcome.

C20. Select all correct statements: I a G:T mismatch can arise by deamination; II U:A necessarily changes sequence relative to T:A; III 8-oxoG can pair with C; IV N7-methylG can lead to an AP site.

Answer and explanation

Answer: I, III, IV. II is false because U:A may arise from precursor incorporation opposite A and transmit the same pairing information as T:A.

C21. RNA output decreases from a gene containing a single lesion on its template strand; the DNA sequence of descendants is unchanged. Does this rule out an effect of DNA damage?

Answer and explanation

Answer: No. A lesion can block RNA polymerase or alter transcription without ever being fixed as a DNA mutation. Compare lesion placement on transcribed and nontranscribed strands, and measure full-length transcripts.

C22. A checkpoint-defective cell continues division sooner after damage than wild type, but its long-term chromosome abnormalities increase. What inference is appropriate?

Answer and explanation

Answer: The checkpoint can delay progression to protect genome integrity. Faster initial growth is not evidence of better repair. Compare initial damage, cell-cycle distributions and long-term viability.

C23. A chemical increases γ-H2AX foci but the number of foci does not match an independent physical break measurement. Why is that not necessarily contradictory?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Foci reflect a signaling response over chromatin regions; they can overlap or occur during replication stress and have different temporal behavior from breaks. A one-to-one conversion requires validation.

C24. A mutT-deficient bacterium shows an increase in substitutions even when a test for one oxidized base in the starting parental DNA detects no difference. Give a plausible explanation.

Answer and explanation

Answer: Oxidized dGTP in the soluble pool can be incorporated during synthesis. The precursor lesion would not necessarily be detected in the original template. Measure precursor oxidation or test the effect of restoring MutT.

C25. A treatment reduces surviving colonies by 90% but increases mutation frequency among survivors only slightly. Can you conclude that it produces few DNA lesions?

Answer and explanation

Answer: No. Blocking or lethal lesions may prevent severely damaged cells from forming colonies; the surviving population is selected. Distinguish lesion burden, survival and mutation frequency among surviving lineages.

C26. A chemical adduct assay shows more N7-methylG than O6-methylG, yet G:C → A:T substitutions dominate. Explain why abundance alone is insufficient.

Answer and explanation

Answer: O6-methylG can directly miscode with T and yield the observed transition; N7-methylG can instead destabilize the glycosidic bond. Relative persistence, bypass probability and selection also affect mutation spectra.

C27. After equal UV exposure, a 6–4PP-specific assay declines more rapidly than a CPD-specific assay. Which of the following follows: (A) CPDs cannot be removed; (B) initial CPD yield was necessarily larger; (C) the measured net disappearance rates differ; (D) the two assays measure every lesion.

Answer and explanation

Answer: C. A and B are unjustified by rate differences, and D is false. Different lesion-specific recognition and processing can account for the observation.

C28. A DNA sample has 100 independent lesions spread over 50 equally accessible fragments. Under a Poisson model, estimate how many fragments are lesion free. Then explain why a clustered distribution could change the answer.

Answer and explanation

Answer: Mean 2 per fragment; 50e^(−2) ≈ 6.77, or about 7 expected intact fragments. A clustered, overdispersed distribution can leave more fragments without events while concentrating damage in others; independence and equal rates are assumptions.

C29. A polymerase stalls after a nick is encountered by the fork; a break marker increases only after cells enter S phase. What alternative explains delayed breaks without new exposure?

Answer and explanation

Answer: Replication-associated conversion of a pre-existing single-strand discontinuity into a one-ended break. Compare nonreplicating and replicating cells, and establish initial nick burdens. A delayed marker can also reflect signaling kinetics.

C30. Two DNA samples have equal sequence mutation frequency after recovery, but one has twice the initial chemical lesion burden. Propose two nonexclusive explanations and a test.

Answer and explanation

Answer: The higher-burden sample may repair more lesions before copying or lose more heavily damaged cells; alternatively its lesions may be less miscoding. Use early and later lesion-specific measurements, long-term survival, and matched mutation scoring. Equal final mutation frequencies cannot establish equal damage susceptibility.
