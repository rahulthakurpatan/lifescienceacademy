1. The biological problem: preserving a regulatory decision

The same inherited genome can support remarkably different cell identities. A cell in the intestinal lining produces proteins that help it absorb nutrients, whereas a muscle cell produces abundant machinery for contraction. These cells do not ordinarily acquire different sets of genes to perform these tasks. Rather, their developmental histories influence which genes are accessible and how strongly those genes are expressed. This observation raises a deeper question than the immediate regulation of transcription: how can a cell retain an expression pattern after the original developmental signal has disappeared? A newly differentiated cell might divide, and the two daughter cells might need to preserve the characteristic expression programme of their parent.

A transcription factor can bind a regulatory sequence and stimulate transcription while an activating signal is present. If the signal disappears, the factor may dissociate or become inactive. This is a satisfactory explanation of a temporary response, but by itself it does not explain why a particular expression programme persists for many generations of cells. A stable regulatory state requires some mechanism by which the molecular conditions favouring that state are reconstructed. DNA methylation is one important means of doing this in many eukaryotes. Its usefulness derives from a property of DNA itself: its complementary strands are copied during replication, and a chemical modification on an old strand can provide information about the state that existed before replication.

Epigenetics is used in more than one sense in biological literature. In this chapter, the central meaning is a stable or heritable regulatory state that does not depend on a change in the order of DNA bases. Heritability in this definition can mean transmission through mitotic cell divisions; it does not automatically imply inheritance from a parent organism to its offspring. Some researchers use epigenetic regulation more broadly for chromatin modifications even when their persistence has not been demonstrated. In an examination, it is important to distinguish the biochemical observation of a modified DNA base from proof that the modification carries regulatory information through cell division. The former can be measured in a single cell population; the latter requires observations across time and generations.

Imagine two cells carrying identical copies of a gene. Both have the same promoter sequence, coding sequence and regulatory elements. In the first cell, the promoter is accessible to an activating protein, whereas in the second cell the promoter is associated with a more restrictive chromatin environment. Transcription may consequently differ even though sequencing finds no nucleotide substitution. If the restrictive condition is maintained through successive divisions, the descendants may retain lower transcription. This kind of regulation does not violate the central dogma. The DNA sequence continues to specify the RNA sequence, but the probability that the DNA is transcribed has changed. Information about which instructions are used is therefore distinct from the instructions themselves.

It is tempting to treat epigenetic state as an additional permanent genetic code. That analogy becomes misleading when it suggests that every modification has a fixed meaning independent of its location and molecular surroundings. A methylated cytosine in a promoter and a methylated cytosine inside a transcribed gene may be associated with very different outcomes. The significance of the modification depends on which proteins recognise the local DNA, whether those proteins tolerate methylation, the position of the modification relative to regulatory sequences and the surrounding chromatin. The chapter therefore develops a causal model rather than a universal rule that methylation switches every gene off.

Stable regulation must also be compatible with change. During development, cells encounter new signals and sometimes need to establish a different expression programme. A regulatory mechanism that can never be reversed would restrict developmental flexibility. DNA methylation can be established, copied and removed by distinct mechanisms. These activities allow persistent expression differences without making those differences equivalent to irreversible DNA mutations. The balance between stability and reversibility is the central biological problem that the following sections address.

2. Chemical identity and sequence context

A DNA nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of the four familiar bases. Cytosine is a pyrimidine base that normally forms Watson–Crick hydrogen bonds with guanine in double-stranded DNA. In the common eukaryotic DNA methylation reaction, a methyl group is attached to carbon number five of the cytosine ring. The product is 5-methylcytosine, abbreviated 5mC. This numbering refers to the base ring, not to the fifth carbon of deoxyribose. The modified nucleotide remains part of the DNA polymer; the phosphate backbone and the ordinary order of bases are not replaced.

The chemical addition of a methyl group changes the surface presented by DNA to proteins. At carbon five, the group projects into the major groove of a conventional DNA double helix, where many sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins examine the chemical features of bases. The Watson–Crick pairing edge is still capable of interacting with guanine. Consequently, a DNA polymerase normally copies a methylated cytosine by incorporating guanine, rather than treating it as thymine. Sequence preservation and modification-dependent recognition can thus coexist. A protein with an appropriately shaped DNA-binding surface may distinguish a methylated cytosine from an unmodified one even though both residues direct incorporation of guanine during normal DNA synthesis.

The methyl group is supplied by S-adenosylmethionine, often abbreviated SAM. SAM is derived from methionine and ATP in a separate metabolic reaction. A DNA methyltransferase catalyses transfer of the methyl group to cytosine, producing S-adenosylhomocysteine, abbreviated SAH, as the donor is consumed. The methylation reaction is not the same as the incorporation of methylated nucleotides by the replication machinery, and the methyltransferase does not need to hydrolyse a molecule of ATP directly for every methyl group that it transfers. Formation and turnover of the donor belong to cellular metabolism, whereas selection and modification of cytosines depend on the enzyme and its DNA substrate.

At a simplified catalytic level, cytosine C5 methyltransferases expose the target cytosine to their catalytic pocket by flipping the base out of the DNA helix. A conserved catalytic cysteine can form a transient covalent bond at carbon six of the cytosine, facilitating methyl transfer to carbon five from SAM. Subsequent elimination restores the aromatic character of the pyrimidine and releases the methylated base. This mechanism explains why the enzyme needs access to a specific nucleotide rather than merely attaching methyl groups indiscriminately to the external DNA surface. Base flipping temporarily changes local base stacking, but does not require permanent separation of the complete DNA duplex.

SAH can inhibit methyltransferase reactions because it resembles the donor after methyl transfer. A change in the availability of SAM or the accumulation of SAH can therefore influence methylation chemistry, but the concentration of a donor alone does not determine the pattern of methylation in a genome. A cell also needs the appropriate enzyme, its recruitment machinery and access to the target. Two loci in the same nucleus experience broadly the same pool of methyl donor, yet one may remain unmethylated while another acquires methylation. This difference illustrates the distinction between the metabolic capacity to perform a chemical reaction and the regulatory specificity that determines where the reaction takes place.

In mammalian DNA, a common target is a cytosine immediately followed by guanine on the same strand in the five-prime to three-prime direction. This dinucleotide is written CpG, where p represents the phosphodiester linkage. It is not shorthand for a cytosine base paired with guanine across the double helix. In a duplex containing a CpG, the complementary strand also contains a CpG when it is read in its own five-prime to three-prime direction. The two cytosines are offset by one base pair. They form a symmetrical recognition pattern called a CpG dyad. Either cytosine can be methylated, and the combination of their modification states provides the substrate information used in the maintenance process.

A CpG dyad can be unmethylated on both strands, methylated on both strands, or methylated on only one strand. The last condition is called hemimethylation. The term does not mean that the methyl group has been chemically divided into halves; it means that one of two corresponding cytosines in a duplex carries the group. It also does not mean that half of the cells in a sample are methylated. Hemimethylation describes the state of one double-stranded DNA molecule at a particular symmetrical target. A bulk measurement reporting fifty percent methylated cytosines does not reveal whether all molecules are hemimethylated or whether some are fully methylated and others entirely unmethylated.

CpG is not a universal sequence requirement for DNA methylation. In plants, methylated cytosines occur in CG, CHG and CHH contexts, where H stands for A, C or T. CG and CHG can form symmetrical contexts on complementary strands; CHH is generally asymmetric and therefore cannot be copied by the same simple symmetrical recognition rule. Mammalian cells also exhibit non-CpG methylation in particular biological contexts, including some stem cells and neurons. These observations are important because a model that successfully explains maintenance of mammalian CpG methylation should not be presented as the mechanism for all modified bases in all organisms.

Cytosine methylation has an additional relationship with genetic change. Spontaneous deamination of ordinary cytosine creates uracil, which is readily recognised as unusual in DNA. Deamination of 5mC creates thymine, a normal DNA base that is now incorrectly paired with guanine. Repair systems can recognise the resulting T:G mismatch, but an unrepaired lesion can become a C-to-T transition after replication. This is a genuine sequence mutation, unlike the original methylation event. Over evolutionary time, this mutational tendency contributes to depletion of CpG dinucleotides in many methylated genomic regions. The distinction between a reversible chemical mark and a mutation arising from chemical damage is particularly useful in questions about sequence versus epigenetic information.

3. How the maintenance hypothesis became a molecular model

The first task in understanding the history of DNA methylation is to separate discovery of the chemical constituent from discovery of its regulatory function. Chemical analysis of DNA established the presence of modified cytosines long before the mechanism of epigenetic inheritance was fully understood. Early surveys of DNA from different organisms showed that the abundance of modified bases varies, indicating that modification is not simply an unavoidable consequence of building a nucleic acid polymer. Such observations raised the possibility of an enzyme-controlled pattern, but they could not identify the particular genes bearing modifications or establish whether methylation affected transcription.

In 1975, Robin Holliday and John Pugh, and independently Arthur Riggs, proposed models in which DNA modification could help maintain cell states during development. Their proposals linked the symmetry of certain target sequences with semiconservative DNA replication. If an enzyme preferentially restored the chemical mark on newly synthesised DNA opposite an old modified target, a local pattern could survive many replication cycles. At that time this was a mechanistic hypothesis, not a complete experimentally established account of mammalian methylation enzymes. The important intellectual advance was to identify how the information needed for maintenance could remain physically associated with the genetic material.

An early way of testing methylation-dependent regulation was to compare DNA fragments that differed in modification but not in nucleotide sequence. If the methylated and unmethylated versions produced different amounts of RNA in otherwise comparable conditions, the sequence itself could not explain the difference. Such an experiment still required careful controls because an enzymatic treatment of DNA might change its integrity or affect its delivery into cells. The general experimental strategy was nevertheless powerful: manipulate the chemical state of a defined sequence while holding the sequence constant, then measure its regulatory consequence.

Another major advance came from genetics of the methyltransferases themselves. Distinct enzymes were implicated in restoring methylation after replication and in establishing methylation at previously unmodified sites. The 1999 work of Okano and colleagues demonstrated the importance of Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b for new methylation patterns and mammalian development. This result provided direct genetic support for the idea that establishment and inheritance can be separable biochemical responsibilities. It did not show that all de novo methylation at every locus depends on identical targeting signals, nor did it establish that the maintenance enzyme has absolutely no activity on unmethylated DNA.

The maintenance model also required an explanation for the preferential selection of hemimethylated targets within a very large genome. In 2007, investigations of UHRF1, also called Np95 in mice, showed its importance in maintaining genomic methylation and recruiting DNMT1. These experiments linked recognition of an old modified strand to the enzyme that restores methylation on newly synthesised DNA. The conceptual advance was the identification of a molecular reader and recruitment system, rather than an assumption that the methyltransferase somehow possesses an independent record of the entire genome.

A further discovery complicated the original image of a binary methylated or unmethylated state. In 2009, Tahiliani and colleagues demonstrated that TET1 could oxidise 5mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine. This finding provided a biochemical route by which an existing methylation pattern could be altered. It also explained why methods that classify every bisulfite-resistant cytosine as 5mC can be misleading in tissues with appreciable hydroxymethylation. Later work connected additional oxidation products to DNA repair and demethylation. The original maintenance hypothesis remained valuable, but it had to be incorporated into a dynamic model in which chemical marks are both copied and transformed.

The historical sequence reveals how several different kinds of scientific evidence answer different questions. Chemical detection establishes that a modified base exists. Comparative mapping establishes its distribution. Genetic loss-of-function experiments identify proteins needed for a process. Biochemical assays determine which chemical reaction a purified enzyme can catalyse. Finally, controlled transcription measurements reveal whether a modification affects expression in a specific context. No one of these experiments is sufficient to prove every part of an epigenetic mechanism. In a Part C problem, identifying the precise question addressed by an experiment is often more valuable than recalling the year in which it was performed.

A mammalian genome contains many CpG sites, but their methylation states are not uniformly distributed. Regions rich in repetitive sequences often carry substantial methylation, while many CpG-rich promoter regions remain relatively unmethylated. A promoter is a DNA region involved in establishing transcription initiation. Its sequence can recruit transcription factors and the transcription machinery either directly or through associated proteins. If a change in methylation interferes with these interactions, it can change the probability that transcription begins. The same chemical change in a region far from the transcription start site may have a different consequence.

A CpG island is a region with an unusually high density of CpG dinucleotides and generally high GC content compared with surrounding mammalian genomic DNA. Computational definitions use thresholds for length, GC content and the ratio of observed to expected CpG frequency. These definitions describe sequence composition, not the methylation state. Thus a CpG island can be methylated or unmethylated; the sequence remains an island after a change in its chemical modification. Many CpG islands overlap promoters and are normally unmethylated, including some promoters of genes that are not being transcribed in a particular cell type.

This last observation is important for understanding the relationship between accessibility and expression. An unmethylated promoter may be permissive for transcription but still lack a required activator. For example, a developmentally regulated gene might have an unmethylated promoter in a cell where its tissue-specific transcription factor is absent. Removal of methylation alone would not necessarily activate the gene. Conversely, a methylated promoter is frequently associated with repression, but an individual methylated site outside critical recognition sequences might have little measurable influence. A mechanistic interpretation requires information about the regulatory elements, transcription factors and chromatin state rather than methylation percentage alone.

Gene-body methylation refers to methylation within a transcribed region rather than specifically at its main promoter. In many mammalian contexts, methylation of gene bodies is compatible with active transcription. It may contribute to regulation of internal promoters or be linked to chromatin states deposited during transcription. Its presence is therefore not equivalent to silencing of the whole gene. This is one reason why a genome-wide average of methylation cannot be converted directly into a genome-wide average of gene expression. A meaningful analysis considers the location of the modification relative to the functional elements of the gene.

Enhancers can also exhibit changes in methylation associated with regulatory activity. An enhancer is a DNA element that influences transcription from a promoter, often from a distance along the chromosome. Binding of transcription factors can protect some enhancer regions from methylation or facilitate the establishment of a less methylated state. In the opposite direction, methylation of certain recognition sequences may reduce factor binding. A correlation between enhancer activity and low methylation can therefore arise through different causal routes. A single comparison between an active enhancer and an inactive enhancer is insufficient to decide whether methylation drove the activity change or followed it.

Repetitive sequences and mobile genetic elements pose another regulatory problem. Uncontrolled transcription of these elements may interfere with nearby genes or permit mobilisation of an active transposon. Methylation and repressive chromatin can contribute to their restriction in mammals. Not all repetitive DNA is identical, and different classes of elements can rely on different combinations of regulatory mechanisms. Nevertheless, their regulation illustrates why a methylation system affects the stability of the genome as well as expression of individual genes. Loss of a genome-wide maintenance enzyme can consequently have indirect effects on expression through changes in genome organisation and cellular stress.

The density and location of methylated sites are distinct variables. A short promoter containing only one methylated site could be strongly affected if that site is essential for recognition by a methylation-sensitive activator. Another promoter might contain several methylated sites with little immediate influence if none interferes with critical interactions. Conversely, a region containing many methylated CpGs may support recruitment of multiple methyl-binding proteins and help stabilise a restrictive chromatin environment. This reasoning explains why both single-site experiments and regional measurements have value, and why neither automatically predicts the result at the other scale.

5. Establishing a new pattern: de novo methylation

A newly established epigenetic state cannot arise solely by copying an existing mark if the target originally lacked that mark. De novo methylation describes the introduction of methylation at previously unmethylated cytosines. In mammals, DNMT3A and DNMT3B are important enzymes for this activity. They catalyse the same fundamental C5 methyl transfer as the maintenance enzyme DNMT1, but differ in their substrate preferences, regulatory interactions and biological responsibilities. The designation de novo therefore describes the establishment task, not a different kind of methyl group.

Establishment must be selective to produce useful regulation. If every accessible cytosine were modified at random, methylation could disrupt regulatory elements needed for normal cell functions. Recruitment of DNMT3 enzymes is influenced by surrounding chromatin and by interactions with regulatory proteins. The structure of a DNA locus also affects whether the enzyme can reach a target cytosine. During differentiation, changes in transcription factor occupancy and histone modifications can help create conditions that favour methylation at some regions while protecting others. Methylation can subsequently reinforce a regulatory condition that was initially created through another mechanism.

A simple two-step model helps clarify this relationship. Suppose a developmental signal reduces binding of an activator at a promoter. The decrease in activator occupancy can reduce transcription before any detectable increase in methylation. Chromatin associated with that promoter may then become more favourable for recruitment of de novo methyltransferases. Once methylation has accumulated, it can oppose return of the activating factor or recruit proteins that favour repression. If the signal is later removed, the promoter may remain less active because the molecular environment has changed. In this model, methylation is initially a consequence of repression and later contributes to its persistence.

This temporal order is not the only possible route. At another locus, recruitment of a methyltransferase might precede loss of an activating protein, so the initial modification could contribute directly to repression. To distinguish these alternatives experimentally, a researcher needs measurements at several times after the initiating signal, ideally combined with a perturbation of methyltransferase activity. If repression begins normally without de novo methylation but recovery becomes easier when the signal is removed, the enzyme may be particularly important for stabilisation rather than initiation. If repression is strongly impaired from the first time point when methylation cannot occur, a role in the initiating transition becomes more plausible.

The structural organisation of DNA methyltransferases helps explain how catalytic and targeting functions can be separated. Mammalian DNMT proteins contain a conserved catalytic region that binds the methyl donor and presents the target cytosine to the active site. DNMT1 also has regulatory regions involved in substrate selection and recruitment to chromatin. Its replication foci targeting sequence, usually abbreviated RFTS, participates in regulation of activity and targeting. DNMT3 proteins possess regulatory modules, including ADD and PWWP domains, that help couple methylation to chromatin context. For examination purposes, the important distinction is between the region that performs methyl transfer and regions that control access, localisation or activity. A mutation outside the catalytic site may still strongly affect methylation in a cell because the enzyme must arrive at an appropriate substrate in a regulated state.

The activity of DNMT3A and DNMT3B should not be equated with automatic promoter methylation. Their effects depend on the cellular context, the available substrates and the regulatory mechanisms that guide them. DNMT3L, a related mammalian protein that lacks conventional DNA methyltransferase catalytic activity, can support de novo methylation in particular developmental contexts. This is a useful illustration of how a noncatalytic partner can be important to an enzyme-mediated reaction. A mutation that disrupts a recruitment interaction may reduce methylation at particular locations even if the catalytic centre itself remains intact.

At the examination level, a distinction between catalytic failure and targeting failure is essential. A catalytic-site mutation can leave a protein able to associate with chromatin while preventing methyl transfer. A recruitment-defective variant might retain activity against purified naked DNA but fail to restore methylation at its normal cellular targets. Measuring only total protein abundance would not distinguish these possibilities. An enzyme assay with a defined substrate and a separate test of localisation or genomic methylation can help identify whether the problem lies in chemical catalysis or in access to the substrate. Such experiments turn a general statement about a regulatory enzyme into a specific and testable mechanism.

Semiconservative replication produces two daughter duplexes from one parental duplex. Each daughter contains one strand that existed before replication and one newly synthesised complementary strand. Consider a fully methylated CpG dyad. Before replication, both of its cytosines carry a methyl group. Immediately after copying, the original methylated cytosines remain on their respective old strands, but the corresponding cytosines on the new strands initially lack the modification. Each daughter molecule therefore contains a hemimethylated target. This intermediate state is the physical connection between the old methylation pattern and its restoration.

DNMT1 is the principal mammalian maintenance DNA methyltransferase. It preferentially acts on hemimethylated CpG substrates, although the distinction between maintenance and de novo activities is not absolute. If DNMT1 methylates the new cytosine at a hemimethylated dyad, the symmetrical methylation pattern is restored. A parental dyad that was unmethylated does not present the same recognition signal. In this way an enzyme can help reproduce a regional pattern of methylated and unmethylated sites without reading a stored map elsewhere in the cell.

UHRF1 contributes to this selectivity. Its SRA domain recognises hemimethylated DNA, including the modified cytosine exposed during recognition. UHRF1 also participates in interactions with chromatin and in recruitment of DNMT1. The result is a connection between the inherited chemical mark and the enzyme responsible for copying it onto the new strand. A failure to recognise hemimethylated substrates can therefore impair maintenance even when DNMT1 protein is present. The distinction matters when interpreting a mutation in a methylation-associated protein: a regulatory defect can resemble a defect in the catalytic enzyme at the level of genomic methylation.

Maintenance is associated with DNA replication, but the completion of methylation does not have to occur simultaneously with incorporation of each individual nucleotide by DNA polymerase. A period of hemimethylation can exist after passage of a replication fork. The rate of restoration varies according to genomic context and the availability of the maintenance machinery. A sample taken immediately after DNA synthesis can consequently show more hemimethylated sites than a sample collected after sufficient time for maintenance. A temporal difference of this kind is not in itself evidence of permanent demethylation.

The maintenance mechanism is also not identical to ordinary sequence copying. DNA polymerase selects a nucleotide according to complementary base pairing. DNMT1 recognises a particular chemical condition in an already synthesised duplex and performs a separate covalent modification. A newly synthesised strand can have a completely correct nucleotide sequence while lacking the methylation pattern that characterised its parent. A cell could therefore have normal replication fidelity but abnormal epigenetic maintenance. This prediction is useful for designing experiments that measure sequence integrity separately from the methylation state.

To understand the effect of complete maintenance failure, start with a fully methylated dyad and assume that no de novo methylation, active removal or cell selection occurs. Following one division, each daughter duplex retains one original modified cytosine. Following a second division, only two of the four descendant duplexes retain an original marked cytosine; the other two are unmethylated. The number of marked residues in the entire descendant population remains constant while the number of available cytosines doubles at each round of replication. The proportion of methylated cytosines therefore decreases even though no enzyme has chemically removed a single methyl group. This is the basis of passive dilution.

A cell that does not divide presents a useful contrast. If its methylation marks are chemically stable and no active demethylation takes place, disabling the replication-associated copying process will not immediately remove existing methyl groups. The absence of the copying enzyme is relevant when new DNA is produced and new methylatable cytosines become available. A slowly dividing cell population may consequently lose methylation more slowly in chronological time than a rapidly dividing population with the same per-division maintenance defect. Comparing samples only by hours after treatment without measuring proliferation can lead to an incorrect conclusion about enzyme activity.

The methylation pattern is a statistical property when examined across a population. Even in normal cells, copying may not be perfectly faithful at every site, and local establishment or removal can modify the outcome. The stability of a regulatory state can depend on redundant interactions among DNA methylation, histone marks and regulatory proteins. Thus the simple maintenance mechanism explains the basic possibility of mitotic epigenetic transmission, whereas the persistence of an entire cell identity depends on a broader network of regulatory processes.

7. Quantitative reasoning about maintenance fidelity

Passive dilution can be expressed mathematically without treating the biological system as more precise than it really is. Define mₙ as the fraction of cytosine residues at a specified collection of CpG dyads that carry 5mC after n completed cell generations. The denominator counts cytosines on both strands, not merely the number of double-stranded sites. This definition matters because a fully methylated dyad contributes two marked cytosines, a hemimethylated dyad contributes one, and an unmethylated dyad contributes none. The fraction is dimensionless and is restricted to values between zero and one. Multiplying by one hundred expresses it as a percentage.

Worked example 1 — Complete passive dilution

In an ideal population with no restoration, no new methylation and no removal, the number of marked residues remains unchanged when the DNA is copied, but the total number of target cytosines doubles. Thus mₙ₊₁ equals mₙ divided by two. Repeated application gives mₙ = m₀(1/2)ⁿ. The exponent is a number of replication cycles, not the number of hours since an inhibitor was administered. For an initially fully methylated collection, the values after one, two, three and four divisions are 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and 0.0625. This model describes dilution through increase in denominator rather than degradation of the numerator.

Worked example 2 — Dyad fraction versus cytosine fraction

The apparent reduction in a population should not be confused with the fate of one original molecule. The original marks remain on particular inherited strands, and the distribution of marked molecules becomes uneven among descendants. After three rounds of replication of one initially fully methylated dyad without restoration, there are eight duplexes, two of which each contain one original methylated cytosine. The fraction of duplexes bearing at least one mark is therefore two out of eight, or twenty-five percent. The fraction of marked cytosines is two out of sixteen, or twelve and a half percent. The two fractions are not interchangeable, although both describe the same biological situation.

Now introduce a parameter p, the probability that a methylated old cytosine is accompanied by a successfully methylated corresponding new cytosine by the end of the maintenance interval. If there were M marked old cytosines, the expected number of additional marks created by maintenance is pM. The total expected number of marks after replication and restoration is M plus pM. The total number of candidate cytosines is twice its previous value. Dividing numerator and denominator gives mₙ₊₁ = mₙ(1+p)/2. Assuming that p is constant from generation to generation, iteration produces mₙ = m₀[(1+p)/2]ⁿ. Perfect restoration gives p equal to one and no net reduction. Complete failure gives p equal to zero and recovers the passive dilution equation.

Worked example 3 — Partial maintenance over five divisions

Consider a culture with an initial methylation fraction of eighty percent at a target region. A hypomorphic enzyme restores a new mark opposite ninety percent of old marks per cycle. The per-generation retention factor is (1+0.90)/2 = 0.95. After five divisions, the expected fraction is 0.80 × 0.95⁵, approximately 0.619. This means that about sixty-two percent of the measured cytosines remain methylated, not ninety percent. Even a relatively high per-cycle restoration probability can allow appreciable erosion when a mark is copied repeatedly without corrective establishment.

Worked example 4 — Inferring maintenance probability

The inverse problem is equally useful. Suppose an initially fully methylated region exhibits a measured fraction of sixty-four percent after two divisions, and all assumptions of the simple model are valid. The retention factor r satisfies r² = 0.64, so r = 0.8, taking the nonnegative root because r is a fraction. Since r = (1+p)/2, p = 2r−1 = 0.6. The inferred maintenance success probability is sixty percent, not eighty percent. Eighty percent represents the overall fraction of pre-replication methylation retained in the new denominator; sixty percent represents successful marking of the corresponding new target given an inherited mark.

It is possible to extend the elementary model to include new establishment. Suppose passive replication and maintenance first give a fraction rm, with r = (1+p)/2. Let q be the probability that a currently unmarked cytosine becomes methylated by a separate establishment process during the remainder of the interval. The fraction still unmarked is 1−rm, and the expected newly marked fraction is q(1−rm). The next generation therefore has m' = rm + q(1−rm) = q + (1−q)rm. This is a phenomenological model: its separate stages and constant probabilities are assumptions, not a claim that all DNMT3 activities always take place after DNMT1 has finished.

Worked example 5 — Steady state with new establishment

At a steady state, the value of m does not change between generations. Setting m' equal to m and rearranging gives m* = q/[1−(1−q)r], provided the denominator is positive. If r is 0.9 and q is 0.1, the denominator is 1−0.81 = 0.19 and the steady-state fraction is about 0.526. Starting above that value, the model predicts gradual reduction toward it; starting below that value, the model predicts accumulation toward it. The equilibrium does not imply that individual cytosines never change state. It is a balance between generation-dependent loss and addition of marks.

These calculations are appropriate only when the measured fractions and biological assumptions match. A mixed tissue may contain several cell populations with different division rates and different methylation profiles. If a low-methylation population grows faster, bulk methylation could decrease even with perfectly normal maintenance within each individual cell type. Active demethylation and cell death can change the simple predictions, as can compensatory DNMT3 activity. A measured fraction alone therefore cannot identify a unique maintenance probability unless alternative contributions are controlled. When an examination provides an idealised model explicitly, use it; when it provides a real biological dataset, identify which assumptions need testing.

One useful graph places the number of completed divisions on the horizontal axis and the methylated fraction on the vertical axis. Perfect maintenance appears as a horizontal line under the ideal model. Partial maintenance produces a gradual decline, while absent maintenance produces a steep decline approaching zero. On a logarithmic vertical axis, a simple exponential dilution model becomes a straight line with slope ln(r) per generation. A change in slope after a perturbation can suggest a change in effective retention, but it does not uniquely identify the enzyme affected. Comparing replication counts, de novo activity and demethylation would be necessary to give that slope a molecular interpretation.

8. How methylated DNA influences transcription factor recognition

Transcription factors recognise a combination of DNA sequence, local shape and chemical features. The major groove of double-stranded DNA displays characteristic arrangements of hydrogen-bond donors, acceptors and hydrophobic groups. Methylation adds a hydrophobic group at a defined position and may alter local DNA shape or flexibility. A protein that previously fitted an unmethylated recognition site may now bind less strongly if its interface cannot accommodate the methyl group. The reduction is not because the base has stopped pairing with guanine, but because the molecular surface used for recognition has changed.

A methylation-sensitive transcription factor provides one direct route from 5mC to changed gene expression. If a transcriptional activator normally binds a site containing a CpG, methylation of a critical cytosine might reduce its binding. Lower occupancy of the activator can reduce recruitment or activation of the transcription machinery at the promoter. The resulting decrease in initiation reduces nascent RNA production. Over time, depending on RNA stability, the mature mRNA abundance can also decline. The sequence of events is thus chemical modification, altered binding, altered transcription and subsequently changed RNA or protein output. Each link can be tested separately.

Not all transcription factors have the same sensitivity. Some bind equally well to methylated and unmethylated target sequences, and others have greater affinity for particular methylated sequences. This is possible because the surfaces of DNA-binding domains differ. If the methyl group fits a hydrophobic pocket or contributes favourable contact with a protein, binding need not be inhibited. Also, the effect of methylation may depend on which CpG in a recognition motif is modified. A useful generalisation is therefore that methylation can alter protein recognition in a sequence- and protein-dependent manner, rather than that it universally prevents binding.

The regulatory sign of the affected factor also matters. If a methylation-sensitive factor normally activates transcription, its loss may lower transcription. If it normally represses transcription at that locus, reduced occupancy may instead increase transcription. Thus the change in output cannot be inferred from the change in binding affinity without identifying the role of the factor. This connects the present chapter to the general regulatory framework of positive and negative control. A chemical mark does not have a regulatory sign independent of the molecular interactions through which its effects are mediated.

A simple equilibrium model illustrates the effect of affinity. Let F denote free concentration of a transcription factor and Kd its equilibrium dissociation constant for one DNA site, both expressed in the same concentration units. Under a one-site equilibrium model with constant free factor concentration and no competing binding states, the occupied fraction is θ = F/(F+Kd). A lower Kd indicates stronger affinity because less free protein is required to occupy half of the sites. The model does not include nucleosomes, cooperative interactions or changes in protein availability, but it isolates one mechanism through which methylation can change occupancy.

Worked example 6 — Affinity and occupancy

Suppose free factor concentration is ten nanomolar. The unmethylated site has a Kd of two nanomolar, while the methylated version has a Kd of forty nanomolar. Occupancy of the unmethylated site is 10/(10+2) = 0.833, and occupancy of the methylated site is 10/(10+40) = 0.2. The same free protein concentration therefore produces very different binding probabilities. If this factor is a required activator, a reduction in transcription is plausible. The magnitude of transcriptional reduction cannot be calculated from occupancy alone unless the relationship between occupancy and initiation rate is specified.

Worked example 7 — Saturation reduces occupancy differences

At a much higher free factor concentration, say one thousand nanomolar, both versions of the site would be nearly saturated in the same model. Their occupancies would be approximately 0.998 and 0.962. Methylation still changes the dissociation constant by twenty-fold, but its influence on equilibrium occupancy is much smaller under saturating conditions. This demonstrates why a biochemical binding difference may have a large physiological effect in one cellular context and a weak effect in another. It also predicts that a factor overexpression experiment may partly overcome an affinity defect without actually changing the methylation state.

An electrophoretic mobility shift experiment can test direct binding differences using equal amounts of purified factor and defined DNA fragments. A DNA–protein complex migrates differently from free DNA in an appropriate native gel. If otherwise identical methylated and unmethylated fragments show different fractions of complex formation across a concentration series, this supports an intrinsic difference in affinity under the assay conditions. Controls should confirm equal DNA loading, the sequence specificity of binding and the absence of contaminating proteins that recognise methylated DNA. A single band shift at one protein concentration is less informative than a series because saturation or insufficient protein can hide the difference.

Direct recognition is only one mechanism. If a purified activator binds methylated and unmethylated DNA equally well, yet the methylated promoter is less active in living cells, another explanation must be considered. The modified site may recruit a different protein, favour a different chromatin state or alter an interaction not represented in a naked DNA experiment. The discrepancy is informative rather than contradictory. It narrows the set of possible mechanisms and directs attention toward the cellular context in which the regulatory effect occurs.

9. Methyl-binding proteins and indirect repression

A methylated DNA site can influence transcription even when it does not overlap a recognition site for an activating factor. One reason is that cells contain proteins that preferentially associate with methylated DNA. These proteins are often described as methylation readers because they convert the chemical state of the DNA into a molecular interaction. A reader does not need to catalyse methyl transfer, and it does not necessarily remove the methyl group. Its primary role in this simplified framework is recognition and recruitment of other molecular activities.

The methyl-CpG-binding domain is a structural module found in several mammalian proteins. Proteins such as MeCP2 and MBD2 provide standard examples of methylation-dependent DNA recognition. Their binding domains can recognise methylated sequences, while other parts of the proteins participate in interactions with additional proteins. Not every member of the broader MBD protein family has identical DNA-binding preferences or regulatory functions. For instance, some family members participate in repair-related activities or complexes without acting as a simple methyl-CpG-dependent repressor. The family name is not sufficient evidence that each protein behaves in the same manner at every locus.

A representative mechanism begins when a methyl-binding protein associates with methylated DNA near a promoter. Through protein–protein interactions it can help recruit corepressor complexes, including histone deacetylase activity in some contexts. Histone deacetylases remove acetyl groups from histone lysine residues, thereby altering chromatin interactions and reducing signals recognised by acetylation-dependent proteins. The resulting chromatin environment can become less favourable for transcription factor occupancy or recruitment of the transcription machinery. This provides an indirect mechanism through which a DNA chemical modification influences histone state and gene expression.

The direct and indirect pathways make different predictions. In direct inhibition, a purified activator may bind poorly to methylated DNA even when no reader protein is present. In an indirect pathway, the same activator may bind equally well to the two DNA substrates in isolation. The reduction of transcription emerges when the methylation reader or its associated activities are available. A combination of purified binding assays and cellular perturbations can distinguish these possibilities. Both pathways may operate together at the same promoter, so detecting direct inhibition does not automatically rule out a contribution from chromatin-mediated repression.

Consider a cell lacking one methylation reader. If that protein is needed to recruit a corepressor, its loss may increase expression without reducing the measured methylation fraction at the corresponding DNA. This outcome separates the existence of the mark from its interpretation. A mutation in a writer might lower methylation itself, whereas a mutation in a reader can impair regulatory output despite retention of the chemical signal. A rescue experiment with a normal reader protein should restore the relevant function if the defect is specifically attributable to reader loss. A variant that binds DNA but cannot interact with the corepressor would help identify which domain is necessary for the transcriptional response.

Such an experiment must not assume that the reader acts only at one gene. A protein that binds many methylated loci may affect multiple regulatory programmes, and a chronic knockout may change cell differentiation or survival. Increased transcription at a target after reader loss could be direct, or it could arise because another regulatory protein is now expressed. A shorter perturbation interval, measurement of local binding, and comparison of primary transcriptional responses can strengthen causal interpretation. No single change in mature mRNA level is sufficient to identify the full pathway.

The functional domains of methylation readers likewise explain why different mutations need not have the same phenotype. A DNA recognition domain provides selective association with particular modified substrates, whereas a separate interaction region can connect the reader to a chromatin regulatory complex. A reader that has lost its recognition domain may fail to occupy the methylated locus. A reader that retains recognition but lacks a necessary interaction region may still occupy the site without producing the usual transcriptional response. If a chromatin-binding assay shows normal local occupancy while expression changes, the defect could therefore lie downstream of recognition. This domain-level reasoning avoids treating the concentration of a protein as an adequate measure of its regulatory function.

The word reader is also used for UHRF1, but its role should not be confused with those of transcription-associated methyl-binding proteins. UHRF1 recognises a hemimethylated substrate to facilitate maintenance by DNMT1. A transcriptional reader can recognise methylated DNA and influence expression through recruited regulators. These two functions may both depend on chemical recognition, yet their immediate biological outcomes are different: one helps copy a pattern and the other helps interpret a pattern. In examination questions, the specific function of a protein matters more than a broad functional label.

10. Cooperation between DNA methylation and chromatin

Chromosomal DNA is not present as an unrestricted naked polymer. In eukaryotic nuclei it is associated with histones and many additional proteins. Nucleosomes influence the accessibility of regulatory DNA, and modifications of histones can change protein recruitment or interactions within chromatin. DNA methylation must therefore be understood within a larger regulatory environment. A cytosine methylation pattern can affect chromatin, while chromatin can influence the establishment and maintenance of that same pattern.

Histone acetylation is commonly associated with accessible regulatory regions. Acetylation of lysine neutralises its positive charge and can change local interactions, as well as provide recognition sites for bromodomain-containing proteins. Removal of acetyl groups can reduce such recognition and favour different chromatin interactions. If a methyl-binding protein recruits histone deacetylase activity, DNA methylation may therefore contribute to reduction of histone acetylation near a regulatory region. It would be too simplistic, however, to equate deacetylation with a fixed degree of physical compaction at every locus. Accessibility emerges from the combined behaviour of nucleosomes, transcription factors, remodelers and other proteins.

Histone methylation involves a distinct chemical target. Methyl groups attached to histone amino acid side chains do not constitute methylation of the DNA sequence. Their interpretation depends on the residue and the methylation state. For example, H3K4 trimethylation is often enriched at active promoters, whereas H3K9 trimethylation is associated with many repressive heterochromatic regions. H3K36 trimethylation is commonly associated with transcribed gene bodies. It follows that neither DNA methylation nor histone methylation can be interpreted by the presence of the word methylation alone. The molecular substrate, genomic location and associated recognition proteins must be identified.

An example of communication from histones to DNA comes from the relationship between H3K4 methylation and the recruitment or activation of de novo DNA methylation machinery. The regulatory domains of DNMT3 proteins can respond to histone tail states, and unmethylated H3K4 can be more compatible with certain DNMT3 regulatory interactions than methylated H3K4. This helps explain why an active CpG-rich promoter enriched in H3K4 methylation may remain protected from de novo methylation. It is a contextual mechanism rather than a rule that H3K4 methylation alone absolutely prevents all methylation of every neighbouring cytosine.

Similarly, transcription-associated chromatin states can contribute to distribution of DNA methylation within gene bodies. The existence of both active transcription and DNA methylation in one genomic region is not paradoxical if the mark is outside the promoter and its establishment is linked to histone states characteristic of transcribed chromatin. The student should therefore avoid treating the whole gene as a single uniformly regulated object. A gene has a promoter, transcribed sequences, possible internal start sites, and regulatory elements at different positions. The functional meaning of a methylation event can differ among those components.

Feedback can arise when methylated DNA recruits proteins that favour a repressive chromatin condition and that condition, in turn, supports recruitment or maintenance of DNA methylation. This creates a possible self-reinforcing state. A modest initiating change in one component can become stabilised because other components subsequently contribute to the same regulatory tendency. Such a feedback loop may explain why removal of the original signal is not sufficient to restore the initial transcription rate. Nevertheless, the existence of reciprocal molecular interactions does not prove that every locus exhibits a sharp binary switch or mathematical bistability. Demonstrating two stable states requires additional dynamic evidence.

A useful experiment distinguishes the role of a mark in establishment from its role in maintenance by perturbing the associated activities at different times. If a histone-modifying enzyme is inhibited before a promoter becomes methylated, the resulting failure to accumulate DNA methylation may reveal a contribution to establishment. If the same intervention occurs after a methylated state has become stable, the response may be weaker because existing marks and other repressive interactions continue to support the state. The difference between the two treatments is not simply an inconsistency. It can indicate that a regulatory transition and the continued preservation of its endpoint require different molecular conditions.

The reverse experiment is equally informative. A loss of DNA methylation might lead to increased chromatin accessibility or acetylation only at a subset of loci. The unaffected loci may retain repression through histone-based mechanisms or may lack transcription factors that can exploit increased accessibility. Therefore, when genome-wide methylation decreases after disruption of a methyltransferase, it is not reasonable to predict that all previously repressed genes will become active. Activation still depends on the complete regulatory machinery appropriate to each gene.

In a simplified two-factor comparison, imagine that transcription requires both an unmethylated activator site and an accessible nucleosomal environment. A treatment that removes DNA methylation but leaves chromatin inaccessible will not restore high transcription. A treatment that increases chromatin accessibility while leaving a critical activator site methylated may also fail. When the two treatments are combined, expression may increase much more strongly. The outcome reflects two constraints on the same process. It does not automatically prove that the enzymes physically interact; independent constraints can produce a combined effect as well. Distinguishing functional interaction from direct molecular contact requires a separate biochemical or structural experiment.

11. Removing or transforming a DNA methylation mark

Epigenetic regulation must allow an established pattern to change when biological circumstances require it. DNA methylation is covalent, and simple dissociation of a methyl-binding protein does not remove the group from the cytosine ring. A decrease in methylated fraction can occur through two broad categories of process. Passive demethylation refers to dilution during DNA replication when restoration is incomplete. Active demethylation refers to biochemical transformations and repair events that can replace a modified cytosine with an unmodified one without requiring dilution through multiple cell divisions. The two processes are not mutually exclusive in living cells.

The TET family of mammalian enzymes comprises TET1, TET2 and TET3. These enzymes are iron- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenases. In a representative reaction, the methyl group of 5mC is oxidised to a hydroxymethyl group, generating 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, abbreviated 5hmC. The reaction changes the substituent attached to carbon five rather than replacing cytosine with a different ordinary DNA base. Oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate participate in the reaction; the latter is converted into succinate and carbon dioxide. It is not a simple reversal of the SAM-dependent methyltransferase reaction.

Further oxidation can generate 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine. These modified residues provide routes toward restoration of unmodified cytosine. Thymine DNA glycosylase, or TDG, can excise certain oxidised cytosine derivatives, particularly 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine, from DNA. Excision creates an abasic site, which lacks its original base. Base-excision repair can then process that site and restore a normal cytosine using the complementary DNA strand as information. The mechanism links epigenetic change to a class of DNA repair reactions taught earlier in Unit 3.

Calling TET a demethylase without qualification can hide important intermediate steps. TET catalyses oxidation, not direct cleavage of the carbon–carbon bond to regenerate cytosine in a single step. A cell that converts 5mC to 5hmC has changed its modification state, but it has not necessarily completed restoration to ordinary cytosine. Hydroxymethylcytosine can itself persist and participate in biological regulation. Its abundance may be particularly significant in some cell types, including neurons. An assay that cannot distinguish 5mC from 5hmC might therefore miss the initial chemical consequence of increased TET activity.

Oxidation can also promote replication-dependent loss of methylation because the altered substrate need not be maintained with the same efficiency as 5mC. If a cell accumulates oxidised bases and subsequently replicates, a decrease in modified cytosines can arise from both active chemical conversion and passive dilution. Therefore, the observation that a TET enzyme is involved does not prove that every final loss of methylation is replication independent. Similarly, a reduction in 5mC measured in a nondividing cell could reflect conversion into another modification rather than completion of all steps to unmodified cytosine. The assay must specify which chemical species it measures.

The metabolic requirements of TET enzymes provide a clear example of interaction between cellular physiology and epigenetic chemistry. Fe(II), oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate availability can influence oxygenase activity, and certain metabolites can compete with 2-oxoglutarate-dependent reactions. However, a change in a metabolic substrate does not predict a universal change in transcription. It may influence the capacity for chemical modification while the final regulatory outcome depends on where TET enzymes are recruited, whether the relevant methylated substrates are available and what other regulatory systems act at the locus.

The difference between active and passive processes can be examined by comparing cells that divide with cells that are held in a nonproliferating state. Suppose a treatment lowers 5mC only in dividing cells, in a manner proportional to the number of completed divisions. This is consistent with a failure of maintenance. If a reduction in 5mC occurs in cells that have not replicated and oxidised cytosine derivatives increase, active conversion is more plausible. Evidence of repair-dependent appearance of unmodified cytosine would further support a completed active demethylation route. In either case, the investigator should verify cell-cycle status, viability and the specificity of the methylation measurement.

The oxidation machinery also illustrates the relationship between structure and cellular regulation. The TET catalytic region must position the modified cytosine and the iron-dependent reaction components so that oxidation occurs at the appropriate substrate. Other regions or interacting proteins can influence the genomic sites at which TET activity is exerted. A mutation disrupting the catalytic centre is expected to reduce oxidation even if the protein remains associated with DNA, whereas a recruitment defect may selectively alter modification profiles at affected loci. The distinction can be examined by measuring activity on a defined substrate separately from cellular localisation. In an integrated system, the observed methylation fraction reflects the contributions of both writers and modification-removal pathways, not simply the concentration of one enzyme.

An important exception to a mammalian-centred model is found in plants, where specialised DNA glycosylases can participate in direct removal of methylated cytosines as part of a base-excision repair mechanism. This illustrates that the biological problem of removing a stable covalent mark has more than one evolutionary solution. It would be incorrect to assert that all eukaryotes require the mammalian TET pathway for DNA demethylation. For this chapter, the mammalian oxidation-and-repair route is the principal mechanistic model, with the plant route serving as a comparative example rather than a detailed separate signalling pathway.

12. Measuring methylation: from chemistry to interpretable data

The central experimental challenge is that standard analysis of DNA sequence may not distinguish an ordinary cytosine from a methylated cytosine. Both normally direct incorporation of guanine, and amplification of the original DNA with ordinary nucleotides usually produces copies without the original covalent modifications. A method for methylation analysis must consequently preserve, reveal or selectively respond to the chemical state before it is lost. This requirement explains why techniques for determining sequence and techniques for determining methylation, although related, are not interchangeable.

A simple classical approach uses restriction enzymes that recognise the same nucleotide sequence but respond differently to a chemical modification. HpaII and MspI are a familiar pair of isoschizomers that recognise the sequence CCGG. Under the standard teaching condition, in which only the internal CpG cytosine is considered, HpaII digestion is inhibited by its methylation, whereas MspI can digest DNA bearing methylation of that internal cytosine. Both enzymes recognise the same sequence. The difference in their behaviour provides information about the chemical state of a target site rather than information about the identity of the underlying sequence. Other modification contexts, including methylation of the outer cytosine, require additional qualifications and should not be forced into this elementary rule.

Worked example 8 — Restriction fragment lengths

To interpret a restriction experiment, imagine a DNA fragment of one thousand base pairs that contains a single relevant CCGG site, three hundred base pairs from one end. Complete digestion at that site produces fragments of three hundred and seven hundred base pairs. An undigested sample produces a one thousand base pair fragment. If MspI yields the two expected fragments but HpaII leaves the original fragment intact, the result supports methylation of the internal CpG cytosine, given the stated assumptions. If both enzymes produce the two fragments, the relevant site is likely unmethylated. If neither enzyme cuts the fragment, the interpretation is uncertain without further controls: a sequence alteration, inappropriate digestion conditions or another modification pattern could produce resistance.

In a heterogeneous sample, the HpaII lane may contain both the intact fragment and the two smaller fragments. This can indicate a mixture of methylation states, but incomplete digestion of an unmethylated substrate can produce the same pattern. An appropriate assay must show that the enzyme was able to cut an unmethylated control sequence under the same conditions. A methylated control helps establish that the expected modification inhibits cleavage. The undigested sample establishes original fragment size, and digestion with the comparison enzyme provides evidence that the recognition site is present and accessible under its assay conditions. Equal loading and suitable quantification of band intensities are needed if proportions are to be estimated.

There is a further distinction between the number of DNA molecules represented by a band and the amount of DNA mass represented by that band. If the detection signal is proportional to DNA mass, a seven hundred base pair fragment gives more signal per molecule than a three hundred base pair fragment under comparable conditions. For the preceding example, the combined signal from both cut fragments can be compared with the signal from the intact fragment when all fragments are recovered and detected proportionally. Comparing only the intensity of the seven hundred base pair band with the intensity of the intact fragment without correcting for length would bias an estimate of molecular fractions. The exact calculation depends on the way the data were generated and normalised.

Restriction assays have an important limitation: they interrogate only sites recognised by the enzyme. A negative result at one CCGG site does not establish that a nearby promoter is completely unmethylated. A locus lacking an appropriate recognition site cannot be analysed by the same restriction comparison. In an examination, if the question asks whether methylation at an untested CpG has been demonstrated, the answer must recognise the limits of the assay. Such a conclusion requires an additional method capable of resolving that position.

Bisulfite-based sequencing provides a different strategy. Under appropriate chemical conditions, ordinary cytosine undergoes deamination to uracil, while 5mC is relatively resistant to this conversion. Subsequent amplification and sequencing represent the converted uracil as thymine and the retained cytosine as cytosine. By comparing the treated sequence with the original sequence, one can infer which positions originally contained a conversion-resistant modification. The apparent C-to-T difference is an analytical consequence of the chemical treatment and copying procedure. It does not mean that the original biological DNA had acquired a thymine mutation.

The conversion method requires careful interpretation of strand orientation. The experiment reads one treated strand or an amplification product derived from it. When the original DNA contains symmetrical CpG methylation, information about one strand does not automatically demonstrate that the corresponding cytosine on the opposite strand is modified in that same duplex. A bulk analysis can describe an average across many original molecules, and a strand-specific analysis can describe the analysed strand. Direct determination of joint modification states of complementary strands requires an approach that preserves or reconstructs their association. A question that supplies ordinary single-strand sequencing data should not be answered as though the dyad state of every individual molecule were known.

A major limitation is that standard bisulfite analysis does not cleanly distinguish 5mC from 5hmC: both can be represented as retained cytosine after the conventional treatment and amplification. Thus a sample enriched in hydroxymethylcytosine can appear more methylated if all retained positions are described as 5mC. Specialised methods can separate the two species, but their detailed protocols are not required here. The conceptual distinction is important because an increase in TET activity may convert 5mC into 5hmC before there is a detectable reduction in the total fraction of bisulfite-resistant cytosines.

Conversion efficiency is another essential control. If some ordinary cytosines fail to convert, they may be incorrectly counted as modified. If sample treatment causes DNA degradation or amplifies certain molecules more efficiently than others, the observed frequencies can become biased. An unmethylated control or a suitable internal conversion indicator helps estimate incomplete conversion. A known methylated control helps assess retention. Independent biological samples are required to estimate variability between specimens rather than merely variability arising from repeated sequencing of the same material.

The original 1992 work by Frommer and colleagues introduced the principle of a conversion-based sequencing method for locating 5mC on individual DNA strands. Its importance was the ability to relate a chemical modification to a particular genomic position rather than infer methylation only from total base composition or selected restriction sites. The modern interpretation has been refined by the discovery of additional modified cytosine species. A good experimental explanation should recognise both the original achievement and the limits of the chemical distinction on which the assay depends.

13. Interpreting methylation fractions, regional patterns and population mixtures

Worked example 9 — Read fractions and relative reduction

Consider a hypothetical sequencing dataset from a single CpG position. In sample A, forty of fifty informative reads retain a cytosine after bisulfite conversion, while ten show thymine. In sample B, fifteen of fifty retain cytosine. The observed conversion-resistant fractions are 40/50 = 0.80 and 15/50 = 0.30. Expressed as percentages, they are eighty and thirty percent. The absolute difference is fifty percentage points. The relative reduction from the initial fraction is (0.80−0.30)/0.80 = 0.625, or sixty-two and a half percent. Both numerical descriptions are legitimate, but they answer different questions.

The denominator in this calculation is the number of informative reads at the position. Reads that do not cover the position or cannot be confidently assigned to the target must not be included. The result estimates the fraction of sampled DNA molecules with a conversion-resistant cytosine, under the assumptions of the method. It does not directly report the fraction of cells expressing the gene. In a diploid cell, the two homologous copies of a locus may have different modification states. Some cells can carry modification on one allele and others on both or neither. Without additional information, a read-level fraction cannot be uniquely converted into a cell-level classification.

A related error arises when average methylation over several CpGs is interpreted as a pattern shared by all molecules. Suppose four sites are examined in a region and each site has a methylation fraction of one half. One possible population consists of equal numbers of molecules with all four sites methylated and molecules with no sites methylated. Another consists of molecules in which exactly two sites are methylated but the positions differ. Both populations can give the same site-specific average. Yet they present very different combinations of binding opportunities to proteins that recognise multiple adjacent modified sites. Averages suppress information about the association of marks along individual DNA molecules.

A teaching example makes this explicit. Four molecules have site patterns 1111, 1111, 0000 and 0000, where one indicates a modified cytosine and zero indicates an unmodified cytosine. At each of the four sites, two of four molecules are modified. In another sample, the patterns are 1100, 0011, 1010 and 0101. Each site again has a modification fraction of one half. The first sample contains fully marked and fully unmarked molecules, whereas the second has exactly two marks per molecule. If repression depends on a cluster of three or more marked sites on the same DNA fragment, the two samples may differ in expression despite their identical regional averages.

Worked example 10 — Bulk methylation in mixed populations

Population composition introduces another layer of interpretation. Suppose cell type A has a methylation fraction of 0.90 at a particular promoter and cell type B has a fraction of 0.10. A specimen containing seventy percent type A and thirty percent type B has an expected fraction of 0.7 × 0.9 + 0.3 × 0.1 = 0.66, assuming equal DNA contribution per cell. If the relative abundance of the two cell types later changes to thirty and seventy percent, the expected bulk fraction becomes 0.34. No methylation change within either cell type is needed to explain the difference. A comparison of heterogeneous tissues can therefore confuse altered composition with altered regulation within a defined cell population.

This example can be inverted when the composition is known to be constant. If samples contain comparable cell types and cell numbers, if DNA dosage is comparable, and if a substantial difference remains at a target site, a change in methylation within one or more of those populations becomes more plausible. However, these conditions must be supported rather than assumed. A tumour sample may contain cells with copy-number differences as well as variable proportions of nonmalignant cells. A developmental sample may contain a shifting mixture of differentiating populations. Control of sample composition is therefore part of mechanistic inference, not merely a technical detail.

Worked example 11 — Sampling precision

Sequencing depth affects the precision of a measured fraction. Four retained cytosines among five reads and eighty retained cytosines among one hundred reads give the same observed fraction of 0.80. If independent reads were unbiased draws from the same underlying distribution, the second estimate would generally be more precise. Under a simple binomial sampling model, the estimated standard error of a proportion is √[m(1−m)/N], where m is the measured fraction and N is the number of independent observations. The units are those of the fraction, not of DNA concentration. At m = 0.8 and N = 5, the estimated standard error is about 0.179; at N = 100, it is 0.04. This calculation is illustrative, because duplicated reads or PCR bias can violate independence.

Technical replication and biological replication answer different questions. Repeated measurements of one DNA preparation may reveal measurement noise, whereas separately collected or cultured biological specimens reveal variation among independent biological units. A large number of reads from one specimen does not replace independent biological samples. In a Part C experiment, a consistent methylation difference across biological replicates is generally more persuasive than a difference based on very deep sequencing of a single specimen from each condition.

Worked example 12 — Pooled versus unweighted fractions

A regional methylation percentage also depends on how it was computed. An unweighted mean of the fractions at ten sites gives equal importance to each site regardless of sequencing depth. Pooling retained and converted reads across sites gives more weight to sites with more observations. If the first site has ninety retained reads out of one hundred and the second has one retained read out of ten, the unweighted average of site fractions is (0.9+0.1)/2 = 0.5. The pooled fraction is 91/110, approximately 0.827. Neither number should be substituted for the other without knowing which biological quantity is intended. In a promoter with a critical CpG motif, a site-specific measure can be much more informative than either aggregate.

14. Establishing causation rather than association

A common observational study compares methylation at a promoter with the abundance of the corresponding mRNA. An inverse relationship is consistent with methylation-dependent repression, but it does not prove that methylation caused repression. Transcription factor loss might first decrease expression and subsequently permit methylation, a developmental signal might change both variables independently, or a tissue mixture might produce the relationship without any change within its constituent cell types. These alternatives lead to the same pair of observations. A causal experiment needs a perturbation whose effects can be distinguished from those competing explanations.

A reporter experiment is one way to isolate a sequence-dependent regulatory effect. A promoter of interest is placed upstream of a measurable reporter gene. Two DNA constructs with the same nucleotide sequence can be compared after selective methylation of the test sequence in one construct. If the methylated version produces less reporter RNA than the unmethylated version in otherwise comparable cells, this provides evidence that the imposed modification can reduce output in that experimental context. The important phrase is in that context: the introduced construct may not acquire all the chromatin features of the endogenous genomic locus, and the methylation pattern may differ from a naturally established one.

Controls must address the entire causal chain. Equal amounts of intact DNA should be supplied, and an independent measure of DNA delivery or copy number should be included. A reporter lacking the test promoter can help determine background transcription. An appropriate treatment control distinguishes methylation from any effect of handling the DNA. Measurement of the construct's methylation after entry into cells tests whether the intended difference persisted. Measuring reporter RNA as well as reporter protein helps locate the effect, because a change only in protein abundance could arise from events after transcription. These controls prevent a difference in reporter signal from being automatically attributed to promoter repression.

An endogenous perturbation can provide complementary evidence. A methyltransferase might be disrupted genetically, or a regulatory protein required for its recruitment might be altered. If a target promoter loses methylation and transcription rises, a role in repression is supported. The strength of the conclusion depends on specificity. A genome-wide loss of DNMT1 can affect cell proliferation, transposon expression and many other genes. The target gene may be activated indirectly by an altered regulatory network. A controlled change focused on the locus, or a rescue that restores the relevant methylation and expression relationship, provides a stronger argument for direct involvement.

A rescue experiment tests whether restoration of a missing molecular function reverses the phenotype. Suppose cells lacking a maintenance methyltransferase exhibit a gradual decrease in promoter methylation and an increase in transcription. Introducing a normal version of the enzyme might restore the capacity for maintenance. However, if the original pattern has already been erased, maintenance alone may not recreate all of it, because there may no longer be an inherited mark at the relevant positions. Failure of late rescue to restore the original pattern does not necessarily mean that the enzyme was irrelevant to its preservation. The design should distinguish restoration of enzyme function from re-establishment of the substrate pattern.

The order of measurements can resolve an ambiguity between initiation and reinforcement. Suppose a differentiation signal is applied to otherwise similar cells and gene transcription decreases within two hours. Promoter methylation remains unchanged at that time but increases over the next two days. If a methyltransferase-deficient culture shows the same early transcriptional reduction but fails to retain repression after removal of the signal, methylation is more strongly implicated in stabilisation than in the first repression event. The conclusion depends on measuring comparable developmental responses and on ensuring that the assay has sufficient sensitivity to detect any relevant early methylation change.

An independent analysis of protein binding can identify the route from methylation to expression. If a proposed methylation-sensitive activator is lost from the promoter after methylation but binds normally to an unmethylated copy, this supports a direct recognition mechanism. A purified binding assay can test whether the modification itself changes affinity. If binding is unchanged on naked DNA but reduced at the endogenous methylated promoter, chromatin-mediated restriction is a plausible alternative. Chromatin accessibility measurements and tests of methyl-binding corepressor recruitment can then determine whether the local environment explains the discrepancy.

Expression data must also be connected to transcription rather than only to steady-state abundance. At steady state, a simple model gives mRNA amount R = s/δ, where s is the synthesis rate in molecules per unit time and δ is a first-order removal constant in inverse time. If an intervention halves R, it may have halved synthesis or doubled removal, or produced some combination of the two. A promoter methylation hypothesis predicts a change in transcriptional synthesis, so measurement of newly produced RNA provides more direct evidence than measurement of total mature RNA alone. Protein abundance introduces still more possible regulation through translation and protein degradation.

A useful experimental comparison measures three variables in parallel: the modification state of the target, the accessibility or protein occupancy of its regulatory region, and the rate of transcription. If methylation increases, activator occupancy falls, and nascent RNA subsequently falls, the sequence of observations is consistent with the proposed mechanism. An intervention that prevents methylation while preserving the other conditions can test necessity. An intervention that imposes methylation at the target in the absence of the initiating signal can test whether the modification is sufficient to promote repression in that context. Necessity and sufficiency are distinct properties; a mechanism can be one without being the other.

The distinction between biochemical specificity and biological context also explains why different experiments may yield apparently conflicting results. A methylated promoter might be repressed in a cell expressing the necessary methyl-binding reader but not in a cell lacking that protein. A methylation-sensitive activator might be absent in a second cell type, making the same modification functionally unimportant there. In both cases, the chemical change is identical but its effect depends on the available molecular machinery. A model that includes that machinery explains more observations than a universal claim about methylated DNA.

All of the datasets in the examples above are hypothetical teaching cases rather than numerical results attributed to the historical studies. The historical evidence establishes that methylation can be recognised, maintained and chemically altered; the teaching datasets show how to reason from controlled observations to a possible mechanism. The distinction matters because an attractive hypothetical result must not be mistaken for documented evidence that a particular natural gene behaves exactly as the example predicts.

The general biological value of a chemical modification is not restricted to mammalian nuclei. A nucleotide can carry sequence information and an additional covalent mark at the same time. Different organisms have exploited this property using different enzymes, target sequences and downstream interactions. The shared principle is that chemical state can influence the recognition of DNA without requiring a change in the base order. The details of mammalian CpG maintenance, however, are only one solution to this regulatory problem.

In bacteria, methylation of adenine or cytosine can serve several functions. One classical example is Dam methylation in Escherichia coli, which adds a methyl group to adenine at the N6 position in GATC sequences. The chemically modified base is therefore different from mammalian 5mC, and the target sequence is different from CpG. Adenine methylation can contribute to the timing of chromosome-related processes and to regulation of some genes. In the classical methyl-directed mismatch repair system, the delay in methylation of newly synthesised DNA helps distinguish old and new strands. This is a different biological use of hemimethylation from the maintenance of a mammalian promoter's cytosine modification pattern.

Bacterial restriction–modification systems also use sequence-specific methylation. A restriction enzyme can cleave DNA containing a particular recognition sequence, while a corresponding modification activity can protect suitable sites in the host DNA. This function concerns discrimination of DNA substrates rather than necessarily transcriptional repression. The fact that an organism has a methyltransferase therefore does not prove that the enzyme is an epigenetic silencer. One must identify the modified base, the recognition sequence, the interacting proteins and the biological consequence.

Some bacterial genes exhibit phase variation, in which members of a genetically similar population can occupy different expression states and transmit those states to descendants with occasional switching. Dam-dependent regulatory systems can help generate such behaviour through competition between methylation and binding of regulatory proteins. If a protein binds a target sequence and protects it from modification, the protected site can remain unmethylated despite an active methyltransferase elsewhere in the cell. After replication, a temporary change in the state of the target may permit a switch in protein binding. This illustrates how self-reinforcing occupancy and modification states can create a form of regulatory memory without invoking mammalian nucleosomes or DNMT1.

Plants provide a more closely related eukaryotic comparison. In Arabidopsis, MET1 performs a major role in CG maintenance and is functionally related to mammalian DNMT1. Plant chromomethylases and DRM2 support methylation in other sequence contexts. CHG maintenance involves interactions with repressive histone states, while CHH methylation must be re-established through mechanisms that do not depend solely on symmetrical CpG copying. A representative plant pathway can use small RNA-directed recruitment of methylation machinery to a target region. The important lesson is that sequence-directed targeting and chromatin state can cooperate with enzyme activity; the detailed machinery of plant RNA-directed DNA methylation lies beyond the present chapter.

The asymmetry of CHH provides a simple structural reason for an alternative maintenance strategy. Take the sequence 5′-CAA-3′. The complementary strand, read in its own five-prime to three-prime direction, is 5′-TTG-3′. There is no corresponding cytosine at the opposite end of a symmetric motif analogous to the two cytosines in a CpG dyad. After replication, an inherited mark on one strand does not automatically specify a cytosine in an equivalent sequence context on the new complementary strand. To recreate the pattern, a cell can use information about the broader local chromatin environment or sequence-directed recruitment rather than relying solely on recognition of an opposing marked CpG.

The occurrence of plant methylation in repetitive elements illustrates the value of regulatory mechanisms that are especially important in genomes containing many mobile DNA sequences. Suppression of transposon transcription helps preserve appropriate gene regulation and genomic integrity. Plant methylation can also influence gene expression and developmental programmes. However, the physiological consequences of altered methylation vary according to which loci are affected. A global methylation mutant may show developmental defects because many regulatory relationships have changed, not because every gene has undergone a uniform decrease or increase in expression.

There are also eukaryotes in which conventional DNA cytosine methylation is absent or very limited. Budding yeast is a familiar model organism that lacks the typical widespread genomic CpG methylation found in mammals. Yet yeast cells can regulate transcription, establish chromatin states and produce stable phenotypic programmes. This comparison demonstrates that DNA methylation is not a universal prerequisite for epigenetic or chromatin-dependent regulation. Protein-based chromatin interactions and regulatory networks can provide alternative means of preserving biological states.

Evolutionary comparisons should not be interpreted as a ranking of organisms by the sophistication of their methylation system. Each lineage has particular genomic structures, developmental requirements and molecular machinery. A useful comparison asks which regulatory problem is shared and how the local molecular strategy solves it. The mammalian maintenance model is appropriate when symmetrical CpG methylation and DNMT1 are involved; a plant non-CG model or bacterial adenine modification requires different assumptions. Examination questions often exploit incorrect transfer of a true statement about one organism to a different organism where the relevant substrate or enzyme is absent.

16. Biological significance, perturbations and applications

During differentiation, the molecular machinery of a cell becomes specialised for its future functions. Some genes need to remain available for rapid induction, others need to be continuously expressed, and still others need to remain repressed except under particular developmental conditions. DNA methylation can contribute to the stability of selected expression states, especially where methylation is integrated with chromatin-based repression. It does not provide the entire specification of cell identity. Transcription factors, signalling pathways, histone modifications and RNA-mediated regulation all contribute to the establishment and preservation of the characteristic programme.

The importance of a stable state becomes clear when considering repeated cell division in a tissue. Newly generated cells must often reproduce the regulatory features appropriate to their lineage rather than return automatically to an earlier developmental state. A mechanism that copies a pattern of DNA modifications can support such continuity. Yet cell division itself is not evidence that a particular mark is functional. Some methylation patterns may reflect the history of transcription without being necessary for the continued expression state. Tests of function must therefore distinguish a mark that records previous activity from a mark whose maintenance contributes causally to future activity.

A developmental transition can involve a deliberate change in the methylation landscape. During mammalian early development and formation of the germ line, substantial changes in methylation occur, although the exact timing, route and degree of reprogramming vary by cell type and developmental stage. These changes show that epigenetic information is not always transmitted unchanged from one generation of cells or organisms to the next. The stability of a state through ordinary somatic divisions is compatible with widespread resetting during specialised developmental transitions. The detailed examples of parent-of-origin expression and chromosome-wide dosage regulation are examined separately in the later node.

Repetitive-element silencing provides a second biological reason for maintenance. When methylation and associated chromatin states keep a mobile element transcriptionally inactive, loss of that repression can increase its transcription. Increased RNA alone does not prove that new insertion events have occurred: mobilisation requires additional steps and appropriate functional machinery. Nevertheless, repeated failures of repression may increase the risk of genomic disturbances in contexts where the element is capable of moving. A methylation phenotype can consequently lead to sequence-level consequences over longer times, even though the initial loss of modification was not itself a sequence mutation.

A perturbation of DNMT1 is expected to have a characteristic relationship with replication. Cells may initially retain substantial methylation on old DNA, followed by a decline during subsequent divisions. Loss of DNMT3A or DNMT3B is more directly expected to impair establishment of new patterns, although existing marks can be affected over time through overlapping enzyme functions and changing developmental context. A defect in UHRF1 can impair recognition or recruitment needed for maintenance. A defect in a methyl-CpG reader may alter transcriptional responses while leaving the chemical marks relatively intact. These distinct predictions help identify the affected molecular step from a combination of data rather than from a single measurement.

Perturbation of demethylation machinery produces yet another pattern. Reduced TET activity can decrease formation of oxidised cytosine derivatives and may allow methylation to accumulate at some target regions. Impairment of TDG-mediated excision can produce a different chemical profile, in which oxidised intermediates accumulate without efficient completion of the repair-dependent route. Because TET proteins and TDG participate in different reactions, the two defects need not produce identical measurements of 5mC and total modified cytosines. The predicted profile depends on which intermediate is measured and on the balance of other reactions at that locus.

One application of methylation analysis is to study altered gene regulation in cancer. Some cancers show reduced methylation in broad genomic regions together with increased methylation at particular promoter CpG islands. These observations are not contradictory, because a global average and a selected promoter describe different scales of genomic organisation. Loss of repression at repeated DNA and reduced expression of some genes with hypermethylated promoters can coexist within the same cell. However, a methylated promoter in a tumour is not automatically the cause of malignant behaviour. Determining whether an alteration is a driver, a consequence or an incidental correlate requires further functional evidence.

A common experimental tool for studying dependence on DNA methylation is a cytidine analogue such as 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine, which can be incorporated into DNA during replication and interfere with DNA methyltransferase function through trapping of the enzyme. Continued cell division can then dilute existing methylation. A change in gene expression after exposure to such a compound may support a connection with methylation, but the treatment can also produce cellular stress and DNA damage responses. Its global effects make it unsuitable as sole proof that methylation at one particular promoter directly caused a transcriptional change. This discussion concerns the molecular principle rather than clinical use or experimental dosing.

In biotechnology, methylation analysis can help evaluate whether introduced DNA remains transcriptionally active, whether expression of a chosen gene differs between cell populations, and whether an apparently silent construct has retained the expected DNA sequence. If a transgene's expression gradually decreases while its sequence and copy number remain stable, a change in regulatory state is a possible explanation. Measuring its methylation, chromatin accessibility and transcript production provides a more informative investigation than immediately attributing the loss of output to a mutation. Alternative explanations such as altered integration environment, cell selection and changes in post-transcriptional processing must also be considered.

In agriculture, plant methylation states can influence gene regulation, transposon restriction and some inherited phenotypic differences. Such an observation is relevant when traits vary despite similar underlying sequences. It is essential, however, to separate a stable epigenetic difference from genetic polymorphism, environmental carryover and developmental history. A phenotype that changes after an environmental exposure does not establish that DNA methylation caused the change or that the state will persist through sexual reproduction. Comparisons across generations with appropriate controls are needed before proposing epigenetic inheritance in the organism-level sense.

The broader biological lesson is that an epigenetic mechanism must be evaluated at the level at which it operates. Chemical reactions occur at particular nucleotides, protein recognition occurs at local DNA sequences, transcription changes at defined regulatory elements, and cell phenotypes emerge from networks of gene products. A change at one of these levels may propagate to the next, but the propagation is not automatic. Understanding the intermediate causal links is what allows a student to reason correctly about unfamiliar experiments rather than simply associate the word methylation with repression.

17. Integrating a complete regulatory mechanism

Consider an initially active gene whose promoter contains a methylation-sensitive activator site. An extracellular developmental signal reduces the nuclear activity of the activator, and transcription falls. The promoter becomes more accessible to de novo methylation machinery in the particular chromatin context, and the critical site becomes methylated. Once the signal is removed, the activator is again available, but its affinity for the modified recognition site is low. Methyl-binding proteins can also contribute to the local repressive environment. In descendants of the cell, maintenance methylation helps preserve the chemical state during DNA replication. This model integrates an initiating signal, a change in transcription factor activity, a newly established modification and a mechanism of persistence.

The model predicts different outcomes for interventions at different times. A treatment that prevents the initial decrease in activator activity may also prevent methylation if occupancy protects the site. A treatment that blocks de novo methylation could allow the initial transcriptional decrease but make it easier for the promoter to become active again after the signal disappears. A treatment that blocks maintenance after the methylated state is established could cause gradual dilution of the modification in proliferating cells. A perturbation of the reader might relieve part of the chromatin-mediated repression without changing the measured mark. A TET-mediated conversion could change the modification even before passive dilution is complete. The outcomes are predictions for the specified model, not general guarantees for every promoter.

A mutation that eliminates the target CpG offers another type of reasoning. If a cytosine is replaced by thymine, the original methylation site no longer exists. But a DNA-binding protein recognises a nucleotide sequence as well as chemical modifications, so the sequence substitution may itself alter binding. A comparison of the mutant promoter with the unmethylated original does not isolate the effect of methylation. To demonstrate a modification-dependent mechanism, the investigator needs methylated and unmethylated versions of the same sequence, or additional evidence about how the sequence change influences binding. This is a central distinction between genetic and epigenetic perturbations.

The persistence of repression also needs to be distinguished from its intensity. A promoter may be partially active while retaining a stable modification pattern; another may be strongly repressed for a short interval without acquiring an inherited mark. The first case exhibits persistence with incomplete repression, while the second exhibits strong repression without demonstrated memory. Measuring expression at one time point cannot distinguish them. A recovery experiment in which the original signal is removed and descendants are followed over several divisions provides information about the duration and heritability of the state.

A final distinction concerns reversibility. Removing DNA methylation from a locus does not automatically return the cell to an earlier developmental identity because other regulatory components may remain altered. Conversely, if expression can be restored while methylation remains, the mark may have become less influential because an alternative transcriptional pathway or chromatin state has changed. Epigenetic regulation is therefore better understood as an interacting molecular system than as a single irreversible lock on the DNA. Its experimental study requires both careful measurements of individual chemical states and an understanding of the biological processes that connect those states to gene expression.

The principles developed here prepare the way for RNA-mediated silencing. Both DNA methylation and RNA-based regulation can contribute to selective control of expression, but their immediate molecular substrates and mechanisms differ. A methyltransferase modifies a nucleotide in genomic DNA, whereas a small RNA can guide recognition of a complementary nucleic acid target. A mature mRNA can be destabilised or translationally repressed without requiring a change in promoter methylation. Later, the integrated study of epigenetic memory will consider specific cases in which DNA modifications, chromatin and regulatory RNA cooperate. The present chapter establishes the chemical and mechanistic framework needed to distinguish those contributions.

Examination traps and misconceptions

All methylation represses transcription

Effects depend on genomic position and the regulatory factor. Gene bodies can be methylated during transcription. A statement with “all” or “always” ignores context; identify the relevant promoter or binding protein.

CpG means a C:G pair

It denotes adjacent cytosine and guanine on one strand. Read sequences in their own 5′ to 3′ direction; distinguish a dinucleotide from a base pair.

Methylation creates thymine

5mC remains a modified cytosine; deamination can produce T as a separate event. Distinguish an epigenetic mark from a fixed transition mutation.

DNMT1 makes every new pattern

DNMT1 principally maintains; DNMT3A/3B establish new methylation, with overlapping activities. Choose according to the biological task rather than assuming exclusive enzyme specificity.

UHRF1 transfers methyl groups

UHRF1 helps recognise hemimethylation and recruit maintenance activity. Separate substrate recognition from catalysis.

Hemimethylation means half the cells are marked

One cytosine of a symmetrical dyad is marked. A bulk 50% result cannot specify complementary-strand states.

Passive demethylation removes methyl groups directly

The fraction falls through replication without sufficient restoration. Track denominator and number of cell divisions.

TET immediately yields ordinary C

It first oxidises 5mC; further processing can restore C. Identify intermediate products and distinguish oxidation from repair.

Bisulfite retained C always means 5mC

5hmC is also conversion resistant under conventional conditions. A TET experiment requires an assay that distinguishes the species.

An unmethylated promoter must be active

It may lack an activator or remain inaccessible through another mechanism. Demethylation is not invariably sufficient for activation.

CpG islands are named for their methylation

They are defined by sequence composition. A change in modification state does not erase the CpG-rich sequence.

Every methyl-binding protein is a repressor

Readers have distinct molecular interactions and roles. Predict the phenotype from the protein's actual function, not its family name.

A methylation-expression correlation proves causation

Repression can precede methylation; both may have a common cause. Look for time-resolved perturbation, appropriate controls and rescue.

Plant methylation follows only mammalian CpG copying

Plants use CG, CHG and CHH pathways with distinct recognition mechanisms. An asymmetric motif cannot be copied by the same symmetrical dyad rule.

Epigenetic inheritance always means transmission to offspring

Mitotic inheritance is distinct from transgenerational inheritance. Developmental reprogramming can reset marks; specify the generation being studied.

A 50% mean specifies the cellular pattern

Allele dosage, cell mixtures and co-occurrence can produce the same mean. Identify the measured unit before converting read fractions to cell fractions.

Integrated revision and active recall

DNA methylation changes a base's chemical surface without necessarily changing sequence. Establishment, maintenance, recognition and removal are distinct processes. Regulatory output depends on the genomic context and the available binding proteins. Stable repression may be reinforced by interactions with chromatin, but neither a single methylated site nor a bulk percentage establishes causation. Compare time-resolved methylation, binding and transcriptional data before drawing a mechanistic conclusion.

Essential equations

  • Passive dilution: mₙ = m₀(1/2)ⁿ.
  • Partial restoration: r = (1+p)/2; mₙ = m₀rⁿ.
  • Establishment model: m′ = q+(1−q)rm; m* = q/[1−(1−q)r].
  • Single-site binding: θ = F/(F+Kd).
  • Read fraction: m = C/(C+T), where C and T are informative read counts after bisulfite.
  • Mixture: m = Σwᵢmᵢ, where weights represent DNA contribution and sum to one.
  • Ideal binomial standard error: √[m(1−m)/N].

These equations describe explicitly defined idealised models. Check the biological assumptions before applying them.
