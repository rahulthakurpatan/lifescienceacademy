1. The chemical integrity of the genetic message

A chromosome is not an inert archive. Its nucleotide sequence can survive many generations, but the individual chemical bonds carrying that sequence are exposed continuously to water, ions, metabolites and radiation. A cell therefore faces two distinct problems. It must copy information accurately when it divides, and it must preserve the chemical usability of the information between divisions. A polymerase that selects every incoming nucleotide correctly cannot solve the second problem by itself. It needs a template whose bases still identify their intended partners and whose backbone still provides a continuous track. Repair pathways provide this maintenance. They are active enzymatic processes rather than consequences of the equilibrium stability of the DNA double helix.

Consider a cytosine in a double helix paired with guanine. The amino group of cytosine can be replaced by a carbonyl oxygen through hydrolytic deamination, generating uracil. This does not automatically delete a nucleotide or sever the strand. The immediate result is a U:G pair at a position previously occupied by C:G. Uracil has the pairing properties of a base similar to thymine, so a polymerase encountering that uracil may insert adenine. After another copying event, a descendant duplex may contain T:A where the original chromosome contained C:G. The conversion of cytosine to uracil is a chemical lesion; the stable replacement of one base pair by another is a mutation. This distinction determines what repair can achieve. Removing uracil before copying preserves an accessible record of the original information in the guanine on the other strand. Once a normal T:A pair has been established, the same repair enzyme cannot infer that the site was once C:G.

The DNA double helix thus has an important functional asymmetry during repair. Although the two strands normally carry complementary information, a lesion may be present on only one strand. The damaged strand supplies the position of the problem; the opposite strand supplies the identity of the base required for restoration. Neither role is assigned permanently to a particular chromosomal strand. A lesion on the other strand would reverse the roles. Enzymes must discriminate chemical damage or an associated structural abnormality before they can use the complement correctly. If the undamaged strand is removed by mistake, copying the damaged strand might stabilize an erroneous sequence. The accuracy of repair depends as much on the choice of strand as on the fidelity of subsequent synthesis.

The term direct reversal describes a solution that does not need the complementary information for the chemical correction itself. An enzyme recognizes a reversible modification and changes the damaged chemical group back to its native state. The original nucleotide remains in the polynucleotide chain. Excision repair follows a different logic: the damaged material is removed, leaving the complementary strand to guide reconstruction of the missing part. Base excision repair, or BER, normally begins at the bond joining a damaged base to its sugar. Nucleotide excision repair, or NER, instead cuts the backbone on both sides of a lesion and removes a piece of the affected strand. All three mechanisms can return the local sequence and DNA chemistry to their previous state, yet their first reactions, intermediate structures, protein requirements and costs differ substantially.

It is useful to distinguish several kinds of discontinuity before considering mechanisms. An abasic site is a deoxyribose residue that lacks its nitrogenous base. Its backbone can initially remain continuous. A nick is a broken phosphodiester bond between two adjacent residues with no nucleotide necessarily missing. A gap is a region from which one or more nucleotides are absent on one strand. An excised oligonucleotide is a physically separate short chain containing several nucleotide residues. These are not interchangeable terms. In BER a glycosylase can generate an abasic site without producing a nick; an endonuclease subsequently creates a backbone break, and processing generates a substrate for repair synthesis. In NER two backbone cuts define a fragment that must be displaced. The distinction explains why an assay measuring strand breaks may report a temporary increase during otherwise successful repair.

Both excision systems rely on the chemistry of DNA polymerase and ligase. A polymerase extends a pre-existing 3′ hydroxyl group by adding a deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate that pairs with the complementary template. The polymerase catalyzes nucleophilic attack by the terminal hydroxyl on the alpha phosphate of the incoming nucleotide, releasing pyrophosphate. The newly incorporated residue retains a free 3′ hydroxyl for the next addition. A ligase instead connects an adjacent 3′ hydroxyl and 5′ phosphate. It cannot synthesize a missing base or replace a nucleotide merely because the ends approach each other. If one nucleotide is absent, polymerase must first fill the gap; if the ends are chemically blocked, they must first be processed. This shared enzymatic logic will recur in different pathways without implying that the pathways recognize the same lesions.

A useful mental model is to imagine a damaged letter in a double-sided coded manuscript. Direct reversal removes the chemical alteration while retaining the original letter. BER removes the altered symbol and reconstructs that local position. NER removes a short strip containing the altered symbol and replaces the strip. The analogy clarifies information conservation but has limits. DNA consists of covalently linked polymers, not independent letters, and enzyme action changes the physical substrate. Removing a base exposes a reactive sugar; removing a fragment exposes single-stranded DNA. A cell cannot allow these intermediate structures to persist indefinitely. Coordinating recognition, processing and completion is a fundamental part of genome maintenance.

DNA repair also has a time dimension. A lesion can be harmless to a particular function for a short interval and harmful when a replication fork or transcription complex arrives. A lesion on a nontranscribed strand may not immediately obstruct RNA synthesis but remains a replication risk. Another lesion might block transcription strongly even before a cell enters S phase. Hence cells can benefit from repair during nondividing periods. Genome maintenance is not simply an emergency response triggered by mitosis. In slowly dividing tissues and stationary cells, chemical changes continue while replication-associated enzymes are less active. Repair enzymes specialized for small patches can restore local continuity without requiring wholesale chromosome duplication.

Different pathways can operate on overlapping categories of lesions. The best predictor is the chemistry and geometry of the actual substrate, not the name of the damaging agent. An alkylating chemical can produce several modified bases, some suitable for direct transfer of an alkyl group, others recognized by glycosylases. UV radiation can form more than one class of photoproduct. A cell may possess both photolyase and NER, which provide alternative means of handling a subset of UV lesions. When interpreting a phenotype after exposure, the student must consider both the mixture of lesions and the repair capacities of the organism. A pathway that repairs one major product may not account for every mutation or every cell death caused by that exposure.

2. How repair became a molecular explanation

A classical clue that damage could be corrected came from observations on the recovery of organisms after ultraviolet irradiation. In the late 1940s Albert Kelner reported improved recovery of UV-treated microorganisms exposed subsequently to suitable visible light; related early work by Renato Dulbecco examined bacteriophage. The observation was important because the damage was inflicted before the recovery light was supplied. Visible light did not have to prevent the original UV exposure to improve biological outcome. This separated prevention of damage from a post-damage response. It also raised a testable mechanistic question: was illumination promoting general metabolism, or was it changing the DNA lesion itself?

An informative design compares equally UV-treated material divided between light and dark recovery conditions. Unirradiated samples exposed to the same recovery conditions are necessary to detect an independent effect of light. A biological endpoint such as viable colony number or infective phage yield measures recovery of function but not the chemical lesion directly. If light improves the survival of irradiated cells, that is evidence of a light-dependent protective process, but the stronger claim of direct reversal requires demonstrating that the covalent link between adjacent pyrimidines disappears without replacement of a DNA segment. Biochemical experiments with photolyase and damaged DNA establish that enzymatic reaction. The historical survival observation and the later biochemical explanation answer related but distinct questions.

The discovery of repair in the absence of recovery light challenged a simple model in which only direct photoreactivation could explain recovery. In 1964 Richard Setlow and William Carrier reported disappearance of thymine dimers from the large DNA fraction and their appearance in smaller material, interpreted as excision from DNA. This observation suggested that cells could remove damaged DNA material rather than reverse the lesion in place. Related work on radiation-sensitive bacterial mutants helped connect the capacity to excise lesions with particular genetic functions. The crucial distinction is the physical destination of the damaged material. If the dimer disappears because the chemical linkage is broken, the two original pyrimidines remain in genomic DNA. If the dimer appears in a short excised fragment, the chemical lesion can remain within that fragment while the chromosome loses it.

Unscheduled DNA synthesis provided another line of evidence. DNA synthesis in a cell outside normal replication can indicate that small stretches of DNA are being rebuilt. The logic becomes clearer if the cell is examined during a period when bulk semiconservative synthesis is absent or controlled. After UV exposure, labeled nucleotide incorporation into chromosomal DNA can reflect repair patches. In 1968 James Cleaver reported deficient repair replication in cells from patients with xeroderma pigmentosum. A central conclusion was that sensitivity to UV could reflect inability to remove and repair lesions rather than unusual production of damage. The condition illustrates the biological importance of NER, but the experimental insight is more general: a repair defect can be detected through failure to carry out an otherwise inducible synthetic response.

It would nevertheless be incorrect to assume every form of DNA repair is measured equally by an incorporation assay. Photolyase and alkyltransferase-type direct reversal require no patch synthesis. A single-nucleotide BER reaction incorporates much less new DNA per repaired lesion than a typical NER event. Consequently equal incorporation signals need not indicate equal numbers of corrected lesions. An experimenter must know the expected repair patch sizes, label specific activity, cell-cycle distribution and whether damaged DNA is being replicated. A student interpreting a graph should ask what the measurement physically represents rather than equating its label with the ultimate biological process.

The discovery of DNA glycosylases changed the definition of excision repair further. An enzyme that releases uracil as a free base from a DNA chain reveals a different initiating chemistry from enzymes that cut the phosphodiester backbone. Tomas Lindahl's work on DNA instability and base damage helped establish the BER framework. The presence of a free base product and a residual abasic sugar is evidence that the glycosidic bond, not the backbone, was attacked in the initiating step. Once the existence of such an activity is demonstrated, other enzymes must be identified to account for the restoration of a complete duplex. Mechanistic reconstruction requires following intermediate structures as well as the disappearance of the initial lesion.

The recognition of multiple pathways is reflected in the 2015 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Lindahl, Aziz Sancar and Paul Modrich for mechanistic studies of DNA repair. Lindahl's work is particularly associated with BER, Sancar's with photolyase and NER, and Modrich's with mismatch repair. These associations are helpful landmarks, not claims that each pathway was discovered or explained by one investigator alone. The broader history includes many geneticists, biochemists and cell biologists who linked phenotypes, chemical intermediates and purified enzyme functions. The enduring scientific lesson is that repair must be established at multiple levels: biological recovery, loss of a particular lesion, identification of intermediates, and reconstruction of a causal enzyme sequence.

3. Recognizing the relevant substrate

Repair enzymes do not possess a copy of every original chromosomal sequence. They identify substrates using chemical features, altered base pairing, local flexibility and shape, or an obstruction to molecular movement. These recognition strategies differ because DNA lesions are chemically diverse. A base with a wrong substituent may retain nearly normal stacking and barely distort the global helix. An adduct spanning two neighboring bases can alter stacking, bending and polymerase movement. A small modification can strongly affect pairing without making a bulky structure. Conversely, a bulky lesion may primarily prevent a polymerase from advancing. The biological harm of a lesion cannot be predicted exclusively from its size.

Direct reversal requires a chemically identifiable group whose removal or reversal restores the normal nucleotide without replacing the chain. O6-methylguanine is an instructive example: addition of a methyl group to the O6 position changes the hydrogen-bonding surface of guanine, increasing the chance of pairing with thymine during replication. An appropriate alkyltransferase removes that group, leaving guanine in place. The enzyme is not repairing a G:T mismatch in the ordinary sense. It is changing the chemical identity of the modified guanine. Even if thymine has already been inserted opposite it, removal of the methyl group alone may leave a G:T mismatch needing separate correction. Chemical repair and restoration of the original genetic information are therefore related but not always identical outcomes.

Glycosylases initiate BER through molecular discrimination of individual bases. The discrimination may depend on a lesion and its opposite partner. Consider 8-oxoguanine. This oxidized base can adopt conformations allowing pairing with cytosine or with adenine. A glycosylase that removes 8-oxoguanine preferentially from a pair with cytosine protects the original G:C information. A different glycosylase can remove the normal adenine paired with 8-oxoguanine, helping prevent fixation of an incorrect transversion. The second reaction initially removes an undamaged chemical base, but its context makes that base an inappropriate pairing partner. Therefore BER is not always accurately described as the removal of only chemically altered bases; it includes specialized responses to pairing contexts that arise from base damage.

NER can recognize a much wider variety of lesions because the recognition machinery need not bind the same altered chemical group in every case. A bulky adduct may destabilize pairing or create an abnormal local structure, allowing surveillance proteins to discriminate it from normal duplex DNA. Subsequent verification reduces the likelihood that an ordinary fluctuation in DNA will trigger unnecessary incisions. In a transcribed region, an RNA polymerase that persistently stalls at a lesion can recruit a transcription-associated repair response. This recognition route detects a functional consequence of damage. It need not depend on the same initial sensor used when the DNA is not being transcribed.

The notion of pathway choice is consequently better represented as a set of questions. Is an enzyme available that can restore the original chemical group in place? Does a glycosylase recognize the lesion or its abnormal pair? Does the lesion perturb the duplex enough for NER recognition, or obstruct an RNA polymerase? Is the opposite strand accessible and intact? The answers depend on both lesion and organism. Human cells lack the DNA photolyase activity used by many bacteria and plants, but they possess efficient NER. Some lesions persist even in cells with intact canonical repair pathways because recognition is inefficient or accessibility is limited. The ability of a pathway to repair a defined substrate in a test system does not establish that it is the predominant pathway for all genomic contexts in vivo.

The common exam phrase “bulky lesions are repaired by NER and small lesions by BER” is a useful initial guide but should not be used as a universal physical law. Size correlates with many structural effects, yet altered hydrogen bonding, DNA bending, helix destabilization and exposure of the base can differ for lesions of comparable size. Direct reversal operates on some small chemical modifications and, in the case of photolyase, on some lesions involving adjacent pyrimidines. The mechanism of recognition and the nature of the excised material give a stronger classification than the size rule alone.

4. Photoreactivation: direct reversal of ultraviolet photoproducts

DNA absorbs ultraviolet radiation because its bases contain conjugated ring systems. Absorption brings an electron into an excited state and can permit reactions that would be unfavorable in the ground state. Adjacent pyrimidines on the same strand are particularly important targets. Their proximity and relative orientation in stacked DNA permit covalent reactions between the bases. A cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer, usually abbreviated CPD, contains a four-membered ring formed by covalent linking of the C5 and C6 positions of two neighboring pyrimidines. The bases involved may be thymine, cytosine, or a combination of the two. The phrase thymine dimer describes a common example rather than the entire class. A second major UV photoproduct, the pyrimidine (6–4) pyrimidone photoproduct, has different bonding chemistry and typically produces a more strongly distorted duplex.

The damage is an intrastrand covalent alteration, not an ordinary hydrogen bond between complementary strands. During normal base pairing, a pyrimidine interacts with a purine on the opposite strand. During CPD formation, two neighboring pyrimidines on the same strand become covalently linked. Neither residue must leave the strand, but the linked bases no longer adopt the usual independent conformations. The consequence for a polymerase is not simply that one of its template letters is chemically wrong. Instead, it must accommodate two residues whose stacking, spacing and relative orientation have been constrained by an abnormal linkage. Depending on the polymerase and photoproduct, this can impede template reading, prevent elongation or promote inappropriate nucleotide insertion. RNA polymerase can encounter related obstacles during transcription.

Photolyase provides a particularly elegant repair strategy because CPD formation is chemically reversible. Rather than cutting DNA around a dimer, a CPD photolyase binds the damaged DNA and supplies the electronic excitation required to split the abnormal covalent linkages. The enzyme uses a reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide cofactor, commonly represented as FADH⁻, as its catalytic chromophore. Many photolyases also contain an additional light-harvesting chromophore that can transfer excitation energy to the flavin; the identity and requirement of this antenna differ among photolyases. The antenna does not replace the catalytic flavin. It improves capture of suitable photons in systems in which it is present. The relevant light is generally blue or near-ultraviolet light, distinct from the UV exposure responsible for forming the original damage.

A simplified catalytic cycle begins when the enzyme encounters a damaged duplex and establishes a stable complex at the CPD. Local deformation and movement of the damaged bases into a binding pocket bring the lesion close to the catalytic cofactor. Lesion recognition can occur in darkness; the photochemical step needs excitation. A photon absorbed by the catalytic flavin, either directly or through energy transfer from an antenna, creates an electronically excited state with a strong capacity to donate an electron. The excited flavin transfers an electron to the CPD. This change in electronic distribution destabilizes the cyclobutane ring and enables cleavage of its abnormal covalent bonds. After the ring has opened, electron transfer back to the flavin restores the catalytically competent redox state. The two pyrimidines can return to normal positions in the helix, and the enzyme can dissociate.

This cycle distinguishes the source of energy from the source of reducing equivalents. Light supplies excitation energy; the flavin participates transiently in electron transfer. The electron need not be lost permanently with each successful repair event because the reaction includes its return. The flavin is not being incorporated into DNA, and the nucleotide sequence is not being copied from the complementary strand. Neither a DNA polymerase nor a DNA ligase is required for the direct splitting reaction itself. If a textbook diagram shows polymerase filling a gap after photolyase has repaired a simple CPD, it has mixed two different routes. Photolyase acts without a repair patch, whereas a cell using NER to eliminate the same dimer must reconstruct a patch.

There are separate photolyase classes for CPDs and (6–4) photoproducts. The fact that an organism has a CPD photolyase does not establish that it can directly reverse all UV-induced lesions. The two classes share a general dependence on flavin-based photochemistry but recognize chemically different substrates, and the detailed rearrangements required for restoration differ. A useful examination distinction is that both kinds of damage may be targets of NER, while direct photoreversal requires an enzyme appropriate to the particular lesion. Other photochemical damage that does not match a photolyase substrate will need other defenses. A graph measuring the disappearance of CPDs therefore cannot automatically be generalized to the disappearance of all damage produced by UV.

Photolyases are widespread among bacteria, fungi, plants and various animals, but their distribution is not universal. In particular, placental mammals, including humans, lack the functional DNA photolyase repair pathway. This explains an important contrast between a photolyase-positive bacterium and a cultured human cell. After equivalent irradiation and subsequent blue-light exposure, the bacterium may gain a direct repair route unavailable to the human cell. Human cells can still remove UV photoproducts by nucleotide excision repair. Exposing human cells or skin to light is not a substitute for this biochemical capability. Related proteins called cryptochromes are evolutionarily connected with photolyases, but their familiar roles in light sensing and circadian regulation must not be confused with the existence of an active human DNA photoreactivation pathway.

The energy of a photon can be calculated from its frequency or wavelength. If its wavelength is λ, the photon energy E is hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant and c is the speed of light. At a wavelength of 450 nanometers, substituting 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule seconds, 2.998 × 10⁸ meters per second and 450 × 10⁻⁹ meters gives 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules per photon. Multiplication by Avogadro's constant converts this value to approximately 266 kilojoules per mole of photons. Shorter wavelengths have greater photon energies, but the effectiveness of illumination depends on absorption by the chromophore and the efficiency of subsequent photochemistry, not on photon energy alone. A wavelength outside the useful absorption spectrum may contribute little to repair even when its photons carry substantial energy. Increased light intensity can raise excitation opportunities at low intensity but may reach a limit imposed by substrate binding, enzyme abundance or other steps. Photon energy is not a direct measurement of activation energy of the entire enzymatic cycle.

An experiment that tests the role of photolyase can separate genetic, environmental and chemical variables. Imagine wild-type bacteria and an otherwise equivalent strain lacking the CPD photolyase gene. Give each strain a matched UV exposure and divide it into light and dark recovery groups. Quantify CPDs immediately after damage and again at a later recovery time, while also monitoring survival. If both strains begin with similar CPD burdens but only the wild type shows substantially faster lesion loss under suitable light, the result supports a photolyase-dependent direct mechanism. A complemented mutant in which the gene has been restored offers stronger genetic evidence. Unirradiated controls and matched recovery temperatures rule out effects of illumination unrelated to the lesion. An NER-deficient background can help distinguish a light-dependent route from excision-mediated disappearance of CPDs, although differences in viability or stress physiology must be considered.

A surviving fraction by itself cannot specify the fraction of lesions removed. A cell may survive even with some residual lesions, or may die after repair if earlier damage has caused another irreversible event. Similarly, a modest repair difference can produce a large survival difference if only a small number of persistent lesions block an essential function. To infer mechanism, direct measurements of photoproducts complement survival data. If repair activity is measured using purified photolyase and a DNA substrate containing a known CPD, restoration in light with no dNTPs present is particularly informative. The absence of dNTPs excludes DNA patch synthesis as the explanation for the reaction under those conditions. An inactive enzyme preparation and dark incubation provide controls for spontaneous changes in the substrate.

Photoreactivation is not the only reason cells may recover following UV exposure. NER operates without the illumination requirement, and damage tolerance can permit continued replication even while lesions remain. If an organism possesses both photolyase and NER, the rate of CPD loss in light may reflect the combined activities. A mutant lacking one pathway may have an intermediate phenotype rather than complete failure of recovery. This illustrates a general lesson in repair genetics: a functional defect can be partially masked by a pathway whose substrate range overlaps. Comparing single and double mutants is often more informative than comparing one mutant with wild type, provided the relevant repair outcome is actually measured.

5. Alkyltransferase-mediated restoration of modified DNA

A methyl group consists of one carbon and three hydrogen atoms. Although it is small, its addition at a critical atom of a base can change the pattern of hydrogen-bond donation and acceptance. This is why O6-methylguanine is highly instructive: the alteration is not dramatic in physical size, but it changes the information read by a replicative polymerase. Normally guanine pairs with cytosine. Methylation of the oxygen at position six favors mispairing with thymine. If the altered guanine remains through replication and the inserted thymine later becomes a template for adenine, the original G:C pair can be replaced by A:T. Such a transition mutation illustrates how a chemical modification can have a specific genetic consequence without initially breaking the strand.

O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase, abbreviated MGMT in humans, directly accepts the alkyl group from the modified guanine. The active-site cysteine participates in a transfer reaction in which its sulfur becomes covalently alkylated. The reaction restores the O6 position of guanine and leaves the original base and its sugar-phosphate linkage intact. The methyl group is transferred away from DNA rather than from one DNA base to another. The product includes a restored DNA base and a modified protein. No gap, displaced flap or excised oligonucleotide is required. Because this is a chemical group transfer, the name methyltransferase does not imply that a methyl group is being added to DNA in this reaction.

The most important consequence of the mechanism is that each active protein molecule can perform only one such transfer. Alkylation of the catalytic cysteine irreversibly inactivates that molecule for further repair. This explains the phrase suicide repair protein: the substrate DNA is corrected while the active repair protein sacrifices its own catalytic capacity. The inactivated protein is subsequently subject to cellular turnover. Unlike a typical enzyme that releases its product and then binds another substrate, MGMT cannot be assumed to sustain a constant catalytic flux indefinitely when its supply is finite. New active molecules must be synthesized if repair demand exceeds the current pool. The amount of active protein is therefore a major determinant of immediate protection from the relevant lesions.

The reaction can be expressed schematically as DNA–G(O6–CH3) + MGMT–SH → DNA–G + MGMT–S–CH3. This notation identifies the atoms involved in transfer but does not show all proton transfers or the full protein structure. The sulfur group acts as the acceptor of the alkyl group. In a structural sense the base can be presented to the active site by movement out of the helix, a strategy similar to the base flipping seen in other DNA-modifying enzymes. What makes MGMT direct reversal rather than BER is the chemical product: guanine remains attached to the sugar and is restored, whereas a glycosylase would release the entire base. Classification must follow the reaction, not the fact that both enzymes may bind a flipped-out nucleotide.

In a simplified finite-pool example, suppose a cell contains 800 active MGMT molecules and receives 1,200 O6-methylguanine lesions. Assume perfect accessibility, one lesion corrected per active molecule, no new protein synthesis and no competing reactions. At most 800 lesions can be corrected by that initial pool, leaving at least 400 lesions unresolved by MGMT. The maximal immediate fraction corrected is 800/1,200, or two thirds. The calculation is an upper bound, not a guaranteed repair outcome. Some enzyme molecules may fail to reach lesions, and some may react with other appropriate alkylated substrates. If new protein is synthesized, the bound must include the new active molecules. A normal Michaelis–Menten calculation with a constant enzyme concentration would be unsuitable for the entire time course when the enzyme is progressively consumed.

More generally, if L₀ is the number of suitable lesions and M₀ is the number of active alkyltransferase molecules, the ideal number of transfers without replenishment cannot exceed min(L₀, M₀). The minimum possible remaining lesion number is max(0, L₀ − M₀). Both variables count molecules and not concentrations, so the formula assumes a common cellular compartment and comparability of access. At a fixed protein supply, a dose-response curve may change shape near exhaustion of the pool: at lower lesion burdens the system can theoretically restore all suitable bases, whereas at higher burdens the uncorrected fraction increases. This threshold is not a literal sharp threshold in every cell, since population variability, enzyme turnover and reaction kinetics blur the idealized behavior.

A related bacterial example is the Ada alkyltransferase in Escherichia coli. Ada participates in the adaptive response to alkylation damage and contains distinct alkyl-accepting functions. Methyl transfer from certain methylphosphotriesters to a regulatory region of Ada can convert the protein into a transcriptional activator of the adaptive response. Its O6-alkylguanine repair function is associated with a different region of the protein. Increased expression of responsive genes can improve protection during continued alkylation stress. E. coli also has Ogt, another alkyltransferase involved in direct repair of relevant alkylated bases. This is an example of regulation by damage-dependent protein modification: the chemical event can function as both repair and a signal. It is not a universal feature of all MGMT-type proteins, and one should not assign the bacterial Ada transcriptional circuit to the human enzyme.

Alkylation produces multiple chemical lesions, so MGMT activity cannot be inferred reliably from a total measurement of alkylated DNA. N7-methylguanine, for example, has different chemical consequences and may destabilize the glycosidic bond. N3-methyladenine can interfere with replication and may be removed by appropriate glycosylases. O6-methylguanine is a particular substrate for direct reversal, not a synonym for DNA methylation generally. A lesion-specific antibody, chemical measurement or defined substrate can help distinguish the pathways. In a mutation spectrum, a change in the frequency of G:C to A:T transitions may support involvement of O6-methylguanine, but other lesions can also generate transitions, so the spectrum alone is not conclusive.

Direct reversal includes a second kind of alkylation repair in which the modified protein is not consumed stoichiometrically. The bacterial AlkB enzyme and certain eukaryotic homologues use oxidative dealkylation of bases such as N1-methyladenine and N3-methylcytosine. In a representative reaction, an Fe(II)- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenase uses molecular oxygen to oxidize the alkyl group. The unstable hydroxylated intermediate breaks down, restoring the base and releasing an aldehyde such as formaldehyde in the case of a methyl group. Oxidative decarboxylation of 2-oxoglutarate yields succinate and carbon dioxide. The enzyme is able to turn over when supplied with the required cofactors and cosubstrates. It differs from MGMT in both the chemical reaction and the relationship between enzyme quantity and lesion number.

This comparison prevents a common mistake: the assertion that all direct-reversal enzymes are single-use proteins. Photolyase can undergo repeated cycles with its regenerated catalytic flavin. AlkB-type oxygenases can perform repeated reactions using oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate. MGMT-type alkyltransferases consume the active alkyl acceptor stoichiometrically. All are direct reversal because the original nucleotide is chemically restored without a replacement patch. Their energetic and regulatory demands are different. In conditions that limit an appropriate cosubstrate, an oxygenase may be constrained without any change in protein abundance. In a condition with a burst of O6-methylguanine lesions, an alkyltransferase may be constrained by the number of unmodified cysteines available. Under darkness a photolyase may bind the lesion but be unable to carry out its light-dependent chemical step.

A standard application of MGMT biology is the relationship between tumor-cell repair capacity and the response to certain alkylating agents. If an agent produces O6-methylguanine, abundant active MGMT can remove a fraction of those lesions before they cause persistent damage. Reduced MGMT expression, sometimes associated with promoter methylation, can decrease that protection. The mechanistic observation is relevant to understanding why repair capacity influences cellular responses, but response depends on additional factors, including mismatch repair and the types of lesions generated. The purpose here is to explain the molecular principle, not to infer an individual patient's response or recommend treatment. It also highlights the difference between regulatory methylation of DNA in a promoter and damaging alkylation at the O6 position of guanine: they are distinct chemical modifications with distinct biological roles.

6. Base excision repair: recognizing a single altered base

A chemically modified base is not always a substrate for direct reversal. In many instances, changing it back would require a reaction for which the cell has no practical enzyme. Instead, the complement provides a reliable solution. Base excision repair starts by removing the base that has become inappropriate and then rebuilds the position using the opposing strand. The crucial advance over simply digesting damaged DNA is precision: the initial reaction removes a nitrogenous base rather than destroying an entire nucleotide chain. A class of enzymes called DNA glycosylases has evolved to perform this task.

The bond targeted by a DNA glycosylase is the N-glycosidic bond linking the anomeric carbon, C1′, of deoxyribose to a nitrogen atom of the base. In a purine nucleotide this is normally N9 of the purine; in a pyrimidine nucleotide it is N1. The backbone instead contains phosphodiester bonds joining the sugar of one residue to the next. Hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond and hydrolysis of a phosphodiester bond produce different intermediates and different chemical products. In a simple monofunctional glycosylase reaction, water participates in breaking the base–sugar linkage, releasing the base and leaving an abasic sugar. The phosphate connections on either side of the sugar initially remain intact. The resulting AP site is named for the absence of a purine or pyrimidine, not for an absence of phosphate.

The active site of a glycosylase must distinguish its substrate from an enormous excess of normal bases. This is possible because the enzyme does not interact only with the exposed edge of a base in a groove. Many glycosylases interrogate DNA by bending the duplex and promoting extrusion of a nucleotide from the stacked helix into an active-site pocket. Within that pocket, hydrogen bonds, steric constraints and electrostatic interactions reveal the chemical identity of the candidate base. Favorable contacts can stabilize the correct substrate and position the glycosidic bond for cleavage. Incorrect candidates may fail to bind tightly or fail to reach the reaction geometry. A residue of the enzyme may occupy the space created when the base is flipped out, stabilizing the local distorted DNA.

Base flipping is not a unique signature of repair. Other DNA enzymes, including methyltransferases, can also move a nucleotide out of the helix for chemical modification. Nor does every glycosylase inspect DNA by an identical sequence of physical movements. The relevant principle is that local DNA deformation and exposure of the candidate base improve the opportunity for chemical discrimination. The energy required to disrupt stacking can be balanced by favorable protein–DNA contacts. A lesion that is more readily extruded may be easier to interrogate, but the ease of extrusion alone is insufficient to predict catalytic specificity. A normal base that enters the pocket may still be rejected before cleavage.

Why does the cell employ multiple glycosylases instead of a single enzyme that removes any unusual base? The chemical diversity of lesions makes a universal active site difficult to reconcile with accuracy. An extra oxygen atom, loss of an amino group, ring opening and addition of a methyl group produce different patterns of shape and bonding. A recognition pocket designed to distinguish uracil from thymine may not be well suited to distinguish an oxidized purine from guanine. Furthermore, the pairing partner can matter as much as the damaged base itself. Several families of enzymes with partially overlapping substrate ranges provide broader coverage while preserving specificity for particular chemical and structural features. Redundancy also means that deletion of a single glycosylase may not eliminate all activity toward a given substrate.

Uracil-DNA glycosylase is a classical example. Uracil differs from thymine by the absence of a methyl group at the fifth carbon of the pyrimidine ring. It can arise in DNA through deamination of cytosine or misincorporation of dUMP during DNA synthesis. These origins create distinct pairing contexts. Deamination of a C:G pair produces U:G, whereas incorporation of dUMP opposite adenine produces U:A. Removal of uracil from a U:G pair followed by synthesis using guanine restores cytosine. Removal from U:A followed by synthesis using adenine restores thymine. The same initiating activity can therefore correct both a potentially mutagenic mismatch arising from chemical damage and the presence of an inappropriate but normally pairing base. The identity of the newly synthesized nucleotide is dictated by the remaining complement, not by the identity of the base that was removed.

Thymine itself is the usual partner of adenine in DNA. When 5-methylcytosine deaminates, however, the product is thymine paired with guanine. Because thymine is a normal DNA base, its recognition as inappropriate requires context. Thymine-DNA glycosylase and MBD4 provide examples of activities that can recognize certain G:T mismatches, particularly in relevant sequence contexts. Their role helps explain why methylated CpG sites can be mutation hotspots despite the availability of repair. The lesion is harder to distinguish from normal DNA than uracil because its deamination product is an ordinary DNA base. Repair is not necessarily absent at methylated sites; rather, recognition must discriminate an inappropriate pairing context, and a missed event can generate a stable transition.

A second source of base lesions is oxidation. Guanine has a relatively low oxidation potential compared with the other common DNA bases and is particularly susceptible to certain oxidative reactions. 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine, often abbreviated 8-oxoG, can pair with cytosine in one conformation and with adenine in another. Its ability to pair with adenine creates a path toward G:C to T:A transversions. A glycosylase directed against the oxidized base must act in the correct pairing context to protect genetic information. Removing oxidized guanine when adenine has already been inserted opposite it could cause synthesis of thymine opposite adenine, fixing the mutation. The cell therefore benefits from distinct recognition activities that repair the oxidized base itself and remove incorrectly inserted adenine.

In the bacterial GO system, MutM, also called Fpg, removes 8-oxoG and related substrates, with efficient repair of oxidized guanine paired with cytosine. MutY removes adenine from inappropriate pairings such as A opposite 8-oxoG. MutT performs a different task: it hydrolyzes an oxidized nucleotide triphosphate in the nucleotide pool, reducing the probability that an oxidized precursor will be incorporated into DNA. MutT does not excise a base from the chromosome. Human OGG1, MUTYH and MTH1 provide related examples of the complementary functions, although enzyme families and exact substrate preferences should not be treated as identical between organisms. These systems illustrate three different opportunities for maintaining sequence fidelity: prevent insertion of a damaged precursor, correct the damaged base in DNA, and correct the inappropriate partner that would otherwise stabilize a mutation.

Follow the information through an 8-oxoG:A pair. Removal of adenine leaves an abasic site on the strand that acquired the incorrect partner. After processing and gap filling, cytosine can be restored opposite the oxidized guanine. Removal of the oxidized guanine from the resulting pair creates an abasic position on the original strand; synthesis using cytosine now installs guanine. This two-stage reasoning shows why sequential correction can recover the original pair even after an incorrect nucleotide has been inserted. The first replacement does not by itself return the entire duplex to normal because the oxidized base remains. Nor is the process guaranteed: the polymerase filling the first repair gap must install the appropriate partner despite the lesion's ambiguous coding properties, and other events may compete. The important conclusion is the complementary logic of the glycosylases rather than a claim that all molecules undergo a fixed ordered sequence.

A mutation in a glycosylase gene can influence a mutation spectrum without abolishing replication itself. For example, if a cell lacks MutY, adenine opposite 8-oxoG may persist and later template thymine. The expected enrichment of G:C to T:A mutations reflects the chemical behavior of the lesion and the missing correction reaction. Yet the presence of this mutation class in a population does not prove that every mutation originated from 8-oxoG. Other damage and polymerase errors can generate the same final sequence. Defined substrates and genetic complementation provide more specific evidence than the final mutation spectrum alone.

7. The AP site and the chemistry of intermediate processing

Base removal is only the first stage of repair. An abasic position has lost the chemical group that ordinarily identifies its complementary partner. Its sugar can exist in different forms, including a ring-opened aldehyde form, and the site can be susceptible to additional chemical reactions. It may be generated spontaneously by hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond even without glycosylase action. This is why the downstream BER machinery is needed not only after deliberate base excision but also after spontaneous depurination or depyrimidination. If a cell accumulates AP sites, the problem is not necessarily excessive activity of a particular glycosylase; spontaneous loss of bases or defective downstream processing can contribute.

A major route for processing AP sites involves an AP endonuclease. In human cells the principal enzyme in this role is APE1. It hydrolyzes the phosphodiester bond on the 5′ side of the abasic sugar, yielding an upstream DNA terminus with a free 3′ hydroxyl and a downstream end carrying a 5′ deoxyribose phosphate residue, often called 5′-dRP. This reaction transforms a continuous strand containing an abasic sugar into a nicked intermediate suitable for further repair. The polymerase cannot simply ignore the residual sugar if the final goal is a normal continuous strand. It must be removed or displaced. The upstream 3′ hydroxyl is the starting point for nucleotide addition, whereas the downstream 5′-dRP must be processed to yield a ligatable end.

The distinction between glycosylase and AP endonuclease can be demonstrated with defined substrates. Suppose a double-stranded oligonucleotide contains one uracil residue. Incubation with uracil-DNA glycosylase may create an AP site but leave the covalent chain intact. A denaturing gel that detects chain lengths will not necessarily show a shorter labeled strand merely because the base has been removed. Subsequent treatment with an AP endonuclease creates a break at a defined location, producing a shorter labeled fragment when the strand is separated under denaturing conditions. An assay that detects free uracil and an assay that detects backbone cleavage report two different steps. Some AP sites can also break under harsh chemical or thermal conditions, so sample preparation must be chosen or interpreted with care.

Glycosylases can be divided mechanistically into monofunctional and bifunctional classes. A monofunctional glycosylase performs base removal without itself cleaving the backbone. A bifunctional glycosylase also has AP lyase activity that can act on the abasic intermediate. The lyase does not perform the same hydrolysis as APE1. Instead, cleavage can occur through elimination chemistry involving the abasic sugar. In beta elimination, a break on the 3′ side of the abasic site leaves an upstream terminus bearing a modified sugar group that blocks ordinary polymerase extension. Some glycosylase-associated lyases can also carry out delta elimination, producing a 3′ phosphate end. The exact end chemistry depends on the enzyme and reaction. These routes explain why a BER diagram that starts with a bifunctional glycosylase must not automatically show a clean 3′ hydroxyl immediately after backbone incision.

End-processing enzymes convert these intermediates into a chemically appropriate substrate. APE1 can help remove certain 3′ blocking residues produced by beta elimination. A polynucleotide kinase phosphatase such as PNKP can remove 3′ phosphate in relevant mammalian BER reactions, making the hydroxyl available for DNA synthesis. In other contexts PNKP can phosphorylate a 5′ hydroxyl to produce the phosphate needed for ligation. These activities solve different chemical problems at the two sides of a nick or gap. A 3′ phosphate must not be mistaken for a 5′ phosphate: the former blocks the hydroxyl required by polymerase, while the latter is part of the normal substrate required by ligase.

The need to process ends explains a common phenotype of defective BER. A cell can show efficient loss of the original base lesion yet accumulate nicked or gapped DNA. Such a cell has initiated repair successfully but cannot complete it at an adequate rate. Indeed, initiation can convert a lesion that was temporarily tolerated into a more disruptive strand intermediate. An increase in AP sites after exposure may indicate active glycosylases, decreased AP endonuclease activity, spontaneous base loss or some combination. The correct interpretation requires measuring the different pools over time. A single endpoint measurement of damage is often insufficient to identify the defective step.

Consider a bacterial example involving a uracil lesion. A monofunctional glycosylase such as Ung releases uracil. AP endonucleases such as exonuclease III and endonuclease IV provide routes for processing the AP site. Despite its name, exonuclease III has AP endonuclease activity; the protein name alone is not a complete description of its biochemical functions. DNA polymerase I participates in repair synthesis and processing, and DNA ligase seals the remaining nick. Bacterial BER shares the general sequence of chemical operations with eukaryotic BER, but the identity and relative contribution of the proteins are different. A question asking for the main human AP endonuclease should not be answered with a bacterial protein merely because the reactions are analogous.

The order of the reactions can be investigated through a set of selective defects. If glycosylase activity is absent, a defined base lesion remains but little AP-site product from that lesion is generated. If AP endonuclease activity is defective after successful monofunctional glycosylase action, AP sites can accumulate and fewer incised substrates enter synthesis. If gap-filling polymerase activity is impaired, loss of the base and incision may still occur, but incompletely synthesized repair intermediates accumulate. If ligation is blocked, synthesis can be substantially complete while a backbone nick persists. These predictions are strongest for a defined substrate and a pathway in which no alternate enzyme bypasses the missing step. In living cells, redundant activities and altered damage responses can complicate the phenotype. The sequential logic remains useful because it specifies which intermediate would be expected to rise immediately after a selective bottleneck.

8. Short-patch and long-patch base excision repair

Once the abasic intermediate has been incised and the relevant ends have been prepared, the cell must restore both nucleotide information and backbone continuity. In mammalian short-patch BER, the usual replacement patch contains a single nucleotide. The pathway is particularly well illustrated by the repair of a uracil lesion by a monofunctional glycosylase, followed by incision by APE1. The incised intermediate has a usable 3′ hydroxyl at the upstream end and a residual 5′ deoxyribose phosphate at the downstream end. DNA polymerase beta, or Pol β, is well suited to this substrate because it combines DNA synthesis activity with a deoxyribose phosphate lyase activity. It can remove the residual sugar-phosphate group and fill the one-nucleotide gap using the intact strand.

The two functions of Pol β must be understood independently. Its polymerase activity catalyzes the formation of a new phosphodiester linkage using a template-directed incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate. Its dRP lyase activity removes the sugar-phosphate remnant that cannot serve as a normal downstream nucleotide. These activities may be coordinated in the same repair complex, and the exact timing of removal and synthesis can vary. The net outcome is an upstream strand whose newly added nucleotide ends in a 3′ hydroxyl next to a downstream DNA segment with a 5′ phosphate. The structure now corresponds to a nick in an otherwise restored sequence. DNA ligase can join the two segments. In mammalian cells XRCC1 and DNA ligase III form a prominent complex in short-patch repair, although ligase I can also participate in BER depending on cellular context.

XRCC1 is a useful example of a scaffold protein. Its importance does not arise from synthesizing nucleotides or cutting the DNA backbone by itself. It interacts with repair proteins and helps organize their action at strand intermediates. By bringing suitable components into proximity, a scaffold can reduce the time during which the DNA remains incompletely repaired. If a protein with this organizing function is deficient, individual purified enzymes might retain normal catalytic activity in separate reactions while the overall cellular pathway becomes inefficient. The distinction between catalysis and coordination is important when interpreting repair mutants. A defect in a binding interaction may change recruitment, stability or handoff of an intermediate without destroying an enzyme's active site.

PARP1 provides another illustration of coordination. It can detect certain strand discontinuities and catalyze formation of poly(ADP-ribose) using NAD⁺. The resulting modification helps organize responses to DNA breaks and can promote recruitment of repair-associated factors, including XRCC1 in appropriate contexts. PARP1 is not the universal first enzyme for base excision, and its detection of a nick does not identify the base lesion that originally generated the nick. Nor does poly(ADP-ribose) replace the missing nucleotide. This distinction is particularly helpful in exam questions that ask whether a protein functions as a damage sensor, an organizing factor or a catalyst for the physical repair of DNA. One enzyme can contribute to multiple pathways because different sources of damage can produce the same intermediate.

A single-nucleotide patch is efficient when the residue at the 5′ end is readily removed by dRP lyase. Some oxidized or chemically altered sugar-phosphate remnants are poor substrates for that activity. Attempting the same short-patch reaction may then leave a persistent blocked end. An alternative is long-patch BER, in which polymerase extends the strand beyond the initial site and displaces part of the downstream segment as a single-stranded flap. Flap endonuclease 1, or FEN1, cleaves an appropriate flap structure, removing the obstructing material as part of a short nucleotide-containing segment. After cleavage and synthesis have generated adjacent chemically compatible ends, DNA ligase I commonly completes the repair.

The replacement tract in long-patch BER contains several nucleotides, often described in textbooks as roughly two to ten, although measured ranges differ with substrate and system. The length is not a fixed molecular ruler. DNA polymerase beta can participate in some reactions, while the replicative polymerases delta and epsilon contribute in other contexts, especially with the assistance of PCNA and its loading machinery. PCNA increases effective processivity and coordinates interactions between DNA synthesis and processing factors. Its participation does not mean that the cell is duplicating an entire chromosome. A sliding clamp that is highly useful at a replication fork is also useful when a polymerase must synthesize a local patch. This illustrates how biochemical machinery can be reused for different cellular tasks.

The physical distinction between a displaced flap and a gap deserves attention. A gap is missing nucleotide material in one strand, leaving an exposed region of the template. A flap is an existing strand segment displaced from its usual pairing position during synthesis. It may remain covalently joined to the downstream strand until FEN1 cleaves at the flap junction. Thus polymerase activity in long-patch BER creates a substrate for a structure-specific nuclease. The nuclease is not expected to remove every single-stranded DNA molecule it encounters; it recognizes features of a particular junction. The original lesion need not remain as a damaged base by this stage, because a glycosylase may already have released that base. The residual chemical obstacle that drives long-patch repair can be a damaged sugar remnant.

Imagine a cell expressing a Pol β variant whose polymerase active site is intact but whose dRP lyase function is compromised. In a defined short-patch reaction with a regular APE1-generated 5′-dRP, the variant may support nucleotide addition but leave a blocked downstream residue. A normal polymerase assay using a clean gap could therefore report little or no defect, whereas an assay requiring removal of dRP would reveal a serious limitation. If the cell can redirect intermediates toward long-patch BER, its survival phenotype may be less severe than predicted from the single reaction. However, cells with a simultaneous deficiency of appropriate flap processing might be especially affected. The prediction depends on functional redundancy and the availability of alternative processing factors, so the genotype alone does not define the quantitative result.

The distinction between short and long patch refers to the number of newly installed nucleotide residues, not the size of the initiating lesion. A single modified base can require more than one replacement nucleotide. Conversely, short-patch BER does not mean that every repair intermediate has only one covalent break or that only one enzyme is needed. Patch size measures a particular outcome of DNA synthesis. It does not count all reactions in the pathway. When interpreting a radiolabeled repair synthesis experiment, a long-patch BER event can contribute several times as much incorporation signal as a short-patch event even when each event removes only one initiating base lesion.

Energetically, BER includes both hydrolytic and biosynthetic reactions. Glycosylase action and AP endonuclease incision can occur without a direct ATP-driven copying reaction at the lesion. DNA synthesis consumes dNTPs, each of which contributes one nucleotide while releasing pyrophosphate. DNA ligase adenylates itself using an energy donor, transfers AMP to a 5′ phosphate at the nick, and promotes attack by the 3′ hydroxyl to form a phosphodiester bond with release of AMP. Mammalian DNA ligases use ATP, while the principal replicative ligase of E. coli uses NAD⁺. Ligase energy use cannot be inferred just by counting repaired bases: a continuous patch containing several newly made residues can require a single final nick-sealing event. Accessory factors may use additional energy or nucleotide cofactors, so the number of dNTPs incorporated is not the total metabolic cost.

An interesting biological consequence of BER is that it can remove base damage in organellar genomes. Mitochondrial DNA is exposed to reactive chemistry associated with metabolism and requires maintenance in its own compartment. Many nuclear-encoded repair proteins are targeted to mitochondria, where BER is a particularly important pathway. The precise protein composition differs from nuclear short-patch BER: mitochondrial DNA polymerase gamma and mitochondrial ligase III are relevant examples. It is incorrect to assume that a nuclear Pol β diagram applies unchanged to every mitochondrial substrate. Conventional nuclear NER is not generally considered an efficient repair route in mammalian mitochondria, so bulky lesions may have different consequences there. The larger lesson is that the availability and localization of enzymes determine whether a theoretically possible pathway can function within a compartment.

9. BER as an experimental sequence rather than a list of enzymes

To identify where a defect occurs, start with the structure that a given enzyme consumes and the structure it produces. Consider a duplex carrying uracil opposite guanine at a known site. Label one end of the lesion-containing strand, keeping the opposite strand unlabeled. The full-length labeled strand initially has a known size. If a purified monofunctional glycosylase releases the uracil without nicking the backbone, the covalent strand can remain the same length. If APE1 is then added, a labeled fragment of predictable size can appear. Addition of the appropriate polymerase and dCTP can restore the missing cytosine, provided the substrate ends are appropriately processed. Finally, a ligase joins the remaining nick and restores the original continuous labeled chain. Different methods can independently measure free base, AP-site formation, strand length, incorporated nucleotide and ligated product.

A series of control reactions provides more information than a complete mixture alone. A no-enzyme control tests spontaneous changes in the substrate under the reaction conditions. A substrate containing the normal C:G pair at the corresponding position tests whether the enzymes act selectively on the uracil-containing duplex. Omission of APE1 reveals whether the glycosylase is capable of backbone incision under the particular conditions. Omission of dNTPs tests whether strand restoration depends on DNA synthesis. Omission of ligase distinguishes nucleotide insertion from complete covalent closure. If the polymerase uses several distinct activities, selective mutants or separate substrates can help distinguish its roles. These controls connect an observed product to a specific chemical event rather than relying on a vague statement that repair occurred.

An AP-site detection assay may take advantage of the aldehydic form of the abasic sugar, whereas a strand-break assay responds to a discontinuity in the DNA backbone. These approaches are not equivalent. In an early phase of BER, the base lesion count may fall while the AP-site count rises. After AP incision, the AP-site signal can change as processed intermediates accumulate. Finally, successful repair reduces the burden of unresolved intermediates and restores DNA continuity. A transient increase in break signals can be consistent with a normal pathway if it is followed by rapid resolution. Persistent breaks or gaps provide stronger evidence of incomplete repair, although other cellular processes can also create these structures.

Consider the following hypothetical comparison: after a defined oxidative challenge, a wild-type cell population has 100 units of oxidized base signal immediately, 30 units after 20 minutes and 8 units after 60 minutes. A glycosylase-deficient population begins at the same 100 units, but retains 85 and 60 units at the corresponding times. A downstream repair-deficient population has 100, 35 and 10 units. If only the original base lesion is measured, the downstream-deficient cells appear almost as successful as wild type. Suppose the same experiment measures persistent AP sites or strand intermediates and finds much higher levels in the downstream-deficient population at 60 minutes. The combined data demonstrate that disappearance of a damaged base is insufficient evidence of completion of repair. A strong conclusion requires the upstream lesion and downstream intermediate measurements to agree with a specific mechanistic model.

Complementation strengthens such inferences by testing whether restoration of the missing factor reverses the phenotype. However, a complemented cell may express more protein than the wild type or express it at the wrong time. A difference in survival could reflect altered regulation or cell physiology rather than solely restoration of a catalytic reaction. A defined substrate assay with controlled protein quantities addresses a more direct biochemical question, while the living-cell experiment addresses whether that reaction is biologically important in its normal environment. Both types of evidence are valuable. In examination problems, the best interpretation usually respects the scale of the experiment: purified proteins reveal what a reaction can accomplish, while cellular phenotypes reveal the integrated consequences of many processes.

The rate at which a BER intermediate disappears is not necessarily the rate at which it is produced. Let U be the number of unprocessed base lesions, A the number of AP sites and G the number of subsequent unresolved repair intermediates. In a simple sequential model without new damage, conversion from U to A removes a lesion from the first pool but adds one to the second. Conversion from A to G removes an AP site from the second pool while creating an intermediate in the third. Completion removes material from the third pool. At early times A can increase even if AP processing is working, because formation temporarily exceeds removal. At later times, when fewer initiating lesions remain, the same processing capacity may rapidly reduce A. The slope of the A curve measures the difference between inflow and outflow, not either rate in isolation. This reasoning is transferable to any multistep biochemical pathway.

One can illustrate the distinction with an initial sample containing 100 unprocessed lesions, no AP intermediates and no completed repair. Suppose during the first interval 60 bases are excised, 20 AP sites are processed and 10 of those processed intermediates are completed. At the end of the interval there are 40 unprocessed lesions, 40 AP sites, 10 further intermediates and 10 completed events. The conservation equation is 40 + 40 + 10 + 10 = 100. A base-specific assay reports a 60 percent decrease in the initial lesion, yet only 10 percent of the original sites have completed the entire pathway. This is a hypothetical count model, not a measured property of BER. It is useful because it reveals how apparently conflicting measurements can both be correct.

These simple experiments also show why DNA repair is an important extension of enzyme kinetics. A glycosylase does not operate on a substrate that remains chemically unchanged while only an assay signal changes. It produces a new DNA structure that may itself be the substrate for another enzyme. Altering one step redistributes material across intermediate pools. A bottleneck in AP processing can lead to accumulation of abasic sites even when glycosylase activity is normal. Excessive glycosylase initiation relative to downstream capacity can also increase the same pool. A careful experimental interpretation must therefore distinguish a low outflow from a high inflow and determine whether both are possible given the observations.

10. Nucleotide excision repair: a broader solution to structural damage

When a lesion links two neighboring bases, carries an extensive chemical adduct or otherwise produces a major local disturbance, removing an individual nitrogenous base may be an inadequate solution. The damaged material can involve more than one nucleotide or can be difficult to expose within the active site of a glycosylase. Nucleotide excision repair solves this problem by cutting the affected strand on both sides of the lesion and removing the short oligonucleotide between the incisions. The complementary DNA strand is retained as the template for reconstruction. This design allows the same general repair machinery to deal with chemically diverse substrates without needing a separate enzyme capable of reversing every abnormal chemical bond.

A useful example is a UV-induced CPD. A cell with a specific photolyase can restore the covalently connected bases directly when suitable light is available. NER does not require splitting the CPD ring itself. Instead, it removes both connected pyrimidines as part of a larger nucleotide segment. The chemical lesion can remain intact in the removed oligonucleotide until that material undergoes degradation. The replacement DNA is synthesized using the opposing strand, so the cell no longer needs to distinguish each of the original modified bases chemically during synthesis. It needs to recognize the affected region, preserve the template and generate appropriate ends for the polymerase and ligase.

Bulky adducts derived from certain environmental chemicals are also common examples of NER substrates. When an adduct changes the dimensions or flexibility of DNA, disrupts base pairing or changes local stacking, a surveillance protein may identify the structural abnormality. The lesion itself need not be a uniform chemical group. Nevertheless, NER does not repair every bulky lesion with equal efficiency. Some DNA adducts produce relatively weak initial recognition signals, and an interstrand crosslink creates a problem involving both DNA strands that cannot be reduced to ordinary one-strand patch replacement. Simple NER may contribute to processing certain complex lesions, but the complete handling of interstrand crosslinks requires additional mechanisms. The present chapter concerns the canonical one-strand excision route.

A central problem for NER is to avoid destructive cleavage of normal DNA. DNA is flexible, and small transient openings occur even in an undamaged duplex. Protein binding, transcription and packaging can also bend DNA without chemical injury. Recognition alone cannot justify cutting an entire nucleotide segment. The pathway therefore involves ordered recognition and verification steps followed by assembly of incision machinery. Once damage has been verified, local unwinding establishes an arrangement in which nucleases can act on the correct strand. The two incisions must bracket the lesion and must not create a break across both complementary strands. Coordination of strand choice, lesion verification and cleavage makes the broad specificity of the pathway compatible with genome stability.

The two cuts in NER are endonucleolytic reactions within a strand, not continuous exonucleolytic digestion from a pre-existing free chromosome end. Their positions depend on the architecture of the repair complex and on the substrate. The removed oligonucleotide contains the lesion along with some undamaged residues. Excision thus carries a larger synthetic cost than the replacement of a single nucleotide in short-patch BER, but it provides a robust way to deal with structurally complex damage. The temporary repair gap requires protection and timely DNA synthesis. If a gap persists, it can interfere with transcription, replication or further chromosome processing. The biological advantage comes from completing the entire pathway, not simply cutting away the original damage.

An exam diagram often depicts a lesion near the middle of the excised segment, but the incision positions are not necessarily symmetric around it. Indeed, typical bacterial and mammalian NER products have characteristic lengths and asymmetric cut positions. The 5′ and 3′ directions must be interpreted on the damaged strand itself. If the diagram places the damaged strand at the bottom and writes it 3′ to 5′ from left to right, the 5′ incision is on the right. The position of an incision relative to a lesion does not tell us the direction in which polymerase will move until the orientation of the damaged strand has been established. This is an important distinction in questions that rotate or reverse the usual textbook diagram.

11. Bacterial nucleotide excision repair: the Uvr system

The Uvr pathway of Escherichia coli is the standard bacterial model for NER. The name derives from the association of ultraviolet sensitivity with the relevant genetic functions. UvrA, UvrB and UvrC participate in lesion recognition and dual incision. UvrD, DNA polymerase I and DNA ligase contribute to removal of the incised material, gap synthesis and completion. The system offers a clear example of functional specialization among proteins: ATP-dependent surveillance, lesion verification, endonucleolytic cleavage, DNA displacement, polymerization and ligation are carried out by cooperating components rather than by one universal repair enzyme.

During initial recognition, UvrA and UvrB cooperate in searching DNA for structural abnormalities. UvrA is an ATPase and can form a dimer associated with UvrB. ATP binding and hydrolysis help regulate the association of surveillance complexes with DNA and the transfer of candidate lesions to UvrB. In a simplified description, the recognition complex samples DNA and identifies a region whose structural properties are incompatible with normal duplex DNA. The details of assembly can differ with the type of lesion and experimental conditions, so it is better to understand the functions of UvrA and UvrB than memorize one fixed arrangement of every subunit. UvrA helps locate damage; UvrB is central to verification and formation of the preincision complex.

After initial localization, UvrB engages the damaged region and helps establish a structure in which the lesion is verified and the correct strand is identified. Its ATP-dependent activity and interactions with DNA are important in establishing this preincision state. UvrA is released from the lesion-associated complex as the pathway progresses. UvrB remains associated and recruits UvrC. This handoff is biologically important. If initial damage recognition occurred but transfer to a stable verification complex failed, the cell would not necessarily proceed to incision. A UvrA-associated signal in a binding assay therefore does not prove that a lesion will be excised.

UvrC supplies nuclease activities that make incisions on both sides of the damage. The UvrABC system is often called an excinuclease to emphasize this dual-incision behavior. In the standard E. coli model, incision occurs near the eighth phosphodiester bond on the 5′ side of the lesion and the fourth or fifth on the 3′ side, with the counting convention and precise positions depending on the lesion. The excised damage-containing fragment is commonly about 12–13 nucleotides long. These numbers are characteristic of a standard bacterial model rather than universal exact sizes for every substrate or organism. The essential principle is that UvrC-dependent cleavage defines a short nucleotide segment containing the lesion, while the complementary strand remains continuous.

After incision, the cut oligonucleotide need not immediately leave the helix because it can remain base paired with the complementary strand or associated with the repair proteins. UvrD is a DNA helicase involved in displacement of the damage-containing oligomer and turnover of repair complexes. Removal and gap filling are coordinated with DNA polymerase I. Polymerase I extends the upstream 3′ hydroxyl using the intact complementary sequence as a template. The resulting newly synthesized stretch restores all missing bases, including the positions at which the original damage occurred. When the last newly added nucleotide lies adjacent to the 5′ phosphate of the downstream DNA, ligase seals the remaining nick. A continuous strand is restored.

These steps show why it is imprecise to describe UvrD as the enzyme that makes both incisions. Its principal role in the canonical sequence is the helicase-associated removal and turnover stage, while UvrC supplies the nuclease activities. Conversely, UvrC is not responsible for synthesizing the replacement patch. DNA polymerase I performs that role in the standard E. coli pathway. An enzyme's involvement in NER does not imply that it carries out the step associated with its pathway name. In a biochemical test, the identity of an accumulated intermediate is more informative than simply whether the bacterium has become UV sensitive.

Suppose a defined substrate carrying one bulky lesion is incubated with UvrA and UvrB but no UvrC. Recognition and preincision complexes may form without the characteristic dual-incision products. Adding functional UvrC should permit cleavage on both sides if the preceding assembly is successful. Omitting UvrD from a complete reaction may impair efficient displacement of the incised fragment and the recycling of repair proteins. It would be misleading to predict that no incisions can ever occur in its absence. Similarly, a DNA polymerase I defect can leave unresolved repair gaps even though the damage-containing oligomer has already been cut and released. A ligase defect can leave nicks after nucleotide incorporation. These predictions distinguish the formation of repair substrates from their completion.

The Uvr system can also be directed toward damage in actively transcribed genes. When a bacterial RNA polymerase stalls at an obstructing lesion, the Mfd transcription-repair coupling factor can interact with the stalled polymerase and DNA. In the classical E. coli model, Mfd promotes displacement of the stalled transcription complex and facilitates recruitment of UvrA-containing repair machinery. The downstream excision process then uses the common Uvr components. Thus transcription coupling changes how damage is located and prioritized rather than inventing a separate chemistry of strand replacement. The precise contribution of Mfd and additional processes depends on context, so the textbook model should be treated as an established example, not a claim that every bacterial transcription-associated lesion is handled only by Mfd.

This priority makes sense when considering the information flow of a cell. A lesion in the template strand of a highly transcribed gene can prevent production of an important RNA even if the complementary strand is intact. Removing the obstruction can permit transcription to resume after appropriate rebuilding. A chemically identical lesion in the non-template strand of the same gene might not block the advancing RNA polymerase in the same way. If both strands are initially equally damaged, a faster rate of removal from the template strand during active transcription provides evidence of strand-specific repair. The result does not require that initial damage formation was lower on the template strand. A reliable test therefore measures or controls initial lesion levels before following recovery.

In a hypothetical bacterial assay, suppose the same number of UV photoproducts is present at time zero in wild-type, uvrC-deficient and mfd-deficient cells. In the absence of photoreactivating light, the wild type may remove photoproducts efficiently. The uvrC-deficient cells may fail to generate typical excision oligomers on a defined Uvr substrate. The mfd-deficient cells could retain much global excision ability but lose part of the preferential repair in a transcribed region. This contrast supports a distinction between core incision and transcription-associated recruitment. The exact survival fraction cannot be predicted from these genotypes alone, because the amount and distribution of damage and the contribution of other pathways remain relevant.

12. Eukaryotic nucleotide excision repair: two routes of recognition

The basic chemical solution in eukaryotic NER is the same as in bacterial NER: identify a lesion, open the surrounding DNA, incise the affected strand on both sides, remove the damage-containing oligonucleotide, fill the gap and ligate. The proteins implementing this solution are different, and nuclear chromatin creates additional constraints on access. Two major recognition branches are particularly important. Global genome NER surveys lesions throughout the genome, whereas transcription-coupled NER initiates preferentially when RNA polymerase II encounters an obstruction on the transcribed strand of an active gene. The branches converge on a shared core excision machinery. These terms refer to distinct routes of lesion recognition, not to different repair products formed by two unrelated types of nuclease.

Global genome NER in mammals relies prominently on the XPC complex. XPC, associated with partner proteins including RAD23B, detects local disruptions in duplex structure, often through features of the undamaged complementary strand that reflect destabilization at the lesion. This is a subtle but important point. A protein that begins recognition need not directly bind the altered chemical group in the damaged base as a glycosylase does. A broad sensor can exploit local disruptions in DNA pairing and expose a candidate lesion to a downstream verification mechanism. Some CPDs distort DNA only modestly and are relatively poor substrates for initial XPC recognition alone. UV-DDB, including DDB2, can assist recognition of such photoproducts in chromatin and promote access by downstream factors.

In transcription-coupled NER, elongating RNA polymerase II serves as the initial detector of an obstruction. RNA polymerase reads the DNA template strand while synthesizing RNA. A bulky lesion on that template may prevent continued movement, leading to a persistently stalled complex. Factors including CSB, CSA and UVSSA help organize the transcription-associated response and recruitment of the repair machinery. The protein complex associated with the stalled polymerase must be appropriately remodeled to allow access to the damaged DNA. The response is more than simply reversing the direction of transcription; it coordinates resolution of an obstructed transcription complex with restoration of its underlying DNA template. Not every transient pause is equivalent to a repair-inducing lesion, and the subsequent verification machinery helps avoid unnecessary excision.

The two recognition routes converge on TFIIH and other core NER factors. TFIIH is a multiprotein complex familiar from transcription initiation, where it contributes to opening DNA at a promoter. In NER it participates in opening and verifying a region around the candidate lesion. Its XPB and XPD components have distinct ATP-dependent roles. XPB functions chiefly as a DNA translocase contributing to opening, whereas XPD is a helicase whose movement and lesion encounter contribute to verification. Describing both simply as interchangeable helicases hides the division of labor important to the pathway. The NER-associated configuration of TFIIH differs functionally from its role at a normal transcription initiation complex.

XPA helps organize the verified preincision structure and coordinates interactions among repair factors. Replication protein A, or RPA, binds exposed single-stranded DNA and helps protect and orient the undamaged strand. A repair bubble exposes DNA in a local configuration that supports structure-specific incision. The bubble is not a replication origin or a replication fork, even though it includes single-stranded DNA and some of the same DNA-binding proteins. The question being solved is the replacement of a damaged local tract on one strand, not duplication of both strands across the chromosome. The relative locations of the proteins help protect the intact template and orient the nucleases toward the damaged strand.

Two major structure-specific nuclease activities cut the damaged strand. XPF–ERCC1 is responsible for the incision on the 5′ side, while XPG supplies the incision on the 3′ side. These identities form a high-yield comparison with bacterial UvrC, which contains the incision functions for both sides in the standard Uvr pathway. The 5′ and 3′ labels refer to the polarity of the damaged strand and must not be interpreted from the positions of proteins in an unlabeled drawing. The incised eukaryotic oligonucleotide is commonly about 24–32 nucleotides long, with the incision positions typically farther from the lesion on its 5′ side than on its 3′ side. The precise lengths and positions vary rather than forming a perfectly invariant pattern.

Excision, repair synthesis and ligation are coordinated. The new DNA patch can be made by polymerases including Pol δ, Pol ε and Pol κ in appropriate cellular contexts, with PCNA and associated factors contributing to efficient synthesis. The exact combination depends on the state of the cell and the repair reaction. Ligation by an appropriate DNA ligase restores backbone continuity; ligase I and the XRCC1–ligase III complex can contribute in different contexts. It is unnecessary to memorize one rigid universal combination of polymerase and ligase for every eukaryotic cell. The examination-level invariant is the need for template-directed local synthesis using a usable 3′ end followed by closure of the remaining nick.

A subtle feature of the eukaryotic pathway is that the order of incision, synthesis initiation and release of the excised oligonucleotide need not be represented as fully separated events occurring in every molecule at exactly the same time. Repair proteins can remain associated with intermediate structures, and synthesis can be coordinated with incision. The simplified pathway sequence is a correct way to identify substrate and product requirements, provided it is not mistaken for a complete kinetic mechanism. This distinction is especially important when interpreting a mutant that retains protein binding but lacks a catalytic activity: a component may contribute structurally to recruitment in addition to its chemical reaction.

The effects of defective NER illustrate the different functions of the two recognition branches. A defect in XPC can compromise global genome recognition while leaving the transcription-coupled recognition route relatively functional. A defect in a transcription-coupling factor can reduce preferential restoration of transcription without necessarily abolishing genome-wide repair. In contrast, impairment of a shared core incision factor can disrupt both branches. A cell with deficient global genome repair and a cell with deficient transcription-coupled repair may therefore respond differently in a strand-specific lesion removal assay, a whole-genome excision assay and an RNA synthesis recovery assay. Distinguishing these measurements provides more insight than attributing every UV-sensitive phenotype to failure of the same initiating sensor.

13. Evidence for NER, strand-specific repair and clinical relevance

The excision product is an especially informative molecular signature of NER. A defined duplex with a single bulky lesion can be used to compare the products formed by a bacterial repair system and a mammalian cell extract. If the label lies within the damage-containing region and the reaction releases an oligonucleotide of characteristic size, separation by denaturing electrophoresis can distinguish the excised fragment from the much longer parental strand. A bacterial system typically produces an oligomer near 12–13 nucleotides; a mammalian system typically produces one near 24–32 nucleotides. The difference does not imply that mammalian DNA contains a larger version of the original lesion. The same chemical adduct can be surrounded by different incision machinery, which determines the size of the removed segment.

In an experiment using this approach, the lesion-free duplex is an important specificity control. If short labeled fragments appear equally from damaged and undamaged substrates, nonspecific nuclease degradation is a plausible alternative to damage-directed excision. The position of the label also matters: an end label far outside the excised tract may detect an incision fragment but not the released lesion-containing oligomer. Product identification requires knowledge of which strand is labeled and where the label lies in relation to the lesion. A mutation preventing damage recognition should reduce specific excision products, whereas a defect in ligation could allow characteristic excision products to appear even as final DNA closure remains defective.

Unscheduled DNA synthesis tests another aspect of repair. After UV exposure of cells whose bulk S-phase replication is excluded or accounted for, incorporation of labeled nucleotide into DNA can indicate reconstruction of short excision patches. In a global-genome NER defect, UV-induced repair synthesis may be reduced substantially because many genomic lesions fail to enter the pathway. A selective transcription-coupled repair defect can produce a relatively modest change in total incorporation while markedly reducing recovery of RNA synthesis. Most DNA in a genome is not simultaneously being transcribed on the same strand, so a global measurement can obscure a large local defect in actively transcribed regions. These two assays measure different outcomes and are complementary, not mutually contradictory.

A strand-specific experiment illustrates transcription coupling more directly. Suppose equal initial lesion signals of 100 units are measured on the template and non-template strands of a transcribed gene. After an identical repair interval, 25 units remain on the template strand and 65 on the non-template strand. The fraction removed is 75 percent from the template and 35 percent from the non-template. If transcription is suppressed before damage and recovery under otherwise matched conditions, preferential template repair might diminish. If a mutant specifically lacks a transcription-coupling factor, the differential may likewise be reduced. These hypothetical observations would support transcription-associated repair. Alternative explanations include strand differences in chromatin accessibility, lesion detection efficiency or initial damage distribution. Measuring both initial burdens and repair kinetics is necessary to exclude the most obvious alternatives.

Xeroderma pigmentosum illustrates the physiological consequences of failed protection against UV damage. Many forms of the condition result from inherited defects in NER components, compromising removal of photoproducts and increasing susceptibility to UV-induced mutations and skin cancer. The classical discovery that affected cells show reduced UV-induced repair synthesis helped establish the biological relevance of NER. An important exception is the XP variant form, associated with defects in DNA polymerase eta, a damage-tolerance polymerase rather than a core NER enzyme. Such cells can retain excision repair yet handle remaining UV lesions poorly during replication. Therefore the disease name alone is not proof that a particular patient cell lacks excision activity. The distinction anticipates the next chapter's treatment of repair versus tolerance.

Cockayne syndrome provides a contrasting example because defects in transcription-associated repair factors such as CSA and CSB can strongly impair recovery of transcription after UV exposure. The clinical consequences are complex and involve functions beyond the simple pathway diagram. At examination level the useful distinction is that global genome repair and transcription-coupled repair are separable at their initiation stages and share core machinery. It is not appropriate to infer an entire clinical phenotype from the sole activity of one pathway. The same protein may have roles in additional processes, and genetic variants can have different effects on protein function. Similarly, TFIIH illustrates functional overlap between transcription and NER, so defects in its components can have consequences extending beyond lesion excision.

14. Quantitative comparison of repair capacity and the timing of genome restoration

Quantitative models become useful when their assumptions match the biology. If the removal of a defined lesion population is approximately proportional to the number remaining and repair capacity is not saturated, a first-order model may describe the early recovery period. Let L(t) be the number of lesions at time t and let k be a positive apparent removal constant with units of inverse time. The rate equation is dL/dt = −kL. Separating variables gives dL/L = −k dt. Integrating from the initial time to t yields ln[L(t)/L(0)] = −kt, or L(t) = L(0)e^(−kt). The half-time is the time at which L(t)/L(0) = 1/2, giving t½ = ln(2)/k. These equations describe disappearance of the measured lesion, not necessarily completion of every downstream repair intermediate.

For example, if a defined photoproduct signal falls from 800 to 200 units in 40 minutes, one quarter remains. Under a first-order assumption, two half-times have elapsed, so t½ is 20 minutes and k = ln(4)/40 = 0.0347 min⁻¹. At the same constant rate, another 20 minutes would reduce the signal from 200 to 100 units. The model assumes no new damage, constant effective removal capacity, homogeneous substrate accessibility and an assay whose signal is proportional to lesion number. These assumptions can fail during active stress, depletion of MGMT-type proteins, saturation of a recognition enzyme or a transition from accessible to less accessible chromatin regions. In such cases the calculated k is an empirical summary rather than an invariant molecular rate constant.

When two independent first-order routes act on the same lesion, a simplified model predicts that their rate constants add. If a photolyase-dependent route removes CPDs with an apparent constant kP and NER removes the same CPDs with constant kN, then dL/dt = −(kP + kN)L. For hypothetical values kP = 0.03 min⁻¹ and kN = 0.01 min⁻¹, the combined half-time is ln(2)/0.04 = 17.3 minutes, while the NER-only half-time is about 69.3 minutes. This calculation does not imply that photolyase and NER repair the same lesion twice. It models competing opportunities for either pathway to remove it. The model fails if pathway interaction changes recognition, if one route alters the accessibility of the other, or if the light exposure produces additional damage. Its value lies in specifying a testable expectation for a simple case.

Repair synthesis can also be considered as a nucleotide budget. Suppose a sample completes 100 short-patch BER events, 20 long-patch BER events averaging six new residues, and 30 NER events averaging 28 new residues. If each replaced residue represents one incorporated dNTP, the total incorporation is 100 × 1 + 20 × 6 + 30 × 28 = 1,060 nucleotides. Only 150 repair events occurred, yet the average patch size is 1,060/150 = 7.07 nucleotides per event. The 30 NER events account for 840 of the 1,060 incorporated residues, about 79.2 percent, despite representing only 20 percent of the events. A radiolabel distributed equally among nucleotide types would therefore weight the contribution of NER heavily. In a real experiment, base composition and the nucleotide species labeled can alter this relationship.

An idealized comparison of two cells further clarifies the problem. Cell A corrects 280 lesions by single-nucleotide BER. Cell B corrects ten lesions through NER with a 28-nucleotide patch each. Both incorporate 280 nucleotides in repair synthesis. They do not correct equal numbers of lesions, and they need not have equal survival outcomes because the lesions may differ greatly in their biological effects. Direct reversal would contribute no repair-patch incorporation despite correcting chemically important damage. A count of incorporated nucleotides becomes a lesion-count estimate only after appropriate information about patch length, pathway composition and detection efficiency is supplied.

The kinetics of DNA repair must ultimately be connected to the arrival of replication and transcription machinery. A lesion corrected just before a replication fork arrives can preserve the original sequence. The same repair reaction after a mispair has already been established may not fully recover that information. If two strands are damaged at nearby positions, the normally reliable complementary template may itself be compromised, and simultaneous processing can increase the risk of a more serious discontinuity. Chromatin organization can delay access to nuclear lesions; DNA-bound proteins and transcriptional activity can alter the relative probability of recognition. None of these observations invalidates the canonical repair mechanisms. They explain why actual repair outcomes depend on the timing, localization and coordination of enzymes as well as their catalytic capabilities.

The importance of these pathways extends beyond animal cells. In photosynthetic organisms, sunlight is simultaneously an energy source and a source of DNA damage. Light-dependent photolyases can contribute to the removal of photoproducts in illuminated tissues, whereas excision pathways and other protective mechanisms provide complementary opportunities for maintenance. A plant mutant that lacks a particular photolyase could therefore be disproportionately affected by the persistence of its corresponding photoproduct under ultraviolet exposure, even if photosynthesis and growth under low-damage conditions are largely normal. An experiment comparing mutant and complemented plants under matched light conditions should distinguish the wavelengths that induce lesions from those that support repair. Direct photoproduct measurements would be necessary before attributing a growth difference specifically to repair. Plant BER also protects DNA against endogenous base alterations, illustrating that an organism can use multiple strategies suited to distinct sources of damage.

In biotechnology, the exquisite substrate specificity of DNA glycosylases makes them useful tools for recognizing modified bases in DNA samples. A glycosylase that converts an otherwise invisible base alteration into an abasic site can be combined conceptually with backbone cleavage and a length-based readout to reveal the location of a lesion. The same reasoning explains why a glycosylase-sensitive strand-break signal is not a direct measure of pre-existing strand breaks: the assay itself introduces the discontinuity after recognizing a damaged base. Appropriate normal-DNA controls and knowledge of enzyme specificity are therefore essential. This application connects the chemistry of BER to molecular biology methods without confusing a diagnostic enzyme reaction performed outside the cell with the complete physiological repair pathway.

The most reliable way to integrate the chapter is to reason from the structure of the substrate to the chemical reaction and finally to its product. A photolyase splits an abnormal covalent linkage without replacing the original bases. An alkyltransferase restores a base through transfer of a substituent. A glycosylase breaks a base–sugar bond, after which backbone processing and synthesis restore the affected position. NER brackets the lesion with two strand incisions and replaces a short nucleotide tract. All three pathways aim to preserve the original genetic message, but only excision repair requires a newly synthesized patch. The next chapter considers what happens when the backbone itself is seriously broken or when replication must continue despite a persistent lesion. Those situations require additional strategies and cannot always be solved by simply copying an intact opposing strand.

Examination traps

  1. Damage ≠ mutation. A chemical lesion may still be corrected using its intact complement; a fixed normal base pair no longer identifies its history.
  2. CPDs are intrastrand. Adjacent pyrimidines are covalently linked, not opposite bases joined by hydrogen bonds.
  3. Humans lack functional DNA photolyase repair. NER removes photoproducts without photoreactivating light.
  4. MGMT is single-use. Its cysteine is irreversibly alkylated; photolyase and AlkB can turn over.
  5. AP site ≠ nick. A monofunctional glycosylase leaves the backbone initially intact.
  6. 3′ phosphate ≠ 3′ hydroxyl. Only the latter can be extended by DNA polymerase.
  7. MutY can remove a normal base. A opposite 8-oxoG is inappropriate; MutT acts on the nucleotide pool.
  8. Long-patch BER can start at one damaged base. The number of replaced nucleotides is not the initial lesion size.
  9. UvrC ≠ UvrD. UvrC incises; UvrD facilitates displacement and turnover.
  10. XPF–ERCC1 ≠ XPG. The former cuts on the 5′ side; the latter on the 3′ side.
  11. NER branches converge. Global and transcription-associated recognition differ, but downstream excision machinery is shared.
  12. Loss of lesion signal ≠ full restoration. AP sites, gaps and nicks can persist after lesion removal.
  13. Repair synthesis ≠ lesion count. Patch lengths differ; direct reversal incorporates no replacement nucleotides.
  14. UV sensitivity ≠ universal NER defect. XP variant involves lesion tolerance rather than core NER.

  1. T
  2. U
  3. C
  4. G
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — T. A templates T during DNA repair synthesis. The same glycosylase can therefore lead to different correct products.

Question 27

A lesion is removed efficiently but AP sites persist at high levels. A defined monofunctional BER system is used. Which step is most likely defective?

  1. Initial glycosylase recognition
  2. AP incision
  3. Photolyase excitation
  4. UvrD displacement
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — AP incision. Base loss indicates initiation is functional, while accumulation of the immediate product implicates its consumption. Other causes require controls in cells.

Question 28

A mutant Pol β retains normal synthesis on a clean gap but fails on a 5′-dRP substrate. Which activity is most directly implicated?

  1. dRP lyase
  2. 3′-side NER incision
  3. Photoreactivation
  4. NER lesion verification
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — dRP lyase. The difference between substrates is the residual dRP group, not the capacity to add nucleotides.

Question 29

An extract removes the base lesion, incorporates the expected replacement nucleotide, but retains a nick with compatible ends. Which missing activity best explains this?

  1. Ligase
  2. MGMT
  3. UvrA
  4. DNA glycosylase
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — Ligase. The initial lesion and missing nucleotide are resolved; ligation is the necessary remaining chemical step.

Question 30

A damaged duplex is restored in light by purified enzyme without dNTPs, and no oligonucleotide is released. Which mechanism is most consistent?

  1. Direct photoreversal
  2. Mismatch repair
  3. Long-patch BER
  4. Nucleotide excision
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — Direct photoreversal. The dependence on light and lack of patch synthesis point to photolyase. Excision routes need synthesis for full restoration.

Question 31

A cell has 350 active MGMT molecules and 500 suitable lesions, with no replenishment or competing reactions. What is the ideal minimum unresolved lesion count?

  1. 350
  2. 0
  3. 500
  4. 150
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D — 150. Each protein corrects at most one lesion. 500 − 350 = 150; actual unresolved count may be greater.

Question 32

If the same cell makes 100 additional active MGMT molecules during recovery, what is the upper bound on the number of corrected lesions?

  1. 500
  2. 100
  3. 450
  4. 350
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — 450. Total active molecules available are 350 + 100 = 450; the initial 500 lesions do not limit that smaller total.

Question 33

An E. coli uvrC-deficient strain binds bulky lesions normally but generates no characteristic excision oligomers. Which interpretation is best?

  1. UvrC is the initial glycosylase
  2. The initial DNA is undamaged
  3. Dual incision is impaired
  4. UvrC supplies the ATP for photolyase
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — Dual incision is impaired. UvrC catalyzes the cuts after recognition and verification; recognition alone does not establish excision.

Question 34

A purified Uvr system forms both incision products, yet the excised fragment remains associated with DNA. Which missing protein is most plausible?

  1. UvrD
  2. MGMT
  3. UvrA only
  4. MutT
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — UvrD. UvrD facilitates displacement and repair-complex turnover. Its absence does not imply that UvrC cannot incise.

Question 35

A mammalian cell shows near-normal global-genome lesion removal but defective recovery of RNA synthesis. Which defect fits best?

  1. All glycosylases absent
  2. CSB-dependent coupling
  3. All ligases absent
  4. Both core NER nucleases completely absent
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — CSB-dependent coupling. Selective transcription-associated impairment can spare much global NER. A complete core nuclease defect would affect both recognition branches.

Question 36

A cell lacks XPC but retains functional transcription coupling and core NER. What is a reasonable prediction?

  1. All BER stops
  2. Global recognition is impaired, but some transcription-coupled repair remains
  3. Both recognition branches are necessarily absent
  4. Direct reversal is impossible for all lesions
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — Global recognition is impaired, but some transcription-coupled repair remains. The recognition branches differ before convergence; XPC is principally involved in global-genome initiation.

Question 37

A labeled lesion-bearing strand produces a 28-nt released product after incubation with a mammalian extract. What is the strongest interpretation if the normal control shows no such band?

  1. One nucleotide has been directly reversed
  2. The product is an entire chromosome
  3. Ligation must be complete
  4. Damage-directed NER-like excision has occurred
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D — Damage-directed NER-like excision has occurred. The product length and damage dependence support excision. They do not independently establish gap filling and closure.

Question 38

The labeled nucleotide lies 50 bases from a lesion in a substrate undergoing 25-nt NER excision. Why may the released oligomer be invisible to that label?

  1. NER never removes DNA
  2. The label must be a protein
  3. The label lies outside the excised tract
  4. The lesion is necessarily absent
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — The label lies outside the excised tract. A distant label can remain in a parental fragment. Label placement determines which products are visible.

Question 39

After recovery, lesion signals are 20 and 60 from initially equal 100 on template and non-template strands. Which fractions were removed?

  1. 80% and 60%
  2. 80% and 40%
  3. 40% and 80%
  4. 20% and 60%
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — 80% and 40%. Subtract remaining signals from 100. Preferential template removal supports transcription-associated repair, not unequal damage induction.

Question 40

A first-order lesion burden falls from 600 to 150 in 30 min. What is the apparent half-time?

  1. 60 min
  2. 30 min
  3. 15 min
  4. 10 min
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — 15 min. 150/600 = 1/4, representing two half-times in 30 minutes. This assumes constant apparent first-order removal.

Question 41

Two independent first-order removal routes have k = 0.02 and 0.03 min⁻¹. What is the combined apparent half-time?

  1. 34.7 min
  2. 13.9 min
  3. 50 min
  4. 23.1 min
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — 13.9 min. Add the constants to obtain 0.05 min⁻¹, then calculate ln(2)/0.05 = 13.86 min. The assumption is independent competition.

Question 42

Fifty short-patch events and ten NER events with 25-nt patches complete. How many nucleotides are incorporated?

  1. 250
  2. 60
  3. 75
  4. 300
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D — 300. 50 × 1 + 10 × 25 = 300. Event count and incorporated nucleotide count are different.

Question 43

A 450-nm photon has approximately what energy?

  1. 450 J per photon
  2. 266 J per photon
  3. 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  4. 4.41 × 10⁻²⁸ J
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. E = hc/λ; convert 450 nm to 4.50 × 10⁻⁷ m. About 266 kJ/mol is the corresponding molar photon energy, not single-photon energy.

Question 44

A cell with MutY deficiency accumulates A opposite 8-oxoG. Which mutation is favored if the A becomes a template?

  1. C:G to G:C only
  2. G:C to T:A
  3. A:T to G:C necessarily
  4. No possible mutation
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — G:C to T:A. A templates T during a later copy, creating the transversion from the original G:C. Actual spectra reflect additional pathways too.

Question 45

A population loses 80 of 100 initiating lesions but has 40 AP sites and 30 later intermediates. Assuming all sites are accounted for, how many repairs are complete?

  1. 10
  2. 20
  3. 80
  4. 30
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — 10. 20 initial lesions remain; 20 + 40 + 30 = 90 unresolved sites. Only ten are fully repaired.

Question 46

Which statement set is correct? I. MGMT consumes active protein. II. Photolyase requires repair synthesis. III. APE1 incises AP sites.

  1. II and III only
  2. I and II only
  3. I and III only
  4. I only
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — I and III only. I and III are correct. Photolyase restores the covalent structure directly without a replacement patch.

Question 47

Match proteins to functions: P. FEN1; Q. XPG; R. MutT; S. UvrD. 1. 3′ NER incision; 2. flap cleavage; 3. oxidized dNTP hydrolysis; 4. fragment displacement.

  1. P3 Q4 R1 S2
  2. P4 Q3 R2 S1
  3. P1 Q2 R4 S3
  4. P2 Q1 R3 S4
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D — P2 Q1 R3 S4. Each function corresponds to a distinct chemical or structural substrate; XPG is not the flap nuclease and MutT does not displace DNA.

Question 48

Two UV-sensitive cell lines have normal initial lesion burdens. Line A shows low unscheduled DNA synthesis; line B has normal excision but poor replication past remaining CPDs. Which pair of mechanisms fits?

  1. Both necessarily XPC defects
  2. A: translation defect; B: BER exclusively
  3. Both direct reversal only
  4. A: NER defect; B: lesion tolerance defect
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D — A: NER defect; B: lesion tolerance defect. Repair synthesis reflects excision-dependent reconstruction; polymerase eta-dependent tolerance can fail despite functional excision.

Question 49

A double mutant loses both photolyase and UvrC. In a CPD assay, why can the double mutant retain more damage than either single mutant?

  1. Two overlapping removal routes are impaired
  2. The two enzymes form one obligatory active site
  3. UvrC creates CPDs
  4. Photolyase makes AP sites
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — Two overlapping removal routes are impaired. Both can act on CPDs through different chemistry. Removing both alternative opportunities can increase persistence.

Question 50

An AP lyase leaves a 3′ phosphate end. What should happen before gap synthesis?

  1. Add another phosphate to the 3′ end
  2. Seal the blocked end immediately
  3. Convert the 3′ end to a hydroxyl
  4. Remove the entire complementary strand
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C — Convert the 3′ end to a hydroxyl. The blocked phosphate must be processed before polymerase can extend. PNKP is one relevant mammalian activity.

Question 51

A researcher claims a cell has repaired every UV lesion because CPD-specific signal is zero. Which additional concern is decisive?

  1. Other UV photoproducts and unresolved repair intermediates may remain
  2. Every UV lesion is a CPD
  3. CPDs are not made by UV
  4. CPD removal always creates a mutation
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A — Other UV photoproducts and unresolved repair intermediates may remain. A lesion-specific assay cannot detect all other photoproduct classes or completion of repair.

Question 52

A bulky lesion lies on the non-template strand of an actively transcribed gene. Which prediction is most defensible?

  1. It must stall RNA polymerase directly in every case
  2. It may still be repaired by global NER even without template-strand stalling
  3. It must be converted to uracil
  4. It cannot be repaired
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B — It may still be repaired by global NER even without template-strand stalling. Global recognition surveys both strands; transcription coupling specifically begins with an obstruction on the strand being read.
