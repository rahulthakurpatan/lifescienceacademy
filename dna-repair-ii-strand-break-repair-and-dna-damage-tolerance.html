1. The problem of a broken DNA backbone

A chromosome carries information in the order of its nucleotide bases, but that order must also be preserved in a physically continuous molecule. A cell can tolerate a temporary interruption in one backbone more readily than an interruption in both backbones at nearly the same position. In the first situation, the opposite strand can hold the pieces together by base pairing and supply the sequence needed for restoration. In the second situation, neither strand spans the damaged region. Two chromosome fragments may separate, become associated with incorrect partners, or fail to segregate properly when the cell divides. The distinction between these situations is the starting point for understanding strand break repair.

Imagine a short double-stranded DNA sequence containing a particular gene. If one phosphodiester bond is missing, the nitrogenous bases on either side of the interruption can remain correctly paired with the opposite strand. The encoded message need not have changed. Nevertheless, a physical defect remains in the molecule. If instead two nucleotides disappear from one strand, the complementary strand still specifies what must occupy the gap. In both examples, a repair mechanism can restore the original order without consulting another chromosome. But if both strands lose the same two base pairs, neither original strand contains the missing information. A cell that wishes to reconstruct the lost sequence must obtain that information from a separate homologous molecule or accept the possibility of a changed junction.

This difference can be understood without memorizing any enzyme names. The essential questions are whether the backbone is broken, whether genetic information is missing, and where a reliable template can be found. The answers determine which chemical reactions can solve the immediate problem. Ligation joins compatible existing termini but cannot manufacture a missing nucleotide. Polymerization adds nucleotides but normally requires a template and a primer terminus. Homologous repair supplies a template from another DNA molecule, whereas direct end joining may restore continuity without reproducing all the original bases.

A fourth problem occurs during replication. A template can remain covalently continuous yet contain a chemically modified base that prevents a replicative polymerase from advancing. Removing the modification is one solution, but the copying machinery may have to continue before removal is possible. Specialized polymerases or access to a different template can enable DNA synthesis across or around the obstruction. Such mechanisms are called DNA damage tolerance. Their immediate task is to complete DNA synthesis, not necessarily to remove the original chemical lesion. A restored replication product and a restored parental template are therefore distinct molecular achievements.

The preceding repair mechanisms, including base excision and nucleotide excision, are important because they prevent some of these more severe problems. Excision also deliberately generates short-lived strand interruptions as part of repair. A cell must coordinate the removal of an unwanted base or segment with DNA synthesis and ligation. Otherwise the repair intermediate may persist long enough to become a new source of genome damage. This chapter examines how the cell responds when the continuity of the backbone is itself threatened, or when synthesis encounters a lesion that has not yet been eliminated.

2. DNA ends: the chemistry that governs repair

The two strands of a DNA duplex are antiparallel. A phosphodiester linkage connects the 3′ oxygen of one deoxyribose to the phosphate attached to the 5′ carbon of the next nucleotide. DNA polymerase uses the terminal 3′ hydroxyl as the reactive group for forming a new bond with the incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate. The template is read in the opposite direction, and the newly synthesized chain grows from its 5′ end toward its 3′ end. This chemical direction is invariant among the ordinary DNA polymerases considered here. A diagram that places an extending polymerase at a free 5′ end without supplying another primer is chemically incorrect.

The terminal groups on a broken DNA molecule are not always those produced by a familiar restriction enzyme. A clean nick has a 3′ hydroxyl and an adjacent 5′ phosphate. A damaged nick may instead possess a 3′ phosphate, a 5′ hydroxyl, a sugar fragment or a protein residue covalently attached to a terminus. These groups obstruct the reactions required for restoration. A polymerase cannot extend a 3′ phosphate as if it were a 3′ hydroxyl, and a conventional DNA ligase cannot simply connect a 3′ hydroxyl to an adjacent 5′ hydroxyl. The chemistry must be corrected before the backbone can be rebuilt.

A nick is an interruption in a single strand with no missing nucleotide between its two ends. A gap is an interruption in which one or more nucleotides are absent. These terms describe structure rather than origin. A gap may be generated deliberately by excision repair or arise during DNA synthesis, while a nick may be created by a damaged sugar, an enzyme, or incomplete processing of a newly synthesized strand. A clean nick can in principle be closed immediately, whereas a gap first requires replacement synthesis or a mechanism that restores the appropriate strand segment.

DNA ligase acts by activating the 5′ phosphate so that the adjacent 3′ hydroxyl can form the final phosphodiester linkage. In ATP-dependent ligases, ATP provides an adenylyl group to the enzyme, generating an enzyme–AMP intermediate with release of pyrophosphate. Transfer of AMP to the DNA phosphate creates an activated DNA intermediate. The 3′ hydroxyl attacks this intermediate, displacing AMP and generating the intact backbone. An analogous sequence operates in NAD-dependent ligases, which obtain AMP from NAD rather than ATP. Many bacterial replicative ligases use NAD, whereas mammalian nuclear ligases use ATP. Neither statement should be interpreted as a universal rule for every organism or every bacterial ligase.

The use of an activated intermediate explains why ligation is not a spontaneous and indiscriminate welding process. Two termini must be correctly positioned relative to one another. In a nick within a duplex, complementary base pairing provides much of that alignment. For disconnected duplex fragments, additional proteins may be required to hold the appropriate ends together. Even when the chemical groups are compatible, the structural context affects the efficiency and accuracy of the reaction. The role of a ligase is fundamentally different from that of a nuclease, which cuts or removes DNA, and from that of a polymerase, which extends DNA using nucleotide substrates.

A clean end is not necessarily a safe end. Consider a chromosome cut by a nuclease that generates two perfectly ligatable termini. If those termini are rejoined to one another without alteration, the original chromosome may be restored. If an end is joined to a fragment from another chromosome, all the necessary chemical reactions may still occur successfully, but the resulting chromosome is rearranged. A repair pathway must therefore solve a problem of molecular identity in addition to a problem of chemical compatibility.

The two ends of a double-strand break may be blunt, meaning that the backbones terminate at approximately corresponding positions, or staggered, leaving a single-stranded extension. Staggered cuts may expose a 5′ overhang or a 3′ overhang. The polarity of the exposed strand is named by its terminal end, not by the direction in which a diagram happens to be drawn. Complementary overhangs can anneal and help align fragments before ligation. Noncomplementary overhangs may require trimming, filling or other processing. The amount of sequence that must be modified varies greatly with the original end structure.

In an examination, the chemical symbols at a break can be more informative than the name of the damaging agent. If a nick is marked 3′-P and 5′-OH, the first required reactions are removal of the obstructing 3′ phosphate and addition of a phosphate to the 5′ hydroxyl. If the same diagram shows a missing nucleotide, DNA synthesis is also necessary. If the termini are already suitable and the base sequence is complete, ligation alone may be sufficient. These deductions follow from chemical constraints and do not depend on remembering a rigid sequence of proteins.

3. How single-strand breaks arise and become consequential

Reactive oxygen species can damage the deoxyribose portion of DNA. The resulting chemical changes may destabilize the sugar–phosphate backbone and produce a strand interruption with unusual terminal groups. The same reactive species can modify bases, which may subsequently be excised and create a repair intermediate. Ionizing radiation can generate strand breaks both through direct energy deposition and through indirect chemical reactions involving water-derived radicals. The exact structure of each break varies, so a population of damaged cells contains substrates that need different processing reactions.

Not every single-strand break is caused by an accidental chemical event. During ordinary DNA metabolism, enzymes transiently interrupt the backbone. Type I topoisomerases, for example, create reversible single-strand cleavage intermediates to permit changes in DNA topology. In a productive catalytic cycle the original backbone is restored. A persistent covalent protein–DNA intermediate can arise if rejoining fails or if the cleavage complex encounters an obstruction. In that situation a normally useful intermediate becomes a lesion requiring additional repair. It is therefore important to distinguish a transient enzymatic intermediate from an unresolved pathological break.

During base excision repair a glycosylase removes a damaged base, leaving an abasic site. Cleavage of the backbone at or near that site creates an interruption that must be processed, filled when necessary and sealed. Nucleotide excision repair similarly produces an excision gap that should be filled and ligated. These pathways illustrate an apparent paradox: maintaining DNA sometimes requires deliberately breaking DNA. The cell resolves the paradox through coordination. Removing an abnormal chemical group is advantageous only when restoration reactions follow in a controlled manner.

A single-strand break can also interfere with transcription. RNA polymerase travels along a DNA template and depends on a stable template geometry. The consequences of a break depend on whether it lies in the transcribed strand, whether it changes the local structure and whether proteins accumulate at the damaged region. A lesion in a nondividing cell may therefore matter even though no replication fork is approaching it. Long-lived differentiated cells need repair mechanisms for continuous maintenance of the genome, not simply for successful chromosome duplication.

The encounter between a replication fork and a nick is especially important. Before replication, the intact complementary strand helps maintain the connection between the two pieces of the interrupted strand. At the fork, helicase separates the parental strands. If the interrupted strand is used as the template in an orientation that causes fork run-off at the discontinuity, a daughter duplex can acquire a broken end. This is often described as conversion of a single-strand break into a one-ended double-strand break during replication. Its topology differs from an ordinary two-ended break made by cutting an already completed duplex. A matching end may not exist nearby to be joined by simple end joining.

The distinction is easiest to see when considering what is actually continuous after strand separation. A nick divides one parental strand into separate covalent fragments; base pairing keeps them close until the fork unwinds the region. Once the duplex is opened, that support is lost. The replication apparatus cannot turn the discontinuous template into a normal continuous parental strand merely by extending its daughter strand. Repair or restart must recover the interrupted replication structure. An intact sister molecule can be a valuable source of information and structural continuity at this stage.

The biological consequences of unrepaired single-strand breaks therefore depend on their lifetime and on what the chromosome is doing. A nick repaired in seconds or minutes may leave no permanent mutation. A similar nick encountered during replication can become much more serious. In a nondividing cell the break may persist and interfere with gene expression or recruit repair activities that consume metabolic resources. This is why counting only newly formed breaks is insufficient for predicting cellular damage; the persistence of the lesions and the timing of cellular processes also matter.

4. The coordination of single-strand break repair

Mammalian nuclear single-strand break repair provides a useful representative model. A common early response is binding of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1, or PARP1, at an interruption. PARP1 recognizes abnormal DNA structure and is activated by DNA binding. It consumes NAD as substrate to synthesize chains of ADP-ribose on itself and other targets. The resulting modification changes the molecular environment around the break and promotes assembly of repair factors. PARP1 is a sensor and signaling enzyme, not the enzyme that synthesizes replacement DNA or completes the phosphodiester backbone.

The modification is reversible. Poly(ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase and related activities remove the ADP-ribose signal after it has served its purpose. This provides a means of turning off recruitment as the repair reaction progresses. Auto-modification of PARP1 can also reduce its retention at DNA and facilitate its release. A repair protein that never dissociates from a repaired or partially repaired site can itself obstruct access by the enzymes that must act next. Dynamic association is therefore part of productive repair, rather than an incidental property of the proteins.

XRCC1 is a central organizer in many mammalian single-strand break repair events. Its importance does not arise from a single bond-forming catalytic reaction. Rather, it interacts with multiple repair proteins, including DNA polymerase beta, DNA ligase III and enzymes that correct damaged termini. By associating with the site and recruiting appropriate partners, XRCC1 helps coordinate the order of reactions. A scaffold defect can impair several apparently distinct chemical activities at once even when all the individual enzymes remain catalytically competent in a purified system.

One useful end-processing example is polynucleotide kinase phosphatase, abbreviated PNKP. It can remove a blocking 3′ phosphate and phosphorylate a 5′ hydroxyl. These two activities move inappropriate DNA ends toward the configuration needed for extension and ligation. Not every single-strand break requires PNKP: a clean nick lacks these specific terminal defects. Consequently, the phenotype of PNKP deficiency should be most pronounced when substrates requiring its activities are abundant, rather than identical for every possible form of DNA damage.

Other enzymes resolve particular blocked termini. Tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 can help remove covalent residues associated with trapped topoisomerase I cleavage complexes, although additional processing may still be necessary after its action. Aprataxin can resolve a DNA adenylate intermediate left by abortive ligation. These examples establish a general principle: the cell needs enzymes that recognize distinct chemical obstacles, because a single general-purpose joining enzyme cannot accommodate every damaged end. At examination level it is more useful to understand the chemistry motivating these activities than to memorize a catalog of rare substrates.

After the ends become chemically suitable, a missing segment may be replaced. DNA polymerase beta commonly participates in short-patch repair in the mammalian nucleus, using the opposite intact DNA strand as a template. If no nucleotide is missing, polymerase action may not be needed. Depending on the substrate, additional synthesis and processing factors can participate. DNA ligase III associated with XRCC1 can seal the remaining nick; DNA ligase I can also contribute to nuclear repair in appropriate contexts. The proteins listed here represent common pathways rather than an obligatory universal sequence in every cell.

An experimentally distinguishable defect in gap filling should leave different substrates from a defect in final sealing. When polymerization is blocked, missing nucleotide positions persist. When synthesis is successful but ligation is blocked, a newly synthesized patch may be present even though the backbone remains discontinuous. Measuring labeled nucleotide incorporation alone cannot establish that repair is complete. A complementary assay for strand continuity or residual damage is required. This distinction connects molecular mechanism directly to experimental reasoning.

5. The special danger of a double-strand break

A double-strand break (DSB) interrupts both covalent backbones of a DNA duplex. Even if the ends remain nearby in a nucleus, the chromosome is no longer continuous. A cell with multiple such breaks faces a further problem: the ends from different break sites can become potential partners for joining. Restoration of a physically intact molecule is not sufficient if the fragments have been reassembled in the wrong order. A DSB is therefore a threat to both local nucleotide sequence and larger-scale chromosome organization.

Ionizing radiation can produce DSBs directly or through clustered damage. Two strand interruptions close to one another on opposite strands can disrupt the duplex even when the positions do not correspond exactly. Enzymes that cut both strands can create more precisely defined breaks. DNA replication through an interrupted template can create a one-ended break, as discussed in the previous section. These sources differ in the structure of their products, and the most appropriate repair response is partly determined by the substrate rather than merely the name of the original insult.

A two-ended break in a linear chromosome generates two fragments. One may contain the centromere and the other may not, depending on the position of the break. At mitosis, a centromere is needed to establish the kinetochore and permit directed chromosome movement. A fragment without a functional centromere has no ordinary mechanism for reliable segregation. Broken chromosomes can consequently produce chromosome fragments that are lost or excluded from daughter nuclei. In cells that continue through division despite unresolved damage, the resulting abnormal distribution of genetic material may be more consequential than the loss of a few individual bases.

Consider a chromosome with two breaks surrounding a segment. If the two outer ends are joined without the intervening segment, a deletion is produced. If that segment is reversed and its ends are joined to the remaining chromosome, an inversion can result. If breaks occur on two chromosomes and ends are exchanged, a reciprocal translocation is possible. These outcomes illustrate why the arrangement of ends must be considered. A single local sequencing assay may show that one junction has formed but cannot establish that the rest of the genome has been correctly reconstructed.

A circular bacterial chromosome presents a related but not identical topological problem. A DSB can convert a circular molecule into a linear one. The broken molecule can become susceptible to further degradation, and its ability to complete replication and segregation may be impaired. Some bacterial systems can use another homologous DNA molecule generated during replication to restore continuity. Their reliance on recombinational repair varies with species, growth state and the available pathways. A model built from a mammalian linear chromosome should not simply be transferred to bacteria without considering these differences.

The cell's two broad solutions to a two-ended DSB are to reconnect the ends or to reconstruct the broken region with information from a homologous DNA template. Classical non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) represents the first strategy. Homologous recombination-directed repair (HR) represents an important form of the second. They are not equivalent choices under all circumstances. If the original ends are compatible and close together, reconnecting them can be very efficient. If sequence information has been lost and a faithful donor is available, copying from that donor can restore the missing bases. If the break is one-ended, simply joining to any available broken end is particularly hazardous.

The distinction between repair strategies is often introduced as error-prone versus error-free repair. That shorthand is useful only when accompanied by its assumptions. End joining can perfectly restore clean compatible ends; homologous repair can copy variants from a donor or produce rearrangements if the wrong template is chosen. The deeper distinction concerns the source of information used to make the repair. A pathway that directly rejoins ends does not require an extended homologous donor, while template-directed repair normally depends on sequence correspondence with a separate DNA molecule. Error rates follow from substrate and pathway details rather than from the names alone.

6. How experimental observations distinguish repair routes

Early radiation genetics established that damage survival was not simply a fixed consequence of the amount of energy deposited. Different organisms and mutant strains could exhibit different sensitivities even under comparable exposure. This suggested that biological processes occurring after damage influenced whether cells retained the ability to grow. However, survival experiments alone could not reveal the molecular mechanism. A mutation that affects repair, cell division, stress signaling or metabolism can all change survival. To identify a repair route, investigators need to examine the damaged DNA and the products of its restoration.

One conceptual approach is to introduce a defined DSB into a DNA substrate whose sequence is known. In a cell-free system, adding protein fractions and measuring the appearance of joined molecules can reveal end-joining activity. In cells, a damaged reporter can regain function after a repair event. When the DNA sequence is recovered and examined, the nature of the repair product helps distinguish precise joining, local sequence changes and copying of information from a donor. These approaches complement one another: biochemical assays show which reactions are possible, whereas cellular genetics reveals which reactions matter in the living system.

Defined donor sequences are especially valuable. Suppose a target gene is interrupted by a break and an otherwise homologous donor carries a silent marker that is absent from the target. A repaired target that acquires this marker provides evidence that information from the donor was transferred during restoration. The marker is valuable because a perfectly restored target sequence alone might have arisen by precise end joining or by template-directed repair using a genetically identical copy. A donor-specific marker makes those outcomes distinguishable. The marker should be placed and interpreted so that its acquisition requires the hypothesized information transfer, not an unrelated random mutation.

Classical genetic reasoning compares the effects of losing specific repair genes. If disruption of a DNA end-binding protein greatly decreases end-joining reporter activity and expression of the normal protein restores it, the combined evidence supports a role for the protein in that pathway. Yet the evidence does not by itself establish whether the protein is a catalytic ligase, a scaffold or a regulator. A separate biochemical experiment can test whether purified protein binds DNA ends, stimulates a joining reaction or has catalytic activity of its own. Genetic and biochemical evidence answer different levels of the mechanistic question.

An informative historical connection emerged from studies of mammalian cells defective in both radiation resistance and immune receptor gene rearrangement. Lymphocytes create regulated DSB intermediates when assembling antigen receptor genes. If a cellular defect impairs repair of accidental radiation-induced breaks and prevents completion of these programmed rearrangements, the two processes may share a joining mechanism. The severe combined immunodeficiency phenotype in certain repair-defective mouse strains helped establish this connection. Identification of DNA-dependent protein kinase components and associated end-joining proteins later clarified the molecular basis of these observations. The clinical and immunological consequences are useful evidence for the general repair mechanism, not a substitute for its biochemical explanation.

A second classic line of evidence arises from the strong effects of defects in recombinational repair genes on recovery from damage. Recombination-deficient bacteria frequently show sensitivity to DNA insults that disrupt replication or chromosome continuity. In eukaryotic systems, the ability to restore a broken reporter by using a homologous donor depends on proteins required for homologous DNA transactions. These findings explain why the same machinery that can exchange genetic information during recombination also has an important role in preserving genome integrity during vegetative growth. Detailed strand-exchange chemistry will be considered in the following chapter.

The design of an experiment determines the strength of the conclusion. If a mutant forms fewer colonies after radiation, it is appropriate to say that it is more sensitive under the conditions tested. It is not appropriate to conclude that a particular enzyme has failed to catalyze ligation unless additional measurements establish that reaction. If a reporter detects only one class of repair products, a decline in reporter activity may mean that repair has shifted to a different pathway, not that all repair has disappeared. Evidence for a pathway must therefore be tied to what the assay actually detects.

In 1994, Rouet, Smih and Jasin used a defined recognition site for the rare-cutting enzyme I-SceI in a chromosomal reporter in mouse cells. The assay made the position of a break experimentally defined rather than relying on the unknown distribution of lesions after irradiation. Recovery of reporter function and examination of DNA products allowed analysis of break-stimulated genetic restoration. The conceptual control is an otherwise comparable reporter without induced cleavage: spontaneous reporter restoration must be separated from the response to a deliberately introduced break. Selection for a functional reporter recovers only a subset of repaired molecules, so the experiment should not be interpreted as counting every end-joining event in the genome.

In 1995, Blunt and colleagues connected the repair and immune-receptor recombination defects of murine scid cells with deficient DNA-dependent protein kinase activity. They compared repair-defective cellular systems and identified defects involving the catalytic component of DNA-PK. The observation linked the radiation-sensitive phenotype and the abnormal completion of programmed DNA rearrangements to an identifiable molecular activity. It did not establish that DNA-PK alone catalyzes all joining reactions: later biochemical and genetic work identified the ligase and associated factors required for productive NHEJ. Together, these experiments show why a phenotype, a molecular activity and a defined genetic defect provide stronger evidence when considered jointly.

7. Classical non-homologous end joining: recognizing and protecting the ends

Classical NHEJ in mammalian nuclei begins when exposed duplex DNA ends become available to repair proteins. The Ku complex, formed by Ku70 and Ku80, is a major early participant. Its structural organization allows it to encircle duplex DNA near a free end. Because a free double-stranded end is unusual within an intact chromosomal region, this binding property makes Ku well suited to detecting a break without requiring recognition of a particular nucleotide sequence. The system can therefore act at many different locations across the genome.

Ku binding also changes the availability of the DNA end for other reactions. An unprotected end can be attacked by nucleases or become involved in uncontrolled reactions. Occupation by Ku helps organize a repair complex and favors conservation of the end structure. This is important because every nucleotide unnecessarily removed from an otherwise repairable end increases the possibility of sequence alteration. A system that rapidly binds and protects ends can sometimes restore the original sequence without extensive synthesis or processing.

In vertebrate cells, Ku can recruit the catalytic subunit of DNA-dependent protein kinase, DNA-PKcs. Association of Ku, DNA-PKcs and DNA ends creates a signaling and structural assembly that participates in coordinating end joining. DNA-PKcs is a protein kinase, so its catalytic action involves transferring phosphate groups to protein substrates using ATP. It does not directly form the missing DNA phosphodiester linkage. Phosphorylation events, including autophosphorylation, contribute to rearrangements of the complex and regulate access to the DNA ends.

End tethering brings the two ends into proximity. A useful way to think about this step is to distinguish proximity from chemical readiness. End-binding and tethering proteins can hold an otherwise difficult substrate in a repair complex even when the terminal groups cannot yet be ligated. Conversely, a chemically ligatable end may not produce a useful repair product unless the correct partner is aligned. End joining requires coordination between spatial organization and terminal processing.

The details of the joining complex depend on the substrate. Some clean, compatible ends can be rejoined with relatively limited processing and fewer required activities than difficult ends. DNA-PKcs and end-processing enzymes are particularly significant in contexts where terminal structure must be modified. Therefore, a simple educational scheme showing Ku followed by DNA-PKcs, processing and ligation should be read as a representative sequence, not as a claim that every protein must act catalytically at every break. The pathway has flexibility because the substrates that it encounters are heterogeneous.

A repair defect can consequently be substrate dependent. A mutation in Ku can interfere with efficient recognition and organization across a wide range of breaks. A mutation in an enzyme needed to open a particular end structure might have a much smaller effect on simple compatible ends. A defect in the final ligase should interfere with production of covalently sealed products even when the recognition and alignment steps occur normally. Predicting these differences is more useful than memorizing a single linear list of components.

8. End processing and the chemistry of final joining

Some double-strand breaks expose complementary single-stranded extensions that can form base pairs with one another. If the ends carry appropriate terminal groups, annealing recreates a locally continuous duplex with nicks in its two backbones. Ligation can seal those nicks without changing the sequence. Blunt ends can also be joined, although their alignment is not stabilized by complementary overhang base pairing. These are important counterexamples to the assertion that NHEJ inevitably produces mutations.

More complicated breaks require processing. A nuclease can remove damaged terminal nucleotides or unpaired flaps, and a DNA polymerase can fill recessed regions or add nucleotides that make the termini more suitable for joining. In vertebrate NHEJ, Artemis is a representative nuclease associated with DNA-PK-dependent end processing. DNA polymerases mu and lambda can participate in end-associated synthesis. Their contributions depend on end geometry and available template information. Neither a nuclease nor a polymerase replaces the final need for backbone ligation.

A useful distinction is between trimming a nucleotide and filling a nucleotide. Trimming removes information from the broken molecule. Filling can restore complementary information when a suitable template is present, but filling does not guarantee restoration of the original sequence if the template has already been lost or if the polymerase uses an alternative arrangement. For example, an overhang that retains the original sequence may guide faithful fill-in of its complementary strand. If both strands of the original sequence have been removed, local fill-in cannot reconstruct that sequence without another source of information.

Short complementary regions at broken ends can help stabilize their alignment. Such local complementarity does not automatically make the reaction homologous recombination. Classical NHEJ can use short base-pairing opportunities while remaining independent of an extended homologous donor molecule. Alternative end joining pathways may make more conspicuous use of short internal sequence matches, often called microhomologies, after limited resection. Finding a few matching bases at a junction is therefore not sufficient to diagnose a particular pathway without additional evidence about the substrate and the proteins required.

In mammalian classical NHEJ, DNA ligase IV works in a complex with XRCC4, with XLF contributing to efficient assembly and joining in many settings. XRCC4 helps stabilize and organize ligase IV; XLF participates in end alignment and stimulation of joining. These accessory proteins should not be described as alternative ligases. The catalytic joining function is supplied by ligase IV, which uses ATP-dependent activation chemistry. The existence of distinct organizational and catalytic functions explains why deficiency of a noncatalytic component can be as consequential for repair as loss of an enzyme's active site.

Each duplex contains two covalent backbones, so a completed repair must restore both. The two sealing events need not take place at the exact same moment. An intermediate in which one strand has been joined but the other remains nicked is chemically possible. A laboratory measurement that detects association or pairing of two DNA fragments can therefore overestimate complete repair if it does not distinguish noncovalently associated or partially joined products from fully ligated molecules. Sequence analysis and measurements of covalent continuity address complementary questions.

Consider a hypothetical clean staggered break with complementary overhangs. If the cell anneals the original partners and seals both backbones without nucleotide addition or removal, the product has no sequence change. Consider instead a damaged break where three base pairs are lost before joining and no homologous donor is used. If no replacement information is provided, the final product contains a three-base-pair deletion. Both events may be classified as NHEJ, yet they have very different consequences for the encoded message. In a coding sequence, a deletion of three nucleotides preserves the downstream reading frame but can still alter protein function; a deletion of two generally changes the reading frame. Location matters as well: a small change in a regulatory motif can have a large effect even outside a protein-coding region.

End joining thus involves an important biological balance. Extensive processing can convert difficult ends into substrates that can be joined, but processing carries a risk of losing information. Rapid restoration of chromosome continuity can preserve viability even when some local sequence is altered. Protection, selective processing, alignment and ligation together make classical NHEJ a versatile response to DNA breaks rather than a single indiscriminate reaction.

9. Template-guided repair: recovering information rather than merely continuity

When a DSB has removed genetic information, reconnecting its ends cannot in itself replace that information. The cell must obtain a sequence from somewhere else. After DNA replication, each chromatid has a sister that normally carries nearly the same nucleotide sequence. The sister chromatid can serve as a donor during homologous recombination-directed repair. This is a central reason why template-guided repair becomes particularly useful during S and G2 phases of the eukaryotic cell cycle. It exploits the temporary presence of a newly produced copy of the chromosome.

A diploid cell also possesses a homologous chromosome inherited from the other parent. Its sequence is similar but not necessarily identical to that of the broken chromosome. If the homolog is used as a donor, allelic differences may be transferred during repair. This possibility distinguishes sequence recovery from restoration of precisely the previous allele. Sister chromatids are usually preferred for repair during ordinary somatic growth because they are nearby and generally provide a closely matching sequence. Homologous chromosomes have a central role in meiotic recombination, a context in which the biological objectives and regulation differ.

Before a broken end can use a homologous donor in the usual HR pathway, the DNA must be processed to expose a single-stranded region. The reason is structural: a strand that remains paired throughout its length with its original partner cannot readily test its complementarity to the bases of a second duplex. End resection removes DNA preferentially from the 5′-terminated strands near the break, leaving 3′ single-stranded extensions. The direction is important. The resulting 3′ terminus can serve as a primer for subsequent repair DNA synthesis after it finds a complementary donor strand.

The processing involves coordinated nucleases. In mammalian cells the MRE11–RAD50–NBS1 complex, usually abbreviated MRN, helps recognize and organize broken DNA ends, and CtIP contributes to initiation of resection in appropriate contexts. Further processing extends the single-stranded regions as required. The proteins that initiate and extend resection are not all functionally interchangeable. At the present level the important principle is that the regulated reaction creates a primer-compatible single-stranded end and changes the substrate from one favorable for direct end joining to one that can participate in homologous interactions.

Exposed single-stranded DNA is itself chemically and structurally vulnerable. It can form secondary structures, become degraded or engage in inappropriate interactions. Replication protein A, abbreviated RPA, binds single-stranded DNA and helps protect it. Protection alone is not equivalent to finding a donor. The strand must eventually participate in a homology search, a function involving the recombinase RAD51. Accessory factors including BRCA2 help assemble RAD51 on the single-stranded substrate, replacing RPA in the appropriate molecular context. BRCA1 has important roles in the regulatory and processing events that favor homologous repair. The different roles of these proteins should not be collapsed into the statement that they are all DNA polymerases.

RAD51-associated DNA can interact with a homologous duplex and identify a region in which complementary base pairing is possible. One of the strands of the donor duplex is displaced locally as the incoming strand pairs with its complementary donor strand. The resulting structure includes a displacement loop. The free 3′ end of the invading strand can now be extended by DNA polymerase using donor sequence information. The broken recipient acquires new DNA complementary to the intact donor, which explains how lost information can be restored despite the original damage to both backbones.

The chemical reason that a 3′ single-stranded extension is useful is now apparent. Resection alone does not restore sequence information; it prepares a substrate for the homology search. Homology recognition alone does not replace the missing nucleotides; it brings a primer and template into productive alignment. Polymerization then performs the copying reaction, after which the repair intermediates must be processed and connected to restore continuous duplex DNA. Each stage solves a distinct problem, and the failure of any one of them can interrupt the overall process.

In a common noncrossover route, newly synthesized DNA can be displaced from the donor and anneal to a complementary region at the other end of the broken molecule. Further synthesis, removal of inappropriate flaps and ligation restore the recipient. This conceptual route is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing. Other forms of homologous repair involve joint molecules whose processing may generate different recombinant products. The detailed Holliday junction mechanisms, branch migration and crossover analysis are the subject of the following chapter. For the present purpose, it is enough to recognize that homologous repair can transfer local sequence information without exchanging whole chromosome arms.

A particularly valuable feature of this mechanism is that the donor molecule need not be sacrificed. It acts as a template, and the genetic information can remain present in the donor after the recipient is restored. This differs from simply taking an existing physical DNA fragment from one chromosome and inserting it into another. Polymerization can reproduce donor sequence information in the recipient while leaving the donor's sequence intact. However, the outcome depends on the route used to process the intermediate; the statement should not be interpreted as a guarantee that all homologous DNA transactions preserve chromosome arrangement.

10. Selecting a donor and choosing between repair pathways

The availability of a suitable template does not depend merely on whether the cell contains DNA with a similar sequence. A donor must also be accessible at the appropriate time, in an appropriate chromosomal context. In G1, before the relevant chromosomal region has been replicated, a sister chromatid is absent. Classical NHEJ therefore has a particularly important role in repairing ordinary two-ended breaks in this phase. In S and G2, sister chromatids become available, and regulated resection and homologous repair can contribute much more substantially. NHEJ nevertheless remains active during these phases. A cell-cycle diagram that assigns NHEJ exclusively to G1 and HR exclusively to G2 misrepresents the overlapping operation of the pathways.

Cell-cycle regulation controls the reactions that make a break suitable for template-directed repair. Cyclin-dependent kinase activity changes as cells progress through the cycle, affecting factors required for resection. In mammalian cells, regulators associated with end protection oppose extensive processing in some contexts, whereas BRCA1-associated regulation can favor resection and homologous repair when appropriate. At examination level, the key causal connection is that processing determines substrate availability. Protecting the double-stranded ends favors joining, while producing extended single-stranded 3′ tails favors homologous interactions. Regulation of the processing step thus influences pathway choice even before the final repair synthesis or ligation takes place.

The decision is not a conscious inspection of a list of repair options. It emerges from competition among molecular activities, their abundance and regulation, chromatin environment, break structure and timing. If an end-binding complex occupies a newly exposed duplex end, it can limit access by nucleases. If resection begins and removes a substantial region of one strand, the original ends are no longer simple joining substrates. The biochemical state of the DNA has changed, making some subsequent reactions more likely and others less favorable. This makes pathway choice a dynamic process rather than a rigid decision tree.

A one-ended DSB arising at a replication fork has a special requirement. Direct joining is attractive only if there is an appropriate second end to join. An unrelated end elsewhere in the genome does not solve the original replication problem and may create a rearrangement. Template-directed repair can instead use an intact homologous molecule to initiate recovery of the broken replication structure. The exact mechanisms of restart differ between organisms and contexts, but the simple topological reasoning remains valid. A broken fork requires restoration of an arrangement that allows synthesis to continue, not indiscriminate closure of whatever termini happen to be available.

Repeats in the genome complicate donor selection. Suppose a broken sequence belongs to a family of repeated elements and several other locations contain similar DNA. Pairing to a sequence at the wrong chromosomal location could transfer inappropriate information or promote a structural rearrangement. A high degree of complementarity is necessary for standard homologous repair, but it does not identify the correct genomic location by itself. Spatial organization and the association between sister chromatids help make the most appropriate donor available. This explains why template-directed repair is highly accurate under favorable conditions without being universally free from genetic consequences.

In a heterozygous diploid, homologous repair using the other parental chromosome can replace one allele with the corresponding sequence from the donor. Imagine a locus containing alleles A and a, where a break damages the chromosome carrying A. If the region is restored by copying from the homolog carrying a, both repaired and donor copies can carry a in the repaired interval. The cell has not necessarily lost an entire chromosome, but it has lost heterozygosity at that locus. The example is intentionally simple; the size of a converted region and its genetic consequences depend on the repair event. It illustrates how a chemically accurate copying reaction can nevertheless change genetic identity.

Repair pathway selection also has implications for experimental interpretation. If a cell population contains different proportions of G1 and S/G2 cells, a comparison of homologous repair reporter activity between two populations may partly reflect cell-cycle distribution rather than a direct alteration of repair chemistry. A mutant that slows progression through G1 could show reduced HR reporter activity even when its HR enzymes are normal. Comparing populations matched for cell-cycle phase or measuring their DNA content helps distinguish these possibilities. The principle is a recurring theme in Part C problems: a change in a measured outcome can have more than one plausible cause.

DNA damage also activates checkpoint signaling, which must be distinguished from the enzymes that directly repair the substrate. In a representative mammalian response, recognition of duplex breaks by MRN helps activate the protein kinase ATM; downstream signals, including CHK2-dependent responses, can alter cell-cycle progression. Extended RPA-coated single-stranded regions are important signals for ATR-associated responses, including CHK1 activation during replication stress. The precise contributions overlap and depend on context. A checkpoint can delay progression and reduce the danger of entering mitosis with unrepaired DNA. It does not itself replace missing nucleotides or ligate termini. Conversely, abolishing a checkpoint can permit continued cycling despite a physical repair defect. This distinction explains why repair success, checkpoint signaling and cell-cycle distribution need separate measurements in a genetic experiment.

11. Other outcomes at broken ends: alternative joining and annealing

The two major pathways do not exhaust the possible responses to a DSB. Cells possess other mechanisms that exploit limited sequence similarity or repeated sequences around a break. These routes are worth understanding at the level of principle because a question that presents an unusual deletion junction may otherwise be misclassified as either precise NHEJ or faithful sister-directed repair. The terminology varies with organism and experimental definition, so the structure of the intermediate and the final product should be examined first.

Alternative end joining can occur after limited resection exposes short complementary sequences near a break. The exposed regions can anneal, and incompatible flaps may be removed before remaining gaps are filled and the backbones sealed. If the matching sequences lie internal to the original termini, intervening nucleotides may be lost during processing. This creates a deletion whose junction may contain one copy of the matching sequence. The term microhomology-mediated end joining is often used for a class of such events. In mammalian systems, polymerase theta participates in an important alternative end-joining route, but not every observed microhomology-associated junction uniquely identifies polymerase theta activity.

Consider a simplified sequence arrangement on the same original strand: a short repeat M, a segment X, and another copy of repeat M. A break between the repeats followed by processing may expose complementary single-stranded regions that correspond to the two copies of M. If repair aligns those repeats and removes unpaired material, the product can contain a single copy of M and lack X together with the redundant repeat copy. Sequencing reveals a shortened junction. The existence of matching sequence at the junction helps explain how alignment occurred, but does not establish the complete enzyme pathway without genetic or biochemical evidence.

Single-strand annealing provides a related but distinct concept when more extensive direct repeats flank a DSB. Resection can expose complementary repeat sequences on opposite sides, which anneal. Nonpaired tails are processed, remaining gaps are filled as needed, and the DNA is ligated. The product characteristically loses the intervening sequence and one repeat copy. Because the pathway uses complementary sequences within the broken molecule, it is homology-dependent in a broad sense. It does not require the same RAD51-mediated invasion of an intact donor duplex that characterizes conventional sister-directed HR. Calling every homology-dependent process faithful homologous repair would therefore obscure an important mechanistic distinction.

For examination purposes the differences can be derived from the location of the template. In classical NHEJ, the original ends are brought together and processed as required without an extended homologous donor. In microhomology-associated alternative joining, short matching regions help connect processed ends. In single-strand annealing, flanking repeated sequences on the broken molecule provide an alignment opportunity, commonly at the cost of deleting DNA. In conventional HR, an intact homologous molecule supplies sequence information that may restore the missing interval. These strategies all solve a continuity problem, but the genetic products can be strikingly different.

An observed deletion can originate from more than one route. Classical NHEJ may generate deletions by terminal trimming; alternative end joining may produce deletions associated with internal matches; single-strand annealing may remove a large interval between repeats. Therefore, deletion size or the mere presence of short matching sequence should not be used as an absolute diagnostic criterion. A stronger conclusion combines junction sequences with pathway-specific genetic dependencies, appropriate reporter design, and controls for the structure of the starting break.

These alternatives also explain why disabling one pathway need not abolish every repair event. If classical NHEJ is impaired, some breaks may be repaired by homologous repair or alternative joining, depending on the cell-cycle phase and substrates. If HR is impaired, other routes may still restore continuity, sometimes with altered sequence outcomes. A survival assay may thus reveal incomplete sensitivity, while a sequencing assay reveals a substantial change in the distribution of repaired products. Both observations can be consistent with a significant defect in one particular pathway.

12. Bacterial break repair and the conservation of template-guided recovery

The principles of strand-break repair apply across living organisms, but the available proteins and the architecture of the chromosomes vary. Escherichia coli is a useful model because its double-strand break repair machinery has been extensively studied and because many of its genetic features can be related directly to classical experiments. It is also a reminder that a bacterium does not possess mammalian G1, S and G2 phases. The ability of a bacterium to use homologous DNA depends on its replication state, chromosome copy number and other cellular conditions, rather than on the names of eukaryotic cell-cycle phases.

A major bacterial route for processing double-stranded DNA ends in E. coli involves the RecBCD complex. This protein assembly combines DNA-unwinding and nuclease-associated functions. As it engages a double-stranded DNA end, it processes the DNA and can alter its activity upon recognition of a Chi sequence in an appropriate orientation. The resulting substrate can support loading of RecA on a 3′ single-stranded region. A useful consequence of this organization is that detection of the end, processing of the substrate and initiation of homologous repair can be coordinated by a relatively integrated bacterial system. It would be misleading to identify RecBCD as a DNA ligase: the complex prepares a substrate for later steps rather than directly restoring a missing phosphodiester bond.

RecA is a bacterial recombinase whose DNA-associated state enables homologous pairing and strand exchange. It has a functional relationship to the eukaryotic recombinase RAD51, although the proteins are not identical and their accessory machinery differs. E. coli uses RecA-associated processes to recover DNA information from homologous templates and to support restoration of damaged replication structures. The detailed base-pairing transactions and recombinant products are addressed in the next chapter. Here, the relevant point is that break processing followed by access to homologous sequence provides a way to preserve the original information even when the damaged duplex cannot itself supply it.

The structure of the starting DNA determines which initial processing system can operate. A broken duplex end is not the same substrate as a single-stranded gap within an otherwise continuous daughter molecule. In E. coli, RecBCD is strongly associated with processing of duplex ends, whereas RecFOR-associated processes contribute to RecA loading at gaps and other single-stranded regions. This distinction is useful because both types of substrate can ultimately require homologous DNA interactions. Finding a RecA-dependent phenotype alone does not establish whether the initial lesion was a duplex break or a single-stranded daughter gap.

In rapidly growing bacterial cells, ongoing replication can generate multiple copies of some genomic regions even though the organism is conventionally described as haploid. Those copies may provide opportunities for homologous repair. In other circumstances, such as a cell with no accessible intact copy of a damaged region, the feasibility of the same pathway can be reduced. Ploidy and template availability must therefore be treated as separate ideas. Describing a bacterium as haploid concerns the normal genetic organization of the organism; it does not prove that every region of its genome is present in exactly one physical copy at all times.

Some bacteria have a form of non-homologous end joining involving a bacterial Ku protein and a multifunctional LigD protein or related components. This system can be valuable when homologous templates are limited, for example in certain nonreplicating bacterial cells. The bacterial system does not require the full mammalian Ku70/Ku80–DNA-PKcs–XRCC4–ligase IV assembly. The shared functional challenge is end recognition, processing and joining, but the molecular solutions differ. E. coli is not a standard example of the bacterial Ku–LigD pathway, so it is important not to generalize the existence of this pathway to all bacteria.

The evolutionary comparison reveals a distinction between conservation of a function and conservation of an entire pathway. The chemistry of phosphodiester bond formation and the need for an appropriately placed 3′ hydroxyl are broadly shared. Homologous pairing is also widespread. Nevertheless, the proteins that deliver these activities, their regulation and their relative importance can differ substantially. An examination question that uses an unfamiliar organism should be solved using the given pathway components and experimental evidence, rather than assuming that the complete mammalian model is universally applicable.

13. Replication arrest is not the same event as chemical damage removal

The high fidelity of normal replicative DNA polymerases depends partly on their ability to reject incorrect nucleotide–template geometries. A replicative polymerase selects substrates in a tightly organized catalytic site, and some replicative polymerases also proofread newly synthesized DNA. These properties minimize mutations on ordinary templates. However, the structural selectivity that confers accuracy also makes many chemical lesions difficult for the enzyme to traverse. A bulky adduct or an abnormal linkage between neighboring template bases may prevent the template from adopting the geometry required for normal synthesis.

A replication fork contains more than one molecular activity. Helicase opens the parental duplex, polymerases extend the daughter strands, and accessory proteins coordinate the movement of these components. When DNA synthesis encounters an obstacle, the response can involve changes in the coupling between unwinding and polymerization. Exposed single-stranded DNA can accumulate. This creates a substrate for single-stranded DNA-binding proteins and damage-response signaling. The fork may slow, undergo remodeling, or leave a gap that is filled subsequently. The precise architecture is context dependent, but the general distinction between stalling of polymerization and physical breakage of the DNA remains important.

A chemically modified template can be completely continuous before replication. If a polymerase cannot extend across the modified region, the daughter strand can remain incomplete even though the parental backbone is intact. The chemical lesion and the daughter-strand discontinuity are therefore separate features. One can measure the persistence of the lesion in the parental template independently of the length or completeness of the newly synthesized DNA. This separation is essential for recognizing DNA damage tolerance.

Damage removal directly changes the damaged substrate. Photolyase, where present and applicable, reverses a specific photochemical lesion. Excision repair removes a damaged base or segment and replaces DNA using an intact complement. In contrast, damage tolerance enables DNA synthesis to proceed despite the lesion. A template can retain its chemical modification after a successful tolerance event. The lesion may be repaired later, persist, or contribute to further problems when the DNA is used again. Bypass is an immediate solution to a synthesis problem, not a guarantee of eventual chemical restoration.

One benefit of tolerance is that it reduces the time for which replication remains obstructed. Persistent replication arrest can lead to incomplete chromosome duplication, activation of checkpoint responses and potentially more severe lesions. A cell that has a way to complete a daughter strand despite a damaged template can sometimes preserve chromosome integrity and survive until repair is possible. The cost is that synthesis across some lesions is less accurate than synthesis across undamaged DNA. When assessing these pathways, survival and mutation frequency must be considered separately: a pathway can increase one while also increasing the other.

The word tolerance has a precise meaning here and should not be confused with an assertion that the cell is unaffected by damage. A cell can have improved ability to continue replication without removing more chemical lesions per unit time. Conversely, a cell can remove lesions effectively yet have trouble replicating when even a small number remain. In an experiment where one population has normal lesion removal but defective post-damage DNA synthesis, a tolerance defect is a plausible explanation. To establish that conclusion, measurements must distinguish lesion persistence from newly synthesized DNA and from simple changes in cell viability.

A lesion that strongly distorts the template may have very different effects on different polymerases. The polymerase that excels at copying normal DNA need not be the polymerase that performs best on a particular altered substrate. Evolution has produced specialized polymerases with active-site properties that allow some otherwise difficult template geometries to be accommodated. This specialization illustrates a common trade-off in enzyme function: optimizing recognition of the ordinary substrate does not necessarily optimize activity on every unusual substrate. The cell can reduce the cost of this trade-off by restricting the access of specialized enzymes to situations where they are needed.

14. Translesion DNA synthesis: polymerase switching and lesion specificity

Translesion DNA synthesis, abbreviated TLS, refers to synthesis that uses a damaged DNA template directly and proceeds past a lesion that obstructs normal replication. A specialized polymerase gains access to a primer–template junction, incorporates a nucleotide opposite or near the lesion, and can extend the resulting primer far enough to permit subsequent synthesis. Depending on the lesion and the polymerase involved, insertion and extension may be accomplished by the same enzyme or by different specialized enzymes. Normal replicative polymerization can resume when an appropriate, relatively undistorted primer–template structure has been re-established.

TLS polymerases still synthesize DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. They require a suitable primer terminus and nucleotide substrates. They do not reverse the ordinary chemistry of DNA synthesis in order to cross a lesion. Instead, their catalytic sites and interactions with DNA differ in ways that permit use of some structurally altered templates. Many specialized Y-family polymerases lack intrinsic 3′ to 5′ proofreading activity and have lower fidelity than replicative polymerases on ordinary DNA. Their structural flexibility can be beneficial at a particular lesion but costly if they are allowed to copy extensive regions of undamaged DNA.

A well-established example is human DNA polymerase eta, a Y-family polymerase encoded by POLH. Ultraviolet radiation can produce a cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer that links neighboring pyrimidines within one strand. A common example involves adjacent thymine bases. The altered arrangement presents a challenge to ordinary replicative polymerases. Polymerase eta has structural features that allow it to accommodate a cis-syn cyclobutane thymine dimer and frequently insert the correct adenine bases opposite the two thymine positions. The original photoproduct remains in the template during this process. The accurate copying event therefore represents lesion bypass rather than reversal of the photochemical reaction.

This example is especially important because it corrects a widespread misconception about TLS. It is often summarized as error-prone synthesis, but accuracy depends on the particular polymerase–lesion combination. Polymerase eta is relatively well suited to certain UV-induced dimers and can reduce mutagenesis compared with less suitable alternative bypass activities. The same polymerase is not a high-fidelity replacement for a replicative polymerase on all undamaged DNA. A specialized enzyme may therefore protect the genome in one context and increase the risk of copying errors in another.

A related human genetic observation concerns xeroderma pigmentosum variant, abbreviated XP-V. Cells with defective POLH have difficulty carrying out the appropriate form of lesion bypass after UV exposure. Classical xeroderma pigmentosum groups associated with nucleotide excision repair defects have a different immediate molecular problem: impaired removal of photoproduct-containing DNA segments. Both defects can increase sensitivity to UV-related damage and the risk of mutagenesis, but they should not be assigned to the same biochemical step simply because the clinical names are related. Comparing the persistence of UV photoproducts and the completion of newly synthesized DNA helps distinguish the two mechanisms.

Other eukaryotic TLS components illustrate the division of labor between insertion and extension. Some polymerases are effective at inserting opposite a difficult base but less effective at extending from the resulting primer. Polymerase zeta can contribute to extension from certain distorted primer termini. REV1 can participate through both catalytic and organizational functions in appropriate settings. The exact combination of enzymes depends on the lesion and the organism. At NET level, a useful conclusion is that TLS may involve sequential polymerase exchange rather than the permanent substitution of one polymerase for another.

The return to a replicative polymerase after bypass is biologically significant. If the specialized enzyme were to continue copying large amounts of normal template, its lower fidelity on that substrate could impose unnecessary mutational costs. A brief change of polymerase activity at the obstructed site allows the cell to retain the accuracy and efficiency of normal replication over most of the genome. Regulation of access, retention and release is consequently as important as the catalytic capacity of the bypass enzyme itself.

TLS can also take place after an initial replication event leaves a daughter-strand gap. The specialized polymerase may act at that gap rather than traveling continuously with the main replication fork throughout the entire process. In either case, what defines TLS is use of the lesion-containing template at the bypass step, not the precise position of the polymerase relative to the moving fork. Examination questions sometimes present a gap behind a fork and ask whether DNA damage tolerance can occur there. The answer is yes, provided that the gap-filling reaction uses the appropriate tolerance mechanism.

A useful way to compare two polymerases experimentally is to measure extension on otherwise identical DNA substrates, one containing a defined lesion and one without it. A replicative polymerase may produce a longer product on the undamaged substrate but stop immediately before the lesion. A specialized polymerase may produce a longer product across the damaged region while being less accurate on the normal substrate. Product length reveals whether synthesis continued; sequence analysis reveals which bases were incorporated. The combination is necessary to distinguish successful bypass from accurate bypass.

Masutani and colleagues examined XP-V extracts in a cell-free synthesis assay and isolated an activity from HeLa cells that restored copying across a UV-induced thymine dimer. Their 1999 studies linked this correcting activity to a specialized DNA polymerase, now called polymerase eta. The crucial comparison was not simply whether a polymerase could synthesize some DNA, but whether the enzyme could extend a primer across a substrate containing the particular obstruction. A damaged template without the correcting activity provided a comparison for the bypass defect. The finding demonstrated why a cell can retain nucleotide excision repair yet display abnormal DNA synthesis after irradiation. Cell-free correction establishes an ability to carry out the biochemical reaction; it does not by itself measure the complete regulation of polymerase access within living cells.

15. Template switching: bypass without copying the damaged base

An alternative way to complete a newly synthesized DNA strand is to obtain information from a template that does not contain the obstructing lesion. During semiconservative replication, the two parental strands are separated and each serves as a template for a newly synthesized complementary strand. If one parental strand contains a lesion while the corresponding region of the other parental strand is undamaged, copying of the undamaged strand can produce a new DNA segment that contains useful sequence information. The blocked daughter strand may temporarily exploit that information rather than attempt to copy the damaged parental nucleotide directly. This is the conceptual basis of template switching during DNA damage tolerance.

The term template switching can be understood by keeping track of strand polarity. Suppose a parental strand contains a damaged thymine position. On the opposite parental strand, adenine occupies the corresponding position. Copying this undamaged adenine produces a thymine in the newly synthesized sister DNA. The blocked daughter strand needs an adenine at its own corresponding position. If it gains access to the newly synthesized sister strand containing thymine, it can obtain the appropriate adenine without using the damaged original thymine as the immediate template. The alternative template is not simply any nearby DNA strand; it must have the sequence orientation and complementarity required for correct extension.

Several related mechanisms can facilitate access to a sister-derived sequence during replication stress. One conceptual model involves remodeling of a replication fork so that newly synthesized strands can interact, allowing a blocked primer end to use a sister-derived template. Other models emphasize repair of daughter-strand gaps through homologous DNA transactions behind the fork. The detailed intermediates depend on the organism and the context. It would therefore be misleading to state that every template-switch event requires one particular fork shape. The shared feature is that synthesis obtains information from a relatively undamaged DNA template instead of forcing a polymerase to accommodate the original lesion.

Template switching is sometimes described as an error-free branch of damage tolerance. The description expresses the advantage of obtaining nucleotide information from an undamaged and appropriately homologous template. It does not mean that the pathway can never create a genetic alteration. Incorrect alignment within repeated sequences or abnormal processing of DNA intermediates may have genetic consequences. A faithful sequence-copying event can also fail to eliminate the original lesion. A distinction must be maintained between preservation of sequence accuracy in the newly synthesized molecule and removal of the chemical abnormality in the parental molecule.

TLS and template switching have different immediate substrates at the critical copying step. TLS works directly on a damaged template. Template switching avoids copying the lesion by obtaining the corresponding information elsewhere. Both may produce a completed daughter strand, and both can reduce the consequences of replication arrest. A measurement of daughter-strand completion alone may therefore fail to distinguish them. If a researcher can introduce a donor-specific marker or selectively alter the availability of an alternative template, the results can become more informative about how the new strand was produced.

Template switching should also be distinguished from homologous repair of a DSB. Both processes may employ homologous DNA interactions and transfer information from an alternative template. However, their immediate structural problems differ. In a typical tolerance event, a lesion blocks synthesis while parental DNA continuity can remain intact; the objective is to complete the daughter strand. In conventional DSB repair, both original backbones are interrupted and the objective is to restore chromosome continuity and sequence. An event may progress from one problem to the other if a stalled fork collapses. The classification should reflect the substrate at the stage under discussion, not merely the presence of a recombination protein.

16. Regulation of damage tolerance in eukaryotic cells

The proliferating cell nuclear antigen, usually called PCNA, is a sliding clamp that interacts with DNA and multiple DNA-metabolizing proteins. During ordinary replication it helps maintain productive association between polymerase and primer–template DNA. Its importance extends beyond processivity because it also provides an interaction platform for proteins that act when the replication machinery encounters difficulties. A change in PCNA-associated proteins can change what reaction is favored at the DNA without requiring the cell to dismantle every component of the replication apparatus.

The attachment of ubiquitin to PCNA provides a standard example of regulation by post-translational modification. In well-studied eukaryotic systems, the RAD6–RAD18 ubiquitination machinery promotes addition of a single ubiquitin to a conserved lysine in PCNA, commonly designated Lys164. This modification can facilitate recruitment or retention of specialized TLS polymerases that recognize PCNA-associated signals. Ubiquitination in this setting is not equivalent to marking PCNA for immediate destruction by the proteasome. Ubiquitin is a versatile regulatory signal, and the consequence depends on the substrate, the type of modification and the proteins that recognize it.

Extension of the ubiquitin modification into certain chains, classically associated with Lys63-linked ubiquitin chains in the budding yeast model, is associated with promotion of template-switching responses. The two forms of modification provide a useful introductory framework for understanding how protein modifications influence the choice of damage tolerance routes. Mammalian pathway regulation is more complex, and the relationship is not an absolute binary switch in every context. A student should understand the association of monoubiquitination with TLS and certain chain extensions with template-switching pathways without assuming that a single modification uniquely determines every event.

Why should the cell regulate specialized polymerases through access rather than synthesize every polymerase anew whenever replication stalls? Rapid control at the DNA can alter enzyme selection without waiting for a large change in transcription and translation. The specialized enzyme need not replace all normal replicative polymerases throughout the nucleus. It can be favored at particular replication-associated structures. This arrangement helps limit the potential mutational cost of low-fidelity synthesis on ordinary DNA while retaining access to bypass activities at damaged sites.

A mutation that prevents a particular regulatory modification can have a phenotype even when the specialized polymerase retains normal activity in a purified biochemical assay. The enzyme's catalytic capacity is not the same as its probability of reaching the appropriate substrate in a cell. Conversely, overexpression of a specialized polymerase might partially compensate for a recruitment defect under some conditions without repairing the regulatory mechanism itself. Such compensation should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that the regulator and polymerase perform identical reactions.

A hypothetical experiment could compare wild-type cells, a cell line with a PCNA modification-site mutation, and a line defective in POLH. Each population could be examined for normal replication, synthesis after UV exposure and persistence of photoproducts. If the two mutants both show defective synthesis after UV while displaying similar photoproduct removal to wild type, the data would be consistent with defects affecting tolerance. Yet the mechanisms could differ: one may impair regulation of several tolerance pathways, whereas the other impairs a particular lesion-bypass activity. The three measurements together provide more information than a single survival value.

This example links damage tolerance to concepts studied elsewhere in the course. Protein modifications can act as signals that alter interactions, rather than merely increasing or decreasing enzyme abundance. Enzymes with different substrate preferences can cooperate through temporary association with a common molecular platform. Cellular regulation can therefore determine pathway output even when the underlying catalytic reactions are unchanged. The same principles recur in signal transduction, cell-cycle regulation and metabolic control, although the specific molecular components differ.

17. The bacterial SOS response: evidence and regulatory logic

The bacterial SOS response is a well-studied example of inducible regulation during DNA damage. In E. coli, this response coordinates expression of genes involved in DNA repair, replication-associated recovery, cell-division control and damage tolerance. It is not a single DNA repair enzyme or a single lesion-removal reaction. It is a regulatory program that changes the cellular availability of multiple activities in response to signals associated with DNA damage.

An important historical observation was that DNA damage could induce cellular changes extending beyond the immediate chemical alteration of DNA. Studies of bacterial responses to ultraviolet radiation, mutagenesis and prophage induction helped establish the idea that damaged DNA could trigger a regulated response. Genetic studies involving recA and lexA were particularly informative because alterations of these genes changed whether inducible functions were expressed. The modern understanding distinguishes RecA's role in DNA transactions from its role in stimulating the regulatory event that relieves repression of SOS genes.

Under ordinary growth conditions, LexA binds operator regions associated with numerous SOS-regulated genes and limits their transcription. Repression does not necessarily mean that expression is zero; basal levels vary among genes. When DNA synthesis encounters damage or other circumstances generate persistent single-stranded DNA, RecA can assemble on the exposed strand in an ATP-associated active state. This RecA-containing nucleoprotein structure stimulates the self-cleavage reaction of LexA. Cleavage reduces the concentration of functional repressor available to occupy its operators, allowing increased transcription of the regulated genes.

It is important to describe the cleavage reaction accurately. RecA is commonly referred to as a coprotease in this regulatory context because it promotes the self-cleavage of LexA. LexA supplies the catalytic machinery for its own cleavage. RecA does not simply act as an ordinary protease that indiscriminately cuts unrelated proteins. The dependence on DNA-associated RecA helps couple regulatory changes to the presence of an appropriate DNA signal. Without the signal, LexA can remain functional and maintain repression of its target genes.

The regulated genes do not all have identical functions. Some contribute to the removal of damaged DNA, some participate in recombinational recovery, and some support tolerance of remaining lesions. In E. coli, SulA can inhibit cell division by interfering with FtsZ assembly. This can give the cell additional time before attempting division with incompletely replicated or damaged chromosomes. SulA does not seal DNA breaks, remove photoproducts or synthesize replacement DNA. Its protective effect concerns coordination of cell division with genome status.

The temporal pattern of SOS induction is also informative. Different LexA-regulated operators and additional regulatory mechanisms can produce differences in the timing and extent of gene expression. Many repair-associated functions can be induced before strong expression or activation of some mutagenic bypass activities. A cell can therefore first attempt to reduce damage through repair and later increase access to tolerance routes if the problem persists. The exact time course depends on the damage, growth conditions and particular genes, so fixed universal induction times should not be memorized.

As recovery proceeds and persistent single-stranded DNA declines, the signal that favors RecA's regulatory activity diminishes. Newly synthesized LexA can again accumulate in a functional form and restore repression. This creates a reversible response with negative feedback rather than a permanent change in the genetic constitution of the bacterium. The distinction between regulated expression and mutation is important: an SOS-induced cell can transiently alter protein abundance without acquiring a permanent mutation, although some of the induced pathways can increase the probability of mutation.

A noncleavable LexA mutant illustrates the logic of the system. If the mutant repressor still binds its operators but cannot undergo the normal self-cleavage reaction, damage-associated RecA activity will not efficiently relieve repression. The genes controlled by LexA will fail to display the usual induced expression pattern, even though some constitutive repair activities remain. A different mutant with impaired RecA function may also fail to induce the response, but in addition may have defects in homologous recombination. These mutants can show similar regulatory phenotypes for distinct mechanistic reasons.

A constitutively derepressed system provides a complementary comparison. If functional LexA repression is lost, many SOS-regulated genes may be expressed even in the absence of the normal damage signal. This does not mean that every induced enzyme is automatically active, because some activities require additional molecular conditions. Nor does it mean that the DNA must be damaged at the time of measurement. A reporter under the control of a LexA-regulated promoter is a measure of regulatory state, not a direct count of DNA breaks or damaged bases. This distinction is essential when interpreting genetic reporter experiments.

18. SOS-associated polymerases and the relationship between survival and mutation

The E. coli SOS response provides an especially clear example of regulated access to different DNA polymerases. The main replicative polymerase is DNA polymerase III, but several other polymerases contribute to damage-associated synthesis. Pol II, encoded by polB, has proofreading activity and can participate in recovery from particular disruptions of DNA synthesis. Pol IV, encoded by dinB, is a specialized Y-family polymerase that can bypass certain lesions. Pol V, whose principal protein components are processed UmuD and UmuC, participates in bypass of a variety of obstructing DNA lesions. Their biochemical properties differ, and it is inaccurate to describe all of them as identical low-fidelity polymerases.

The activation of Pol V illustrates why gene expression and enzyme activity are distinct. Expression of umuDC is subject to LexA-dependent regulation, but newly produced UmuD also undergoes a RecA-facilitated self-cleavage reaction, generating a processed form called UmuD′. The processed UmuD′ protein contributes to formation of the Pol V complex with UmuC. Productive bypass activity also depends on interaction with RecA-associated regulatory machinery and ATP. At the level of examination reasoning, the crucial point is that removal of repression does not automatically cause maximal active Pol V to appear instantaneously. Multiple levels of control help restrict use of a potentially mutagenic activity.

If a cell is unable to remove a persistent replication-blocking lesion, it may face a choice between failure to complete DNA replication and using an alternative copying reaction that risks a sequence error. A lesion-bypass polymerase can improve survival by allowing more DNA molecules to be completed. Some completed molecules may contain a wrong base opposite the lesion or an altered short sequence. If that altered sequence escapes subsequent correction and is copied in another replication cycle, the change can become fixed in the progeny. The mutation is therefore a possible consequence of the copying event, not an obligatory consequence of the original DNA lesion.

A useful way to reason about bacterial SOS mutagenesis is to separate the number of viable survivors from the fraction of survivors carrying mutations. Suppose one genotype survives a DNA insult poorly but most survivors carry the original sequence at a test locus. A second genotype survives more frequently but has a greater fraction of mutant survivors. The second genotype may possess a tolerance pathway that permits survival at the cost of greater mutagenesis. Neither survival nor mutation frequency alone can establish whether the tolerance route has been beneficial in terms of the preservation of genetic information. Both measurements have to be interpreted in relation to their denominators.

The presence of an induced mutagenic pathway does not imply that the cell deliberately creates useful mutations. Mutations produced during imperfect bypass are not selected in advance for their usefulness. Natural selection can subsequently enrich cells carrying variants that confer an advantage under particular conditions. This separation between generation of variation and selection of variants is central to evolutionary genetics. The SOS example provides a molecular explanation of why the supply of mutations may change with cellular stress without requiring a directed mutation mechanism aimed at producing a predetermined beneficial sequence.

It is also possible for a defect in specialized bypass to reduce mutation frequency at a particular locus while reducing survival. If cells with the most obstructive lesions die instead of undergoing bypass, those cells no longer contribute to a measurement of mutation frequency among survivors. This creates a selection effect in the experimental population. A lower observed mutation fraction should not automatically be interpreted as better repair. It can reflect loss of cells that would otherwise have survived with sequence alterations, changes in which lesions were bypassed, or differences in the number of replication cycles completed before sampling.

19. Quantitative reasoning: keeping different endpoints separate

Many repair questions become straightforward when the measured endpoint and the appropriate denominator are identified first. Let N0 denote the number of viable cells initially subjected to a damage treatment, with the initial viability assessed using a comparable untreated population. Let Ns denote the number of viable survivors after the treatment. The survival fraction S is Ns divided by N0. It is dimensionless because both quantities are cell counts. Multiplication by 100 expresses it as a percentage. If a treated population yields 240 viable colonies for an equivalent untreated input yielding 1,200 colonies, the estimated survival fraction is 240/1,200 = 0.20, or 20 percent. This interpretation assumes that the colony counts are comparable estimates of viable starting and surviving units and that differences in plating efficiency or later growth are not confounding the comparison.

Now suppose Nm denotes the number of survivors carrying a detectable mutation at a reporter locus. The observed mutant fraction among survivors is f = Nm/Ns. The number of mutant survivors relative to the original input is m = Nm/N0. Since Nm/N0 can be rewritten as (Ns/N0) × (Nm/Ns), it follows that m = S × f. This identity simply separates survival from the conditional mutant fraction. It does not imply that every cell has the same probability of survival or mutation, nor does it estimate a mutation rate per generation. Mutation rate estimation requires a model of when mutations arise and how descendants accumulate.

Consider two hypothetical populations of 100,000 initially viable cells. Population A produces 20,000 survivors, among which 40 are mutant at the reporter. Population B produces 60,000 survivors, among which 300 are mutant. For A, survival is 0.20 and the mutant fraction among survivors is 40/20,000 = 0.002. For B, survival is 0.60 and the mutant fraction is 300/60,000 = 0.005. B therefore has three times the survival fraction and 2.5 times the observed mutant fraction among survivors. Relative to the original input, A produces 0.0004 mutant survivors per initial cell and B produces 0.003, a 7.5-fold difference. The numbers are illustrative, not measurements from a published experiment. They demonstrate how conclusions can change when the denominator changes.

Break disappearance can also be described quantitatively using a simple first-order approximation. Suppose B(t) is the average number of unrepaired breaks per cell at time t, and the rate of disappearance of breaks is proportional to their remaining number. If no additional breaks form during the observation period, the model is dB/dt = −kB, where k is a positive rate constant with units of inverse time. Separating variables gives dB/B = −k dt. Integration from an initial value B0 at time zero gives ln(B/B0) = −kt. Rearranging gives B(t) = B0 exp(−kt). The predicted half-time is the time when B(t)/B0 = 1/2, so t½ = ln(2)/k.

For example, if a hypothetical cell population has an average of 80 detectable breaks per cell immediately after damage and 20 after two hours, the model gives 20/80 = 0.25 = exp(−2k). Taking natural logarithms yields k = ln(4)/2 ≈ 0.693 h⁻¹. The apparent half-time is ln(2)/0.693 ≈ 1 hour. After three hours the model predicts 80 × (1/2)^3 = 10 breaks per cell. This is a prediction under a simplified model, not an experimentally established constant for any repair pathway.

Real repair curves may depart substantially from a single exponential. Different classes of DNA ends can have different processing requirements, cells may vary in cell-cycle phase, new damage may arise during the observation period, and an assay signal may persist after a physical break has been sealed. A rapid initial decline followed by a slower component might reflect a mixture of easier and harder substrates rather than a sudden change in the catalytic activity of one enzyme. A model with two independent first-order components would have the form B(t) = Bf exp(−kf t) + Bs exp(−ks t), where the subscripts denote initial fast and slow components. This is useful as a descriptive model, but the mere ability to fit two exponentials does not identify two molecular pathways.

A further caution concerns average values. If the mean number of detectable breaks per cell falls from ten to five, it does not follow that each cell has repaired exactly five breaks. Some cells may have repaired nearly all their damage, while others retain many breaks. An average can hide substantial heterogeneity, and the biological outcome may depend particularly on the subpopulation with persistent damage. A cell with a single strategically located unrepaired DSB can have a different fate from another cell with several readily repaired lesions. Counting molecular events is valuable, but their distribution and context are part of the interpretation.

For repair-pathway reporters, the relevant denominator is often the number of viable, successfully manipulated cells eligible to produce a measurable signal. Suppose 800 of 4,000 such cells show a restored fluorescence reporter. The apparent reporter-positive fraction is 20 percent. If another population produces 500 positive cells from 2,000 eligible cells, the fraction is 25 percent even though the absolute number of fluorescent cells is smaller. Comparing raw counts would favor the first population, while comparing fractions favors the second. The correct comparison depends on what the question asks and whether the denominator has been measured reliably. If the two reporters detect different subsets of products, their percentages cannot automatically be added to estimate all repair.

20. Experimental interpretation: from damage measurements to causal conclusions

A direct measurement of DNA sequence at a repaired junction is particularly informative about local genetic outcome. It can reveal a deletion, insertion, exact restoration or transfer of a donor-specific marker. However, it does not necessarily reveal the complete pathway that produced the junction. Different repair mechanisms can converge on similar sequences. Conversely, two cells using the same pathway may produce different products because their original ends were not identical. To infer mechanism, sequencing evidence is strongest when combined with known starting substrates, appropriate genetic perturbations and suitable controls.

DNA break assays provide a complementary view. In a comet assay, DNA migration during electrophoresis can be used as an indicator of damage-related changes in DNA integrity. Alkaline conditions reveal single-strand discontinuities and alkali-labile sites as well as other contributions to migration; neutral conditions are commonly used to investigate double-strand break-associated damage. These measurements do not automatically identify the molecular identity of every lesion. Experimental conditions and calibration influence interpretation. A reduction in a comet tail can support recovery of DNA integrity, but it does not demonstrate that every restored junction retains its original sequence.

The phosphorylation of histone H2AX near DNA damage in mammalian cells produces γH2AX, a commonly used marker of DNA damage signaling. Immunofluorescence can reveal discrete nuclear regions enriched for this modified histone. A rise in focus number or intensity after damage is consistent with activation of damage-associated signaling. However, γH2AX is a chromatin response rather than a direct count of broken phosphodiester bonds. Foci can persist after some repair events, multiple lesions can contribute to one focus, and other cellular contexts may generate the signal. Therefore, a decrease in γH2AX foci is not sufficient by itself to prove that accurate repair occurred.

A hypothetical comparison illustrates the value of combining measurements. Suppose cell type X exhibits rapid disappearance of break-associated foci but extensive deletions at a reporter junction. Cell type Y exhibits slower disappearance of the foci but predominantly accurate reporter restoration. It would be misleading to conclude that X is necessarily superior at genome maintenance simply because the marker disappears more rapidly. X may reconnect damaged ends quickly while losing information. Y may take longer to restore the original sequence. The comparison requires independent assessment of timing, sequence fidelity and survival.

Another useful experimental approach is to examine proteins at sites of damage. Accumulation of a repair factor can indicate recruitment or retention at an abnormal DNA structure. If an upstream factor is deleted and recruitment of a downstream factor decreases, this can support a dependency in the recruitment pathway. However, accumulation does not prove successful catalytic activity. A catalytically defective enzyme can sometimes remain associated with a site longer than the active enzyme because the reaction does not progress. In such a case, more prominent foci could accompany worse repair. Localization data should therefore be interpreted alongside functional outcomes.

To examine DNA damage tolerance specifically, a researcher can compare lesion persistence with synthesis of daughter DNA. Suppose two populations receive the same initial UV insult. A lesion-specific assay indicates similar removal of photoproducts over time, but one population has much shorter newly synthesized DNA segments following treatment. The difference is consistent with impaired recovery of synthesis in that population. If reintroduction of the relevant specialized polymerase restores synthesis without changing lesion removal, the case for a bypass defect is strengthened. It remains important to exclude altered cell-cycle composition or reduced general replication capacity as alternative explanations.

Controls should address the simplest alternatives to the proposed mechanism. An untreated control provides the baseline for ordinary DNA synthesis and survival. A damaged wild-type population establishes the normal response under the test conditions. A genetically corrected mutant helps distinguish the effect of the candidate gene from unrelated differences in the mutant population. A catalytically inactive rescue protein may distinguish a requirement for enzyme activity from a structural role. Measurements of cell-cycle distribution help interpret experiments comparing end joining and HR. No single control removes every possible ambiguity, but each addresses a specific causal question.

21. Biological significance and applications of pathway choice

Genome maintenance is not limited to avoiding point mutations. Chromosome continuity is essential for reliable inheritance, and preservation of the original sequence is essential for retaining functional genetic information. Strand-break repair occupies the intersection of these two requirements. A mechanism that rejoins a chromosome can prevent loss of a large chromosome fragment while occasionally introducing a local change. A mechanism that copies a sister-derived template can recover sequence information, but it requires suitable donor DNA and a regulated series of molecular transactions. The balance between these strategies helps explain why cells retain multiple pathways rather than relying on a single universal repair reaction.

Programmed DSB formation in lymphocytes shows that DNA breaks can serve a biological function when they are carefully controlled. During antigen receptor gene rearrangement, selected DNA segments must be brought together in new combinations. Classical end joining helps connect the appropriate processed ends. The same general machinery that protects chromosomes after accidental damage is therefore used in a regulated developmental context. This connection will be important in immunology, where the generation of receptor diversity is considered in greater detail. Failure of appropriate joining can prevent production of functional receptors even if other DNA repair mechanisms remain active.

The distinction between end joining and donor-directed repair is also useful for understanding the products of genome editing. A targeted DNA break can be restored by joining that preserves or changes the local sequence, or by a donor-directed mechanism that copies intended sequence information when a suitable donor and cellular conditions are available. Editing outcomes are not determined solely by the enzyme that initially makes the break. The repair activities of the cell influence which products appear. A cut without any alteration at the final junction is a possible outcome, as is a change that does not eliminate gene function. A claim about the effect of an editing event therefore requires assessment of the final DNA sequence and, when relevant, gene expression or protein activity.

In cancer biology, defects in genome-maintenance pathways can allow abnormal chromosome rearrangements and changes in nucleotide sequence to accumulate. The outcome is not inevitable for every damaged cell: checkpoint arrest, successful repair or cell death can prevent transmission of serious damage. The loss of a repair pathway can create a dependence on another pathway that supports survival. The general genetic concept of synthetic lethality describes a situation in which either of two individual defects is compatible with viability but their combination is not. A standard example concerns the relationship between homologous repair deficiency and inhibition of PARP-associated functions in suitable cellular contexts. Such examples illustrate pathway interdependence; they do not establish a universal clinical response or reduce the actions of PARP inhibitors to a single simple chemical event.

The same principles apply to plant cells and microorganisms without requiring that every organism possess the same set of proteins. Exposure to environmental radiation, endogenous metabolic products and replication-associated stress challenges chromosome integrity throughout biology. Selection favors mechanisms that allow cells to maintain genomes under these conditions. The relative importance of a given pathway depends on life history, replication status, genome organization and environmental pressures. A broad biological comparison is most informative when it identifies the conserved problem and then distinguishes the organism-specific solution.

The transition to homologous recombination follows directly from this discussion. Template-guided repair depends on recognition of homologous sequence and productive interactions between separate DNA molecules. Understanding exactly how recombinases promote those interactions, how joint DNA intermediates are processed and how different genetic products arise requires a closer examination of strand exchange. That deeper topic builds upon the chemical and cellular foundations established here: a compatible 3′ primer, a reliable template, regulated DNA processing and the distinction between restoration of chromosome continuity and preservation of genetic identity.

  2. Obligatory chromosome loss
  3. No sequence change possible
  4. RNA editing
Answer and reasoning

A. Copying donor information can change the recipient allele without losing a chromosome. HR is not necessarily restoration of the original allele.

25. Numerical and multiple-statement reasoning

Original examination-style questions. Numerical values and simplified experimental scenarios are hypothetical. Select an answer before opening the explanation.

41. A treated population yields 360 colonies and a matched untreated population yields 1,800. What is survival?

  1. 80%
  2. 5%
  3. 20%
  4. 36%
Answer and reasoning

C. 360/1,800 = 0.20, or 20%. The ratio assumes comparable input and plating conditions.

42. From 10,000 initial viable cells, 2,000 survive and 30 survivors are mutant. What is the mutant fraction among survivors?

  1. 15%
  2. 20%
  3. 0.3%
  4. 1.5%
Answer and reasoning

D. 30/2,000 = 0.015 = 1.5%. 30/10,000 = 0.3% is relative to initial input, not survivors.

43. A population has 25% survival and a mutant fraction of 0.04 among survivors. What fraction of initial cells are mutant survivors?

  1. 0.04
  2. 0.16
  3. 0.29
  4. 0.01
Answer and reasoning

D. Multiply S × f = 0.25 × 0.04 = 0.01. The result is 1% of the original input.

44. In a hypothetical first-order model, 100 breaks fall to 25 in 4 h. What is the half-time?

  1. 1 h
  2. 2 h
  3. 4 h
  4. 8 h
Answer and reasoning

B. A fourfold reduction represents two half-times, so t½ = 4/2 = 2 h. This assumes no new breaks and first-order disappearance.

45. Using a first-order model with k = 0.3466 h⁻¹, what fraction remains after 4 h?

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.125
  3. 0.25
  4. 0.50
Answer and reasoning

C. exp(−0.3466 × 4) ≈ exp(−1.3864) ≈ 0.25. k has inverse-time units.

46. Reporter A gives 300 positives among 1,500 eligible cells; reporter B gives 180 among 600. Which has higher positive fraction?

  1. Equal
  2. Cannot calculate
  3. A
  4. B
Answer and reasoning

D. A = 20%, B = 30%. Raw positives alone would reverse the apparent comparison. The percentages apply only to the reporter-defined endpoints.

47. Select correct statements: I. NHEJ can be precise. II. HR can copy a donor variant. III. TLS always removes lesions. IV. γH2AX is a signaling marker.

  1. II, III
  2. I, III
  3. III, IV
  4. I, II, IV
Answer and reasoning

D. I, II and IV are correct. TLS completes synthesis without necessarily removing the template lesion.

48. Select correct statements: I. RecA promotes LexA self-cleavage. II. SulA directly ligates DNA. III. Pol II has proofreading activity. IV. E. coli universally uses Ku–LigD.

  1. I and III
  2. I and IV
  3. II and III
  4. All
Answer and reasoning

A. SulA regulates division, not ligation. Ku–LigD is found in certain bacteria but is not the standard E. coli repair system.

49. Match 1. Ku; 2. RPA; 3. LIG4; 4. Pol η to P. ssDNA binding; Q. DSB end recognition; R. joining; S. CPD bypass.

  1. 1-S, 2-R, 3-P, 4-Q
  2. 1-Q, 2-P, 3-R, 4-S
  3. 1-P, 2-Q, 3-S, 4-R
  4. 1-R, 2-S, 3-Q, 4-P
Answer and reasoning

B. Ku binds duplex ends; RPA coats ssDNA; ligase IV catalyzes NHEJ sealing; Pol η bypasses suitable UV photoproducts.

50. Match 1. G1; 2. S/G2; 3. 3′-P nick; 4. LexA noncleavable to P. restricted SOS induction; Q. sister-donor availability; R. favors NHEJ for two-ended breaks; S. end processing required.

  1. 1-P, 2-S, 3-Q, 4-R
  2. 1-S, 2-P, 3-R, 4-Q
  3. 1-R, 2-Q, 3-S, 4-P
  4. 1-Q, 2-R, 3-P, 4-S
Answer and reasoning

C. The matching follows template availability, terminal chemistry and repression logic, not an absolute restriction of NHEJ to G1.

26. Assertion–Reason

Original examination-style questions. Numerical values and simplified experimental scenarios are hypothetical. Select an answer before opening the explanation.

51. Assertion: Classical NHEJ can restore an original DNA sequence. Reason: Compatible ends may be sealed without nucleotide removal or addition.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. The reason establishes how direct rejoining can preserve every nucleotide. Mutagenicity depends on processing and alignment.

52. Assertion: TLS can improve completion of DNA synthesis without eliminating the original lesion. Reason: A specialized polymerase can use a lesion-containing template directly.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. The bypass polymerase solves the copying problem without chemically reversing or excising the original lesion.

53. Assertion: HR predominates exclusively in all S/G2 mammalian cells. Reason: Sister chromatids are available after replication.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

D. Sister availability favors HR but does not exclude NHEJ or guarantee predominance for every substrate.

54. Assertion: RecA deficiency can affect both recombinational recovery and SOS induction. Reason: RecA participates in homologous interactions and stimulates LexA self-cleavage.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Two mechanistically distinct RecA functions can explain the combined phenotype.

55. Assertion: A decrease in the proportion of mutant survivors necessarily means improved DNA repair. Reason: Mutation fraction among survivors is conditioned on survival.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

D. Selection of survivors can change the measured fraction. Survival and mutation frequency must be measured separately.

Essential definitions

Vocabulary
TermMeaning
NickOne missing backbone linkage without a missing nucleotide.
GapOne or more missing nucleotides in a strand.
DSBDiscontinuity in both strands of a duplex.
ResectionProcessing that exposes ssDNA, commonly 3′ tails for HR.
NHEJEnd joining without an extended homologous donor.
HRTemplate-guided restoration using homologous DNA.
TLSDirect DNA synthesis across a lesion-containing template.
Template switchingBypass using alternative undamaged sister-derived information.
SOSInducible bacterial damage-response regulon.
Mutant fractionDetected mutant survivors divided by eligible survivors.

Formula sheet

S = Ns/N0; f = Nm/Ns; m = Sf; B(t) = B0 exp(−kt); t½ = ln(2)/k. All fractions are dimensionless; k has units of inverse time.

Common traps

  • Ligation cannot fill a missing nucleotide.
  • A 3′ phosphate is not a primer hydroxyl.
  • PARP1 and XRCC1 are not DNA ligases.
  • NHEJ is not always mutagenic.
  • HR need not restore the original allele.
  • NHEJ also acts in S/G2.
  • Microhomology is not unique evidence for an alternative pathway.
  • TLS does not necessarily remove damage.
  • Pol η can accurately bypass suitable CPDs.
  • LexA cleaves itself with stimulation by RecA.
  • Pol II has proofreading activity.
  • Fewer γH2AX foci do not prove exact repair.
  • Survival and mutant frequency have different denominators.

Active recall

  • Predict the fate of a nick carrying 3′-P/5′-OH in the absence of PNKP.
  • Explain why a one-ended break is an unsuitable substrate for arbitrary end joining.
  • Trace the role of Ku, DNA-PKcs, Artemis and LIG4.
  • Explain why resection creates a primer-compatible 3′ tail.
  • Compare sister-chromatid repair with a donor carrying a different allele.
  • Distinguish direct lesion bypass, template switching and removal.
  • Predict SOS transcription in a LexA noncleavable mutant.
  • Design independent readouts for lesion removal, survival and mutation.

Mastery checklist

  • Explain break geometry and end chemistry from an unlabeled diagram.
  • Predict effects of end-processing, ligation and HR defects.
  • Distinguish clean end joining from sequence-altering repair.
  • Explain the role of cell cycle without treating it as an absolute rule.
  • Classify tolerance by the template used.
  • Explain RecA–LexA regulation and distinguish it from RecA strand exchange.
  • Calculate survival, mutant fractions and model half-times.
  • Interpret reporter, sequencing and signaling data with appropriate controls.

Concept map

DNA interruption → examine strand geometry → assess termini and missing information → join compatible ends or obtain a donor. Replication blockage without loss of parental continuity → remove lesion when feasible or complete synthesis by TLS or template switching. Separate physical continuity, sequence accuracy and survival when interpreting data.
