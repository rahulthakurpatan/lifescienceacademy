# Genetic Code and Principles of Translational Decoding

## 1. From a nucleotide message to an amino-acid sequence

A mature messenger RNA is not a small protein waiting to be unfolded. It is a chemically different polymer. Its backbone contains ribose and phosphate, and its variable groups are the bases adenine, guanine, cytosine and uracil. A polypeptide, by contrast, is a succession of amino-acid residues joined through peptide bonds. The variable side chains of those residues determine important properties of the eventual protein. A mechanism that merely copies complementary nucleotides could reproduce a nucleic acid sequence, but it could not directly produce the chemically diverse side chains of a protein. Translation solves this problem by linking the information in one kind of polymer to the construction of another.

The distinction between transcription and translation is therefore fundamental. In transcription, the sequence relationship between the DNA template and newly synthesized RNA follows complementary base pairing. In translation, the relationship between an RNA sequence and the primary sequence of its product follows the genetic code. There is no universal chemical attraction that makes a free molecule of phenylalanine bind specifically to three uracils on messenger RNA. The cell establishes this relationship through an adaptor and the proteins that attach amino acids to those adaptors. Ribosomes organize the repeated use of the adaptors along the message. It is the coordinated system that implements the code, not the RNA sequence acting in isolation.

Consider the difference between the information required to describe a protein and the information required to produce it at the right time. The first problem involves specifying which residues are arranged in what order. The second involves determining whether a message is accessible, where translation starts, how rapidly elongation proceeds and whether the resulting product persists. The genetic code chiefly addresses the residue assignment problem. Many synonymous changes that preserve the encoded sequence can nevertheless influence production. A student should therefore avoid equating a preserved amino-acid sequence with a guarantee of identical expression or biological function.

The messenger is read in the direction from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end. The resulting polypeptide is synthesized from its amino terminus toward its carboxyl terminus. These two directions describe different chemical structures: a nucleic acid backbone and a chain of residues. They become related because the codon encountered first in the selected reading frame directs the first incorporated residue, and successive codons normally direct successive additions to the carboxyl end of the chain. A peptide sequence presented from the carboxyl terminus must first be reoriented before it is compared with a conventional messenger RNA coding sequence.

A particular sequence of bases may lie within a protein-coding region, an untranslated region or a noncoding RNA. Its location matters. The triplet AUG appearing in a transfer RNA sequence is not automatically an instruction to insert methionine. The same three letters occurring in the untranslated tail of an mRNA are not automatically decoded. The genetic code specifies a correspondence only when the translation system presents a triplet as a codon in a particular reading frame. This observation also explains why searching a genome for every occurrence of a codon associated with a stop signal does not reveal every protein boundary.

The correct product depends on more than the order of bases. If the message is read from the wrong position, perfectly normal nucleotide letters will direct an abnormal sequence of residues. If an adaptor is attached to the wrong amino acid, the correct RNA triplet may be recognized while the wrong residue is incorporated. If the release mechanism fails, a valid termination signal may not result in efficient release. These are distinct categories of decoding failure. Their separation is useful in designing experiments because different molecular defects can generate superficially similar decreases in functional protein.

## 2. Why the basic code is a triplet code

Before individual codon assignments were experimentally established, researchers could examine the arithmetic requirements of a coding system. Suppose a fixed-length word contains exactly k positions, and every position can independently contain one of four nucleotide types. The first position has four possible choices. For each of those choices, the second position again has four possibilities. Continuing this multiplication yields 4^k possible words. Repeated bases are allowed: AAA is a possible word just as ACG is. The order of the letters matters, so ACG and GCA are different words.

For single-base words, this calculation gives four alternatives, insufficient to distinguish twenty standard amino acids. For two-base words, there are sixteen alternatives, still insufficient under the assumption that each residue must have at least one distinct word. For three-base words, there are sixty-four alternatives. Triplets are thus the shortest fixed-length words that can accommodate twenty separate residue assignments using a four-letter alphabet. This reasoning does not itself establish that nature uses triplets. It establishes a constraint conditional on the model of equal-length words, direct residue assignment and unrestricted use of the four letters. An alternative hypothetical system with variable-length words or additional information would not obey the same requirement.

The experimental code is indeed organized around triplets. A codon consists of three consecutive nucleotides of a message in its chosen reading frame. In the standard code, sixty-one triplets are sense codons and three are conventional stop codons. The sense triplets distribute their assignments across twenty standard amino acids. A stop codon is a signal to end normal decoding rather than the name of another amino acid. A conventional initiation codon is not a sixty-fifth triplet; it is a triplet used in a particular functional context. AUG, for instance, normally specifies methionine within a standard coding sequence and can also establish its beginning.

It is instructive to distinguish capacity from actual assignment. The arithmetic tells us that 64 words exist but does not tell us that GGU specifies glycine. That assignment is empirical. The arithmetic does not determine how many words will specify each amino acid. Nature could in principle have distributed the excess words differently. The actual distribution has unequal degeneracy, with some residues specified by six triplets and others by only one. Memorizing the count of sixty-four without understanding these limitations risks treating a mathematical possibility as an experimental discovery.

For an RNA fragment of length L nucleotides, the number of distinct strings of exactly that length is 4^L, under the same unconstrained alphabet assumption. For a chain of n residues selected independently from twenty standard amino acids, the number of ordered sequences is 20^n. Neither expression is the number of functional genes or viable proteins. Many RNA strings contain stop signals in unsuitable positions, and many hypothetical proteins would not fold into useful structures. Combinatorial capacity measures possibilities before biological constraints are applied. It is an upper count in a specified abstract sequence space.

An alternative way to express the triplet argument is to consider binary information capacity. With four equally probable and independent letters, one nucleotide carries two bits of maximum information, because four equals two squared. A three-base word has six bits of capacity. Distinguishing twenty equally likely residue labels requires log base two of twenty, approximately 4.32 bits. These numbers illustrate why two nucleotides, providing at most four bits, cannot encode all twenty labels directly under the fixed-word model. In a real transcript, however, bases are neither always equally frequent nor statistically independent. Six bits is the maximum capacity of an unconstrained triplet alphabet, not a measured information content for every biological codon.

A code with more words than residue types creates opportunities for redundancy, but it does not make a message unnecessarily long in the ordinary sense. Three nucleotides are the minimum fixed word length under the assumptions already discussed. Once the word length is three, several unused words remain unless the assignments include alternative words for the same amino acid, punctuation-like signals or other contextual meanings. The genetic code uses all of its standard triplets through sense or termination assignments. No standard triplet should be assumed to have no meaning simply because it is absent from a particular transcript.

## 3. Establishing the reading frame

A nucleotide sequence has no visible spaces between codons. Its phosphodiester bonds connect neighboring nucleotides in the same general fashion whether they lie inside one codon or at the boundary between codons. The grouping therefore has to be imposed by a decoding mechanism. In an ordinary coding region, the initiation process fixes the first triplet, and subsequent translocation usually advances the ribosome by one codon at a time. The boundaries are maintained by that coordinated molecular cycle. The absence of literal punctuation between consecutive triplets is described by saying the standard code is read without commas.

Take the sequence 5′-AUGGCUAAACCGUAA-3′. With the first nucleotide as the beginning of the frame, the triplets are AUG, GCU, AAA, CCG and UAA. The encoded nascent chain is Met-Ala-Lys-Pro, followed by termination. Starting instead at the second nucleotide produces UGG, CUA, AAC and CGU, with two ungrouped nucleotides at the end. The potential residue string is Trp-Leu-Asn-Arg, but this is a reading-frame calculation rather than evidence that the second frame would be biologically initiated. Starting at the third nucleotide produces GGG, UAA, ACC and GUA, with one nucleotide left over. An early stop signal in that grouping interrupts ordinary translation before the later potential assignments are used.

The offset of a reading frame can be represented by zero, one or two relative to a specified reference position. A zero offset begins with the first position of that reference sequence. A one-base offset begins with its second position, and a two-base offset begins with its third. A shift by three positions returns to the same grouping phase, although some initial nucleotides have now been skipped. A reading frame is therefore a phase, not a particular absolute genomic position. Exam questions sometimes label the three frames as +1, +2 and +3, but the underlying grouping is identical. Check the numbering convention before applying a formula.

If the first base of the first codon is at coordinate s in one-based messenger RNA coordinates, the first base of codon number j is s + 3(j − 1). Its third base is s + 3(j − 1) + 2. For an RNA nucleotide at coordinate x downstream of s, the codon number is floor((x − s)/3) + 1, and the position within the codon is ((x − s) mod 3) + 1. These expressions are valid when the frame is continuous between the chosen start and x. They do not guarantee that the codon is actually translated, because an earlier termination signal or a programmed change in frame might intervene.

For example, if the first translated nucleotide is at position 17, codon number 8 occupies positions 38, 39 and 40: 17 + 3 times 7 equals 38. Position 46 is the first nucleotide of codon 10, since its distance from the starting position is 29, not 30; more carefully, 46 − 17 = 29, which gives quotient 9 and remainder 2, so position 46 is actually the third base of codon 10. Position 47 starts codon 11. The calculation illustrates why subtracting the actual start coordinate before dividing by three is essential. A coordinate numbered from the transcript is not necessarily numbered from the coding sequence.

The conventional non-overlapping nature of translation means that, within one uninterrupted reading event, the first and second adjacent codons do not share nucleotides. A code using overlapping triplets at every step would treat nucleotide positions one through three as the first word, two through four as the second and three through five as the third. Under such a scheme, a mutation at an interior position would generally affect three words at once, and the identity of one word would constrain the next two. Observed protein sequence diversity and mutational behavior are consistent instead with the ordinary non-overlapping triplet scheme.

Non-overlapping decoding must not be confused with non-overlapping genes. Two distinct open reading frames can overlap in the genome or even on the same RNA. Each translation event may use its own frame. One mutation can then alter codons in two different protein products, even though neither ribosome reads overlapping triplets in its own ordinary elongation cycle. The compact genomes of many viruses provide familiar examples of overlapping coding regions, but the detailed regulation of such expression is outside the present topic. The relevant lesson is that overlap at the level of genes and overlap at the level of neighboring codons are different concepts.

A double-stranded DNA fragment has two possible strand orientations. If both strands are considered as possible coding strands and each is written five-prime to three-prime, each gives three possible reading frames. There are therefore six conventional genomic reading frames to examine in an unspliced double-stranded fragment. A specific mature mRNA, however, already has a defined direction and has only three ordinary forward grouping phases. The three reverse-strand frames describe potential products of different transcripts, not a ribosome moving backward on the original message. Introns and other processing events introduce further requirements in a eukaryotic genome; a long unbroken DNA frame is not sufficient to establish the exact mature coding sequence.

## 4. Distinguishing transcription initiation and translation initiation

A transcription start site is the first nucleotide incorporated into an RNA by RNA polymerase at the relevant transcription unit. The site is frequently designated +1 on a transcription map. A translation start site is the first nucleotide of the initiation codon used to begin a polypeptide. Although both positions are beginnings, they are beginnings of different chemical processes. An RNA can contain an untranslated segment between its first nucleotide and its first translated codon. This segment is termed the five-prime untranslated region, or 5′ UTR.

Consider the conceptual RNA sequence 5′-GGCAAC-AUG-GAA-UUC-UGA-CAC-3′, with the hyphens introduced solely for readability. The first six bases constitute the specified upstream untranslated segment. AUG begins the coding sequence, GAA directs glutamate, UUC directs phenylalanine and UGA signals termination in the standard code. CAC lies downstream of that stop and is not translated in this event. The transcript contains twenty-one nucleotides, but its product has only three residues. Neither the initial GGC in the UTR nor the final CAC should be counted as an incorporated amino acid simply because they are valid triplets in the codon table.

In bacterial messages, different coding regions on a polycistronic RNA may have distinct initiation sites. One upstream transcription start can thus produce a transcript that supports several independently initiated polypeptides. In a typical eukaryotic nuclear mRNA, a major translated region is selected by the initiation machinery acting on a processed transcript; an untranslated segment precedes it. These general comparisons explain why a promoter mutation and a start-codon mutation can have very different outcomes. The former may change the supply of RNA, whereas the latter can change where or whether a protein starts even when a normal amount of RNA is present. Exact initiation-factor mechanisms will be developed in the corresponding later chapters.

The initiator codon must be distinguished from all chemically identical occurrences inside a coding sequence. An AUG encountered midway through an established reading frame usually directs incorporation of methionine without restarting the protein. An AUG at the initiation position is recognized by a specialized initiation process and is associated with the initiator transfer RNA. In a typical bacterial example the first incorporated residue is N-formylmethionine; in eukaryotic cytosolic translation it is ordinarily methionine without the formyl group. Subsequent removal of the initiating residue may yield a mature protein whose first residue differs from the first residue originally inserted. A protein's mature amino terminus is consequently not an infallible direct record of the initiating codon.

Not every AUG is a start, and not every genuine start is AUG. In bacteria, GUG and UUG are familiar alternative start codons, although their internal standard-code assignments are valine and leucine respectively. The initiator tRNA supplies the initiating methionine derivative rather than the amino acid normally associated with an internal GUG or UUG. Eukaryotic systems also have context-dependent non-AUG initiation in particular circumstances. When a question gives a sequence and explicitly states that a particular GUG is used for bacterial initiation, assigning valine as the first residue would be an error even if the remainder of the sequence is translated correctly. If GUG occurs later in the frame, valine is the appropriate ordinary assignment.

An open reading frame is an operational sequence concept. Depending on the search method, it may denote a stretch without an in-frame stop or a candidate region beginning with an allowed start and ending at an in-frame stop. These computational criteria help identify potential coding sequences, but do not alone establish biological translation. A short random sequence can contain an AUG followed by several sense triplets and a stop. An experimentally supported coding region requires additional evidence such as conservation, transcript context, ribosome association in the relevant frame, or detection of the encoded protein. A predicted ORF and a verified protein-coding gene should therefore not be treated as interchangeable terms.

## 5. Understanding the code table rather than memorizing a list

The standard genetic code is best examined as a systematic arrangement of the four possibilities at each of three positions. A conventional table organizes codons by the first base, then the second base, then the third. The reader starts with the written mRNA codon in the five-prime to three-prime direction. When given a template DNA strand, it must first be converted to the appropriate complementary RNA sequence. When given the coding DNA strand in the correct direction, replacing thymine by uracil produces the corresponding coding RNA letters, provided that any necessary RNA processing is already accounted for.

For the codon GCU, select the first-base group G, then the second-base subgroup C, and finally the third base U. The table assigns alanine. Changing the final base yields GCC, GCA and GCG; all four also specify alanine. In this four-codon family the identity of the first two positions determines the residue assignment, while the third position can vary among all four bases without changing the encoded residue. This pattern does not apply to every first-two-base combination. The UG subgroup contains cysteine codons UGU and UGC, the stop codon UGA and the tryptophan codon UGG. Merely knowing that a substitution occurs at the third position is insufficient to predict its effect.

A useful exercise is to compare codons related by a single base substitution. UUU and UUC both specify phenylalanine; UUA and UUG specify leucine. A change from UUU to UUA thus crosses a residue-assignment boundary, whereas UUU to UUC remains within the same assignment. The four triplets all share their first two bases. The difference lies in the codon table's partitioning of the third-position possibilities. Biological conclusions about a substitution must follow the actual grouping rather than the oversimplified rule that third-position substitutions are always harmless.

Another exercise is to locate the single-codon residues. Under the standard code, methionine has the sense codon AUG and tryptophan has UGG. Changing any one position in either of those two triplets necessarily changes the ordinary standard-code residue assignment or produces a stop. This result is conditional on using the standard table and examining internal decoding. It does not say that a change at AUG cannot leave bacterial initiation possible; a non-AUG start might still be used in a suitable context, with a different efficiency. Nor does it describe mitochondria whose code assignments differ. The same question can therefore have distinct answers when it refers to an internal methionine codon, a bacterial initiation site or an alternative genetic code.

The genetic code is degenerate, not ordinarily ambiguous. Degeneracy means that more than one distinct codon may specify the same residue. Unambiguity means that, within a specified code and the ordinary internal translation context, a codon has a particular assignment rather than arbitrarily directing different residues. The two properties describe opposite directions of a mapping. Leucine has six synonymous codons, but each of these normally specifies leucine rather than a mixture of unrelated amino acids. The apparent paradox disappears when one distinguishes many-to-one mapping from one-to-many assignment.

Degeneracy can also be counted across the entire table. Methionine and tryptophan each have one codon. Nine amino acids each have two: phenylalanine, tyrosine, cysteine, histidine, glutamine, asparagine, lysine, aspartate and glutamate. Isoleucine has three. Alanine, glycine, proline, threonine and valine each have four. Leucine, serine and arginine each have six. The sum is 2 times 1 plus 9 times 2 plus 1 times 3 plus 5 times 4 plus 3 times 6, equal to 61. Adding the three stop signals accounts for the full 64-triplet alphabet. The number of different amino acids represented by this grouping is 2 + 9 + 1 + 5 + 3 = 20.

The six-codon families reveal why a simple rule involving only the last position cannot describe every synonymous relationship. Leucine is assigned both the CUN four-codon family and UUA/UUG. Serine is assigned the UCN family as well as AGU and AGC. Arginine is assigned the CGN family as well as AGA and AGG. The first or second base may therefore differ between synonymous codons. In particular, some pairs of synonymous codons differ in two positions; not every synonymous change can be reached in one substitution without passing through an intermediate sequence with a different assignment. The distribution of synonymous words is not a uniform pattern repeated identically throughout the table.
