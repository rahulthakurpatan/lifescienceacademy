## 6. Discovering the triplet mechanism: the power of frameshift genetics

The triplet hypothesis can be examined by asking what happens when an extra nucleotide is inserted into an otherwise functional sequence. A single substitution leaves the sequence length unchanged, but an insertion potentially alters the grouping of all downstream letters. If the message is read in fixed groups of three from a particular beginning, the insertion changes the downstream grouping phase by one. A second insertion changes it by another position, whereas a third insertion restores the original phase beyond the third insertion. The original stretch between mutations may have become different, but sufficiently far downstream the original grouping can resume.

In 1961, Francis Crick, Leslie Barnett, Sydney Brenner and Richard Watts-Tobin investigated this issue with mutations in the rII region of bacteriophage T4. Their approach used the behavior of mutant phages on bacterial hosts as a genetic readout. An important class of mutations generated with acridine treatment could be studied in combinations. The researchers inferred that mutations of two opposite classes caused opposite changes in reading phase; these were conventionally described as plus and minus mutations. A plus-type mutation could be compensated by a minus-type mutation at another nearby position, whereas combinations of three changes of the same sign could also restore a measure of function. The experimental evidence linked the number and signs of mutations to restoration of functional reading.

A critical feature of the experiment was that it did not merely count viable and nonviable phages after an arbitrary mutagen treatment. The arrangement and combination of defined genetic changes were central. If a fixed-length code had words of two nucleotides, two equivalent one-base shifts would restore phase. If the words had length four, four equivalent shifts would be needed. Restoration after particular combinations of three same-sign mutations, together with opposite-sign compensation, is what supported a triplet reading scheme. The results were obtained using functional genetic tests before individual RNA codons had all been biochemically identified.

This interpretation is more subtle than claiming that three insertions always repair a gene. Consider an original coding sequence AUG-AAA-GGC-UUC-CCG-GAA-UAA. A nucleotide insertion shortly after the start changes the grouping. Another insertion in a later region changes it again, and a third restores the grouping downstream of that position, provided translation has not already stopped. The interval between the first and last mutations can encode many altered residues. A gene may tolerate changes in a relatively dispensable stretch yet fail if an essential catalytic residue lies in that interval. Restoration of reading phase is therefore a necessary condition for one particular kind of compensation, not a general sufficient condition for restoration of protein activity.

It is also important to distinguish a deletion from an insertion without relying on the visual spacing of letters. The sequence after a deletion is shorter by one nucleotide, so positions originally downstream of that site move one coordinate toward the start. The new triplets may consist of the second and third positions of one original codon followed by the first position of the next. An insertion changes the relationship in the other direction. Both are frameshifting events when their length is not divisible by three and they occur inside an ordinary uninterrupted coding region. Their exact peptide products must be obtained by regrouping actual altered nucleotide sequences, not merely by giving the event a plus or minus label.

The mathematical generalization is straightforward. Let delta denote the cumulative number of inserted nucleotides minus the cumulative number of deleted nucleotides upstream of a reference location, assuming unchanged initiation. The downstream grouping aligns again with the original grouping when delta is divisible by three. A value of zero may represent cancellation of an insertion and deletion. A value of three may represent a net gain of one codon's worth of bases. A value of minus three may represent a net loss of that amount. These possibilities are distinct at the sequence level even though all have the same phase modulo three.

An experiment can demonstrate the distinction through an original educational example. Suppose a reporter has an essential downstream enzyme domain that functions only when its original reading frame is retained. The wild-type construct gives normalized signal 100. A one-base insertion gives signal 2, a two-base insertion gives signal 3, and a three-base insertion gives signal 70. A one-base insertion followed by a nearby one-base deletion gives signal 65. These are hypothetical values, not historical measurements. Their pattern is consistent with a triplet mechanism because the two frame-restored variants regain much of their activity. The restoration is incomplete, which may reflect altered residues, changes in expression or effects of the inserted or deleted sequence on folding.

For a meaningful test, the constructs must be compared with appropriate controls. The wild-type sequence establishes the assay's reference activity. A construct without the reporter establishes the assay background. Sequencing the variants verifies that the observed mutations really are the expected lengths and locations. Measuring transcript abundance helps distinguish altered RNA production from changed protein function. Measuring protein size and abundance provides another check on whether the loss of activity arises because translation ends prematurely or because a full-length but inactive protein is produced. A functional phenotype alone does not identify all of these intermediate molecular events.

The historical frameshift evidence and the arithmetic argument answer related but different questions. The capacity argument asks how long a fixed code word must be to accommodate the known amino-acid alphabet. The frameshift results ask how the actual translating machinery groups sequence information. Neither establishes that a particular triplet corresponds to a particular residue. For that, experimental biochemistry was needed. A complete interpretation of the genetic code therefore required investigators to combine genetic evidence about grouping with biochemical evidence about assignment.

## 7. Deciphering individual assignments through synthetic RNA

The decisive insight of early cell-free protein synthesis experiments was to remove the need to interpret an entire living cell at once. A cell extract can provide ribosomes, soluble factors, transfer RNAs, enzymes and the chemical substrates needed for translation. If the experimenter supplies a defined RNA template, changes in newly synthesized polypeptide can then be attributed to that input more directly than in an intact cell containing many endogenous messages. This does not make the system chemically simple, but it makes the relationship between template and product experimentally accessible.

In their 1961 work, Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei used an Escherichia coli cell-free translation system and tested synthetic polyribonucleotides. An RNA composed of repeated uracils caused a strong stimulation of phenylalanine incorporation into material with the properties of polyphenylalanine. The result was especially informative because any uninterrupted three-base grouping of a homopolymer of uracil yields UUU. Once the triplet organization was understood, the assignment of UUU to phenylalanine followed naturally from this biochemical observation. Their paper presented the experimental result before all features of the complete code had been settled, and it should not be described as having independently solved the entire triplet question.

The assay required a way to distinguish newly incorporated amino acids from the large pool of free amino acids supplied to the extract. Radioactively labelled amino acids gave a measurable signal when incorporated into the product fraction. A set of parallel reactions could differ in which amino acid was labelled while the other amino acids remained available. A strong increase in incorporation of the labelled phenylalanine in the presence of poly(U) indicated a template-directed relationship rather than merely a general stimulation of protein production. The comparison with background synthesis in the absence of added template was essential.

A homopolymer is a powerful but limited probe. Poly(U) cannot distinguish UUU from a hypothetical repeated two-base word UU solely on the basis of its product, because every overlapping or non-overlapping grouping contains uracils. Poly(A) and poly(C) can similarly provide evidence for assignments associated with repeated adenine and repeated cytosine, yielding lysine and proline respectively under standard-code decoding. Other homopolymers may be difficult to use in the same way because of RNA structural properties and assay conditions. Absence of an incorporation signal is not automatically evidence that a triplet has no assignment.

The limitation of a random mixed polymer is different. Suppose a synthetic RNA is prepared with a known average proportion of A and U. A translation system may incorporate a mixture of residues. If the bases are independently distributed, the experimenter can estimate the frequencies of triplets containing two U bases and one A base, for example. However, AAU, AUA and UAA have the same base composition but different orders and different standard assignments. A random polymer with only known composition cannot by itself identify which particular ordered sequence produces a particular amino acid. It can narrow the possibilities and support statistical associations, but sequence-defined probes are needed to resolve the order.

The probability reasoning behind such experiments is worth understanding. If the fractions of A and U are 0.25 and 0.75 and neighboring bases are independent, the expected proportion of the specific ordered triplet UUA is 0.75 times 0.75 times 0.25, equal to 0.140625. The ordered triplets UAU and AUU have the same expected frequency. The combined proportion containing two U bases and one A base is therefore three times 0.140625, equal to 0.421875. A researcher who measured only the total frequency of products associated with that composition could not distinguish those three triplets without further information. The hypothetical calculation is a model of the inferential difficulty, not a reproduction of a particular historical incorporation experiment.

Real incorporation measurements also depend on properties not represented by base-composition probabilities. A message with abundant stop codons might yield shorter products. RNA secondary structure can affect access by the translation machinery. Different tRNAs and amino acids can be supplied or recognized at different efficiencies. The incorporation signal reflects the entire biochemical process, not simply the arithmetic frequency of a triplet in a template. The usefulness of the synthetic-polymer approach depended on comparing multiple experiments rather than treating one incorporation ratio as a direct universal codon census.

Har Gobind Khorana and colleagues advanced the problem by preparing defined repeating nucleotide sequences. An alternating RNA with a repeated UC pattern, for instance, contains UCU and CUC as successive triplets when read in one phase. These specify serine and leucine. Reading the same alternating polymer beginning at the other position reverses which of those codons appears first, but the repetitive character of the resulting sequence is unchanged. The observed association of repeating templates with particular peptide compositions supplied information that composition-only methods could not provide.

A repeated trinucleotide template behaves differently from a repeated dinucleotide template. In an infinite repetition of AUG, starting at the A yields consecutive AUG codons, starting at the U yields consecutive UGA codons, and starting at the G yields consecutive GAU codons. These have methionine, stop and aspartate assignments in the standard code. The example illustrates the effect of the phase of translation on a periodic template; it is not a claim that a real cell-free assay would initiate efficiently in each phase. Initiation and termination impose additional constraints. Precisely because phase and sequence order matter, well-defined polymers provided complementary evidence about the reading mechanism.

A general periodicity argument can be made without advanced mathematics. Suppose a template repeats a sequence of length m. Every time a new ordinary codon begins, the position within that repeating pattern advances by three modulo m. The same position is reached again after the smallest positive number of codons q for which 3q is divisible by m. That number equals m divided by the greatest common divisor of m and three. A repeat of length two therefore has a two-codon cycle; a repeat of length three has a one-codon cycle for each fixed phase; a repeat of length four has a four-codon cycle. This is a statement about grouping a periodic nucleotide string, not about the biological ability to translate the complete cycle when a stop signal occurs.

For a hypothetical repeat of GUAC, the sequence written in groups of three beginning with G is GUA-CGU-ACG-UAC, and then the cycle returns to GUA. Using the standard table, the cycle specifies valine, arginine, threonine and tyrosine. A sample of peptide containing all four residues in that order would be consistent with the specified phase. An observation of the four residue types without information about order is less decisive: it could support a set of candidate codons without establishing the precise phase or direction. The example teaches why the design of a template determines what kind of inference can be made from a product.

The breakthrough in individual ordered triplet assignment came through a method that did not require a long chain to be synthesized. Nirenberg and Philip Leder described a ribosome-binding assay in 1964 in which defined trinucleotides stimulated binding of corresponding aminoacyl transfer RNAs to ribosomes. The radioactive label could identify which amino acid was associated with the retained tRNA. Free tRNA and ribosome-associated tRNA behaved differently in the separation step. The resulting signal linked an ordered triplet to an aminoacyl-tRNA recognition event. This approach enabled direct testing of synthetic codons in a way that random mixed polymers could not.

The binding assay and the cell-free peptide-synthesis experiment address different stages. A binding assay demonstrates an association of a triplet, ribosome and particular aminoacyl-tRNA under specified conditions. It does not alone establish the rate of elongation, the efficiency of initiation on a full mRNA or the final functionality of a protein. A complete synthesis assay can establish incorporation into a chain but may make individual assignments difficult when the template has mixed sequences. Their combined use made the inferences stronger. The presence of a cognate binding signal and the corresponding synthesis product connect recognition to incorporation.

In a hypothetical binding assay, a radiolabelled alanyl-tRNA is supplied with ribosomes and synthetic GCU. Retained radioactivity is 850 units compared with 30 without added triplet and 35 with UUU. A labelled phenylalanyl-tRNA instead gives 810 units with UUU and 25 with GCU. The values are invented for reasoning practice. The reciprocal specificity is more informative than a single positive sample, because it argues against nonspecific retention caused by any RNA triplet. The no-triplet sample provides a background estimate, and a no-ribosome sample would help exclude retention of free tRNA by the filter. Equal input radioactivity and validated aminoacylation would strengthen the comparison.

A lack of ordinary aminoacyl-tRNA binding in response to a stop triplet must be interpreted cautiously. Stop signals normally recruit release machinery rather than a cognate ordinary tRNA. The absence of a particular tRNA signal is compatible with this mechanism, but a poorly functioning assay can also yield low signals for genuine sense codons. A direct termination experiment using a ribosome carrying a nascent chain provides complementary evidence. The important general lesson is that a negative result needs both a positive control establishing that the assay works and independent evidence that distinguishes the plausible explanations for the absence of signal.

The discoveries recognized by the 1968 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine represent complementary contributions. Nirenberg and Khorana were central to determining the code's assignments through biochemical and synthetic-nucleic-acid approaches, while Robert W. Holley's work on tRNA helped reveal the molecular adaptor that gives the code a physical basis. The complete modern understanding did not emerge from a single demonstration. It emerged from convergence among sequence chemistry, functional genetics, biochemical recognition and the study of the molecules that connect codons to amino acids.

## 8. The adaptor principle and molecular orientation

Transfer RNA solves the central chemical problem of translation by combining two functional regions on one molecule. One region contains an anticodon capable of recognizing a complementary triplet in mRNA. Another region carries an amino acid attached to the acceptor end. Because these regions are separated in the folded tRNA, the nucleotide recognition event can be linked to a residue that has no direct chemical resemblance to its codon. A molecular mechanism can therefore associate UUU with phenylalanine, GCU with alanine and many other triplets with their assigned amino acids.

The anticodon occupies a defined region of the tRNA anticodon loop. Conventional numbering labels its three bases 34, 35 and 36 when tRNA is read in its own five-prime to three-prime direction. The first base of the mRNA codon pairs with anticodon base 36, the second with base 35 and the third with base 34. This reversed correspondence follows directly from the antiparallel organization of paired RNA segments. It is a common source of examination errors because authors may write an anticodon in either orientation while describing the same molecular interaction.

For a strictly complementary example, the codon 5′-AUG-3′ pairs with the anticodon 3′-UAC-5′. Written in the conventional five-prime to three-prime direction, that same anticodon is 5′-CAU-3′. The letters UAC and CAU are not contradictory when their orientations are specified. To obtain the anticodon in the five-prime to three-prime convention from a given codon, first complement each base, then reverse the order. To align a codon and anticodon as they pair, write the two sequences in opposite directions and complement position by position. Confusing an alignment notation with a written sequence notation can turn a correct base-pairing concept into a wrong numerical answer.

Consider a codon 5′-GAC-3′, which specifies aspartate. Its exact complement as an aligned anticodon is 3′-CUG-5′. Its reversed sequence is 5′-GUC-3′. If an examination gives an anticodon sequence GUC with no indicated direction, the conventional assumption is five-prime to three-prime; under strict complementary pairing it recognizes GAC. However, a chemically modified or wobble-capable anticodon can recognize additional synonymous codons. The initial reverse-complement operation identifies exact pairing, not necessarily the complete range of codons recognized by that tRNA in vivo.

Anticodon recognition is only one part of the specificity of translation. Before a tRNA is used in normal decoding, it must carry the correct amino acid. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases establish the chemical association by recognizing appropriate tRNAs and amino acids. They are not a set of enzymes that simply read each messenger codon. The tRNA itself is the intermediary. A correct anticodon combined with an incorrectly attached amino acid can deliver the wrong residue at a correctly read codon. Consequently, one cannot infer aminoacylation accuracy solely from evidence of correct codon–anticodon pairing.

This separation is demonstrated conceptually by considering a tRNA whose anticodon normally recognizes a glycine codon but whose acceptor end has been experimentally supplied with alanine. If that tRNA is admitted to the translating ribosome and participates in peptide-bond formation, the residue delivered at the glycine codon is alanine. This statement is conditional: altered tRNAs can be affected by other recognition steps, and their efficiency may vary. It does not mean that ordinary cells routinely tolerate incorrect charging. It explains why the ribosome's reading of the anticodon and the synthetase's selection of the amino acid are complementary requirements for fidelity. The full charging mechanism belongs to the next chapter.

The energetic requirements of translation should also be located correctly. Pairing of a codon and an anticodon is a reversible molecular recognition interaction; it is not itself a chemical reaction that consumes an ATP for every base pair. Charging the tRNA requires energy and creates an activated linkage, while the translation machinery uses additional energy-dependent steps to select, move and recycle molecules. The detailed ATP and GTP bookkeeping belongs to the later lessons on charging and elongation. For the present chapter, the key concept is that correct decoding requires both sequence recognition and a supply of activated aminoacyl-tRNAs. The genetic code table alone does not describe the machinery or its energetic cost.
