## 12. Translating DNA and RNA sequences systematically

Sequence questions become much easier when each operation is performed separately. First establish the identity and orientation of the supplied strand. Second establish which RNA sequence is actually present in the mature message. Third identify the specified initiation point or reading phase. Fourth partition the sequence into consecutive triplets, using the correct genetic code for the biological system. Fifth stop at the first relevant in-frame termination signal unless the question expressly describes a recoding event. This order prevents errors in strand conversion from being hidden by an otherwise correct use of the codon table.

Suppose the coding DNA strand of a continuous, already specified coding region is 5′-ATG GAC TTT CCA TAA-3′. It corresponds to the RNA sequence 5′-AUG GAC UUU CCA UAA-3′, and that RNA encodes Met-Asp-Phe-Pro before termination. The DNA template is complementary and antiparallel to the coding DNA, so in aligned notation it is 3′-TAC CTG AAA GGT ATT-5′. Given this template, complementary RNA synthesis generates the same five-prime to three-prime RNA sequence. These two routes must converge if strand identity and direction have been handled correctly.

A more challenging question might present the template DNA in its conventional five-prime to three-prime form, as 5′-TTA TGG AAA GTC CAT-3′. Transcription reads this template from the other end. Simply complementing the displayed letters without reversing their order would give the wrong RNA orientation. Reversing the order and taking the RNA complement produces 5′-AUG GAC UUU CCA UAA-3′. Once a sequence has been converted, the amino acid assignments remain the same regardless of which form of the DNA was originally supplied. The molecular sequence is not altered merely by changing how the strand is printed.

RNA processing may complicate this relationship. A eukaryotic genomic sequence can include introns that will not appear in the mature coding RNA. Suppose two coding exons contribute the sequences 5′-AUGGC and 5′-UAAAUAA when considered in their spliced RNA orientation. After removal of the intervening intron, their direct join is AUGGCUAAAUAA. The triplet GCU crosses the exon junction: its first two bases derive from the first exon and its final base derives from the second. The complete product is Met-Ala-Lys, followed by UAA. One cannot translate the two exons independently as separate complete groups and then concatenate their amino-acid strings. Exon boundaries and codon boundaries need not coincide.

This issue is particularly important when an examination problem describes alternative splicing. If an exon containing a multiple of three coding nucleotides is skipped, the downstream phase can be preserved, provided that the same start is used and the exon is entirely within the uninterrupted translated region. The peptide sequence can still change substantially because residues disappear and new junction codons may form. If the removed coding length is not a multiple of three, the downstream phase generally changes. An intron removed during normal splicing does not cause such a change relative to the normal mature message; it was never part of that reference coding sequence. The effect of exon skipping must be compared with the correctly spliced reference transcript rather than with raw genomic length.

The first and last complete codons are equally important when a fragment does not contain the whole coding sequence. Imagine that the question supplies 5′-GCUUAACG-3′ and states that the first letter is the first position of a codon in a larger transcript. The complete codons are GCU and UAA, and the remaining CG lacks its third base in the supplied fragment. If this fragment is translated as part of a longer ordinary reading frame, alanine is incorporated before termination. There is no reason to search for an AUG within the fragment, because the problem has already specified the reading frame and the fragment may begin internally. If the question instead asks whether the isolated fragment can autonomously initiate translation, the available information is insufficient to establish a normal initiation event.

An analogous issue occurs when a nucleotide sequence ends without a stop. If the end of the supplied sequence is merely the boundary of an excerpt, the final protein length is unknown. It is legitimate to report the amino acid string encoded by the complete triplets shown, but not to claim that the protein terminates after the last displayed codon. The absence of a depicted stop is not a stop signal. A full mRNA lacking a usable termination signal presents a different biological problem involving stalled ribosomes or quality control; that cannot be inferred simply from an abbreviated examination sequence.

When counting lengths, distinguish nucleotide coordinates from base-pair counts and residue numbers. A coding region of 600 nucleotides on one strand corresponds to 600 base pairs of double-stranded DNA if the relevant region is continuous and no extra bases are counted. It does not correspond to 1,200 base pairs merely because both strands contain 600 nucleotides. The double-stranded region contains 1,200 nucleotide residues in total but 600 paired positions. If the 600-nucleotide coding segment includes a terminal stop, its nascent peptide has 199 residues; if it consists exclusively of sense triplets and excludes the stop, it encodes 200. The difference of one triplet is small numerically but conceptually important.

The molecular mass of a peptide can sometimes be estimated from its length using a mean residue mass, often about 110 daltons per incorporated amino-acid residue for a rough protein-level estimate. A 200-residue chain would then be estimated at approximately 22,000 daltons, or 22 kilodaltons, before additional modifications. This is an approximation, not an exact relationship. Exact mass depends on the identities of the residues, terminal groups and modifications. Likewise, the mass of the full RNA cannot be obtained exactly from its length without knowing its composition and chemical state. A length-based estimate should be clearly distinguished from a sequence-specific calculation.

## 13. Degeneracy as a quantitative and biological property

The relation from a known RNA coding sequence to an ordinary peptide sequence is much less underdetermined than the reverse relation. Given a standard-code reading frame with no recoding, each codon has a specified assignment. Given only a peptide sequence, however, there are usually many possible RNA sequences that could encode it. Reverse translation is a problem of choosing among synonymous words. This difference is important in interpreting a protein sequence obtained from an experiment. It can constrain possible genes without uniquely determining every nucleotide in them.

Let d(a) be the number of standard sense codons assigned to amino acid a. For a specified sequence of n residues a1 through an, and independent choice of a synonymous codon at every position, the number of possible coding RNA sense sequences is the product d(a1) × d(a2) × ... × d(an). The independence assumption means that any synonymous codon is allowed at each position without additional constraints on initiation, RNA structure, splicing or expression. If the first residue is methionine and AUG is fixed as the only allowed initiator in the model, its multiplicative factor is one. If an ordinary terminal stop is also required and any of the three stop triplets is allowed, a further factor of three appears.

For the peptide Met-Ala-Gly-Lys-Trp, the degeneracies are 1, 4, 4, 2 and 1. The product is 32 possible sense-region strings. Allowing any of the three conventional stop codons gives 96 strings for the complete specified start-to-stop region. If a problem additionally fixes the lysine codon as AAG, the two alternatives at that position become one, reducing the total to 48 with a freely chosen stop. The calculation demonstrates how explicit biological or experimental restrictions change a combinatorial answer.

The possible coding sequences for a peptide of fixed length should not be confused with the total number of standard sense-codon strings of that length. If all sixty-one sense triplets may be independently chosen for each of n positions, the total number of sense-only nucleotide strings is 61^n. The number of possible amino-acid strings of the same length is 20^n. The mapping from the former set to the latter is uneven because different peptides have different products of degeneracy. A peptide containing many leucine residues has more synonymous coding possibilities than one of the same length containing only methionine and tryptophan.

For example, two-residue chains made from standard residues have 400 possible ordered amino-acid sequences. There are 61 squared, or 3,721, possible ordered pairs of sense codons. Dividing 3,721 by 400 gives 9.3025 possible encoding strings per peptide on average if peptide sequences are given equal weight. This average does not imply that any particular peptide has nine or ten encodings. Met-Trp has one, while Leu-Arg has thirty-six. The average describes the entire abstract sequence space. Examination questions often ask for a count for one specified peptide, in which case using the average would be incorrect.

A related probability question assumes that each of the 64 triplets is equally probable in a random nucleotide string with equal base frequencies. Under the standard code the probability of a stop in a given independent codon is 3/64, and the probability of a sense triplet is 61/64. The probability that n consecutive codons in a fixed frame are all sense is (61/64)^n. For ten codons, this value is approximately 0.619. The calculation assumes independent nucleotide choices and a frame already selected. It does not estimate the chance that a real genomic region is translated or that a random peptide would function.

The distribution of the number of sense codons preceding the first stop under that random model follows a geometric pattern. Let p = 3/64. The probability that exactly n sense codons precede the first stop is (1 − p)^n p. The expectation of this count is (1 − p)/p = 61/3, approximately 20.33 sense codons. If counting the stop-containing triplet as well, the expected total is 1/p = 64/3, approximately 21.33. Neither mean is a prediction for actual protein lengths, since natural coding sequences are subject to strong constraints on premature termination, biological function and initiation. The model instead provides a simple null expectation for randomly grouped sequence.

Real genomes also differ in base composition. If pA, pC, pG and pU denote independent base frequencies, the probability of a standard stop in a randomly grouped triplet is pU pA pA + pU pA pG + pU pG pA. In a toy example with A and U each at 0.30 and C and G each at 0.20, the three terms are 0.027, 0.018 and 0.018, giving a stop probability of 0.063. With a different base composition, the expected frequency can change even when the code table itself is unchanged. Statistical properties of random sequences are therefore not identical to properties of the genetic code.

The assumptions deserve as much attention as the arithmetic. Neighboring nucleotides in actual DNA or RNA may be correlated; initiation is not random; the distribution of synonymous codons reflects mutation, selection and other biological processes; and coding regions are not random samples drawn from the whole genome. A probability formula using independent bases is useful for reasoning in an explicitly artificial problem. It should not be used as empirical evidence about a particular species without sequence data and a model that accounts for those dependencies.

Degeneracy can reduce the amino-acid consequences of some nucleotide substitutions. A third-position U-to-C change in many twofold families preserves the original assignment, and any third-base change within a fourfold box preserves the assignment. This property permits variation in coding sequences while maintaining a protein's primary sequence. Nevertheless, the table is not a universal error-correcting code. Many single-nucleotide changes alter an amino acid, and some introduce premature stops. Even a synonymous change can influence other features of the mRNA. The code's organization supplies a pattern of robustness for particular classes of changes, not protection against all mutations.

## 14. Mutational effects as tests of decoding logic

A synonymous substitution changes the nucleotide sequence while preserving the amino-acid assignment at the affected codon under the applicable code. A missense substitution changes a sense codon into another sense codon that specifies a different residue. A nonsense or stop-gain substitution changes a sense codon to an ordinary stop. These terms describe consequences relative to a particular coding sequence and reading context. The same base substitution may have a different effect if it occurs in another frame or in a different translation system.

Take the ordinary internal glutamate codon GAA. A change to GAG is synonymous. A change to GUA produces a valine assignment and is missense. A change to UAA produces a conventional stop and is nonsense. The last substitution changes only the first base but can have a much larger consequence for peptide length than a substitution at another position. The code table, rather than the mere physical position of a mutation in the codon, determines its immediate classification.

The nine one-base substitution neighbors of a triplet can be enumerated because each of its three positions can change to any of the other three bases. For GAA, the first-position neighbors are AAA, CAA and UAA; the second-position neighbors are GCA, GGA and GUA; the third-position neighbors are GAC, GAG and GAU. There is one synonymous neighbor, GAG, and one stop, UAA. The remaining seven neighbors specify other amino acids. If a hypothetical problem assumes every single-position substitution alternative is equally likely, the probabilities of synonymous, nonsense and missense outcomes from this codon are 1/9, 1/9 and 7/9, respectively. Actual mutation probabilities may be unequal, so the stated assumption is necessary.

A fourfold codon such as GCU has three synonymous neighbors at its third position. Its other six single-base neighbors can be examined individually. The first-position variants UCU, CCU and ACU specify serine, proline and threonine. The second-position variants GAU, GGU and GUU specify aspartate, glycine and valine. All six are missense. The hypothetical equal-neighbor probabilities are 1/3 synonymous and 2/3 missense, with no single-substitution stop neighbor. A degeneracy of four guarantees three synonymous third-base alternatives in this box, but it is not enough by itself to determine whether other positions can also change without changing the assignment in more complex families.

Transition and transversion distinctions can sharpen a question. Transitions replace a purine with the other purine or a pyrimidine with the other pyrimidine, whereas transversions exchange bases between the two classes. In UUU, changing the third U to C is a transition and preserves phenylalanine. Changing the third U to A or G is a transversion and produces leucine. These observations do not establish that every transition is synonymous or every transversion is missense. They illustrate that mutation spectra can interact with the organization of the code. A probability model that weights transitions more heavily than transversions will give different outcomes from the equal-neighbor model.

In sickle hemoglobin, the familiar glutamate-to-valine change in the beta-globin chain is often represented by the coding RNA change GAG to GUG. The example connects a single base change to a change in residue chemistry: glutamate normally has an acidic side chain, while valine has a nonpolar side chain. The physical consequences of the substitution depend on its structural location within hemoglobin and the interactions of the altered proteins. The code table can establish the substitution in primary sequence, but understanding the full disease mechanism also requires protein structure and physiology. The same Glu-to-Val change in an unrelated protein would not necessarily produce the same phenotype.

A stop-gain mutation can shorten a nascent chain, but the effect on the detectable protein depends on several biological events. In an appropriate eukaryotic transcript context, premature termination may trigger RNA surveillance and reduce transcript abundance. Some truncated products are unstable or rapidly degraded. Others may persist and retain partial function. A student should not automatically equate a premature stop with a stable protein of the length predicted by the codon table. That prediction describes the possible nascent chain before additional regulation and degradation are considered.

Insertions and deletions require careful grouping. In the reference coding sequence AUG-GCU-AAA-CCG-UAA, the product is Met-Ala-Lys-Pro. If the first G of GCU is deleted, the resulting sequence is AUG-CUA-AAC-CGU-AA, whose displayed complete codons specify Met-Leu-Asn-Arg. The terminal AA is incomplete in this excerpt; the original UAA is no longer in the same phase. The mutation changes sequence information after the deletion, not merely the single alanine assignment. The exact new termination point cannot be predicted without more downstream sequence. The example contrasts sharply with a one-base substitution, which does not shift grouping in an ordinary reading event.

If instead the complete GCU triplet is deleted, the sequence becomes AUG-AAA-CCG-UAA. The new nascent chain is Met-Lys-Pro. The downstream grouping and terminal stop remain aligned because three nucleotides were removed at a codon boundary. A three-base deletion that begins within a codon can affect the identities of adjacent residues by forming a new junction codon, even though the downstream phase is preserved. Thus the general rule for multiples of three is preservation of phase, not necessarily deletion of exactly one intact original amino acid for every three bases removed.

Compensatory changes can occur at different molecular levels. An insertion followed by a deletion may restore a reading phase at the sequence level. A suppressor tRNA may instead recognize a triplet that normally terminates translation, allowing some ribosomes to continue without changing the original mutant RNA sequence. The latter case does not repair the DNA sequence. It changes the decoding environment. A nonsense suppressor may insert a residue different from the one originally encoded, so full-length protein production does not prove complete restoration of the original primary sequence.

Consider a reporter with a normal UAU tyrosine codon changed to UAG. A suppressor tRNA able to recognize UAG could restore synthesis of a full-length product in an appropriate system. If the inserted residue happens to be tyrosine, the original residue may be restored at that site. If the suppressor delivers another amino acid, the product is full length but altered. A comparison of reporter activity, product size and sequence identity is therefore more informative than product size alone. This kind of reasoning links the code to genetic suppression without needing to introduce the detailed inheritance patterns reserved for the genetics unit.

## 15. Integrating decoding with experimental interpretation

The availability of a complete codon table makes sequence translation appear deterministic, yet experiments measure outputs of real biological systems. A reduction in a reporter's protein abundance may be caused by fewer mRNA molecules, less frequent initiation, slower or failed elongation, changes in amino-acid incorporation, premature termination, or increased degradation. Identifying a synonymous or nonsense mutation is a valuable initial clue, but the observed phenotype must be interpreted by considering the intervening steps. The code specifies a local assignment under given conditions; the experiment tests which biological process actually occurred.

Suppose a synonymous substitution converts GCU to GCC in a reporter. A researcher measures the same mRNA abundance in wild-type and mutant cells, but the mutant protein accumulates to only half the reference amount. Since both codons specify alanine, the result cannot be explained by a conventional missense substitution at that position. Possible explanations include altered translation efficiency, changes in mRNA folding that affect access by the translation machinery, or a change in the stability of the protein product mediated indirectly through synthesis. Equal steady-state mRNA abundance does not establish equal translational initiation rates or protein turnover. Further controls are needed to separate these explanations.

A comparison of newly synthesized protein over a short measurement interval with steady-state protein abundance can help distinguish reduced synthesis from increased degradation. If newly synthesized product accumulates normally while steady-state protein is reduced, altered turnover is a plausible contributor. If newly synthesized product is also lower, reduced synthesis is supported, although the exact translation step is not yet identified. These are broad experimental inferences suitable for the genetic-code chapter. The detailed analysis of translational regulation and protein degradation will be developed in subsequent nodes.

Another experiment may compare reporters differing only in which synonymous codons occur within an otherwise identical coding sequence. If supplying a relevant tRNA improves the expression of one variant preferentially, the result supports a role for that tRNA's decoding availability. A nonmatching tRNA and empty-vector controls help establish specificity. Measurements of mRNA and peptide identity help ensure that the apparent improvement is not caused by unrelated changes in transcript supply or production of an abnormal product. Such experiments illustrate how wobble and tRNA repertoire can matter biologically without treating the standard amino acid assignments as variable.

Ribosome-associated RNA fragments provide another way to infer which reading phase is being used. In a typical ribosome-profiling interpretation, protected fragments from translated coding regions may exhibit three-nucleotide periodicity after suitable alignment and assignment of ribosomal positions. A strong periodicity in one frame supports its use, whereas a shift in downstream periodicity may indicate a change in decoding phase. However, library preparation, mapping ambiguity and the method of assigning the relevant ribosome position can influence observed patterns. A local signal must be considered alongside independent protein and sequence evidence. The idea is useful here because it relates modern observations back to the original question of fixed triplet grouping.

In a hypothetical educational experiment, 90% of appropriately mapped ribosome positions upstream of a candidate shift fall in the original frame, whereas 80% of positions downstream fall in a different phase. A variant that disrupts a candidate RNA signal restores the original downstream periodicity. This pattern is consistent with a sequence-dependent change in reading phase, but it does not by itself establish the exact molecular mechanism. An alternative overlapping coding region could contribute to the signal. A reporter designed to isolate the two phases, combined with peptide evidence and the appropriate sequence controls, would distinguish these interpretations more effectively.

The connection between decoding and protein identity is also important in biotechnology. A standard coding sequence can often be expressed in a different organism because the ordinary code is shared, but promoters, RNA-processing requirements, initiation signals, tRNA abundance and protein maturation may differ. A eukaryotic genomic fragment containing introns may not be decoded correctly by a bacterial expression system simply because both use the standard genetic code. A suitable processed coding sequence is needed, and expression signals must be compatible. The broad conservation of codon assignments is a necessary advantage for recombinant expression, not a guarantee that every native gene can function unchanged in every host.

Finally, the code provides a link between evolutionary comparison and molecular mechanism. Two homologous coding sequences can differ at many nucleotide positions yet encode identical or closely related proteins. Synonymous differences help explain why DNA divergence may exceed amino-acid divergence. Conversely, conserved residues across organisms may indicate functional or structural constraints, although conservation alone is not proof of a specific role. The later genetics and evolution units examine these patterns more fully. At this stage the student should be able to explain the underlying mapping: multiple nucleotide words may produce the same residue, whereas changes crossing assignment boundaries may alter protein sequence.

The concepts of this chapter converge on a single causal picture. The nucleotide message must have a defined direction and a chosen grouping. Its triplets must be interpreted through the applicable code. Adaptors must recognize those triplets and carry the correct amino acids. Initiation and termination define the boundaries of an ordinary translation event, while limited wobble helps a finite adaptor repertoire cover synonymous assignments. Exceptions show why the biological context must always accompany the table. With this framework in place, the next chapter can examine how a tRNA becomes correctly associated with its amino acid and how synthetase proofreading protects the correspondence that the genetic code requires.
