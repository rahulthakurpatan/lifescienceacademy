## Transfer RNA export: recognition of a folded RNA cargo

Transfer RNA solves a different biological problem from messenger RNA. An mRNA carries a sequence of codons that can be read by ribosomes. A tRNA is an adaptor that connects an anticodon with a particular amino acid when correctly charged. Its function depends critically on its folded structure and chemically defined ends. Nuclear genes encoding tRNAs are generally transcribed by RNA polymerase III, and their initial products often require trimming, addition of the CCA sequence when it is not encoded, nucleotide modification and, for selected tRNAs, intron removal. These processing reactions produce or prepare a compact RNA structure capable of interacting with the translation machinery.

Because a tRNA is a distinct class of cargo, its route out of the nucleus need not be the same as the bulk mRNA pathway. Exportin-t is a well-characterized tRNA export receptor in vertebrates, and Los1 is a corresponding receptor in budding yeast. It belongs to the karyopherin family and cooperates with nuclear RanGTP to bind appropriate tRNA cargo. Structural features of the tRNA, including aspects of its folded acceptor and T-arm regions and the state of its ends, contribute to recognition. This form of recognition illustrates a more direct relationship between the three-dimensional organization of an RNA and its transport competence than is usually found in the broad adaptor-mediated selection of diverse messenger RNAs.

Imagine two tRNA precursors whose primary sequences differ only at a terminal processing site. The first is trimmed to produce an appropriate acceptor stem and properly formed ends. The second retains a long unprocessed extension. Even if both RNAs contain the correct anticodon, the extension can disrupt the normal structural surface or make the ends unsuitable for exportin recognition. Therefore the anticodon is not the sole determinant of tRNA export. The receptor must recognize a molecule with a suitable overall organization. The ability of a tRNA to base-pair with a codon cannot compensate automatically for every defect in maturation or transport.

The export cycle begins in the nucleus, where a suitable tRNA and RanGTP cooperate in forming an exportin complex. Reversible interactions between the receptor and pore constituents permit translocation. At the cytoplasmic side, hydrolysis of Ran-bound GTP and subsequent rearrangement of the transport machinery promote dissociation. The liberated tRNA can participate in further maturation or other steps necessary for its use in translation. The receptor returns for reuse. The spatial nucleotide-state cycle discussed previously thus has a direct example in RNA biology rather than being merely a general scheme for protein trafficking.

The examination-level model should include exceptions. Not all tRNAs follow an absolutely identical sequence of maturation and transport. In budding yeast, the processing of certain intron-containing tRNAs involves splicing at the mitochondrial surface after their initial nuclear export. Cytoplasmic tRNAs can also return to the nucleus in some eukaryotic systems, for example during changes in nutritional conditions or through normal recycling pathways. Several export routes can overlap in function, and loss of Los1 or exportin-t does not necessarily eliminate all tRNA export. These observations are important because they demonstrate that a transport pathway can have multiple routes and that the destination of a molecule can change over its lifetime.

A common error is to infer that every tRNA must be fully modified, spliced and aminoacylated before it can exit the nucleus. Nuclear export requirements depend on the organism, tRNA species and pathway. In particular, aminoacylation is an important step in preparing tRNA for its decoding function, but it should not be stated as a universal requirement for initial nuclear export. The correct approach is to identify the specific route being discussed, and distinguish recognition for transport from recognition for amino acid charging or binding to the ribosome.

A tRNA export defect may produce a consequence different from a messenger RNA export defect. Suppose a cell has normal cytoplasmic levels of a particular messenger RNA but a shortage of cytoplasmic tRNAs. Protein synthesis can still decline because the ribosome lacks an adequate supply of aminoacylated adaptors. If specific tRNA species become limiting, the effects could differ among coding sequences according to their codon composition and the availability of alternative decoding tRNAs. This connects RNA transport with the later topics of genetic code and translational fidelity without implying that codon recognition itself occurs at the nuclear pore.

To distinguish the two classes experimentally, one could examine the location of representative mature tRNAs and polyadenylated mRNAs in the same cell. A selective increase in the nuclear fraction of tRNA with preserved bulk mRNA localization would support a cargo-specific defect. A general disturbance of nuclear pores or of cellular energy balance might instead alter both distributions. Because a tRNA may undergo additional processing after export, the probes used in such an experiment must distinguish the RNA species relevant to the hypothesis. A probe that recognizes an intron-containing precursor does not necessarily report the distribution of the same molecules as a probe specific for mature tRNA.

## Different destinations for ribosomal, small nuclear and other RNAs

Ribosomal RNA illustrates why RNA export cannot be defined solely as delivery of a naked nucleotide chain. In eukaryotes, much of rRNA synthesis and early ribosomal subunit assembly takes place in the nucleolus. The rRNAs associate with ribosomal proteins, many of which have been synthesized in the cytoplasm and imported into the nucleus. The growing preribosomal particles must eventually reach the cytoplasm, where the small and large subunits complete relevant maturation steps and participate in translation. Export of such assemblies involves cargo-specific adapters and transport factors, including Ran-dependent export mechanisms in well-studied systems.

A representative example is the adaptor Nmd3, which helps link a pre-60S ribosomal subunit to the export receptor CRM1, also called exportin 1 or XPO1. This illustrates the difference between receptor and adaptor once again. The RNA-containing ribosomal precursor is not simply recognized as a generic mRNA. Rather, an appropriate assembly state permits interaction with an adaptor that communicates with the export machinery. Additional factors can participate, and export routes for the two ribosomal subunits should not be assumed to be identical. For this chapter, the important comparison is between transport of an assembled preribosomal particle, a folded tRNA and a typical messenger ribonucleoprotein.

If the large subunit export pathway fails, the presence of normal mRNA in the cytoplasm does not guarantee normal protein synthesis. The ribosome requires both subunits for productive translation. A cell may accumulate immature ribosomal particles in the nucleus, display an altered subunit balance, and synthesize fewer proteins. This phenotype must be distinguished from a defect in rRNA transcription itself. For example, an experiment comparing the abundance of newly synthesized rRNA with the distribution of ribosomal proteins and precursor particles could help separate failure to make the particle from failure to deliver it. The nucleolus is an assembly site, not the normal site of cytoplasmic protein translation.

Several small nuclear RNAs have a more surprising itinerary. Although their eventual role is associated with nuclear processes such as splicing, some spliceosomal snRNAs in animals undergo an initial cytoplasmic stage during their maturation. Representative RNA polymerase II-derived spliceosomal snRNAs are exported with the help of cap-associated factors and CRM1-dependent machinery. They associate with characteristic Sm proteins in the cytoplasm and undergo further maturation before the resulting snRNPs are imported into the nucleus. It is incorrect to assume that every RNA with a nuclear final function must remain inside the nucleus from the moment it is transcribed.

U6 snRNA provides a useful contrast because it is synthesized by RNA polymerase III and does not undergo the same canonical cytoplasmic Sm-core assembly route as the representative Pol II-derived spliceosomal snRNAs. Its processing and association with Lsm proteins follow a different organization. The distinction matters not because an examination student should memorize every accessory factor, but because the use of the term snRNA identifies a functional class rather than a universal pathway of transport. RNA identity, processing and assembly state jointly determine the relevant itinerary.

Other RNA molecules may be retained in the nucleus because their normal roles require that location. Small nucleolar RNAs contribute to the processing and modification of other RNAs in the nucleolus, and many long noncoding RNAs are enriched at nuclear locations where they perform their functions. Their nuclear enrichment should not automatically be diagnosed as defective export. In fact, inappropriate export of a normally retained RNA could itself disrupt function. Conversely, some noncoding RNAs act in the cytoplasm. The absence of an open reading frame encoding a conventional protein cannot be used as a reliable criterion for deciding whether a molecule will be exported.

The broad principle across these examples is that the cell controls RNA traffic according to function and assembly history, rather than following a single rule that all RNA must travel outward. Some molecules are intended for one-way delivery to the cytoplasm, some return to the nucleus, some remain mainly nuclear, and some move between compartments in response to physiological conditions. These diverse routes allow eukaryotic cells to organize the steps of gene expression in space as well as in time.

One way to reason about these routes is to ask two separate questions. What molecular entity crosses the nuclear pore, and what does that entity become afterward? For an mRNA the answer might be an export-competent mRNP that becomes a cytoplasmic translation substrate. For a tRNA, it may be a folded RNA bound to an exportin that becomes available for later charging or other maturation. For a preribosomal subunit, it is a large RNA–protein assembly that continues maturation. For certain snRNAs, it is a precursor that must return to the nucleus after interacting with cytoplasmic assembly machinery. Classifying by these transitions is more useful than merely drawing all RNA types on one arrow from nucleus to cytoplasm.

## Cytoplasmic RNA localization: delivering the instruction rather than the protein

Arrival in the cytoplasm does not end the problem of spatial organization. The cytoplasm contains many regions with different structures and functions. A migrating cell builds a leading edge, a neuron possesses processes that can extend far from its cell body, and an egg can establish distinct regions that will contribute to different embryonic structures. A uniform concentration of every protein in such cells would not reproduce their functional asymmetry. One strategy is to synthesize proteins close to the sites at which they are needed by transporting the corresponding mRNAs.

A localization element is a sequence or structural feature of an RNA that contributes to its intracellular destination. Such an element is often called an RNA zipcode. Many known examples are in the 3′ untranslated region, although localization information can also occur elsewhere. The term untranslated does not mean functionless. A 3′ UTR can interact with RNA-binding proteins without changing the amino acid sequence of the protein encoded upstream. A mutation in this region may therefore alter the spatial organization of gene expression even if the protein sequence is completely unchanged.

The transport-competent cytoplasmic RNA particle contains RNA-binding proteins and, in many examples of directed transport, connections to cytoskeletal motors. Microtubules and actin filaments provide polarized tracks. Kinesins and dyneins are major microtubule motor families, whereas myosins move on actin. Motor proteins couple ATP-dependent conformational changes to movement along their tracks. The precise motor and adaptor combination depends on the cell type and RNA cargo. A student should not infer that every kinesin family member moves toward the plus end, or that an anatomical destination such as the cell periphery always corresponds to the same microtubule end in every cell. The organization of the tracks must be specified.

Directed motion is not the only way to establish enrichment. An RNA can diffuse through the cytoplasm and be captured at a destination by local binding partners. Alternatively, molecules can be produced or distributed relatively broadly but degraded preferentially outside one region. If the RNA is protected at a particular site, its steady-state abundance becomes higher there even without a motor delivering every molecule. These mechanisms can coexist. A motor can deliver cargo to a region, an anchoring protein can retain it, and local RNA stabilization can prolong its residence. A static localization image cannot identify which of these contributions was decisive.

The difference between directed transport and selective stabilization can be illustrated using a hypothetical cell divided into two equal regions. Suppose the same number of RNA molecules enters each region per minute, but the degradation rate is much lower in the first. At steady state the first region contains more RNA because its molecules remain there longer. An observer seeing the final distribution might conclude that RNA was transported preferentially toward that region, but this conclusion would be unjustified without measuring movement or lifetime. A localization phenotype is a spatial outcome, not a direct demonstration of the mechanism producing it.

One well-known model is localization of beta-actin mRNA toward the leading edge of motile fibroblasts. A zipcode in the 3′ UTR contributes to association with zipcode-binding proteins, notably ZBP1. Localization of this message helps coordinate actin production with regions of cell movement. Deleting or altering the localization element can disrupt the distribution of the RNA without changing the actin coding region. The consequences can include altered local protein availability and cell polarity. However, actin protein itself also has mechanisms for assembly and redistribution, so loss of RNA localization should not be interpreted as proof that no actin can ever reach the leading edge.

A second model arises during early development of Drosophila. Localization of maternal bicoid mRNA toward the anterior region of the egg establishes a spatial source of Bicoid protein after translation begins. The protein distribution contributes to developmental patterning. Here, transporting or anchoring the message can specify where a regulatory protein first appears, which influences the formation of positional information. The biological implication differs from the fibroblast example: rather than supporting a continually moving cell edge, maternal RNA localization helps organize the body plan of a developing organism. Both examples nevertheless demonstrate the same broad principle of establishing local protein production through RNA placement.

Neurons illustrate the importance of distance and time. Some RNAs are delivered into axons or dendrites where local translation can contribute to cellular responses. A signal acting at a distant process need not always wait for a new protein to be synthesized near the nucleus and transported from the cell body. Local RNA can provide an available template whose translation is regulated by conditions at the destination. Not every neuronal protein is made locally, and RNA localization is only one aspect of neuronal organization. The point is that placing the instruction close to its site of use can change the temporal and spatial properties of a response.

An RNA that is actively transported can remain translationally repressed during transit. This may prevent premature synthesis of the encoded protein away from its intended destination. Upon arrival, changes in RNA-binding proteins or local signaling can permit translation. The RNA may then remain anchored or may be released again. Localization, anchoring and translation are distinct steps. A mutation affecting an anchoring protein might allow RNA to arrive transiently but fail to accumulate. A mutation affecting translational activation might allow normal RNA enrichment but produce insufficient local protein. A motor defect might prevent RNA from reaching the region in the first place. Each would require different evidence for diagnosis.

The endoplasmic reticulum supplies another important comparison. Many messages encoding secreted or membrane proteins become associated with ribosomes at the ER because the emerging polypeptide contains a signal recognized by the signal recognition particle. This is a protein-targeting mechanism that can bring the translating mRNA–ribosome complex to the ER. It should not be confused with a zipcode-mediated mechanism in which the RNA sequence or structure itself is recognized for localization. Some RNAs also associate with the ER through other interactions. The distinction illustrates how an RNA can acquire a spatial distribution either directly through RNA-binding machinery or indirectly through the protein being synthesized from it.

For Part C reasoning, always ask which property a mutation changes. A coding-region mutation that abolishes a signal peptide can disturb cotranslational targeting even if a localization element remains intact. A 3′ UTR mutation can disrupt an RNA-binding site without modifying the encoded peptide. A motor mutation can alter many cargoes and cell structures simultaneously. An RNA-binding protein mutant that specifically loses affinity for a zipcode may have a narrower effect. Appropriate control experiments are necessary to separate direct changes in RNA positioning from secondary changes in cell organization or RNA abundance.
