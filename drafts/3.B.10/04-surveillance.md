## Nuclear retention and the decision between repair, export and removal

RNA molecules differ in how long they remain in the nucleus. Some are retained because their normal function requires a nuclear location. Others are delayed because their assembly is still in progress. Still others fail to leave because they are defective and interact with quality-control machinery. These categories are biologically different even if the molecules appear in the same compartment under a microscope. A good interpretation begins by identifying what a particular transcript normally does and what processing state the experiment has detected.

Consider an intron-containing pre-mRNA while its last intron is still being removed. It is not necessarily defective; it may simply be an intermediate on a normal pathway. If splicing proceeds, the RNA can acquire the appropriate protein associations and enter the export pathway. However, if the splice donor is mutated so that removal becomes inefficient, the transcript may spend longer in a state that is not suited for export. Retention provides time during which processing can potentially be completed, but it also limits the delivery of an inappropriate template to the translation machinery. A delay thus has different implications depending on whether it is reversible and whether the transcript can eventually mature.

Retention can arise through more than one mechanism. Inefficient loading of an export receptor can leave a molecule unable to interact effectively with the pore. Alternatively, RNA-associated proteins can actively promote nuclear retention even when some export-related factors are present. Incompletely processed messages may remain near their transcription sites, associate with nuclear structures, or be delayed near the nuclear basket of the pore. The relative importance of these mechanisms differs across organisms and RNA substrates. The result is not a perfectly uniform gate that examines all messages in precisely the same way.

An important model system is budding yeast, where the nuclear basket-associated protein Mlp1 has been implicated in restricting the escape of some unspliced transcripts. The mammalian nuclear basket has corresponding proteins, including TPR, but the details of nuclear surveillance cannot be transferred uncritically from one species to another. The examination-level lesson is that components located at the nuclear entrance to a pore can contribute to cargo discrimination as well as to physical transport. A defect in such a retention mechanism could increase the cytoplasmic abundance of certain abnormal RNAs even when normal messages still cross the pore.

When a molecule cannot complete its required processing, degradation provides a way to prevent indefinite accumulation. The nuclear RNA exosome is an important RNA-processing and decay complex. In this context, exosome refers to a multiprotein complex involved in RNA metabolism; it is not an extracellular membrane vesicle. Its associated catalytic activities can degrade RNA from the 3′ end and, in relevant eukaryotic systems, perform internal cleavage through particular catalytic components. Accessory proteins help bring RNA substrates to the complex and facilitate processing of structured molecules. The composition of the accessory machinery differs among yeast, mammals and other organisms.

A nuclease cannot necessarily distinguish a valuable message from a defective message just by recognizing the chemical structure of a phosphodiester bond. The same kinds of bonds occur in both molecules. Selectivity instead depends in part on the accessibility of RNA ends, RNA-binding proteins, particular sequence or structural features, and the pathways that deliver the RNA to a nuclease. This is one reason why correct mRNP assembly protects an RNA in a functional manner. The proteins associated with the RNA can influence not only its transport but also its vulnerability to degradation.

The exosome illustrates another important distinction between processing and complete destruction. A nuclease can remove a specific extension from an RNA precursor, thereby generating a functional product, or it can continue degrading an RNA until the original molecule no longer exists as a usable template. Whether the final result is maturation or elimination depends on the substrate, the enzymes involved and regulation of their access. Accordingly, detecting a nuclease associated with an RNA does not by itself establish that the RNA is being destroyed. One must determine whether a correctly formed product appears or whether the original RNA and its useful products disappear.

A defective 3′ processing signal can illustrate the competition between maturation and surveillance. Normally, a suitable cleavage event helps establish the transcript's 3′ end and contributes to formation of a competent particle. If cleavage fails, the transcript may extend beyond its normal endpoint and remain associated with transcription or nuclear processing machinery. Its ability to recruit the correct export factors may be diminished, and it may become susceptible to nuclear degradation. Several outcomes are possible: some molecules may use an alternative cleavage site, some may accumulate transiently, and others may be destroyed. A single mutation does not guarantee that every transcript produced from the gene follows exactly the same fate.

Retained introns require similarly careful interpretation. Some intron-retaining RNAs are unstable or poorly exported; others are regulated transcripts that remain nuclear until conditions favor processing. Still others can appear in the cytoplasm and may be translated or recognized by cytoplasmic surveillance. A retained intron is not always an accidental defect, and the presence of an intron in a cytoplasmic RNA is not proof that the nuclear quality-control system has completely failed. The effect depends on whether intron retention is normally part of the gene's expression pattern and whether the resulting transcript remains functional.

The concept of quality control also does not mean that RNA production is perfect in a healthy cell. Gene expression generates intermediate and sometimes nonproductive molecules. Surveillance reduces their harmful consequences and helps maintain useful RNA populations. Even normal RNA species eventually undergo turnover. The cell does not need to preserve each correctly synthesized molecule indefinitely; it needs to maintain sufficient functional molecules while responding to changing conditions. A nuclear decay pathway that removes abnormal products therefore contributes both to fidelity and to the regulation of the size of the RNA population.

Consider a mutant lacking an important nuclear decay activity. Defective nuclear RNAs may increase in abundance because they are no longer efficiently degraded. Their export need not improve. If the mutation affects the nuclease but leaves retention intact, much of the abnormal RNA may simply accumulate inside the nucleus. A different mutant that compromises retention might allow some defective RNA to reach the cytoplasm, where it can encounter additional surveillance. The two mutants distinguish the gate that delays or prevents transport from the machinery that destroys a substrate. These functions are often coupled, but they are not synonymous.

This reasoning also explains why a strong nuclear signal may not appear in every processing or export mutant. If defective transcripts are degraded very quickly, their nuclear concentration may remain low even though their probability of reaching the cytoplasm is severely reduced. When a second mutation slows degradation, the otherwise hidden population may become visible. Consequently, failure to observe accumulated nuclear RNA does not establish that export is normal. It is necessary to consider how production, retention, maturation and decay jointly determine the number of molecules detected.

## Translation-dependent surveillance: nonsense-mediated mRNA decay

The preceding mechanisms address whether an RNA is prepared for export. They cannot guarantee that every exported message will direct synthesis of the correct protein. Some defects become apparent only when the message is read by a ribosome. A premature termination codon is an important example. A base substitution can convert an amino acid codon into a stop codon, or a frameshift can bring an inappropriate stop into the reading frame. Alternative splicing can also produce a message with an early stop without requiring a permanent change in genomic DNA. Translation-dependent quality control helps limit the consequences of such messages.

Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, usually abbreviated NMD, is a eukaryotic surveillance pathway that reduces the abundance of many transcripts whose translation terminates in an inappropriate context. The word nonsense refers to the termination signal rather than to a message that lacks all meaningful information. A premature stop codon can be read correctly as a stop by the translation apparatus while still being inappropriate for the intended full-length protein. If the message remains abundant, translation may produce shortened polypeptides that lack necessary domains. NMD can reduce repeated synthesis of these abnormal products by lowering the availability of the RNA template.

The crucial feature of NMD is its dependence on translation in the canonical pathway. A splice junction or a nucleotide substitution does not by itself cause the pathway to operate without the relevant molecular context. The ribosome encounters a termination codon, and proteins associated with the RNA and translation termination contribute to deciding whether that event is appropriate. Thus nuclear RNA retention and NMD represent distinguishable opportunities for surveillance. A defective message that never reaches a translating ribosome may be eliminated through another route; it should not automatically be assigned to canonical NMD.

In mammals, exon junction complexes provide a particularly useful model for explaining why the position of a stop codon matters. During splicing, complexes are generally deposited about 20–24 nucleotides upstream of exon–exon junctions. When a ribosome translates through the coding sequence, it can displace complexes that lie in its path. If the normal stop codon is encountered after all relevant coding-region complexes have been displaced and there is no suitable downstream NMD-promoting context, the message can undergo ordinary termination and remain available for further translation.

Now suppose that a stop codon appears sufficiently far upstream of a downstream exon junction. A ribosome terminates before reaching the downstream complex. An exon junction complex remaining beyond the terminating ribosome can help recruit or stabilize interactions involving NMD factors. A widely used empirical mammalian rule is that a stop located more than roughly 50–55 nucleotides upstream of the final exon–exon junction often promotes NMD. The distance is measured to the junction, not to the 3′ end of the poly(A) tail and not directly to the center of the exon junction complex. It reflects the relationship between the terminating ribosome, the position of the junction-associated complex and the ability of the complex to remain downstream.

UPF1 is a central NMD factor and an ATP-dependent RNA helicase. UPF2 and UPF3 participate in many pathways of NMD, linking RNA-associated information to the surveillance machinery. In a commonly taught mammalian model, the termination event involves the eukaryotic release factors eRF1 and eRF3, and appropriate interactions stimulate phosphorylation of UPF1 by the kinase SMG1. Phosphorylated UPF1 can promote recruitment of RNA degradation machinery. Different downstream routes can involve internal cleavage, deadenylation, decapping and exonucleolytic degradation. This framework explains how an event in translation becomes coupled to removal of the message without requiring an extensive catalogue of all pathway proteins.

The mechanism has an important negative consequence for interpretation of protein measurements. Suppose an early stop codon is introduced into a gene and very little shortened protein is detected. One might initially think that the mutant RNA is never translated. In fact, limited translation may have occurred and initiated NMD, reducing the number of templates available for later translation. Alternatively, the shortened protein may be intrinsically unstable. A measurement of protein abundance alone cannot distinguish these explanations. Measurements of RNA synthesis, RNA stability and translation are needed to understand which layer of surveillance contributed.

The junction-distance rule is a useful predictor, not a universal law. A premature stop in the last exon often escapes the classic downstream-EJC-dependent mechanism because there is no later splice junction. A stop close to the final junction may also escape if the relevant complex is displaced or not retained. Other features of a transcript, including the termination environment and 3′ untranslated region, can influence NMD independently of a downstream exon junction. The pathway differs among species, and budding yeast does not follow the mammalian EJC model as a universal requirement. Therefore an examination question using the 50–55 nucleotide rule should specify an appropriate mammalian context and should not demand an unconditional prediction for all genes.

NMD can also influence the abundance of some normal cellular transcripts. Some regulated alternative splice forms contain termination contexts that promote NMD, making the combination of splicing and decay a means of controlling gene expression. This does not mean that every transcript targeted by NMD originates from a mutation or that every such event is harmful. The pathway responds to molecular features, and cells can exploit these features for regulation. The detailed control of particular gene circuits belongs to a later discussion, but recognizing this broader role prevents an overly narrow definition of RNA quality control.

A practical way to test an NMD hypothesis is to compare otherwise similar reporter RNAs with different stop-codon positions. If the message bearing an early stop decreases in abundance, whereas an otherwise matched message with a stop in the final exon remains relatively stable, the result is consistent with a positional effect. Reducing UPF1 activity or preventing relevant translation can help test whether the loss depends on NMD. These interventions have their own broader cellular consequences, so appropriate controls and rescue experiments strengthen the conclusion. The essential logic is to show that the RNA is produced, reaches a context in which it can be translated, and undergoes accelerated loss because of the surveillance mechanism.

The distinction between nuclear and cytoplasmic defects becomes particularly important when a mutation changes splicing. An altered splice product may retain an intron, be poorly exported, or contain a premature stop after successful export. It can therefore be sensitive to nuclear surveillance, NMD, or both in different fractions of the RNA population. The student should resist choosing one mechanism merely because the mutation is a splice-site mutation. The relevant evidence is the actual processed RNA sequence, its location, the timing of its loss, and its dependence on translation and specific quality-control factors.

## RNA degradation as a regulated fate rather than an accidental disappearance

When discussing RNA surveillance it is easy to confuse the trigger for degradation with the chemical reactions that carry it out. The trigger identifies an RNA or a state of the RNA as suitable for removal. Nucleases execute cleavage and degradation. A single nuclease can be used by multiple recognition pathways, and a recognition pathway can use more than one route of degradation. The molecular logic of specificity is therefore not always located in the catalytic enzyme itself.

An endonuclease cuts within an RNA chain. An exonuclease removes nucleotides progressively from an accessible end. These terms describe the position and direction of the reaction rather than whether the RNA was originally normal or defective. In many eukaryotic cytoplasmic pathways, shortening of a poly(A) tail can facilitate subsequent degradation. Removal of the cap creates a 5′ end accessible to suitable 5′-to-3′ exonucleases such as XRN1. Other substrates can be degraded from the 3′ end by exosome-associated machinery. Internal cleavage can generate new ends that become substrates for both directions of exonucleolytic removal.

The order of reactions is not identical for every RNA or every surveillance pathway. For example, an internal cleavage event can bypass the need to wait for complete shortening of a poly(A) tail before the fragments are degraded. Conversely, normal messages can undergo regulated deadenylation and decay without a premature stop codon. A decrease in poly(A) signal does not necessarily mean that the entire RNA has already disappeared. It could reflect shortening of the tail below the detection threshold of a particular probe or a transition to a decay intermediate.

Quality control also extends beyond recognition of early termination. A ribosome can stall on an RNA or reach the end of a coding template without encountering a proper stop. No-go and nonstop surveillance refer to other classes of problems associated with translation. Their triggers differ from a premature termination event recognized by NMD. At the level required for this chapter, it is enough to distinguish the nature of the defect and recognize that cells have several ways to monitor whether an RNA is functioning appropriately. Detailed pathways of ribosome rescue and protein quality control belong to the later translation and gene-regulation chapters.

The overall biological advantage is that errors need not have permanent effects. A cell can destroy a defective RNA while retaining the DNA sequence from which it was transcribed. If the defective molecule arose through a temporary error in processing, later transcripts may be normal. If the genomic sequence itself contains a mutation, repeated transcription can generate new defective molecules, and surveillance may continue to remove them. RNA decay does not repair the DNA mutation. It changes the amount of mutant information expressed as RNA and potentially protein. This is a useful connection with earlier chapters on DNA repair and later chapters on genetics.

When a decay pathway is inhibited, more RNA can be detected even if the rate of transcription remains constant. When an export pathway is inhibited, nuclear RNA may increase, cytoplasmic RNA may decrease, or both may decline if nuclear degradation is enhanced. These different outcomes illustrate why RNA quantity is not a direct measure of transcription and why localization measurements should be combined with temporal information.
