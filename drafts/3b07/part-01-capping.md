# Node 3.B.07 — Eukaryotic mRNA End Processing: 5′ Capping and 3′ Polyadenylation

> Unpublished working manuscript. Not approved for main or student-facing deployment. Part 1: capping foundations and mechanism.

## Why a newly transcribed messenger is not necessarily a mature message

A molecule of DNA and a molecule of RNA can carry corresponding sequences without having identical biological roles. A gene in a eukaryotic chromosome is a persistent source of information, whereas a messenger RNA is a temporary working copy that must be delivered to ribosomes and eventually removed. The cell therefore needs a means of recognizing which RNA molecules are sufficiently mature to be used, protecting them while they are transported, and controlling how long they can contribute to protein synthesis. The two ends of an mRNA provide important sites at which these requirements are addressed.

To see why the ends matter, imagine an RNA molecule containing an intact protein-coding sequence but lacking the normal molecular structures at its termini. The sequence can still specify an amino acid chain in principle. However, if the molecule is rapidly destroyed or fails to recruit the proteins needed for translation, the coded information will have little opportunity to be expressed. The sequence specifying a protein and the molecular features permitting that sequence to be used are distinct aspects of gene expression. The mature messenger is consequently not merely a string of codons.

An ordinary nuclear protein-coding gene has a transcription start site, a transcribed region containing sequences that may be retained or removed, and sequences that guide formation of the mature RNA end. The translation start and stop codons delimit the coding region, not the entire mRNA. The retained sequence preceding the start codon forms the five-prime untranslated region, and the sequence between the stop codon and the mature three-prime end forms the three-prime untranslated region. These regions are transcribed but generally do not specify the amino acid sequence of the major protein product. Their presence enables regulation without altering that product.

The cap is attached at the extreme five-prime end of the RNA rather than at the translation start codon. The polyadenosine tail is appended at a processed three-prime end rather than directly after the stop codon in most genes. Thus a messenger can contain several hundred nucleotides after its stop codon before reaching the cleavage site. A mutation in that region can impair protein production while leaving every codon intact. Such a mutation tests our understanding of the whole gene-expression pathway instead of just the genetic code.

In nuclear eukaryotic gene expression, RNA polymerase II generates the primary transcript. Processing machinery normally begins to act before polymerase II has finished its journey along the gene. The term pre-mRNA refers to the RNA before completion of maturation, not necessarily to a freely floating, full-length molecule that waits in the nucleus for every processing event. Capping occurs near the beginning of synthesis. The three-prime end can only be formed after the relevant sequence signals have been transcribed. Splicing often overlaps these events in time. The sequence of events is constrained by the order in which the information emerges from the polymerase, but it is not a universal rigid assembly line.

This temporal organization is important in experimental reasoning. A short, newly made transcript at an early point in elongation can already be capped and still lack the sequences required for cleavage and polyadenylation. Its absence of a poly(A) tail is not in itself evidence of a defective capping reaction. Conversely, a transcript that has reached the downstream processing region but has not been cleaved may have a perfectly normal cap. Each processing event needs its own molecular assay.

## The chemical asymmetry of an RNA molecule

RNA is assembled from ribonucleotides connected by phosphodiester bonds. The ribose sugar gives each nucleotide a five-prime carbon and a three-prime carbon. In the interior of an RNA chain, a phosphate links the three-prime oxygen of one sugar to the five-prime oxygen of the next. This orientation gives the polymer a polarity that is retained regardless of whether it folds into a helix or remains single stranded. The notation five-prime to three-prime describes the chemical direction of the backbone, not the position of the molecule in a drawing.

When RNA polymerase initiates a transcript without an external primer, the initiating nucleoside triphosphate is the first residue of the new chain. Subsequent nucleotides become incorporated by attack of the growing chain's terminal three-prime hydroxyl group on the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphate. The beta and gamma phosphates of that incoming nucleotide leave as pyrophosphate. The first nucleotide is unusual because its own five-prime triphosphate can remain attached to the beginning of the nascent RNA. The RNA therefore begins with a triphosphate group and ends with an available three-prime hydroxyl group, provided that it has not undergone any other modification.

The cell uses this chemical distinction to modify the two ends differently. At the five-prime end, an enzyme recognizes a terminal phosphate configuration and converts it into a substrate for attaching a guanosine in an unusual orientation. At the three-prime end, the canonical pathway first generates a new end by cutting the RNA and then uses the hydroxyl on the retained portion as the site at which additional adenosines are polymerized.

Neither reaction should be confused with ordinary elongation of the RNA by polymerase II. Polymerase II selects each incoming nucleotide using base pairing with DNA. Capping instead attaches a particular nucleotide through a linkage not normally present within the RNA backbone. Polyadenylation repeatedly incorporates the same nucleotide without using a DNA template to specify each successive addition. Although all three pathways involve nucleotide chemistry, their information sources and products are not interchangeable.

The meaning of the term terminal modification is also worth clarifying. When a cap is added, the original first transcribed nucleotide remains in the RNA; the cap guanosine is a new chemical attachment on its five-prime side. When a poly(A) tail is added, the newly cleaved RNA retains the genomic sequence up to the chosen cleavage position and acquires an additional sequence of adenosines after that position. The newly synthesized tail is part of the RNA, but its individual residues need not correspond to a long series of thymidines in the DNA template.

## The structure and nomenclature of the five-prime cap

The characteristic cap of most nuclear eukaryotic mRNAs contains a guanine base methylated at its nitrogen at position seven. Its common shorthand is m7GpppN, where N represents the first transcribed nucleotide, ppp represents the three phosphates connecting the two nucleosides, and m7G denotes seven-methylguanosine. In this notation the base modification should not be confused with methylation of the ribose sugar. The cap guanosine is attached through its own five-prime position to the five-prime position of the original first nucleotide.

The five-prime to five-prime triphosphate bridge is chemically distinct from the three-prime to five-prime phosphodiester connections found in the remainder of the RNA. This unusual linkage has two consequences. It creates a molecular pattern recognized by cap-binding proteins, and it removes the ordinary exposed end configuration that certain exonucleases need for efficient degradation. Methylation of the guanine enhances recognition by specialized proteins. It is not merely a decorative addition to a guanosine that already functions identically.

Number the first two transcribed residues one and two. A cap containing methylated guanine but no additional two-prime ribose methylation at these residues is called cap zero. If the ribose of residue one is methylated at its two-prime oxygen, the product is called cap one. If the corresponding ribose at residue two is also methylated, the product is called cap two. These distinctions describe the chemical modifications, not the number of cap guanosines. All three structures have one terminal cap guanosine. Cap one does not contain two guanosines and cap two does not contain three.

The two-prime oxygen is part of the ribose hydroxyl group, and methylation replaces the hydrogen of that hydroxyl with a methyl group. It does not replace the nucleotide's base, remove its three-prime hydroxyl, or alter the order of codons in the downstream reading frame. The cap pattern varies across organisms and RNA classes; cap one is common among mammalian cellular mRNAs, but a universal requirement for cap two would be an incorrect generalization.

An exam diagram may depict the guanosine at the left of the RNA using a conventional line that resembles the phosphodiester backbone. The labels on the linkage, rather than the visual position of the symbols, establish the identity of the cap. In an ordinary RNA chain, a residue has a different orientation relative to its preceding neighbor. The cap is often called inverted because its attachment joins equivalent five-prime ends. This does not mean the RNA polymerase has switched its direction of synthesis or that a stretch of the RNA sequence has been reversed.

The cap guanine is not counted as the first genomically encoded residue when the transcription start site is identified. If the coding DNA strand at positions plus one through plus six reads ACGTTA, the RNA sequence starts ACGUUA, before accounting for its cap. The modified guanosine attached to the first A should be described separately. Writing GACGUUA as though the first G were transcribed from DNA obscures the distinction between transcription initiation and enzymatic capping.

## Capping as a sequence of three chemical reactions

The canonical pathway begins with a newly synthesized RNA bearing a five-prime triphosphate, which can be written pppN-RNA. It is useful to distinguish the three phosphate groups by position relative to the first ribose: alpha is closest to the ribose, beta is intermediate, and gamma is farthest away. This naming convention is the same one used for free nucleoside triphosphates. An RNA triphosphatase removes the terminal gamma phosphate by hydrolysis. The remaining RNA has a diphosphate at its beginning and the removed phosphate is released as inorganic phosphate. No guanosine has yet been attached.

The next reaction is catalyzed by RNA guanylyltransferase. Its donor substrate is GTP. The enzyme forms a covalent intermediate with the guanosine monophosphate portion of GTP, releasing pyrophosphate. A nucleophilic lysine side chain in the canonical guanylyltransferase participates in this temporary covalent bond. The activated GMP is subsequently transferred to the diphosphate-bearing RNA. The donor's alpha phosphate and the two remaining RNA phosphates make the three-phosphate bridge. Counting the phosphates from their respective substrates helps explain why the product is not simply guanosine joined to RNA by a normal phosphodiester bond.

A simplified reaction ledger writes the first step as pppRNA plus water yields ppRNA plus inorganic phosphate. The next overall step is GTP plus ppRNA yields GpppRNA plus pyrophosphate. The intermediate enzyme-GMP species is absent from the overall equation because the enzyme is regenerated. The product at this stage is a guanosine-capped RNA that still lacks the characteristic N7 methyl group. The distinction becomes particularly useful when a student is asked to predict the product in a reaction mixture that contains only the first two enzymatic activities.

The third step uses a guanine-N7 methyltransferase and S-adenosylmethionine, abbreviated SAM. SAM provides the transferable methyl group. The product is methylated guanosine attached to RNA, together with S-adenosylhomocysteine. SAM is not the donor of the guanosine, GTP does not supply the methyl group, and ATP is not the direct nucleotide donor for formation of the cap. Although cellular metabolism is needed to replenish all these substrates, the stoichiometric distinction is essential when individual reactions are examined in vitro.

The order of the reactions follows from their substrates. Guanylyltransferase normally acts on the diphosphate RNA produced by the triphosphatase. The methyltransferase can recognize the guanosine-containing product of guanylyl transfer. A loss of the triphosphatase should therefore interfere with formation of the normal cap even if both downstream enzymes are present. A defect restricted to N7 methylation can leave a GpppN structure present without the mature methylated cap. Measuring total attached guanosine and measuring mature methylated guanosine are accordingly different tests.

In mammals the triphosphatase and guanylyltransferase activities are contained in a bifunctional capping enzyme, while N7 methylation is performed by another enzyme. Budding yeast organizes the three activities differently, using separate proteins. These differences in protein organization should not be mistaken for differences in the fundamental chemical transformations. The term capping enzyme can designate a particular multifunctional protein or the collection of enzymes involved in the pathway, so a question describing an enzyme mutant should state which activity has been disrupted.

The subsequent two-prime ribose methylations use additional methyltransferases and methyl donors. They create cap variants rather than the basic five-prime to five-prime linkage itself. If an assay detects N7 methylation but not ribose methylation, it can still detect cap zero. Conversely, a methyl group detected on RNA is not automatically evidence of a cap because other RNA bases and riboses can be methylated during distinct processing reactions. An assay must distinguish the chemical identity and location of its product.

## Why capping is coordinated with early elongation

The first end of a growing RNA emerges from the RNA exit path of polymerase II while transcription is still in progress. The cap can be made once enough RNA is accessible to the enzymes, commonly when the transcript is only a few tens of nucleotides long. This early timing limits the interval during which an uncapped end is exposed and provides a platform for RNA-associated factors while the transcript is being extended.

The largest subunit of RNA polymerase II contains a carboxy-terminal domain, often abbreviated CTD, with repeated heptapeptide motifs. The phosphorylation pattern of this domain changes during the transcription cycle. During initiation and early elongation, phosphorylation at serine five in the repeats helps recruit the capping machinery. This is a protein interaction and recruitment mechanism. Phosphorylation of the polymerase does not itself convert an RNA triphosphate into a cap, transfer guanosine, or methylate guanine.

The usefulness of recruitment is easiest to understand in terms of local concentration. If polymerase II carries a factor near its emerging RNA, encounters between the factor and its appropriate substrate become more probable than if the factor had to locate every transcript independently throughout the nucleus. Recruitment also establishes temporal order. A processing factor that is most useful near the beginning of transcription can be enriched during that stage, while proteins needed for the three-prime end become effective later. The actual cell contains overlapping interactions rather than an absolute switch between isolated sets of proteins.

An early CTD phosphorylation defect can cause an apparent capping phenotype without changing the catalytic site of any capping enzyme. If the enzyme retains activity against purified diphosphate RNA in a test tube but produces less capped RNA inside the cell, defective recruitment is one possible explanation. Another is that the perturbation also impairs transcription initiation or elongation, reducing the number of RNA substrates. These alternatives can be distinguished only by measuring the amount of synthesized RNA and the fraction that acquires a cap, alongside direct assessment of enzyme activity or localization.

The existence of early recruitment also explains why capping is associated with polymerase II rather than simply being applied to all RNA molecules containing a terminal triphosphate. A ribosomal RNA precursor synthesized by polymerase I may start with a triphosphate, but it normally enters a different maturation pathway. Its initial chemistry is not a sufficient predictor of its ultimate terminal structures. Polymerase identity, associated factors and RNA sequence or structure all contribute to substrate selection.

There are important boundaries to this comparison. Several polymerase II-derived noncoding RNAs receive caps, so capping does not prove that a molecule encodes a protein. Some small nuclear RNAs undergo further cap modifications in their particular maturation pathways. Conversely, various organellar and viral RNAs use distinct mechanisms. The canonical nuclear mRNA cap is therefore best treated as a widespread feature of a particular gene-expression system, not as a defining property of every RNA in eukaryotic cells.

## Cap binding, protection and communication with the translational machinery

Formation of a chemical cap creates a binding site for proteins that interact selectively with it. In the nucleus, the cap-binding complex contains CBP80 and CBP20, also known as NCBP1 and NCBP2. Recognition depends on the intact cap, and the bound complex contributes to the assembly of a messenger ribonucleoprotein particle. An mRNA in the cell is normally associated with proteins rather than behaving as a permanently naked polymer.

Protection against five-prime-directed decay is partly chemical and partly a consequence of protein association. Many five-prime to three-prime exonucleases, including the familiar cytoplasmic XRN1 enzyme, prefer RNA bearing a five-prime monophosphate. The cap's triphosphate bridge and attached methylated guanosine do not provide that ordinary substrate. A decapping reaction can remove the cap and generate a suitable monophosphorylated end, allowing rapid exonucleolytic decay. The cap is thus a reversible protection rather than a guarantee of permanent stability.

The cap-binding complex helps coordinate subsequent maturation and export. It is incorrect, however, to imagine that a cap alone functions as a universal nuclear export ticket. The RNA must also assemble an appropriate complement of proteins and pass quality-control processes. Unprocessed or incorrectly processed RNA can be retained or degraded despite the presence of an intact cap. The export of mature RNA is developed more fully in Node 3.B.10.

In canonical cytoplasmic cap-dependent translation initiation, eIF4E recognizes the methylated cap. It associates with eIF4G, a scaffold that organizes other factors, and eIF4A, an RNA helicase participating in the preparation of structured RNA for ribosome recruitment. This cap-associated machinery facilitates recruitment of the small ribosomal subunit with its associated initiation factors. The ribosome does not generally begin translation by interpreting the cap guanosine as the first codon. It locates the translation initiation site within the RNA. The cap supports access to the message without changing the meaning of the coding sequence.

A cap-binding mutation can therefore decrease protein output even when the amount of messenger RNA is initially unchanged. Conversely, an uncapped transcript may disappear rapidly, causing reduced protein expression indirectly because less message remains. The two mechanisms can produce a similar endpoint in a reporter assay. Measuring RNA amount and protein output separately is necessary to establish whether the translation efficiency per RNA molecule has changed. A biochemical assay showing reduced eIF4E affinity supports a direct recognition defect but does not, on its own, quantify RNA degradation.

Cap-independent translation strategies also exist, including internal ribosome entry in appropriate contexts. Such examples demonstrate that the cap-dependent pathway is not the only means of recruiting ribosomes. They do not mean that the cap is dispensable for ordinary nuclear mRNA expression. A useful exam answer specifies the assumed pathway before predicting the consequence of removing a cap.
