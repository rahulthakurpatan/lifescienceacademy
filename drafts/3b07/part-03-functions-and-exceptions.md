## The mature tail as a protein-binding surface

The adenosines at the three-prime end of a messenger are not primarily intended to become part of the protein product. A ribosome normally encounters a translation stop signal before entering the tail. Instead, the tail constitutes a region where proteins involved in the lifetime and use of the messenger can bind. Cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding proteins recognize stretches of adenosines and establish interactions with additional RNA-associated factors. It is these protein interactions as well as the RNA sequence itself that give the tail its biological significance.

An RNA molecule without a tail can sometimes be translated. This is especially clear when one considers the normal existence of particular nonpolyadenylated mRNAs. The correct claim is that a poly(A) tail commonly contributes to the expression of canonical polyadenylated messengers in eukaryotes. Its effect depends on the transcript, the organism, the associated proteins, and the stage of development. Treating every RNA without a long poly(A) tail as a nonfunctional molecule would erase important biological exceptions.

The terminal location of the tail is useful because it allows a cell to change the state of an RNA without necessarily modifying its coding sequence. During gradual removal of adenosines by deadenylases, the sequence specifying amino acids can remain intact for a considerable period. A shortening tail can nevertheless change the RNA's complement of bound proteins and its susceptibility to subsequent decay. This separates modification of an RNA's regulatory state from destruction of the information-bearing part of the molecule.

Consider the protective effect of the tail in terms of substrates for enzymes. Three-prime-directed nucleases remove nucleotides from exposed or appropriately presented three-prime ends. A tail can provide an expendable region before the unique sequence of the messenger is reached, but this is not the whole explanation of protection. Proteins bound to the tail influence access of other enzymes and communicate with regulatory machinery. The poly(A) tail itself can also be a substrate for dedicated enzymes that actively shorten it. It is therefore more accurate to describe tail-associated protection as regulated than to imagine that the adenosines form an impenetrable physical shield.

A reduction in tail length may precede removal of the cap and rapid five-prime-directed degradation, or it may be followed by decay from the opposite end through different pathways. The details of RNA turnover are developed elsewhere in the curriculum. At this stage the important insight is that both ends of the messenger are integrated into its lifetime. The cap and tail do not operate as two independent permanent seals. Their associated proteins can participate in coordinated transitions from a messenger that is available for protein synthesis to one committed to removal.

A transcript population can exhibit a mixture of states. Some molecules are newly made and have recently acquired their nuclear tails. Others have spent time in the cytoplasm and carry shorter tails. Still others may be undergoing active changes in tail length that accompany regulation. An average tail length measured from such a population is not automatically the initial length specified during nuclear processing. This distinction becomes particularly important when comparing two tissues with different transcription rates or different distributions of messenger ages.

## Functional cooperation between cap and tail

The five-prime cap and three-prime tail are physically separated along the linear sequence, but the RNA is flexible. Proteins interacting with the two termini can bring them into functional proximity. In a widely used model of cap-dependent translation, eIF4E binds the cap and eIF4G provides interaction surfaces for other initiation factors. Cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein can bind eIF4G while it associates with the tail. This arrangement provides a plausible molecular basis for cooperation between the two ends in recruitment of translation machinery.

The term closed-loop model describes a messenger whose ends are brought close through such protein interactions. It does not mean that an additional covalent phosphodiester bond is formed to connect the two RNA ends. A closed-loop messenger can still have chemically distinct five-prime and three-prime termini. If the proteins are removed, the covalent structure of the RNA is still linear. This is different from a truly circular RNA in which the RNA backbone itself forms a continuous circle.

The model explains why combining the two normal terminal features can have a stronger effect on translation than either feature alone in suitable experimental systems. Binding at one end can stabilize or promote the recruitment of machinery associated with the other. A messenger bearing a cap and a tail is therefore not just the sum of two unrelated chemical modifications. The functional state depends on interactions among the proteins recruited by those modifications.

Nevertheless, a model of a protein-mediated loop should not be taken as proof that every actively translated messenger forms a stable loop at every instant. The conformation of an RNA changes, and different translation states need not have identical spatial arrangements. A reduction in translation after interfering with the PABP-eIF4G interaction supports functional cooperation in that system, but it does not by itself measure the geometric shape of individual messenger molecules.

A useful experimental approach is to construct four otherwise identical reporter RNAs. The first has neither terminal feature, the second has a cap only, the third has a tail only, and the fourth has both. If the doubly modified RNA produces far more reporter activity than the others, the result is consistent with cooperative effects. But the comparison becomes more informative when the investigator measures the concentration of each reporter RNA at the time of protein measurement. An increase in reporter output can result from longer survival, better translation per molecule, or a combination of the two.

Suppose two reporters have equal RNA concentrations at the end of a short translation assay, but one produces twice as much protein during a controlled measurement interval. If protein removal and the prior histories of RNA concentration are negligible or matched, the difference supports more efficient production of protein from that reporter. If the RNA concentrations are unequal, protein output alone cannot identify the contribution of translation efficiency. Measuring the rate of protein synthesis rather than only accumulated protein helps to resolve this issue.

The principle of end cooperation also explains the utility of perturbing the recognition proteins rather than altering the RNA itself. A reporter can possess a chemically correct cap yet fail to exploit it efficiently if eIF4E recognition is disrupted. A normal poly(A) tail can coexist with impaired functional cooperation if an interaction surface on eIF4G is altered. Such experiments distinguish the structural presence of a modification from its downstream biological interpretation. They are relevant whenever an examination asks whether a normal molecular structure is sufficient for normal activity.

## Multiple cleavage sites and transcript diversity

A gene need not always use a single three-prime processing site. If two or more functional processing signals occur in the same transcription unit, the processing machinery can generate messenger populations with different ends. This phenomenon is known as alternative cleavage and polyadenylation. The difference is not merely variation in the number of adenosines attached to a common end. The position of the retained genomic sequence itself can change.

Imagine an exon containing a stop codon followed by a long untranslated region. There is an upstream polyadenylation site within that untranslated region and a second site farther downstream. If cleavage occurs at the first site, the mature messenger contains the shorter portion of the untranslated region. If cleavage occurs at the second, it contains the additional downstream sequence. Both molecules can specify the same amino acid chain because the stop codon and all preceding coding residues are unchanged. Their regulatory behavior can differ because the longer untranslated region provides additional binding sites for RNA-associated proteins and other regulators.

The downstream sequence can contain an element that reduces RNA stability in a particular cell type. A transcript using the upstream site would lack this element, potentially increasing its stability relative to the longer isoform. However, this is a conditional prediction, not a universal rule that shorter untranslated regions always make more protein. Some downstream sequences promote stability, localization or efficient translation. Removing such a sequence could decrease expression or change the place in the cell where the message is used. The biological consequence depends on the identity and activity of the regulatory information retained.

Alternative end formation can also affect protein structure in certain genes. If the selected sites occur in different terminal exons or are coupled to alternative splicing in a way that changes the coding region, the resulting messages can specify proteins with different carboxy-terminal sequences. These cases should be separated from alternative site selection within a shared terminal untranslated region. Without a diagram of the coding and untranslated portions, one cannot infer from the mere existence of alternative polyadenylation whether the proteins differ.

The order of transcription introduces an additional constraint. Polymerase encounters a proximal site before a distal one. Efficient processing at the proximal site can remove the upstream RNA from the continuing transcript before the distal site becomes available for that same messenger. If proximal processing is inefficient, more transcripts can continue far enough to make the distal option accessible. This provides a simple framework for understanding competition between sites. Actual usage also depends on the strength of each site, the rate at which RNA is transcribed and the availability of processing factors.

One possible outcome of weakening a proximal signal is an increase in distal isoform formation. The increase is not guaranteed if the distal site is itself ineffective, if uncleaved transcripts are degraded, or if transcription fails to reach the distal region. A reduction in the proportion of proximal isoforms may therefore be accompanied by an increase, a decrease or little change in the total amount of mature RNA. Relative proportions and absolute abundances must be distinguished.

Consider a sample with 80 molecules using the proximal site and 20 using the distal site. The proximal fraction is eighty percent. Following a perturbation, there may be 20 proximal and 20 distal molecules. The distal fraction has increased from twenty to fifty percent, although the absolute distal abundance has not increased at all. A graph of percentages alone could mislead a student into claiming increased distal production. The two isoforms may also have different decay rates, so steady-state abundance is not always equivalent to the frequency of their formation.

These distinctions are particularly useful in analytical questions. To infer which processing site is selected during synthesis, a measurement of newly formed ends is more direct than a count of accumulated RNA molecules. To infer the functional consequences of site usage, protein output, stability and localization may be relevant. A single measurement rarely answers every question about production and function. The molecular explanation should begin by identifying what is being measured.

## A regulated tail after the RNA leaves the nucleus

Although the canonical tail is first synthesized in the nucleus, tail length need not remain unchanged in the cytoplasm. Deadenylation can shorten existing tails, and cytoplasmic polyadenylation can lengthen selected tails. These reactions provide a way to modify the behavior of RNA molecules that are already present without requiring another round of transcription from DNA.

The biological value of this possibility is apparent during early animal development. Some maternal messenger RNAs are stored in oocytes and used at later developmental stages. The amount of a particular protein may need to rise at a time when new transcription is limited or when the cell must respond quickly to developmental signals. Regulation of pre-existing messenger RNA can meet that requirement. Lengthening the tails of selected maternal RNAs can be associated with increased translation in such contexts.

Cytoplasmic polyadenylation should not be confused with the standard endonucleolytic processing event in the nucleus. An existing cytoplasmic messenger already possesses its mature genomic end. Additional adenosines can be appended to its tail without repeating the original cleavage event. The distinction matters when an experimental RNA shifts toward a longer apparent size after a developmental signal. If the genomic sequence of the three-prime untranslated region remains unchanged and the added length is due to adenosines, extension of an existing tail is a plausible explanation. If the genomic boundary changes, alternative cleavage must instead be considered.

Cytoplasmic polyadenylation can be controlled by sequences in the untranslated region and proteins recognizing those sequences. Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding proteins provide a familiar example of a system through which selected messages can be regulated during development. The essential lesson here is the capacity to alter the terminal state of a message selectively. The full signaling networks that direct developmental gene expression belong to the relevant developmental and gene-regulation chapters.

A longer tail is often associated with a more active translation state in oocytes and early embryos, but a direct positive relationship between tail length and translation efficiency is not equally strong in all cell types. In many mature somatic cell contexts, substantial differences in tail length do not produce a simple proportional change in translation. Several additional factors determine how effectively a particular messenger engages ribosomes. A question supplying data from a defined cell type should be interpreted using those data rather than a presumed universal proportionality.

Deadenylation and cytoplasmic extension may also complicate a pulse-chase experiment. A labeled RNA population might exhibit a changing tail length without a change in the amount of unique RNA sequence. If the investigator measures only total RNA size, the experiment may appear to show disappearance or production of particular RNA species. Separate identification of the genomic portion and the terminal tail makes it possible to recognize that a pre-existing population has undergone terminal remodeling.

## The exceptional three-prime ends of replication-dependent histone mRNAs

One of the most important counterexamples to a simple textbook description of eukaryotic messenger RNA is provided by canonical replication-dependent histone transcripts in animals. Histones needed for packaging newly replicated DNA are produced in coordination with the cell cycle. Many of the corresponding messengers do not receive the conventional long poly(A) tail. Instead, their mature three-prime ends include a conserved stem-loop structure and nearby sequence features involved in specialized processing.

The stem-loop is an RNA secondary structure formed when complementary sequences within the same molecule pair to produce a short helix joined by a loop. The stem-loop binding protein, abbreviated SLBP, recognizes this structure. A downstream histone element participates in recognition by U7 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein through RNA base pairing. Together with processing factors, these interactions support cleavage at the appropriate histone RNA end. CPSF73, the endonuclease used in canonical messenger cleavage, also participates in histone end formation in the specialized machinery.

Notice both the similarity and the difference. In the two pathways a nuclear transcript is cleaved to generate a defined mature boundary, and some catalytic machinery is shared. The signals that guide recognition differ, and the replication-dependent histone messenger does not normally proceed through the typical long-tail addition step. A lack of poly(A) in this context therefore reflects an alternative normal processing system rather than an incomplete ordinary messenger.

SLBP also contributes to the proper expression and regulated turnover of the histone messages. The cell can thereby coordinate histone availability with DNA synthesis. If production of histone mRNA continued unchecked after DNA replication ended, inappropriate histone amounts could be problematic. The distinct end structure provides an opportunity for regulatory control adapted to the biological needs of this group of messages. Detailed cell-cycle signaling and histone metabolism are beyond the scope of the present discussion.

The qualifier replication dependent is essential. Histone genes and histone variants are not all processed identically. Many replication-independent histone variant messages are polyadenylated, and there are organism-specific differences. A question that simply asks whether all histone mRNAs lack tails is therefore asking an overbroad question. The well-established examination exception refers particularly to the canonical replication-dependent histone mRNAs in animals.

The exception has direct methodological implications. Oligo(dT) selection enriches RNAs containing accessible stretches of adenosine. A normal nonpolyadenylated histone messenger can be underrepresented or absent in the selected fraction while present in total RNA. Failure to recover it from that fraction does not demonstrate that its gene is not transcribed. An investigator studying this RNA should choose a method that does not assume every messenger has a long terminal adenosine chain.

The same reasoning applies to other RNA classes. Ribosomal and transfer RNAs have their own normal maturation pathways and do not require the canonical mRNA tail to perform their respective functions. Their depletion from poly(A)-selected samples is useful for some gene-expression measurements but should not be interpreted as evidence that the depleted molecules are biologically unimportant.

## Distinguishing eukaryotic tails from bacterial and organellar RNA tails

It is tempting to treat a polyadenosine sequence as a universal indicator that an RNA has been stabilized. Comparative biology shows why this is an unsafe rule. Bacterial RNA processing and degradation differ from the nuclear messenger maturation pathway discussed so far. Bacterial transcripts typically do not acquire the characteristic eukaryotic N7-methylguanosine cap and long stabilizing tail as a standard coupled maturation program. Nevertheless, adenosine-containing tails can be added to bacterial RNAs.

In bacteria such as Escherichia coli, polyadenylation can facilitate degradation of certain RNA molecules. Structured RNA ends may resist attack by some three-prime-directed exonucleases because the enzyme needs accessible single-stranded RNA to engage the substrate. An added tail provides such an accessible extension. It can allow an enzyme to begin degradation and approach the structured region. This role differs from the familiar protection and translation-associated functions of the canonical nuclear eukaryotic messenger tail.

The comparison illustrates an important principle of molecular biology: the effect of a chemical feature depends on the machinery that recognizes it. The same class of nucleotides at an RNA terminus can recruit stabilizing proteins in one context and facilitate access by degradative machinery in another. The function cannot be inferred from the base composition of the tail alone. One must identify the organism, RNA class, subcellular compartment and participating proteins.

Many bacterial transcripts also undergo transcription and translation in the same cellular compartment, allowing ribosomes to interact with RNA before transcription is completed. Nuclear eukaryotic messages generally pass through additional maturation and export steps before they encounter cytoplasmic ribosomes. These contrasting organizational arrangements contribute to differences in RNA handling. They should not be reduced to the simplistic claim that bacterial RNA never undergoes processing or that bacterial messages are always unmodified.

Organellar RNAs provide further examples of context-dependent terminal modification. In chloroplasts, addition of adenosine-rich tails can participate in RNA decay, whereas in mammalian mitochondria polyadenylation can help complete stop codons of certain transcripts whose encoded sequence ends before a complete stop triplet. Mitochondrial messenger tail functions vary by transcript and organism. These examples are sufficient to establish the distinction between a canonical nuclear messenger pathway and other RNA systems without importing unrelated details of organellar gene expression.

For an examination, first specify the system. A question about a typical mammalian nuclear messenger expects cleavage-directed addition by poly(A) polymerase and the roles of nuclear and cytoplasmic binding proteins. A question about bacterial RNA turnover may expect the opposite association between tail addition and degradation. A question about mitochondrial stop-codon completion concerns a separate biological context in which added adenosines can contribute to the stop codon itself. The apparent contradictions vanish when the particular molecular pathway is identified.
