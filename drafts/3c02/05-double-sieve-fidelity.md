## 13. The double-sieve principle and the isoleucine–valine problem

The double-sieve model, developed from biochemical studies of synthetase specificity, explains how two sites with different selectivities can achieve higher fidelity than either alone. The first sieve is the synthetic site, which selects the cognate amino acid and excludes many unsuitable alternatives. In the classic example, isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase, IleRS, must select isoleucine despite the presence of the closely related valine. The synthetic pocket is adapted to isoleucine but may activate valine. The second sieve is an editing site whose selectivity is arranged differently: an incorrect smaller aminoacyl group can be accommodated in an orientation that supports hydrolysis, whereas the larger correct aminoacyl group is poorly accommodated or excluded.

The physical metaphor of a sieve is useful only if it is understood as a model of substrate discrimination. A literal sieve separates particles primarily according to size, while an enzyme evaluates molecular surfaces, hydrogen-bonding patterns, side-chain geometry and conformational changes. In the IleRS example, a difference in size is especially helpful for understanding the complementary selectivities. The first pocket rejects many larger competitors but admits some smaller ones. A second pocket that is too small to accommodate the cognate amino acid can remove a subset of the smaller incorrect products. The two selection processes act in series, so an incorrect molecule must survive both to become a usable product.

Consider the fate of valine that encounters IleRS. It may initially bind and be rejected without activation, in which case little or no ATP need be expended by that encounter. If it is activated, the resulting valyl adenylate may undergo pre-transfer editing or be transferred to tRNA^Ile. In a post-transfer pathway, the aminoacyl end of the resulting Val-tRNA^Ile can be directed to an editing site. Hydrolysis releases valine and regenerates tRNA^Ile. If none of the corrective mechanisms act before the RNA is released and delivered for translation, valine may be incorporated at a position normally specified by an isoleucine codon. This chain of possible events illustrates that an error is a trajectory through competing reactions, not just an unfavorable binding event.

Correct isoleucyl-tRNA follows a different trajectory. It is formed through the same general adenylate and transfer chemistry, but the editing region is much less favorable for hydrolysis of the correctly charged product. The correctly aminoacylated RNA can therefore accumulate and be released. The distinction is not that all incorrect substrates bind strongly to the editing site and all correct substrates fail to bind at all. Productive hydrolysis requires an appropriate orientation and catalytic environment, so a correct product might contact part of the editing region without undergoing efficient cleavage. The model concerns the probability and rate of correction, not an absolute binary gate at every molecular encounter.

Threonine editing by valyl-tRNA synthetase provides a complementary example. Valine and threonine share certain geometrical properties, but threonine possesses a hydroxyl group. The editing reaction can exploit this chemical difference to remove a noncognate threonyl group. Alanyl-tRNA synthetase faces errors involving small amino acids such as glycine and serine. The correction mechanisms in these systems cannot always be reduced to a simple large-versus-small rule. The double-sieve framework is a general teaching model for complementary selection; the detailed determinants of productive activation and editing vary among synthetases.

One can now predict the outcome of a specific class of mutation. Suppose a mutation in the editing region of IleRS destroys hydrolytic activity but leaves the synthetic site's normal selectivity and the enzyme's ability to charge tRNA with isoleucine largely intact. In a reaction containing only isoleucine, the mutant may look almost normal. In a reaction containing abundant valine, however, Val-tRNA^Ile may accumulate because the second safeguard is missing. The defect is conditional on the presence of potential wrong substrates. Assaying only correct-product formation in a chemically simple mixture may therefore miss the biological role of the editing site.

A mutation in the synthetic region has a different range of possible consequences. It could reduce amino acid activation, alter ATP utilization, impair selectivity or disrupt positioning of the acceptor end. Some synthetic-site mutations may lower both correct and incorrect product formation. Other mutations may increase activation of a noncognate amino acid without substantially lowering correct activity. A functioning editing site might compensate for some increased wrong activation, at the cost of greater futile ATP expenditure. If the editing capacity is exceeded, the wrong product could escape despite the continued existence of the correction mechanism.

In a real cell, the two regions of an enzyme can also be functionally coupled. A mutation far from the synthetic active site might change the movement of the tRNA acceptor end between reaction regions. A mutation in a tRNA identity determinant could influence editing as well as synthesis. It would therefore be premature to classify every mutation solely by its position in a protein sequence. Classification is strongest when supported by separate measurements of activation, aminoacyl transfer and deacylation. The ideal experiment asks what each partial reaction can accomplish with suitable substrates, rather than relying exclusively on a final steady-state measurement.

### Estimating the improvement from two sequential selections

A simple probability model demonstrates the advantage of a second selection. Suppose that in a hypothetical mixture there are 9900 correct activation events and 100 incorrect activation events. Assume all correct events produce acceptable aminoacyl-tRNA and that proofreading removes 99 out of every 100 incorrect events before they escape. The number of surviving incorrect products is one, while the number of correct products remains 9900. The initial incorrect fraction was 100/10000 = 0.01. The fraction among released products is 1/9901, approximately 0.000101 or 0.0101%. Under the stated assumptions, proofreading has improved the fraction of errors by roughly two orders of magnitude.

The denominator is important. The probability that an arbitrary activation event is wrong is not identical to the probability that an arbitrary released product is wrong, because editing removes some events from the released pool. Let p be the initial fraction of incorrect activation events, s be the fraction of wrong events that survive subsequent correction, and c be the fraction of correct events that survive and become released products. The expected fraction of incorrect released products is:

f = ps / [(1−p)c + ps].

If p is small and c is close to one, f is approximately ps. This approximation is the origin of the intuitive rule that the error probabilities of successive checkpoints can multiply. The exact expression is more informative when correct-product survival is substantially below one or when upstream errors are frequent.

For instance, consider p = 0.02, s = 0.005 and c = 0.98. The numerator is 0.0001. The correct released fraction per original activation event is 0.98 × 0.98 = 0.9604. The total released fraction is 0.9605, yielding f approximately 0.0001041. Expressed as a percentage, this is about 0.0104%. The values are an educational example, not an experimentally measured fidelity for a named synthetase. The calculation illustrates that a system can have excellent product accuracy even if it expends some effort producing and rejecting unsuitable intermediates.

The energy cost in a simple repeated-attempt model can be estimated from the yield of correct product per activation. If the probability that an ATP-consuming attempt yields a correct product is q and independent attempts have the same probability, the expected number of attempts needed for one correct product is 1/q. This follows by summing the geometric distribution or by noting that the average number of correct products per attempt is q. With q = 0.95, approximately 1.053 ATP molecules are consumed per correct product on average, or about 2.105 phosphate-bond equivalents under the conventional accounting. Individual products cannot consume fractional ATP molecules; these values are averages over many cycles. The result assumes that all failed attempts consume one ATP and that no other reaction costs are included.

## 14. Translational proofreading is not one molecular checkpoint

There are at least two conceptually distinct opportunities for an incorrect amino acid to enter a chain. The first is the formation and survival of an inappropriate aminoacyl-tRNA. The second is selection of a tRNA whose anticodon does not correctly correspond to the codon being decoded. These opportunities are often collectively discussed as translational fidelity, but their molecular mechanisms must be distinguished. Synthetase editing examines the relationship between an amino acid and a tRNA; ribosomal selection evaluates the interaction between the codon and the presented anticodon.

Suppose an mRNA contains 5′-AUU-3′ at an elongating position. In the standard genetic code, AUU specifies isoleucine. A suitable isoleucine tRNA can be recognized at that codon. If the tRNA carries isoleucine, selection of the correct adaptor gives the expected residue. If the same adaptor instead carries valine because of an upstream charging error, the codon–anticodon pairing may still be appropriate. The incorrectly attached side chain is not positioned in the small-subunit decoding center, so the usual nucleotide-pairing test cannot reliably detect that error. The ribosome may incorporate valine at the isoleucine position.

Now consider the reverse type of problem. The cellular aminoacylation machinery has correctly produced Val-tRNA^Val, but that tRNA is erroneously accepted at an isoleucine codon. The cargo is appropriate for the tRNA's own identity. A synthetase editing mechanism has no reason to remove it merely because the RNA might later be selected at an inappropriate codon. Instead, the delivery and decoding system must discriminate against the incorrect codon–anticodon interaction. If it fails, the protein can contain the same valine substitution as in the preceding scenario. The identical amino acid change therefore does not reveal whether charging or decoding was responsible.

During ordinary elongation, a GTP-dependent delivery factor brings many aminoacyl-tRNAs to the ribosome, where codon pairing and conformational changes influence acceptance. GTP hydrolysis and subsequent selection steps can improve discrimination against near-cognate tRNAs. The details of the sequence of ribosomal events belong to the elongation chapter. Here the important point is that this kinetic proofreading mechanism is different from hydrolysis of an erroneous aminoacyl ester by a synthetase. GTP consumption by a delivery factor should not be counted as the ATP expenditure of aminoacylation. The substrate and the chemical event being checked are distinct.

The division between the two processes is not perfectly independent in living systems. A delivery factor can recognize features of the tRNA body and attached amino acid, and certain mischarged products may be poor substrates for delivery. A tRNA mutation can simultaneously influence synthetase recognition and interaction with the decoding apparatus. The proper conceptual conclusion is that a correctly paired anticodon is not sufficient proof of correct charging, while a correctly attached amino acid is not sufficient proof of correct ribosomal selection. Biological fidelity results from multiple overlapping safeguards.

A useful experimental approach is to combine analyses of aminoacyl-tRNA identity with analyses of the protein produced from a known mRNA. Direct measurement of the attached amino acid can establish whether an inappropriate charged species exists. A defined translation reaction containing a deliberately mischarged RNA can test whether its cargo is incorporated at the codon selected by the adaptor. Conversely, normal charging in the presence of increased substitutions at near-cognate codons suggests a downstream decoding defect, provided other sources of error are controlled. Comparing these observations with the activity of purified synthetases and delivery factors can help localize the failure.

The distinction has implications for genetic reasoning. A missense mutation within a protein-coding gene can replace one encoded amino acid with another at a defined site. A defect in charging fidelity may instead affect many sites across many proteins that use the affected tRNA family. It need not affect every occurrence of the relevant codon, because correctly charged and incorrectly charged molecules can compete in the same cellular pool. The products may be heterogeneous, with some molecules retaining the normal residue and others bearing the substitution. This pattern is characteristic of an error in a shared biosynthetic process rather than a uniform change in one coding sequence.

A basic mathematical description of multiple error sources is also useful. Suppose charging and decoding have small independent probabilities a and b of generating an incorrect outcome, and suppose either kind of error alone causes a wrong residue, with no cancellation. The probability that at least one occurs is a + b − ab. For small a and b, this is close to their sum. In a more complete model, the effects need not be independent, and two errors could in rare circumstances offset each other at the level of amino acid identity. The simplified formula is therefore appropriate only when its assumptions are specified. It is different from the multiplicative survival model for successive corrective checkpoints acting on the same error.
