The experimental origins of messenger regulation

The discovery of cyclic AMP helped establish an important separation between the arrival of a hormonal instruction and the reactions through which a target cell implements it. In the 1950s, Earl Sutherland and Theodore Rall investigated biochemical changes associated with hormone action. Their 1958 papers examined production of a cyclic adenine nucleotide by tissue particles and the fractionation and characterization of that compound. The work established a chemical intermediate through which extracellular stimulation could be related to intracellular metabolic regulation. The cyclic nucleotide was neither a conventional extracellular hormone nor simply a phosphorylated form of the final metabolic enzyme. It was an intracellular intermediate whose concentration could be altered by regulation of its formation and disappearance.

A consequence of identifying a messenger is that a complete mechanistic explanation must account for its turnover. In an illustrative fractionation experiment based on this principle, a membrane-containing fraction and a soluble fraction can be examined separately for their ability to generate a cyclic nucleotide from ATP or to hydrolyze a supplied cyclic nucleotide. Detection of the product in one fraction does not prove that all of the machinery for the hormonal response is present there; other cellular fractions may contain enzymes needed for subsequent steps. Recombining fractions can help establish functional relationships. This is a conceptual reconstruction of the experimental logic, rather than a claim that the particular fractionation, controls or numerical outcomes were reported in the original papers.

The distinction between synthesis and removal becomes especially important when studying a hormone that increases messenger concentration. A larger concentration after adding the hormone is consistent with increased synthesis, but a decrease in breakdown could produce the same qualitative observation. If the hormone directly stimulates the activity of a membrane-associated cyclase in an assay where substrate availability and product removal are controlled, the evidence for increased formation becomes stronger. Conversely, a measurement of faster disappearance of a supplied messenger in a preparation incapable of synthesizing it would support increased breakdown. The two types of measurement answer different biochemical questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The later identification of receptor kinases and arrestins likewise changed the way receptor regulation could be explained. A receptor's ability to bind ligand and its ability to activate a G protein became experimentally distinguishable properties. Addition of an inhibitory protein to an isolated receptor system, or removal of that protein through a genetic perturbation in intact cells, could be assessed by measurements of transducer activation. The emerging picture was that receptor occupancy can coexist with diminished coupling efficiency. Modern studies have further shown that arrestin-associated receptor states can participate in other signaling interactions. Consequently, early evidence of reduced G protein coupling should not be generalized into proof that the receptor no longer has any signaling function.

These discoveries collectively demonstrate a recurring experimental strategy: isolate a candidate intermediate, distinguish the reactions that produce and remove it, and ask whether changing one reaction alters the biological output. Historical accounts can teach this strategy without requiring memorization of every experimental preparation or the names of all participating researchers. The student should also distinguish an experiment that establishes the existence of an intermediate from later experiments that identify how the intermediate is regulated. Demonstrating that cyclic AMP exists, for instance, does not by itself establish the identity of every cyclase, phosphodiesterase or cyclic nucleotide-binding protein found in modern descriptions.

A specialized example: terminating a signal in a photoreceptor

Photoreceptor cells provide an instructive example of why rapid termination is as important as rapid activation. A photon absorbed by the light-sensitive receptor rhodopsin initiates a biochemical change that can be propagated through the rod cell's signaling machinery. Activated rhodopsin promotes nucleotide exchange on the G protein transducin, whose active alpha subunit promotes the activity of a cyclic GMP phosphodiesterase. The resulting decline in cyclic GMP changes the behavior of cyclic nucleotide-gated ion channels. Because the stimulus is a discrete photon absorption event, persistence of the biochemical consequences would limit the cell's ability to distinguish separate events over time.

The lifetime of activated rhodopsin is regulated through phosphorylation by rhodopsin kinase and binding of visual arrestin. This example is particularly helpful because it separates termination of receptor activity from hydrolysis of GTP on transducin. A receptor that remains active can continue producing active transducin, even if individual transducin molecules hydrolyze GTP normally. A hydrolysis defect in transducin can instead prolong downstream activity after active rhodopsin has been quenched. Both changes may lengthen the light response, but they act at different positions in the regulatory sequence. The interpretation of a prolonged response therefore requires identification of the affected molecular state.

In rod phototransduction, a decline in cyclic GMP closes channels that normally permit cation entry. This means that greater activation of the upstream G protein can produce less of a downstream messenger, not more. The effect of a signaling protein on an experimentally measured messenger depends on whether its effector synthesizes or removes that messenger. This is a useful contrast with examples in which a G protein stimulates a cyclase. One cannot infer the sign of the final response simply from the observation that a receptor or G protein becomes more active.

Recovery also involves restoration of cyclic GMP. Changes in intracellular calcium during the light response contribute to regulation of guanylyl cyclase activity through calcium-sensitive regulatory proteins. The resulting adjustment of cyclic GMP synthesis helps restore the state of the channels. This example distinguishes two aspects of recovery: termination of the processes that lower cyclic GMP, and restoration of the messenger through synthesis. Blocking only the process responsible for messenger destruction is not equivalent to restoring the full physiological system, because the ability to generate new messenger and the state of the downstream channels also matter.

Phototransduction belongs primarily to sensory physiology when its electrophysiological consequences are considered in detail. Here it is useful because it provides a concrete instance of several general mechanisms already introduced: amplification, receptor phosphorylation, G protein hydrolysis, phosphodiesterase-mediated messenger removal and feedback involving a different intracellular messenger. The specialized identity of the receptor is less important for this chapter than the general lesson that several distinct termination reactions must be coordinated to restore readiness for a subsequent stimulus.

A molecular feedback loop built from cyclic AMP and protein kinase A

A cyclic AMP-dependent pathway provides a useful molecular example of how the same messenger can participate in activation of a response and in its later attenuation. When cyclic AMP binds regulatory subunits of protein kinase A, it promotes access of the kinase's catalytic activity to appropriate substrates. In some cellular complexes, a long form of phosphodiesterase 4 can itself be a substrate of protein kinase A. Phosphorylation can enhance the phosphodiesterase's activity, allowing the enzyme to hydrolyze more cyclic AMP. An increase in cyclic AMP can therefore initiate a process that increases its subsequent removal. The sign of the entire loop is negative even though the kinase activates the phosphodiesterase.

The regulation can be particularly effective when the kinase and phosphodiesterase are organized near one another by an A kinase-anchoring protein. Such organization allows a local rise in cyclic AMP to activate a nearby kinase, which in turn modifies a local removal enzyme. This architecture can limit messenger accumulation near a specific group of substrates without necessarily abolishing cyclic AMP signaling throughout the entire cell. A whole-cell assay that fails to detect a large concentration change does not exclude a significant local regulatory effect. The magnitude of the local signal depends on the interplay of formation, diffusion, buffering and removal.

A study of this principle can distinguish feedback from simple constitutive messenger hydrolysis. Suppose a mutant phosphodiesterase hydrolyzes cyclic AMP normally in its basal state but cannot be stimulated by the relevant phosphorylation event. Before agonist addition, the control and mutant might have similar messenger concentrations. After stimulation, however, the control could acquire greater removal capacity, causing its local cyclic AMP signal to decay earlier than the mutant's. The experiment compares a regulatory-site defect with complete loss of the enzyme: complete loss could change basal messenger concentration as well as stimulus-induced feedback. These are distinct genetic perturbations and are not expected to produce identical time courses.

Direct activation of protein kinase A by an experimental input that bypasses receptor stimulation could help test this particular feedback mechanism. If it increases the activity of the normal phosphodiesterase but not that of the phosphorylation-site mutant, the observation supports regulation at the enzyme rather than altered receptor availability. A second assay that measures the phosphorylation state of the phosphodiesterase can strengthen the link. However, the phosphorylation state alone is insufficient: it must be associated with a measurable change in hydrolytic activity or in the time course of messenger clearance.

The same logic applies when interpreting a cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterase inhibitor. Its most immediate relevant action is to reduce the rate of hydrolysis of the susceptible cyclic nucleotide. Increased activation of a cyclic nucleotide-dependent effector may follow, but feedback can secondarily affect the production of messenger, receptor sensitivity and the activity of other regulators. In a cell that contains several phosphodiesterase types, inhibiting one may have a substantial effect near one effector and a small effect near another. This is why a single phosphodiesterase should not be assumed to control every cellular pool of cyclic AMP or cyclic GMP.

Scaffold-dependent specificity in yeast and animal cells

The budding yeast mating response illustrates how cells can organize a signaling module so that particular inputs communicate preferentially with selected outputs. The scaffold Ste5 associates with several members of the mating MAP kinase cascade, including the kinases Ste11, Ste7 and Fus3. The organized assembly allows stimulation by mating pheromone to influence the relevant response machinery. Because yeast also uses related or shared kinase components in other signaling responses, assembly of the mating module contributes to the discrimination between biologically different inputs. The importance of the scaffold is not simply that it increases the activity of every kinase throughout the cell, but that it organizes appropriate reactions in a particular signaling context.

Scaffold proteins are not necessarily conserved as identical molecular structures between distant organisms. Animal cells use proteins such as kinase suppressor of Ras, KSR, to organize components of the Raf–MEK–ERK module. The shared principle is organization of a multicomponent kinase relay rather than an assertion that yeast Ste5 and animal KSR are the same protein or that their regulatory mechanisms are identical. Experiments showing attenuated rather than completely absent ERK activation in some KSR-deficient systems also illustrate why a scaffold's role must be established experimentally. A scaffold may enhance signaling efficiency without being the only determinant of specificity or the sole means by which pathway components can interact.

An examination problem can use a simplified scaffold with three independent binding sites. Imagine one hundred molecules of scaffold and equal amounts of each of three kinase partners. If binding is tight and each partner preferentially occupies the sites on the same set of scaffolds, a large fraction of the kinases can be assembled into complete complexes. If the number of scaffolds is made much greater than the number of kinase partners and occupancy of the sites is independent, many scaffolds may carry only one partner. The abundance of scaffold-bound kinase could then increase relative to a low-scaffold condition while the abundance of fully assembled complexes decreases. This distinction explains why an assay measuring the binding of one partner to a scaffold cannot substitute for an assay of productive signaling.

One way to test the contribution of spatial organization is to compare a normal scaffold with a mutant that retains the relevant protein-binding sites but loses a localization signal. If the mutant binds its partners in a soluble preparation yet fails to restore signaling in the correct membrane region, the result supports the importance of the scaffold's localization. A different mutant lacking a kinase-binding site could have normal localization but fail to organize the complete catalytic relay. The two mutants separate the contribution of localization from that of molecular assembly. Expression levels and folding should be checked, because a protein mutation that destabilizes the entire scaffold would not provide a clean test of either property.

The mechanistic lesson extends to anchoring proteins in cyclic nucleotide signaling and to receptor-associated complexes. The cell can regulate signaling without changing the total number of a particular enzyme by changing the probability that the enzyme meets a relevant substrate. This principle provides a link between cell organization and signal transduction. Membranes, organelles, protein assemblies and restricted diffusion are not merely structural features of a cell; they establish the physical circumstances under which biochemical reactions take place.

Examples of integration across signaling and physiological systems

Different intracellular messengers can regulate common effectors or influence one another's production. Some adenylyl cyclase isoforms are stimulated by calcium-sensitive regulatory mechanisms, whereas others can be inhibited by calcium or respond differently depending on the cellular context. Consequently, activation of a pathway that raises cytosolic calcium can alter cyclic AMP signaling even if the calcium-generating receptor does not couple directly to the same G protein as a cyclic AMP-generating receptor. The presence of an appropriate cyclase isoform is a mechanistic condition for this particular route of cross-talk. It would be incorrect to infer the direction of the cyclic AMP response from a calcium elevation without information about the enzymes present.

Calcium-dependent phosphatases supply another possible connection between messenger pathways. Calcineurin is activated through calcium-dependent regulatory mechanisms involving calmodulin, and it can dephosphorylate selected substrates. A calcium elevation can thus alter a protein's phosphorylation state through activation of an opposing enzyme rather than through activation of a kinase. The net phosphorylation of a substrate can reflect both calcium-dependent phosphorylation and dephosphorylation if the appropriate enzymes are expressed. A decrease in phosphorylation following calcium elevation is not intrinsically paradoxical; it can result from activation of the phosphatase arm of regulation.

Growth-factor signaling and cellular metabolism also illustrate integration. A cell receiving a growth-promoting signal may activate proteins involved in biosynthesis, but the ability to carry out biosynthesis depends on energy and nutrient availability. Signals reporting nutrient status and energetic conditions can modify the activity of shared regulatory machinery. Therefore a growth-factor receptor can remain capable of activation while a downstream biosynthetic response is limited by an inhibitory input from another pathway. Detailed metabolic control belongs elsewhere in the curriculum; the relevant concept here is that the final output represents integration of several biochemical conditions, not a direct measure of occupancy of a single receptor.

Signal regulation can also coordinate receptor pathways with the regulation of gene expression. A transcriptional regulator activated by one pathway may induce a protein that inhibits a second pathway. Because synthesis of the new protein requires time, an early response to the second ligand may be normal whereas a later response is altered. In a hypothetical experiment, short stimulation with ligand A followed immediately by ligand B might produce the same B response as B alone. Prolonged pre-exposure to A could reduce the later B response. To investigate the involvement of new protein synthesis, one could compare the time course of candidate inhibitor expression with the change in B response and use a suitable genetic perturbation. A general block of translation alone would have many other effects and would be a less specific test.

Integration is particularly important when predicting the effect of a mutation in a shared signaling component. If a protein is required for transmission of both pathway A and pathway B, deletion can affect the response to both ligands even if there is no regulatory interaction between their receptors. If the component is instead a negative regulator produced in response to A that acts only on B, deletion may specifically prevent A from attenuating B. The distinction between a shared positive component and a directional regulatory connection can be resolved by analyzing the responses to each input alone and in combination. The pattern of changes, rather than the mere number of affected outputs, provides the basis for reconstructing the network.

A quantitative model of the G protein switch

Let g represent the fraction of a fixed pool of G alpha subunits in the GTP-bound state. In a simplified two-state model, active receptor drives nucleotide exchange with effective first-order rate constant e and GTP hydrolysis occurs with effective rate constant h. The rate of increase of the active fraction is e multiplied by the inactive fraction, while the rate of decrease is h multiplied by the active fraction. The corresponding equation is dg/dt = e(1−g)−hg. This equation assumes that nucleotide availability is not limiting, that the effective rates remain constant and that the measured protein can be represented by two relevant states. It does not describe every conformational intermediate in a real G protein cycle.

At a steady state, e(1−g) = hg, giving g = e/(e+h). Suppose e is 0.6 per second and h is 0.4 per second during receptor activation. The predicted active fraction is 0.6. If an RGS protein doubles the effective hydrolysis rate while exchange remains unchanged, h becomes 0.8 per second and the predicted fraction becomes 0.6/1.4, or approximately 0.429. The active fraction decreases even though the number of G alpha subunits and the exchange activity have not changed. Under these assumptions, a regulator that promotes GTP hydrolysis reduces the sustained activity produced by a given receptor input.

When exchange ceases following removal of the activating stimulus, the simplified equation reduces to dg/dt = −hg. The active fraction then decays exponentially. Without the increase in hydrolysis, a rate constant of 0.4 per second gives a half-life of approximately 1.73 seconds. With h equal to 0.8 per second, the half-life is approximately 0.87 seconds. The RGS protein can therefore influence both the steady-state activity during stimulation and the duration of the response after withdrawal. The distinction between these measurements helps a student identify whether a question is asking about the magnitude of activation or the lifetime of the active nucleotide state.

The rate constants in this model are effective parameters rather than automatically equal to intrinsic catalytic constants measured for purified proteins. A cellular measurement may reflect the abundance of regulators, access to the transducer and other influences on the relevant reactions. A change in the experimentally inferred value of h need not imply a mutation in the catalytic site of G alpha. It could result from altered abundance or activity of an RGS protein. Conversely, an RGS protein may be present but unable to interact with a mutant G alpha. This is why a genetic or biochemical conclusion must be supported by experiments beyond fitting a simple mathematical model.

If receptor activation is reduced by desensitization, the effective exchange rate e can fall during sustained ligand exposure. The active fraction will then change over time even if h remains fixed. A measurement of the steady fraction alone cannot determine whether e decreased or h increased. If exchange is deliberately stopped in both control and treated cells, the subsequent decay can provide an estimate of h under the model assumptions. Combining a measurement during stimulation with a measurement after withdrawal supplies more information than either measurement alone. This strategy is applicable to many opposing reaction systems, including phosphorylation and dephosphorylation.

Genetic ordering, rescue and pathway bypass

The sequence of a regulatory pathway can sometimes be inferred by activating a downstream component independently of its normal upstream input. Suppose a receptor mutant fails to produce cyclic AMP and fails to activate a cyclic AMP-dependent target. If an experimentally supplied, appropriate membrane-permeable cyclic AMP analogue restores the target response, the downstream machinery can evidently respond to that input under the test conditions. The defect is therefore more consistent with a step at or before messenger-dependent activation than with complete loss of the downstream target. However, rescue with an analogue does not uniquely identify the receptor as the defective molecule; G protein coupling and cyclase activation remain possible sites of failure.

A failure to rescue requires equally careful interpretation. An experimental analogue may not reach the relevant compartment, may have a different preference for cyclic nucleotide-binding proteins or may not reproduce the temporal characteristics of the endogenous signal. Therefore a negative rescue result is not sufficient to establish that the defect lies downstream of the point at which rescue was attempted. The availability and effectiveness of the bypass input should be demonstrated in a control cell. Ideally, a complementary test measures the activity of a downstream component directly and evaluates whether its abundance and localization are appropriate.

A double-mutant experiment can test whether one regulatory effect depends on the presence of another signaling component. Imagine that deletion of a phosphatase elevates the phosphorylation of a particular kinase after stimulation. Deletion of the upstream activating kinase eliminates phosphorylation at the site under study. If the double mutant also lacks that phosphorylation, loss of the activating kinase prevents the phosphatase-deficient phenotype for this particular molecular readout. This supports a model in which the phosphatase normally opposes the phosphorylation generated by the activating pathway. It does not establish that the phosphatase directly recognizes the measured kinase, because it could act on an intervening component.

The interpretation of genetic ordering is more complicated if two pathways converge on the same output or if feedback causes a downstream change to influence upstream activity. A mutation in an opposing regulator may elevate a signal that then stimulates an additional inhibitor. Removal of the first regulator could consequently produce a smaller change at a particular time than would be predicted from the simple linear pathway. In this situation the choice of molecular readout is essential. Measuring several nodes during the early phase of activation and during subsequent adaptation can reveal which direct changes precede the compensatory response.

A well-designed rescue distinguishes the presence of a protein from its function. Re-expression of wild-type phosphatase in a deficient cell may restore normal signal termination. If a catalytic-site mutant is expressed at the same level and in the same location but fails to restore termination, catalytic activity is implicated. If the mutant retains catalytic activity in a purified assay but lacks the appropriate cellular localization, failure of rescue instead suggests that access to the relevant substrate is impaired. Such experiments connect molecular mechanism with the organization of the intact cell and form a useful pattern for Part C reasoning.

Biological consequences of losing regulatory balance

Regulation permits a biological response to match a physiological demand that changes over time. A cell mobilizing stored energy in response to a transient hormone should be able to reduce that mobilization when the demand subsides. A cell responding to a growth factor should not infer unlimited growth permission merely because a small basal amount of receptor activity persists. A cell producing a calcium-dependent response should restore the ion gradients and intracellular stores needed for subsequent stimulation. These examples involve different effectors, but the underlying requirement is the same: signaling must be reversible where appropriate and coordinated with the state of the cell.

Persistent activity of a growth-promoting pathway can arise through several mechanistically distinct changes. A receptor might be activated in the absence of normal ligand, a GTP-binding switch might fail to hydrolyze its nucleotide efficiently, an opposing phosphatase might be lost or an inhibitory feedback mechanism might be impaired. These changes can all increase the activity of some downstream targets, but they predict different responses to removal of the ligand and to selective inhibition of the pathway. This conceptual distinction is important for understanding oncogenic signaling without attempting to cover the separate topic of cancer diagnosis or treatment. A persistent downstream signal should not be assumed to identify a particular genetic lesion.

Attenuated signaling can likewise have different causes. A receptor may be absent, present but poorly coupled, redistributed away from its normal signaling compartment or opposed by an unusually active removal mechanism. A low final response alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. For example, a rapid phosphodiesterase can restrict a cyclic AMP response even when the receptor and cyclase are normal, whereas a defect in receptor expression reduces the initiation of that response. A messenger measurement immediately after stimulation and a direct comparison of receptor abundance would be useful in distinguishing these mechanisms. The larger lesson is that the same physiological phenotype can arise through perturbation of different molecular processes.

Biotechnology uses this reasoning when constructing reporter systems for testing cellular signaling. A reporter intended to measure a receptor pathway must be selected with attention to the molecular event it actually detects. A reporter of kinase-dependent gene expression integrates signaling and transcription over a relatively long interval; it may continue to produce a measurable signal after receptor activity declines. A sensor designed to detect an intracellular messenger can provide a more immediate measurement but may itself buffer the messenger if expressed at excessive levels. A biochemical assay of a phosphorylated protein gives information about the state of the substrate at the sampled time rather than a continuous history. These methods are complementary rather than interchangeable.

The most useful overall model of signaling regulation treats the cell as an organized collection of reactions that operate at different rates, locations and levels of biological organization. Specificity arises from the molecular recognition and context of each interaction; amplification arises from catalytic production of downstream states; termination arises from reversal, removal or redistribution; and integration arises because pathway components are connected to other cellular processes. The student who can identify the input, the measured intermediate, the opposing reactions and the biological output can reason through unfamiliar signaling systems without requiring an exhaustive memorized diagram of each one.
