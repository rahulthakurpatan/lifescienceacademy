1. The biological problem: coordinates before organs

A newly activated fruit fly egg has a long, approximately oval shape. One end will eventually contribute to the head and the other to the rear of the animal. A surface facing one side will contribute to the ventral tissues, including the mesoderm, while regions toward the opposite side will produce dorsal tissues. None of these structures is already present as a tiny adult. Instead, the egg contains molecular differences that allow nuclei to begin different programs of gene expression. The developmental problem is therefore not simply to produce more cells. It is to give those cells information about where they are before they begin to build tissues.

An axis is an imaginary reference direction along which positions can be distinguished. The anterior–posterior axis extends from the future head end to the future tail end. The dorsal–ventral axis runs between the future back and belly surfaces. These terms refer to embryonic anatomy, not the direction in which a specimen happens to be lying on a microscope slide. A researcher can turn a preparation upside down without converting a ventral nucleus into a dorsal nucleus. The molecular distribution and developmental history of the nucleus establish its positional identity.

Polarity means that two opposite ends or sides are biologically different. A gradient is a quantity that changes progressively with position. These concepts are related but not interchangeable. For example, the two poles may both activate the same signaling pathway while the central region does not. Such a distribution supplies information about proximity to an end but cannot by itself distinguish the front from the back. A gradient that falls continuously away from an anterior source can supply a directional coordinate. The embryo combines several such signals rather than asking one molecule to represent the entire body.

To visualize this problem, imagine two nuclei located halfway along the anterior–posterior axis. One lies near the ventral midline and the other lies on the dorsal surface. Both can receive comparable anterior–posterior inputs but very different dorsoventral inputs. Conversely, two ventral nuclei located near opposite poles may experience similar dorsoventral signals yet have different anterior–posterior identities. The full positional instruction requires at least these two classes of information. A terminal signal adds further distinctions close to the ends.

The distinction between a prospective fate and a determined fate is important at this stage. A normal fate map describes what a region usually becomes when development proceeds without experimental disturbance. It does not prove that an early nucleus has permanently lost all alternative developmental possibilities. A transplanted or genetically altered region might respond to new instructions. Thus an expression boundary in the blastoderm is evidence of a molecular decision in progress, not necessarily proof of irreversible differentiation.

This chapter follows the maternal instructions that establish these coordinate systems. The next chapter will examine how zygotic genes interpret the coordinates to generate repeated segments and regional identities. Keeping the two problems separate prevents the common error of treating a maternal determinant as a segment-polarity gene simply because its mutation ultimately changes segments.

2. How oogenesis builds a polarized egg

The egg is produced within an egg chamber in the female ovary. An egg chamber contains a germline cyst of sixteen related cells surrounded by somatic follicle cells. One germline cell develops as the oocyte and the other fifteen serve as nurse cells. Cytoplasmic connections called ring canals allow nurse cells to provide RNA, proteins and other materials to the growing oocyte. The surrounding follicle cells contribute signals and the layers of the egg covering. The resulting egg is not homogeneous, even if the cytoplasm looks uniform at low magnification.

The oocyte grows much larger than its neighboring cells. Its cytoskeleton serves several purposes: it organizes intracellular material, enables directed transport and helps establish asymmetry. An RNA molecule cannot specify a pole merely because it encodes a useful transcription factor. It must also be placed at the correct location or translated preferentially at that location. Many maternal RNAs contain regulatory sequences that allow RNA-binding proteins to identify them and form transport complexes. The movement and anchoring of these complexes can create a local source for a protein later in development.

Microtubules are polar polymers built from tubulin. Motor proteins can move along these polymers using chemical energy from ATP hydrolysis. This is distinct from the thermal movement of freely diffusing molecules. Directed transport is useful for placing cargo across distances that would otherwise be traversed only slowly and imprecisely. Local retention can be equally important, since transport without anchoring would allow the RNA to redistribute. The exact orientation of microtubules in the developing oocyte is more complex than a single array extending neatly from one pole to the other, so textbook arrows should be interpreted as net transport tendencies rather than a complete microscopic map.

Communication between the oocyte and surrounding follicle cells helps reorganize the oocyte. During oogenesis, Gurken protein made from localized gurken RNA signals through the epidermal growth factor receptor on nearby follicle cells. An earlier posterior signaling event contributes to the establishment of anterior–posterior oocyte polarity. Later, the oocyte nucleus occupies an eccentric anterior position; Gurken produced near that nucleus signals to nearby follicle cells and helps establish dorsal identity. The same ligand can therefore have different developmental consequences when its source, responding cells and developmental time differ.

The surrounding follicle cells are not part of the embryo's nuclear genome in the usual sense. Nevertheless, their activities influence the embryo because they construct and modify the egg environment. This explains why some maternal-effect genes must act in the female germline and others in somatic follicle cells. A female may have a morphologically normal adult body yet produce embryos that cannot establish a particular axis. The relevant question is whether the maternal tissues made the necessary product at the appropriate time.

Anterior bicoid RNA localization depends on regulatory sequences in the RNA and an appropriate transport and anchoring system. Posterior organization involves oskar RNA localization and translation, which helps organize the pole plasm, the specialized cytoplasm at the posterior. This region can recruit determinants including nanos RNA and contributes to the establishment of germline precursors. The oskar pathway is mentioned here because it creates a posterior source, rather than because all of its molecular components must be memorized.

A defect in RNA localization need not change the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein. Suppose a mutation removes a localization element but leaves the coding sequence intact. Translation of the RNA in a test tube may still yield a normal protein. In the embryo, however, that protein may appear in the wrong place, or the RNA may not receive the normal local translational activation. A developmental phenotype can thus arise from spatial misregulation without any defect in protein catalysis or DNA binding.

Oogenesis supplies a spatially structured starting point for embryogenesis, while egg activation permits many stored maternal messages to be used. Egg activation in Drosophila can occur independently of fertilization; the presence of a sperm genome should not be confused with the activation of every maternal transcript. Fertilization supplies paternal genetic material and enables normal embryogenesis, but the early localization of maternal determinants has already been organized in the developing egg.

3. Maternal effects and the experimental logic of inheritance

A maternal-effect phenotype is determined by the genotype of the mother because an essential gene product is supplied during egg formation or through the maternal egg environment. This definition concerns the source and timing of a product. It does not mean that only the mother transmits the corresponding DNA sequence. A nuclear gene can show an ordinary Mendelian pattern of allele transmission while its early developmental phenotype depends on the mother's genotype.

Consider a recessive maternal-effect gene m. A homozygous mutant female mm crossed to a wild-type male MM produces embryos with the genotype Mm. If the mutant female cannot deposit functional M product and the critical developmental event precedes effective zygotic rescue, all of those embryos can exhibit the maternal-effect phenotype even though they have received a wild-type allele from the father. In the reciprocal cross, a wild-type female MM crossed to a mutant male mm produces embryos with the same Mm genotype. The embryos receive functional maternal product and can carry out the early process normally. The two crosses produce identical nuclear genotypes but potentially different phenotypes.

A conventional recessive zygotic mutation behaves differently. In that case, an early phenotype depends primarily on the embryo's own genotype when the relevant zygotic gene must be expressed. Both reciprocal crosses producing heterozygotes would ordinarily give the same recessive phenotype expectation. The distinction is powerful, but not absolute for every developmental trait: some genes have both maternal and zygotic functions, and residual maternal product can mask the effects of a zygotic mutation for a time.

Maternal-effect inheritance should also be distinguished from mitochondrial inheritance. A mitochondrial mutation may follow a maternal transmission pattern because most mitochondria of the zygote originate in the egg. The maternal-effect genes discussed here are nuclear genes whose products act through the maternal contribution. Their alleles can be inherited from either parent. Geneticists must therefore establish whether the observed reciprocal-cross difference is caused by maternal nuclear genotype, cytoplasmic organelle inheritance, genomic imprinting or another mechanism.

Large-scale genetic screens in Drosophila made this distinction experimentally useful. Researchers could maintain mutations through crosses, examine the offspring of females carrying particular mutant combinations, and compare embryonic morphology. Cuticle preparations of late embryos revealed which regions had formed, disappeared or been duplicated. The appearance of a reproducible class of abnormalities after a specific maternal mutation suggested that the gene product was required for a defined early patterning process. This approach was especially informative when combined with RNA localization, protein detection and experimental rescue.

The absence of a structure in a mutant is evidence that the altered gene is necessary in the tested context. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the gene encodes the positional signal. The missing structure could result from defective transport, failed receptor activity, impaired transcriptional response or a later survival problem. To identify the role of a maternal determinant, one should compare the distribution of the relevant RNA and protein, the activation of early molecular markers and the final developmental phenotype. These observations constrain alternative explanations.

Rescue experiments strengthen causal inference. If a mutant mother produces embryos lacking an anterior pattern and a properly placed wild-type RNA restores anterior structures, the restoration links that RNA to the missing function. A second experiment may place the RNA at an abnormal location. If the new source can induce anterior developmental programs where they would not normally arise, it provides evidence of instructive action rather than merely a permissive requirement. A correct interpretation still asks whether the introduced amount was physiological and whether the responding nuclei were competent at the time of intervention.

A germline clone experiment can help separate germline functions from somatic maternal functions. A genetically altered germline in an otherwise differently genotyped female allows a researcher to ask whether the required activity must be supplied by the cells giving rise to the egg. A complementary somatic clone in follicle tissue can test a contribution from the surrounding cells. In an examination question, the relevant inference is the source tissue, not the technical details of making the clones. Neither approach automatically proves the downstream molecular target of the gene.

4. The syncytial embryo as a field of responding nuclei

Early cleavage in the fruit fly differs from the familiar picture of a fertilized egg repeatedly dividing into smaller membrane-bound blastomeres. The early embryonic nuclei undergo rapid divisions within a common cytoplasm. A syncytium is a cytoplasmic compartment containing multiple nuclei; consequently, the early fly embryo offers a setting in which spatially distributed regulatory proteins can reach many nuclei before the entire embryo is partitioned into individual cells.

Initially the nuclei divide within the interior of the egg. Many nuclei then migrate toward the cortex. At the posterior, some become enclosed in membranes earlier than the general somatic cellularization process, forming pole cells that contribute to the germline. The remaining cortical nuclei continue dividing in the syncytial blastoderm. Cellularization during nuclear cycle fourteen encloses the somatic nuclei in separate cells, establishing the cellular blastoderm. These stage distinctions are important because the availability of a common cytoplasm changes how signals can spread.

A protein such as Bicoid can be present in the cytoplasm and enter nuclei distributed along the cortex. Once inside, it can regulate transcription of genes whose regulatory DNA recognizes the protein. Although neighboring nuclei share cytoplasm, they need not contain equal concentrations of the transcription factor. A spatial difference can persist if production, transport, degradation and nuclear exchange do not completely homogenize the protein population.

Membranes are not required for a nucleus to transcribe differently from its neighbor. The necessary components are local regulator concentrations, appropriate DNA regulatory elements, and a functioning nuclear transcription apparatus. Nevertheless, cortical cytoplasm is organized rather than being a perfectly mixed solution. Cytoskeletal structures and transient membrane furrows influence local behavior. Simplified diagrams of the syncytium are useful for illustrating access to a shared protein pool, provided they do not imply that the embryo is an unstructured bag of fluid.

Zygotic transcription begins before the main wave of genome activation. The maternal-to-zygotic transition is therefore not an instantaneous switch at which all maternal products disappear and all embryonic genes become active. Maternal products continue to regulate development while zygotic transcription progressively increases. This overlap allows maternal spatial information to be converted into localized embryonic transcription. A question that says no zygotic transcription occurs at any time before cellularization uses an unjustified absolute.

Nuclear concentration can be measured by imaging a tagged protein or staining fixed embryos. If a protein is excluded from nuclei at one position and enriched in nuclei at another, its transcriptional action may differ even when total protein abundance looks similar. For that reason, the relevant measurement must match the mechanism. For Bicoid, nuclear concentration is informative about transcriptional input. For Dorsal, nuclear localization is the defining readout of the dorsoventral patterning gradient. RNA imaging alone cannot establish either protein's functional concentration.

The syncytial setting also explains an experimental difference between Drosophila and embryos that undergo complete cleavage from the beginning. A local injection into a shared cytoplasm may influence multiple nuclei through distribution of the introduced substance. In a cellular embryo, the same molecule might remain within one blastomere unless a transport or signaling mechanism enables it to cross cell boundaries. The general developmental principle is shared, but the physical implementation depends on the organism's cleavage architecture.

5. Bicoid: from anterior RNA to nuclear positional information

The anterior system begins with a localized source of bicoid messenger RNA. The maternal gene is transcribed during egg formation, and its RNA accumulates toward the anterior pole of the oocyte. Following egg activation, translation supplies Bicoid protein. The protein is not confined to the precise region occupied by most of its RNA. It spreads through the early embryo and establishes an anterior-high distribution. As the blastoderm nuclei become exposed to this distribution, they acquire different levels of nuclear Bicoid.

Bicoid contains a homeodomain, a protein domain capable of recognizing particular DNA sequences. Binding of Bicoid to regulatory regions of target genes contributes to transcriptional activation. An RNA encoding Bicoid cannot perform this transcriptional role merely by being placed near a nucleus. It must first be translated; the resulting protein must have a functional DNA-binding domain, reach the nucleus and participate in a productive regulatory complex. These stages can be separated experimentally by changing the RNA localization sequence, the protein's DNA-binding ability or its nuclear access.

The nuclear concentration of Bicoid is highest near the anterior and generally declines toward the posterior. Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard measured the protein distribution and investigated the dependence of positional responses on its concentration in two classical studies published in 1988. Their observations and perturbations helped establish Bicoid as a particularly important example of a morphogen. A morphogen supplies position-dependent instructions that can generate distinct responses at different levels, rather than merely permitting survival everywhere it is present.

The demonstration of a spatial gradient is not sufficient on its own to identify a morphogen. For example, an abundant protein might be distributed unevenly because cells have already differentiated. In such a case it could be a consequence of patterning rather than its cause. The stronger evidence for Bicoid came from combining localization with changes in maternal dosage and the resulting shifts in developmental response. Moving or altering the supply changed the pattern, supporting an instructive function.

One important target of Bicoid is zygotic hunchback transcription. Anterior nuclei with sufficient Bicoid can activate appropriate hunchback regulatory sequences. This produces zygotic hunchback RNA in addition to maternal hunchback RNA that was already provided to the egg. The distinction matters: if a laboratory probe recognizes both maternal and zygotic RNA, its image may not directly show the zygotic transcription boundary. A probe for newly transcribed intronic sequences, or a suitably designed transcriptional reporter, could distinguish transcriptional activation from inherited RNA.

Bicoid also contributes to repression of translation of maternal caudal RNA in anterior regions. This activity operates at a different level of gene expression from activation of hunchback transcription. The caudal RNA is not absent throughout the anterior simply because its protein is low there. The same maternal regulator can influence more than one molecular process, and it is essential to state whether a particular effect occurs at DNA transcription, RNA stability or protein synthesis.

A maternal loss of bicoid function leads to severe defects in anterior structures and permits posterior-like structures at the anterior end in strong mutant conditions. The resulting embryo is not a viable normal animal with two fully functional tails. It is a patterning mutant with specific altered regional identities. Conversely, an ectopic localized source of Bicoid can promote anterior patterning at an abnormal location under suitable experimental conditions. The phenotype depends on dose, location, time and the other patterning systems that remain present.

The anterior system is a graded positional input rather than a list of preformed instructions for each segment. A concentration threshold is a useful first model: one target could require relatively high Bicoid while another could respond to lower concentrations. Real transcriptional responses also depend on enhancer organization, other regulators, time and repression. Therefore an examination statement that all Bicoid-responsive genes have borders determined solely by independent fixed Bicoid thresholds is too strong. The maternal gradient supplies a major input; the embryonic gene network processes it.

6. A quantitative model of positional information

A gradient can be analyzed without assuming that an embryo literally measures distance with a ruler. Consider an idealized one-dimensional system in which a protein is supplied at the anterior end, diffuses through the embryo, and is removed by a first-order process. Let x be distance from the anterior, measured in micrometres. Let C be the local concentration, D the effective diffusion coefficient in square micrometres per second, and k the removal constant in inverse seconds. The rate of removal per unit concentration is then k.

Diffusion creates net transport from regions of higher concentration toward regions of lower concentration. If a local region has a curved concentration profile, the difference between inflow and outflow can change its concentration. In the simplest continuum approximation, diffusion contributes D times the second spatial derivative of concentration. First-order removal contributes negative k times concentration. These two terms have the same units, concentration per second, so they may be added.

At steady state the concentration at each position remains constant with time, although molecules still move and are removed. Away from the source, the equation becomes D times the second derivative of C with respect to x minus kC equals zero. Trying an exponential form C equals C0 times exp of negative x divided by lambda gives a second derivative equal to C divided by lambda squared. Substituting into the equation yields D divided by lambda squared equals k. The decay length lambda therefore equals the square root of D divided by k.

The decay length has units of distance. At x equal to lambda, the concentration is exp of negative one times the anterior concentration, or about 36.8 percent of C0. At two decay lengths it is about 13.5 percent. Increasing diffusion while holding removal constant increases lambda, allowing protein to reach more distant regions in appreciable amounts. Increasing removal while holding diffusion constant reduces lambda and confines the signal closer to its source.

For a target responding when concentration reaches a threshold T, the boundary location in this model is x equals lambda times the natural logarithm of C0 divided by T. The ratio inside the logarithm is dimensionless because both concentrations must be expressed in the same units. A lower threshold is reached farther from the source, provided the source concentration exceeds the threshold. If C0 is smaller than T, the target is never activated under this model; a negative calculated position is not a physically meaningful embryonic boundary.

As a hypothetical example, set C0 equal to 100 arbitrary concentration units and lambda equal to 80 micrometres. A target requiring 50 units has a boundary at 80 times ln 2, or 55.5 micrometres from the anterior. A second target requiring 20 units has a boundary at 80 times ln 5, or 128.8 micrometres. These are teaching values, not measurements of a specific Bicoid target in a living embryo. The calculation demonstrates how continuous molecular information can generate different expression domains.

If the source concentration doubles while lambda stays fixed, the boundary of any fixed-threshold target shifts by lambda times ln 2 toward the posterior in the idealized model. This result follows by subtracting the old boundary equation from the new one. It does not depend on the original threshold provided both solutions lie in the modeled region. A real embryo may regulate the effective gradient or combine additional inputs, so the result should be treated as a prediction under explicit assumptions rather than a universal experimental law.

The simple exponential profile assumes effectively steady conditions, a localized source, homogeneous effective transport and removal, and a sufficiently extended domain with negligible influence from the far boundary. The fly embryo has finite length, a changing nuclear population and time-dependent regulation. A measured profile can approximate an exponential without proving that this particular mechanism is the only way the gradient formed. Distinguishing a descriptive curve fit from a mechanistic explanation is a central skill for experimental reasoning.

The same equation allows a useful distinction between absolute and relative position. If two embryos of different lengths possess identical decay lengths and source concentrations, their fixed-threshold boundaries will occur at equal absolute distances from the anterior. Those distances represent different fractions of total embryo length. Pattern scaling refers to the maintenance of comparable relative positions as embryo size changes. A fixed absolute threshold applied to an unchanged gradient does not automatically guarantee scaling. Additional changes in the gradient or its interpretation may be required.

7. Nanos and the posterior translational control system

The posterior end requires more than a weak anterior signal. In the absence of a positive anterior instruction, the embryo does not automatically construct the correct abdomen. A specific maternal system is needed to remove an inhibitory activity from the posterior region. Nanos supplies a major part of that system. Its action illustrates a different strategy for organizing development: a localized regulator changes where another regulatory protein can be synthesized.

During egg formation, the posterior pole plasm helps concentrate nanos RNA in the posterior. Most nanos RNA outside the localized pool is normally prevented from contributing strongly to Nanos production. Localized RNA becomes a source of Nanos protein in early development. As the protein spreads from the source, its effective concentration is highest toward the posterior and lower toward the anterior. This orientation is opposite to the anterior-high Bicoid distribution. However, opposite orientation does not imply that the two proteins act through the same biochemical process.

Nanos is an RNA-associated translational regulator rather than a homeodomain transcription factor acting directly at the promoters of all abdominal genes. Its conserved zinc finger region contributes to RNA-associated regulatory functions. One important partner is Pumilio, an RNA-binding protein that recognizes sequence elements in the untranslated region of maternal hunchback RNA. The untranslated region is part of the RNA that is not converted into amino acids. It can still determine where the RNA is translated, how long it remains stable and which regulatory proteins associate with it.

A useful starting observation is that maternal hunchback RNA can be present across much of the egg, even though the maternal contribution to Hunchback protein is reduced toward the posterior. A transcriptional explanation alone would be insufficient because the relevant RNA has already been made by the mother. Instead, posterior Nanos cooperates with Pumilio and other factors such as Brain tumor to repress translation of the RNA. The posterior therefore contains less Hunchback protein than would be produced if the inherited RNA were translated without spatial restraint.

One established component of this regulatory process is deadenylation, the shortening of the polyadenosine tail at the end of the messenger RNA. In many contexts, an intact polyadenosine tail helps promote translation and RNA stability through interactions with protein factors. Removal of the tail can reduce translation. Experiments also show that repression of maternal hunchback can include mechanisms independent of tail removal. It is therefore useful to learn the direction of regulation first, while avoiding the claim that deadenylation is the sole possible molecular route to repression.

Hunchback is itself a transcriptional regulator. When maternal Hunchback remains excessively high in posterior regions, it interferes with the normal activation or maintenance of abdominal developmental programs. Lowering Hunchback in the appropriate region makes those programs possible. This is a double-negative causal relationship: Nanos represses an activity that would otherwise interfere with posterior development. It is not necessary to imagine Nanos directly binding every gene that eventually participates in the construction of the abdomen.

In a strong maternal nanos loss-of-function background, the posterior repression of maternal hunchback is defective. Maternal Hunchback extends abnormally into posterior territory, and the embryo loses much of its normal abdominal pattern. This prediction follows from the molecular relation between the two factors rather than from memorizing a list of mutant body parts. The anterior Bicoid input can remain present; the posterior phenotype is not evidence that every maternal coordinate system has failed.

A classic genetic reasoning test asks what happens if maternal hunchback activity is reduced in an embryo lacking Nanos. If the major relevant consequence of Nanos loss is excess posterior maternal Hunchback, removing that target should alleviate the inhibitory problem for abdominal development. Genetic suppression of a phenotype in this way can reveal which regulator acts through which target. The prediction is not that every phenotype of a nanos mutant disappears: Nanos also has important roles in germline development, and reducing maternal Hunchback cannot replace unrelated Nanos activities.

Consider a different mutation that affects the nanos response elements within the maternal hunchback RNA. The hunchback coding sequence remains unchanged, so a normal Hunchback protein can still be produced. Nanos and Pumilio may also be present in normal amounts. Nevertheless, the mutated RNA can fail to respond correctly to posterior repression. Such a cis-regulatory mutation demonstrates that the target RNA contains part of the information needed to generate the protein distribution. Testing a wild-type reporter and a response-element mutant reporter under the same maternal background distinguishes target recognition from changes in the abundance of the regulator.

An ectopic source of Nanos in the anterior can repress maternal Hunchback there if the target response machinery is present. The consequences are not necessarily an exact mirror image of the nanos loss-of-function phenotype. Anterior Bicoid can still activate zygotic hunchback transcription, and other anterior programs may remain active. The experimental outcome depends on the strength and timing of the ectopic source and on whether the RNA species being measured is susceptible to the translational control. The prediction that anterior maternal Hunchback decreases is more direct than a prediction about every eventual anatomical detail.

It is also essential to distinguish the posterior somatic pattern from the formation of pole cells. Both processes depend in part on the organization of the posterior cytoplasm, but they are not identical. A mutation affecting posterior determinants may alter abdomen formation, germ cell development or both. An embryo with a defect in germ cells cannot be assumed to lack its entire posterior somatic axis. Marker expression in germline precursors and expression of genes involved in abdominal patterning must be evaluated separately.

8. How Hunchback and Caudal integrate the opposing maternal systems

Four familiar proteins are frequently drawn together in diagrams of the anterior–posterior axis: Bicoid, Nanos, Hunchback and Caudal. Their distributions provide a convenient summary, but a scientifically useful diagram must distinguish their molecular origins. Bicoid and Nanos derive from spatially regulated maternal RNA sources. Hunchback protein has both maternal and zygotic contributions, while a major early Caudal gradient arises through spatial control of translation of maternal caudal RNA. A picture of the final proteins alone conceals these distinctions.

Hunchback is a zinc finger transcription factor that regulates developmental genes. Maternal hunchback RNA is available broadly, and the Nanos system lowers its translation toward the posterior. Bicoid stimulates zygotic hunchback transcription in anterior domains. The two mechanisms reinforce an anterior-biased Hunchback distribution during the relevant early stages. They are not simply two ways of activating the same process: Bicoid adds transcripts through transcriptional control, whereas Nanos changes the use of inherited transcripts through post-transcriptional control.

The later expression pattern of hunchback is more complicated than a smooth maternal protein gradient. Zygotic regulatory interactions change spatial expression during blastoderm development. A late image may show domains that cannot be inferred solely from the early maternal input. In a timed experiment, the distinction between maternal protein, maternal RNA, zygotic RNA and the combined protein pool is crucial. If the problem specifies a stage before substantial zygotic expression, the maternal translation mechanism is the appropriate starting point.

Caudal is a homeodomain transcription factor associated with posterior developmental programs. Maternal caudal RNA is broadly distributed, but protein production is relatively restrained in anterior regions by Bicoid-dependent translational repression. Thus Caudal protein is more abundant toward the posterior during the early patterning interval. The origin of this gradient differs from the origin of Nanos: one depends strongly on a localized posterior source, while the other can arise from differential translation of a broadly supplied transcript.

This relationship provides a second example of why RNA and protein measurements cannot be treated as equivalent. If an RNA localization image shows uniform caudal RNA, it does not refute the existence of a Caudal protein gradient. A researcher might combine in situ hybridization for RNA with antibody staining for protein and find strikingly different patterns. The two observations support a post-transcriptional mechanism if the difference is not explained by differential protein degradation or another alternative. Perturbing Bicoid or its response element in caudal RNA then tests the proposed cause.

Suppose that a maternal bicoid mutation leaves Nanos activity intact. Two direct molecular consequences are expected: impaired Bicoid-dependent activation of anterior zygotic hunchback transcription and reduced repression of anterior maternal caudal translation. Maternal hunchback RNA can still be repressed in posterior territory by Nanos. It is therefore incorrect to predict that all Hunchback protein must disappear everywhere. A distinct maternal contribution remains, and the interpretation of total protein depends on the stage and strength of the mutation.

Similarly, loss of Nanos should not be described as a direct loss of Caudal transcription. Nanos acts on maternal hunchback in the mechanism considered here. Abnormal Hunchback can alter downstream developmental gene networks, so indirect effects are possible, but an indirect phenotype is not equivalent to a direct molecular target. In examination questions, the best answer often identifies the earliest expected defect before considering more remote consequences.

The idea of an opposing pair of gradients is useful as a conceptual coordinate system. If a nucleus lies close to the anterior pole, it is likely to encounter abundant Bicoid and relatively little Nanos. Near the posterior pole the opposite situation is more likely. But it would be misleading to suppose that all nuclei calculate a literal numerical ratio of Bicoid to Nanos and then assign a unique segment. Many nuclei respond through transcription factors and RNA regulation, and zygotic gene interactions refine those responses. A ratio is a possible mathematical description, not a universal molecular mechanism established merely by plotting two curves.

An important consequence of integrating several inputs is that position can be interpreted in a way that resists some biological variation. A single gradient may change in magnitude between embryos, and a target regulated by both activation and repression can respond differently from a target controlled by one activator alone. Later cross-regulatory interactions among embryonic genes can refine boundaries. This does not mean that all patterns are perfectly robust to arbitrary changes. It means that a coordinated network provides opportunities for compensation and sharpening that are absent from a one-signal threshold model.

One can design a simple reporter to distinguish transcriptional and translational control. A promoter containing Bicoid-responsive regulatory DNA can drive a fluorescent protein RNA with a neutral untranslated region. A second reporter can use a broadly active promoter with caudal untranslated sequences that confer translational responsiveness. In the first reporter, the principal spatial response is expected at the level of transcription. In the second, RNA may be widespread while fluorescent protein is depleted anteriorly. Reporter position in the genome, expression timing and protein stability should be controlled before attributing differences to the chosen elements.

The connection between these maternal systems and zygotic segmentation is now apparent. Bicoid and Caudal provide spatially contrasting transcriptional activities. Hunchback is regulated by both inheritance and zygotic transcription. Nanos helps remove an inhibitory influence from the posterior. Embryonic genes interpret the resulting pattern. The full gap, pair-rule and segment-polarity network belongs to the next chapter, but the maternal inputs explain why the first zygotic domains are spatially organized rather than activated everywhere alike.

9. Torso: how the two ends acquire terminal identity

An anterior-to-posterior coordinate does not completely solve the developmental problem at the ends of the embryo. The extreme anterior and posterior regions must develop terminal structures that differ from the intervening segmented trunk. The terminal system supplies information about proximity to the poles. Its geometry is distinctive: the relevant activity is elevated at both ends and low through much of the center.

Torso is a receptor tyrosine kinase. A receptor of this class contains an extracellular region that participates in ligand-dependent activation, a membrane-spanning region and an intracellular kinase domain. The kinase catalyzes transfer of phosphate from ATP to suitable protein substrates. Activation of the receptor can lead to phosphorylation events and recruitment of intracellular signaling proteins. This converts an extracellular input into changes in intracellular regulatory activity, including the control of gene expression.

Maternal Torso is broadly available along the early embryonic plasma membrane. If receptor abundance were the only determinant of signaling, the receptor could potentially be active throughout the embryo. Instead, the ligand system restricts activation primarily to the two poles. Trunk is the ligand for the embryonic terminal pathway; Torso-like is a spatially restricted maternal factor associated with the egg covering at the ends. Local action of this system promotes productive Torso activation in terminal regions. Torso-like should not be confused with Torso itself, and it should not simply be called the receptor's ligand in place of Trunk.

The distinction between receptor distribution and receptor activity is a recurring theme in developmental biology. A protein antibody that detects total Torso may stain the embryo broadly. A marker for activated signaling, such as phosphorylated ERK, can reveal a very different spatial pattern. The first measurement establishes receptor availability; the second is evidence that the pathway is functioning. A region with abundant inactive receptors may have little downstream response. This distinction also prepares the learner to understand the Toll pathway later in the chapter.

Following Torso activation, a Ras–MAP kinase pathway transmits the signal. The general sequence involves recruitment of signaling components, activation of Ras through exchange of GDP for GTP, and a kinase cascade involving Raf, MEK and ERK. The activated downstream kinase changes the regulatory state of target proteins. The chemical energy required for phosphorylation is supplied by ATP; GTP binding and hydrolysis regulate the state of Ras. The direction of signaling is a biochemical consequence of these activation cycles, not a simple movement of a receptor molecule toward the nucleus.

One important feature of terminal signaling is relief of transcriptional repression. In regions with little Torso pathway activity, the regulatory factors Capicua and Groucho help maintain repression of terminal target genes. At the poles, pathway activity counteracts repression, permitting expression of zygotic terminal genes including tailless and huckebein. These genes encode regulators that help establish the terminal pattern. It is misleading to describe the process as a direct binding of Torso receptor to DNA; the membrane receptor changes nuclear regulation through an intracellular signaling cascade.

The two terminal target domains need not be identical. Their expression can depend on signal level, other maternal inputs and the organization of their regulatory DNA. Furthermore, anterior and posterior terminal structures are different even though Torso activity is found near both ends. Anterior Bicoid and other anterior-specific activities contribute information not supplied by the terminal pathway alone. The combined coordinate systems distinguish a front terminal region from a rear terminal region.

Loss of maternal Torso activity impairs normal terminal patterning. The embryo lacks or alters specific terminal structures rather than losing its entire anterior–posterior axis in the same way as a bicoid mutant. Internal trunk patterning can be relatively preserved compared with the terminal defects. Conversely, constitutive activation of Torso signaling over an abnormally broad region can expand terminal gene expression and interfere with the pattern of the intervening trunk. The two phenotypes establish complementary causal relations: loss of pathway activity removes a requirement, while excessive activity can alter the spatial domain of the response.

A mutation in torso-like can be particularly informative. A broadly distributed receptor can be present and functional in principle while failing to become normally activated at the ends because the spatial activation system is defective. If a constitutively active Torso receptor restores pathway activity independently of the missing Torso-like function, the result supports placement of Torso-like upstream of receptor activation. However, uniformly activated signaling would not necessarily restore the correct anatomy; restoration of biochemical activity must be distinguished from restoration of the normal spatial pattern.

A spatially resolved assay can test this reasoning. Imagine that wild-type embryos show ERK phosphorylation near the two poles, while a receptor-null mutant shows little of this terminal phosphorylation. A torso-like mutant with normal total Torso but absent terminal phosphorylation suggests defective upstream activation. A constitutively active receptor that produces broad phosphorylation supports a role for spatial restriction rather than receptor localization alone. To interpret the assay, one should compare embryos at the same developmental stage and use appropriate controls for the antibody and total protein abundance.

The terminal system is not a universal template for all animals or even all insects. Comparative studies indicate that the canonical Drosophila combination of terminal pathway components has an evolutionary history of assembly and modification. Some insects use different regulatory inputs for terminal patterning. The useful general principle is that position-specific activation of a broadly available signaling apparatus can create a local developmental response. It is not necessary for every organism to use Torso or the same set of downstream target genes.

10. From the oocyte's dorsal side to the embryo's ventral signal

The dorsoventral system presents an apparent puzzle: the localized signal during oogenesis helps define dorsal follicle cells, yet the later embryonic transcription factor Dorsal accumulates in nuclei on the ventral side. The solution is to follow the information through two stages and two tissue compartments. A dorsal instruction in the ovary establishes a pattern of inhibition that permits a ventral embryonic signal later.

During the relevant stage of oogenesis, the oocyte nucleus lies near the future dorsal-anterior region. The localized gurken RNA supplies Gurken protein near this region. Gurken is a ligand of the epidermal growth factor receptor in the surrounding follicle cells. Receptor activation changes the behavior and gene expression of the nearby follicular epithelium. The localized dorsal follicle response is therefore positioned relative to the oocyte nucleus.

Follicle cells that receive strong Gurken-dependent receptor signaling adopt dorsal properties. A particularly important consequence is repression of pipe expression in the dorsal domain. Pipe encodes a sulfotransferase involved in modifying components associated with the egg covering. Ventral follicle cells, which do not receive the same dorsal Gurken signal, can express pipe. The polarity of pipe expression thus lies opposite to the high Gurken signaling region.

It is helpful to keep a chronological map of compartments. First, a localized signal is produced within the developing oocyte. Second, it activates a receptor in maternal somatic follicle cells. Third, the follicle cells establish a spatially patterned extracellular environment around the egg. Fourth, the embryo uses that environment to localize an extracellular proteolytic activation pathway. Fifth, that pathway activates an embryonic membrane receptor and causes a graded intracellular nuclear response. A single diagram labeling all factors inside the embryo would obscure the first three steps.

The extracellular environment persists after the maternal follicle cells have completed their contribution to the egg covering. The embryo can therefore inherit spatial information even if the original signal-producing cells are no longer present in the same configuration. This provides a striking example of a maternal contribution that is not simply the concentration of a protein dissolved inside the egg cytoplasm. Maternal somatic cells help encode an axis through the organization of extracellular material.

A loss of appropriate Gurken signaling can allow ventral follicle characteristics to extend into regions that would normally be dorsal. Ectopic pipe expression can contribute to expansion of ventral embryonic signaling. Strong ectopic dorsalizing activity in follicle cells can instead suppress the ventral pipe domain, reducing the embryonic ventral cue. The exact appearance of a gurken mutant can also reflect its earlier role in anterior–posterior oocyte organization. An examination problem about the later dorsoventral function should specify the stage or isolate the relevant perturbation before assigning a unique phenotype.

Pipe is not a transcription factor that enters embryonic nuclei and turns on twist directly. Its maternal follicle-cell function lies upstream of spatial activation of the extracellular pathway. If pipe is absent from the maternal follicle cells, downstream Toll signaling can fail to attain its normal ventral pattern even when embryonic Toll and Dorsal proteins are available. This prediction follows from the chain of information transfer: availability of the downstream molecular apparatus does not rescue absence of the localized activating cue.

One way to distinguish maternal germline and follicular requirements is to analyze appropriately restricted rescue. If a wild-type pipe transgene is restored only in the relevant follicle cells of a pipe-deficient mother, it may re-establish the upstream extracellular information. Expressing the same gene in the embryo after the egg covering has formed would not necessarily reconstruct the correct maternal spatial modification. Rescue must occur in the right tissue and at the right developmental stage; a negative rescue result outside that context does not prove that the protein is nonfunctional.

The oogenesis step also helps explain why the embryonic dorsoventral axis is not assigned by a gradient of Dorsal messenger RNA. The RNA and protein for the embryonic transcription factor can be supplied broadly, while the maternal extracellular pattern determines where the pathway releases it for nuclear entry. In the next section, the molecular conversion of this stored extracellular information into a nuclear concentration gradient will be examined.
