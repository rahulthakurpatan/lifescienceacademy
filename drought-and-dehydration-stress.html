1. The biological problem: when water supply cannot match demand

A plant may be growing in soil that contains considerable water and nevertheless lose water faster than its roots can replace it. This observation provides a useful starting point for drought physiology: the presence of water is not equivalent to the availability of water at the rate required by living tissue. A leaf exposed to sunlight and moving dry air evaporates water through microscopic pores called stomata. The resulting tension draws liquid through the xylem, while roots obtain replacement water from the soil. The entire sequence can maintain a steady supply only when the pathways remain conductive and the soil can provide enough water. Water deficit develops when the balance between supply and loss becomes unfavorable.

Drought describes an environmental shortage of available water, whereas dehydration describes a reduction in the water content of an organism or tissue. The first is a cause or environmental condition; the second is a physiological state. A well-watered plant can become transiently dehydrated during an afternoon of intense evaporative demand. Conversely, a plant with effective stomatal control and access to deep moist soil may remain relatively hydrated during a period without rainfall. It is therefore important to ask whether a measurement describes the environment, the water status of the plant, its regulatory response, or the injury that has followed.

Water serves more functions than supplying electrons for photosynthesis. It is the solvent in which metabolites and ions move, the medium for enzymatic reactions, the source of turgor pressure and the means of long-distance transport. Water absorbed by a root ultimately supplies evaporative cooling as well as the small fraction incorporated into biomass. When cellular water content decreases, the concentration of dissolved compounds rises, the vacuole contracts and the plasma membrane experiences mechanical changes. These disturbances can initially be reversible. If dehydration proceeds too far, membrane organization, enzyme function and hydraulic integrity can be compromised. Stress does not necessarily imply damage; a regulated response can prevent damage even while growth is temporarily inhibited.

A young expanding leaf illustrates why growth is often among the first functions to decline. Its cells enlarge when water enters and the walls yield under turgor. If the pressure inside a cell falls below the threshold needed for irreversible wall extension, further expansion slows even though respiration and photosynthesis have not stopped. The leaf may therefore become smaller without experiencing lethal injury. The reduction in expanding surface area also decreases later evaporative demand. This immediate consequence of water limitation can contribute to longer-term conservation, but it must not automatically be interpreted as a genetically programmed adaptation. A controlled experiment must separate the physical constraint from regulated growth responses.

The meaning of drought resistance depends on the biological endpoint. A genotype that remains alive by closing stomata for weeks may have poor grain yield because the reproductive organs receive less carbon. Another genotype may use more water early, sustain assimilation and set seed before a terminal dry period. The latter can produce a better yield despite losing more water during part of the season. Drought escape refers to completing sensitive life-cycle stages before severe water shortage; avoidance refers to keeping tissue water status relatively favorable; tolerance refers to maintaining viable or functional tissue despite unfavorable water status. These mechanisms overlap in real plants. They are not interchangeable measures of a single universal trait.

Distinguishing environmental drought, tissue dehydration and irreversible injury becomes especially important when comparing species. A mesophytic herb, a desert succulent and a resurrection plant can experience the same atmospheric conditions while maintaining very different internal water contents. A succulent stores water and reduces the rate of depletion. A resurrection plant can tolerate much more extensive cellular drying. Both may survive, but the underlying mechanisms are fundamentally different. The following sections trace these mechanisms from the physics of water to molecular signaling, cellular protection and recovery.

Water storage, exposure and the progression of injury

The distinction between flux and stored amount is fundamental in understanding why drought symptoms develop gradually. A plant contains water in its vacuoles, cell walls and conducting tissues. If uptake exceeds loss for a period, some of this water storage may be replenished; if loss exceeds uptake, the stored amount falls. In a simplified whole-plant balance, the rate of change of stored water equals uptake minus transpiration, with smaller terms for exudation, growth and other exchanges omitted. If a potted plant takes up 15 g of water per hour while losing 20 g per hour, its stored water decreases by 5 g per hour during that interval. The calculation does not state how much water each organ loses. Different organs have different water storage capacities and hydraulic connections.

Plant tissues with substantial storage can buffer transient differences between supply and demand. This behavior is described using hydraulic capacitance, the change in stored water per unit change in water potential. A stem or leaf with a high capacitance releases more water for a given decline in potential than a tissue with a low capacitance. Such storage can delay the development of severe tension during brief periods of strong transpiration. It cannot replace continuing uptake indefinitely. Once accessible stored water has been depleted, the same imbalance between supply and loss can result in a more rapid decline in water potential. Capacitance also helps explain why transpiration measured from a canopy and water flow measured near the stem base may have different hourly patterns.

Students frequently encounter midday wilting in crop fields. A leaf may become less rigid at midday and regain firmness later without rain. The recovery is consistent with a temporary imbalance in which afternoon water loss exceeds supply but evening demand decreases enough for tissue water stores to be replenished. If the soil continues drying, the evening recovery may become less complete. This is distinct from a situation in which membranes have lost integrity or meristems have died. Wilting is a visible consequence of altered mechanical properties and turgor; it is not a direct count of dead cells. The same visible symptom can arise from injury to roots, infection of vascular tissue or a very saline root environment. The cause should be identified before attributing all wilting to soil drought.

One useful way to examine the chronology is to compare a repeated daily water-status record with growth and physiological measurements. At the start of a moderate dry period, predawn water potential may remain relatively favorable while midday potential becomes more negative. Stomatal closure and reduced leaf elongation may occur without measurable loss of dark-adapted PSII efficiency. Continued drying can depress both predawn and midday water potential, and compatible solutes or protective proteins may accumulate. Severe drought may eventually cause high electrolyte leakage, hydraulic failure or loss of viability. This sequence is an illustrative pattern rather than a fixed developmental rule. The timing of each response depends on species, temperature, tissue and the rate of soil drying.

Drought imposed briefly during the vegetative phase may permit compensatory growth after rain. Chronic water limitation, by contrast, can alter the architecture and phenology of newly developing organs. Leaves that expand under drought often differ from leaves that developed before drought, and the comparison may reflect developmental acclimation as well as immediate responses. For example, a measured difference in stomatal density between a small drought-grown leaf and a large control leaf can partly arise from differences in epidermal expansion. Comparing pore number per unit area alone can obscure changes in the total number of stomata on a leaf. Physiologists therefore specify which tissue and stage were sampled.

Field drought also has a spatial dimension. Within one root system, the surface layer can be dry while deeper layers remain wet. Leaves exposed above a canopy can experience greater radiation and atmospheric demand than shaded leaves on the same individual. Some developmental sinks may continue receiving assimilates while older leaves senesce. This heterogeneity is biologically important: an average soil or leaf measurement may hide the conditions experienced by the tissues that control growth, reproduction and survival. Drought physiology is consequently an exercise in connecting the scale of measurement to the scale of the proposed mechanism.

2. Water potential, soil drying and hydraulic supply

Water potential expresses the chemical potential of water relative to pure water at a specified reference state, divided by the partial molar volume of water. It has dimensions of pressure and is commonly reported in megapascals (MPa). Under ordinary conditions the reference potential of pure water at atmospheric pressure is zero. Dissolved solutes lower the chemical potential of water, so the osmotic or solute potential is negative. Positive hydrostatic pressure raises water potential; tension can make the pressure component negative. Water moves spontaneously from a higher water potential to a lower water potential when a suitable pathway is available.

For a hydrated living cell in a simple laboratory situation, the approximation Ψw = Ψs + Ψp is useful. Here Ψw is total water potential, Ψs is osmotic potential and Ψp is pressure potential. The approximation neglects gravitational and matric contributions where they are small. Matric potential describes the effect of water binding to surfaces, which is especially important in unsaturated soil. Gravity contributes approximately 0.0098 MPa per metre of height relative to a reference level. In a short cell-level calculation that contribution is negligible, but it can matter in tall trees. Do not assume that the turgor pressure of a living mesophyll cell and the pressure in an adjacent xylem vessel must have the same sign.

Consider two living cells, one with Ψs = −0.80 MPa and Ψp = +0.40 MPa, and the other with Ψs = −0.65 MPa and Ψp = +0.10 MPa. Their total potentials are −0.40 and −0.55 MPa. If a permeable connection exists, water tends to move from the first to the second cell, despite the first cell having the more negative osmotic potential. This example shows why comparisons of solute concentration alone can give the wrong prediction. Solute potential and pressure potential must be considered together. If the water potentials become equal, net flow vanishes even though individual water molecules continue moving in both directions.

For dilute ideal solutions, the van't Hoff approximation is Ψs = −iCRT. The factor i represents the number of effective dissolved particles produced by each formula unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. When C is in mol L⁻¹, R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and T is in kelvin, the result is in MPa. At 298 K, a 0.20 M solution of a non-dissociating solute has Ψs ≈ −0.496 MPa. Real cytoplasm contains many solutes, ions and macromolecules; their activities and compartmentation limit the accuracy of the ideal approximation. The equation is a model for reasoning, not a direct assay of a leaf.

As a soil dries, its larger pores empty first. Water remains in smaller pores and films on mineral particles, and increasingly negative matric potential is needed to describe its state. At the same time, continuity and thickness of the water films decrease, so unsaturated hydraulic conductivity can fall steeply. Two soils with the same gravimetric water content may therefore supply roots very differently. A clay-rich soil can retain substantial water at a strongly negative potential, while a sandy soil loses readily drainable water quickly. Field capacity and permanent wilting point are useful operational descriptors, not universal thresholds for every species and soil.

A simplified steady-state hydraulic relationship is J = K(Ψsource − Ψsink), where J is the flow rate, K is the conductance of the specified pathway and Ψsource and Ψsink are water potentials at its boundaries. Units of conductance must be consistent with the units of flow and pressure; for example, mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ for a leaf-area-normalized pathway. If soil potential is −0.30 MPa, leaf potential is −1.10 MPa and K is 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹, the predicted flow is 1.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Halving conductance halves the predicted flow at the same gradient. In a real plant, conductance can change as dehydration advances; storage and time-dependent changes also violate the steady-state approximation.

Water passes through a series of resistances in soil, the root–soil interface, roots, xylem and leaves. If one component becomes very resistive, an increase in another component's conductance cannot fully compensate. Drying soil may shrink away from a root, producing a gap or poor contact. Fine roots may lose hydraulic function, while living cortical membranes can change their permeability. Within the leaf, water moves from veins through tissues toward evaporating surfaces. The resulting distribution of water potentials is not uniform. A bulk measurement of leaf water status cannot directly reveal every microscopic gradient.

Vapor pressure deficit (VPD) represents the difference between the vapor pressure at saturation and actual vapor pressure. For transpiration the relevant deficit is between the moist leaf interior and the surrounding air, using leaf temperature to estimate saturation vapor pressure. At 25 °C saturation vapor pressure is about 3.17 kPa. If air and leaf are both at that temperature and relative humidity is 50%, the deficit is about 1.59 kPa. At 30 °C, saturation vapor pressure is about 4.24 kPa, so the same relative humidity gives about 2.12 kPa. Thus unchanged relative humidity does not mean unchanged evaporative demand. Leaf warming during stomatal closure can further change the deficit.

In a simplified diffusion model, transpiration per unit area E ≈ gwD/P, where gw is total conductance to water vapor in mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D is leaf-to-air VPD and P is atmospheric pressure in the same units as D. At gw = 0.20 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D = 2 kPa and P = 100 kPa, E is approximately 0.004 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ or 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Stomatal and boundary-layer resistances both affect the effective conductance. If stomatal closure decreases gw to 0.10 while VPD doubles to 4 kPa, the model predicts the same E. An observation of unchanged transpiration does not prove that stomata failed to respond.

The distinction between soil and atmospheric drought is experimentally useful. Researchers can independently vary soil moisture and VPD while controlling light, temperature, carbon dioxide and plant age. A dry atmosphere may induce rapid changes in stomatal conductance without a major change in measured bulk soil moisture. Progressive soil drought can reduce water supply even when the atmosphere is relatively humid. Under field conditions these factors commonly occur together. Their interactions mean that measurements at one time of day should not be treated as a complete description of drought severity.

From chemical potential to turgor and the soil–plant continuum

The water-potential equation can be understood by separating two influences on the same molecule. In pure water at a reference pressure, water molecules are relatively free to participate in molecular exchange with another region of pure water. Dissolving a solute decreases the activity of water. Raising hydrostatic pressure does work on the liquid and increases its chemical potential. For an incompressible approximation, the change in chemical potential due to pressure is the partial molar volume of water multiplied by the change in pressure. Dividing the chemical-potential difference by this volume permits both influences to be expressed in pressure units. The sum is useful because a semipermeable membrane does not respond independently to two contradictory arrows: the net tendency reflects their combined effect.

A simple derivation clarifies the sign of the solute term. For an ideal dilute solution at constant temperature and reference pressure, the solute effect on the chemical potential of water can be represented by RT ln(a_w), where a_w is water activity. Since a_w is less than one in such a solution, its logarithm is negative. In the dilute limit, this term divided by the partial molar volume of water approaches the negative of the familiar ideal osmotic pressure, −iCRT. Osmotic pressure itself is the positive pressure that would be needed to oppose water entry across an ideal semipermeable membrane. Osmotic potential is negative relative to pure water; confusing the positive magnitude of osmotic pressure with the sign of osmotic potential produces incorrect predictions.

Consider a cell in a large external solution with Ψw = −0.60 MPa. Initially the cell has Ψs = −1.00 MPa and Ψp = +0.70 MPa, so its water potential is −0.30 MPa. Water will tend to leave because the external potential is lower. As water leaves, solutes become concentrated, making Ψs more negative, and turgor declines. Both effects reduce cellular Ψw. Equilibrium is approached when the internal and external potentials are equal. It would be incorrect to assume that the cell must lose all turgor merely because the external solution has negative potential. A possible equilibrium state, under a suitable pressure–volume relationship, is Ψs = −1.10 MPa and Ψp = +0.50 MPa, giving −0.60 MPa.

Loss of turgor is related to, but not identical with, plasmolysis. As a turgid cell loses water, wall pressure decreases until the protoplast is no longer exerting appreciable positive pressure on the wall. Further water loss in a sufficiently hypertonic external medium can cause the protoplast to contract away from parts of the wall. This is commonly demonstrated with epidermal cells in a concentrated solution. A wilting leaf in drying air is a heterogeneous tissue, not a collection of identical cells equilibrated with a simple osmotic solution. The exact geometry of shrinkage and the relative contributions of pressure, wall mechanics and apoplastic water differ. A classroom plasmolysis experiment demonstrates the role of osmosis but is not a full simulation of whole-plant drought.

Resistance relationships provide another way to understand the continuum. In a steady flow approximation, conductance K is the reciprocal of hydraulic resistance R_h. If a series of pathway components carries the same flow J, the potential drop across each component is J multiplied by that component's resistance. Adding the drops yields the overall source-to-sink difference. Thus R_total = R_soil + R_root + R_stem + R_leaf for this simplified series pathway. For an illustrative root and shoot with resistances of 0.30 and 0.20 MPa per unit flow and soil resistance of 0.10, total resistance is 0.60. At a driving difference of 0.90 MPa, flow is 1.50 units. If drying raises soil resistance to 1.00 while the others remain constant, total resistance becomes 1.50 and flow falls to 0.60 units. Raising stem conductance alone would make only a limited difference.

An additional distinction concerns the potential of atmospheric water vapor. The air can have an extremely negative equivalent water potential when unsaturated, but transpiration across the gas-phase pathway is normally described using vapor pressure and diffusion conductance rather than inserting atmospheric potential directly into a liquid hydraulic conductance equation. For example, the same leaf water potential does not guarantee the same transpiration in a humid and dry chamber. The gas-phase concentration gradient differs even if the liquid-phase status is initially identical. A model that treats soil, xylem and air as if they shared the same constant hydraulic conductance would therefore be physically misleading.

For practical comparisons, soil moisture sensors and soil water-potential measurements complement one another. A moisture reading estimates how much water is present, while potential measurements describe how tightly that water is held or how favorable it is for movement. The relationship is described by a soil water-retention curve and varies among soil types. If two experiments use different potting media, imposing a fixed percentage of maximum soil moisture does not guarantee the same water-potential treatment. This is particularly important in comparing drought-tolerance studies, because the apparent severity of the imposed treatment can depend strongly on the substrate.

3. Hydraulic signals and abscisic acid

A plant cannot wait until every cell is severely dehydrated before responding. Because the root–shoot hydraulic pathway connects distant tissues, changing water availability can be transmitted through changes in water potential, tension and cellular hydration. A change in leaf hydration or root hydraulic conductance may occur faster than the transport of a newly synthesized regulatory molecule over the same distance. Such changes provide information that can influence stomata, growth and hormone metabolism. Chemical signaling supplements rather than necessarily replaces this hydraulic connection.

Abscisic acid, commonly abbreviated ABA, is a central regulator of drought responses in flowering plants. Its concentration depends on biosynthesis, catabolism, reversible conjugation, release from conjugated pools and transport between tissues. ABA is synthesized from carotenoid-derived precursors; the cleavage of 9-cis epoxycarotenoids by NCED enzymes is an important regulated step. Later reactions generate the active hormone. Under dehydration, ABA accumulation often results from increased synthesis together with other changes in metabolism. The occurrence of a drought-induced ABA signal should not be taken to mean that ABA is produced exclusively by roots. Roots, leaves, vascular tissues and guard cells can contribute, depending on species and conditions.

An early model emphasized ABA made in drying roots and transported through xylem to leaves. Split-root experiments remain informative: one root compartment can be dried while the other continues receiving water. If the shoot remains relatively hydrated yet stomatal conductance falls, the result suggests a root-associated signal beyond a simple measurement of bulk shoot water deficit. However, such an experiment does not by itself identify the signal as ABA. Local hydraulic changes, altered nutrient transport, xylem sap chemistry and shoot ABA synthesis may also contribute. Direct hormone measurements, genetically impaired ABA biosynthesis, controlled ABA application and carefully selected graft combinations help distinguish alternatives.

An ABA-deficient mutant and an ABA-insensitive mutant provide a classical logical contrast. The first has an impaired ability to produce sufficient active hormone but may retain the receptors and downstream machinery. If external ABA restores stomatal closure under suitable conditions, the result is consistent with a biosynthetic defect. The second may accumulate ABA but fail to respond normally because signaling is impaired. Exogenous ABA would not necessarily rescue that phenotype. Real mutants may have partial defects, redundant paralogs or additional developmental differences, so the comparison is strongest when the genetic lesion and physiological conditions are known.

The hormone signal is not simply a measure of total leaf ABA. The concentration experienced by specific receptors depends on tissue distribution and transport. In addition, response intensity depends on receptor abundance and downstream regulatory state. Two leaves containing similar amounts of ABA may exhibit different stomatal conductance if one differs in sensitivity or hydraulic limitation. Drought physiology therefore requires the distinction between amount of a hormone and responsiveness to that hormone. This same distinction is familiar from other hormone systems introduced in Unit 6.D.

Drought is accompanied by changes in calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species, membrane properties and protein phosphorylation. These are interconnected signals rather than isolated parallel pathways with identical effects in every tissue. The next section focuses on the ABA pathway because it supplies a clear causal example of how water deficit becomes a measurable physiological response, not because all drought responses require ABA.

Evidence for hormone perception and interacting signals

The discovery of ABA as a growth-regulating substance preceded the discovery of its receptors by several decades. Physiologists could observe effects of the hormone on dormancy and stomatal behavior without knowing exactly how a cell detected the molecule. Genetic analysis then provided an entry into the signaling pathway. A mutant that responded poorly to ABA could reveal a necessary component of perception or downstream signaling. A mutant whose tissues responded unusually strongly could reveal a negative regulator. Such phenotypes supplied a framework for distinguishing the hormone from the machinery that interprets it.

The identification of the PYR/PYL family in 2009 provides an example of how chemical genetics and biochemistry can converge. Park and colleagues investigated pyrabactin, a synthetic compound that mimicked selected ABA responses. By finding altered responses to this compound and identifying the relevant protein family, they obtained a candidate for hormone perception. A key prediction was that a protein involved in perception should interact with downstream regulators in a hormone-dependent manner and alter their activity. The study connected ABA-binding PYR/PYL proteins to inhibition of PP2Cs. The conclusion did not rest solely on a change in seed germination or gene expression.

Ma and colleagues independently studied regulators of PP2C activity, including RCAR proteins, providing complementary evidence that ABA detection and phosphatase regulation are linked. A conceptual reconstruction of the decisive biochemical logic is straightforward. Compare phosphatase activity when PP2C is present alone, when ABA is added without receptor, when receptor is present without ABA and when both receptor and ABA are present. A substantial reduction associated with the receptor–hormone combination supports a regulatory interaction. The precise behavior of different receptor and phosphatase isoforms can vary, so the simplified four-condition experiment is a model for exam reasoning rather than a literal reproduction of every measurement in either original paper.

Structural work subsequently established how conformational changes in ABA receptors can create an interface that accommodates the phosphatase. A useful general description is that hormone binding favors closure of flexible receptor regions around the ligand and permits a productive inhibitory complex with PP2C. Details of amino acid contacts and the differences between receptor oligomeric states are beyond the present syllabus. The important functional point is that the hormone changes protein–protein regulation rather than simply supplying energy to the downstream kinase. The kinase performs phosphorylation using ATP; the receptor regulates whether the inhibitory phosphatase restrains that signaling.

The initial physical stimulus also deserves careful interpretation. Osmotic changes can alter cell volume, membrane tension and protein activity, and some calcium-permeable channels participate in responses to these perturbations. However, no single receptor should be assumed to detect every form of drought in every plant. A dry root, a suddenly exposed leaf and a dehydrating seed present different cellular contexts. Calcium changes can precede, accompany or follow changes in hormone signaling depending on the response being measured. When two signals increase at similar times, the time course alone does not establish which lies upstream.

Grafting experiments can separate contributions of root and shoot genotype. Suppose an ABA-deficient rootstock supports a normal shoot, while a normal rootstock supports an ABA-deficient shoot. If the normal shoot still closes its stomata during a treatment, the result is consistent with an important shoot contribution. It is not evidence that roots never send information. Grafting may alter vascular conductance, and transported hormone or precursors may complicate the comparison. Reciprocal grafts, self-grafted controls, tissue hormone measurements and similar developmental stages strengthen the interpretation. The analysis asks where synthesis or perception is required, not simply where a compound can be detected.

The distinction between hydraulic and chemical signals is also not a strict either–or choice. Hydraulic changes can stimulate ABA synthesis, and ABA can alter transpiration and hydraulic conductance. A feedback loop results: lower hydration can enhance closure, closure decreases water loss, and the changed water balance modifies the original stimulus. Depending on the time at which measurements are made, the same plant may show substantial ABA accumulation with little detectable water deficit because the regulatory response has already protected tissue hydration. Failure to observe low water potential at the endpoint does not establish that the plant never experienced a transient hydraulic perturbation.

4. From ABA perception to stomatal closure

A stoma consists of a pore bounded by two specialized guard cells. Their walls and shape convert changes in turgor into changes in pore aperture. In many dicots, hydrated guard cells become curved as their turgor rises and the pore opens; as turgor decreases, the pore narrows. Grasses have dumbbell-shaped guard cells and associated subsidiary cells that exchange ions and water with them. The mechanical organization differs, but regulated solute movement and water redistribution remain central.

In a favorable environment, activation of the plasma membrane proton ATPase promotes proton extrusion and makes the cell interior electrically more negative. This hyperpolarization favors inward potassium transport through suitable channels. Potassium, counterions such as chloride or malate, and organic osmolytes contribute to lowering guard-cell osmotic potential. Water then enters along a water-potential gradient and raises turgor. Light, carbon dioxide concentration, circadian state and water availability influence opening. A channel is not a pump: the ATPase uses ATP to establish an electrochemical gradient, while channels allow ions to move in directions determined by their electrochemical driving forces.

The ABA core module consists of soluble PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, clade A protein phosphatases of type 2C (PP2Cs) and SnRK2 protein kinases. In the absence of substantial ABA signaling, PP2Cs restrain the activity of relevant SnRK2 kinases by dephosphorylation. A major guard-cell kinase is OST1, also called SnRK2.6 in Arabidopsis. When ABA binds to its receptor, the resulting receptor complex interacts with a PP2C and inhibits the phosphatase. This releases inhibition of the kinase. The logic is a double negative: ABA inhibits an inhibitor through the receptor, permitting kinase-dependent phosphorylation.

Activated OST1 can phosphorylate the slow anion channel SLAC1, increasing anion efflux from the guard cell. Other anion channels and calcium-dependent kinases contribute in appropriate contexts. Efflux of negative charge depolarizes the plasma membrane, favoring outward potassium flux through channels such as GORK. Inward potassium transport and proton-pump-driven opening are also opposed during closure. As osmotically active solutes leave the guard cells or are redistributed, their osmotic potential becomes less negative. Water exits, turgor decreases and the aperture narrows. SLAC1 is an anion channel, not a potassium channel or a water pump.

Reactive oxygen production by NADPH oxidases can participate downstream of ABA signaling. Hydrogen peroxide and calcium signals can reinforce or modify channel activity through calcium-dependent kinases. The network is not a simple linear chain in every cell: parallel branches, feedback and partial redundancy mean that removal of one component may attenuate rather than completely eliminate closure. A biochemical demonstration that a kinase phosphorylates a channel is valuable, but determining whether the phosphorylation matters in the intact plant requires physiological measurements and appropriate genetic controls.

A drought-stressed guard cell presents an apparent paradox. Most plant cells benefit from maintaining turgor through osmotic adjustment, but closure requires guard cells to lose turgor relative to surrounding tissues. The paradox disappears when cell identity and function are considered. A mesophyll or growing root cell can benefit from retaining solutes; a guard cell must change its solute content dynamically to regulate the pore. The organism can therefore conserve water precisely because a specialized cell population relinquishes turgor in a controlled way.

A dominant ABA-insensitive PP2C allele can remain capable of inhibiting downstream signaling despite ABA exposure. Its phenotype differs from a simple loss of a negative regulator, which may instead enhance sensitivity. In a simplified epistasis experiment, removal of a positive kinase can block the effect of removing an upstream negative regulator if that kinase is indispensable for the measured response. Actual ABA networks contain multiple PP2Cs and SnRK2s, so predictions must specify which component and phenotype are being examined. This is a useful example of why mutation type is as important as pathway position.

Stomatal closure reduces transpirational water loss but also increases resistance to carbon dioxide entry. In moderate drought, photosynthesis may initially become limited by stomatal diffusion rather than a loss of the photosynthetic apparatus. During stronger or prolonged drought, mesophyll conductance, enzyme activity, photochemical function and carbon metabolism may also become limiting. A low assimilation rate alone cannot identify the causal limitation. The interpretation requires simultaneous measurements of conductance, carbon assimilation and other indicators of photosynthetic function.

Electrical and osmotic reasoning in guard cells

An ion channel changes the movement of a charged particle according to an electrochemical potential difference. The electrical term matters because a guard cell has a membrane voltage, defined conventionally as the inside potential relative to the outside. When the plasma membrane proton pump is active, outward movement of positive charge contributes to a negative inside potential. The resulting electrical force favors inward movement of positively charged potassium ions if the total electrochemical gradient is suitable. The chemical concentration gradient can oppose or reinforce this force. A channel cannot choose an arbitrary direction of net movement independently of these gradients.

An introductory Nernst calculation shows why depolarization can change the preferred direction of potassium flux. For an ideal monovalent cation at approximately 25 °C, the equilibrium potential can be written E_K ≈ 59 mV log10([K⁺]_outside/[K⁺]_inside). If external potassium is 10 mM and internal potassium is 100 mM, E_K is about −59 mV. At a membrane potential of −120 mV, the electrical and chemical forces together favor inward potassium movement through an open channel. At −30 mV, the net driving force favors outward movement under these simplified conditions. Actual guard-cell ion concentrations change during opening and closing, and inward and outward rectifying channels have distinct gating behavior. The calculation demonstrates how a change in voltage can switch the favored direction without converting a passive channel into a pump.

Anion efflux through activated channels removes negative charge from the cytoplasm, making the membrane potential less negative. This is depolarization. It is the depolarization and the potassium electrochemical driving force, together with appropriate channel gating, that permit outward potassium movement. If a hypothetical mutant prevents anion release, the normal depolarization may be attenuated even when upstream ABA perception is intact. Conversely, an experimentally imposed voltage change can help determine whether a potassium channel responds normally despite a defect in anion signaling. Such logic underlies many exam questions involving a pathway and a perturbation at a particular step.

Potassium and chloride are not the only contributors to guard-cell osmolality. Malate can be formed or metabolized in connection with organic acid metabolism, and sucrose can contribute to the solute pool under certain conditions. Vacuolar transport is necessary because the vacuole stores much of the water and many osmolytes of an open guard cell. Changes across the tonoplast and plasma membrane must be coordinated if guard-cell volume is to change efficiently. A diagram that shows only one plasma membrane potassium channel describes a small part of the process; the physiological prediction depends on the net redistribution of osmotically active compounds.

Guard-cell turgor is also not translated into pore aperture by water alone. The cell wall architecture imposes mechanical constraints. Differential wall thickness and the orientation of wall components affect the pattern of deformation in many kidney-shaped guard cells. The pressure and geometry of neighboring epidermal cells contribute to aperture as well. In grasses, subsidiary cells participate in rapid exchanges of ions and water, facilitating movement of the dumbbell-shaped guard cells. These differences illustrate why a mechanism based on the same broad osmotic principles can generate distinct quantitative opening and closing behaviors in different plant groups.

An abrupt increase in VPD can sometimes cause a transient mechanical opening before regulated closure develops. A possible explanation in species showing this behavior is that water loss changes the pressure of surrounding epidermal cells differently from that of guard cells, briefly reducing the mechanical constraint on opening. The subsequent active response can reverse that initial change. The transient behavior is not a contradiction of ABA-mediated drought conservation; it shows that mechanical and biochemical processes can have different kinetics. A measurement made immediately after the environmental change may therefore differ from a measurement after several minutes.

Experiments on isolated epidermis, intact leaves and whole plants answer related but distinct questions. An epidermal preparation makes it easier to apply ABA directly and measure pore aperture under controlled ionic conditions. It removes many contributions of long-distance transport and mesophyll signaling. An intact leaf preserves more of the natural mechanical and metabolic context, while a whole plant includes root supply and total water use. Agreement among these preparations strengthens a proposed mechanism. Disagreement can be informative because it indicates that an omitted tissue or environmental feedback may matter.

A stomatal aperture measured by microscopy and stomatal conductance estimated from gas exchange are not identical variables. Aperture describes geometry of a pore, whereas conductance reflects the ease of diffusion through many pores per unit leaf area. Pore depth, density and size distribution also influence conductance. An experiment comparing mutant and wild-type leaves of different developmental stages should therefore avoid interpreting a change in average aperture as a precise proportional change in total transpiration. Measuring both aperture and conductance can connect cellular mechanics to the whole-leaf consequence.

5. Longer-term molecular acclimation

Ion-channel regulation can change a stomatal aperture within minutes. Longer drought exposures require changes in metabolism and gene expression that develop on different time scales. An existing enzyme can be activated rapidly by phosphorylation, whereas synthesizing a protective protein requires transcription, RNA processing, translation and accumulation of functional protein. Distinguishing these processes helps explain why pretreatment or gradual drying can alter the response to a later stress.

ABA-activated SnRK2 kinases can phosphorylate transcription factors of the AREB/ABF group. These basic leucine zipper proteins regulate genes through ABA-responsive elements, often abbreviated ABREs, in suitable promoters. The resulting expression changes can include proteins associated with water limitation, osmotic protection and signaling feedback. The presence of an ABRE-like sequence does not by itself establish that the associated gene is ABA responsive. Promoter context, transcription factor availability, chromatin state and additional cis elements influence actual transcription.

A complementary pathway involves dehydration-responsive element binding proteins (DREBs), members of the AP2/ERF transcription factor family. DREB2-related factors are especially associated with dehydration and heat responses in Arabidopsis, whereas DREB1/CBF factors are classically associated with cold acclimation. These are useful tendencies rather than mutually exclusive assignments. A DRE/CRT element in a drought-induced promoter can support transcriptional regulation that is not dependent solely on ABA. ABA-independent does not mean independent of all signaling, calcium, phosphorylation or other environmental inputs.

Transcript accumulation should not be confused with protein accumulation or biological function. A transcript may rise substantially while translation is suppressed or the corresponding protein is unstable. To infer that a drought-induced gene improves survival, compare an appropriate loss-of-function line with a complemented line and the wild type at comparable tissue hydration. If an overexpressing line survives simply because it is smaller and uses less water, it may have acquired dehydration avoidance rather than greater cellular tolerance. Measuring soil water depletion and plant size helps expose that alternative.

Acclimation also involves feedback that limits the duration and cost of a response. Some ABA-responsive genes encode negative regulators of signaling, and the hormone can be inactivated when stress declines. Persistent activation of stress pathways under favorable conditions may restrict growth unnecessarily. A regulatory system that activates protection when needed and reverses it after recovery is consequently different from a constitutively activated system. This distinction matters when considering crop improvement, where stable yield under fluctuating conditions is often a more meaningful objective than survival under a single extreme challenge.

Testing drought-responsive gene regulation

The same promoter can receive several inputs. For a gene whose expression increases during drought, one regulatory element may respond to ABA-dependent factors while another responds to changes in osmotic status through a different signaling branch. The two inputs can be additive, synergistic or conditionally redundant. A reporter driven by the intact promoter might be activated strongly by dehydration and moderately by applied ABA. Removing one element may reduce only one component of the response. Removing both may greatly reduce induction, while basal expression remains because other promoter elements still function. The conclusion requires comparison with matched control constructs rather than a single reporter measurement.

An ABA biosynthesis mutant provides a useful tool for dissecting this network. Imagine a drought-induced transcript that still rises in such a mutant, whereas a known ABA-dependent control transcript does not. The result supports a component of induction that does not require normal ABA accumulation. However, it does not demonstrate complete ABA independence if the mutant retains residual hormone or if the sampled tissue received ABA from another source. An ABA signaling mutant can provide a complementary test. Using two distinct types of genetic perturbation reduces the chance of confusing an unusual biosynthetic phenotype with the signaling requirement of the target gene.

There is also a difference between necessity and sufficiency. If removing a DREB gene reduces expression of a protective target, the factor may be necessary for full induction under the tested condition. If expression of a DREB factor in a well-watered plant activates that target, it may be sufficient in the particular experimental context. It does not follow that the factor is sufficient for drought survival, which is an integrated phenotype involving hydration, membrane protection, metabolism and recovery. Redundancy can also mask the effect of an individual knockout. A weak phenotype is not always evidence that the deleted factor is biologically irrelevant.

Because transcription and translation require resources, gene expression during drought also involves prioritization. A cell can reduce processes associated with expansion or proliferation while maintaining enzymes and structural components necessary for survival. During recovery, expression programs must change again so that resources are redirected toward growth and repair. A transcriptional program that is advantageous during severe water deficit may impose a disadvantage during favorable conditions. This is one reason inducible promoters and developmental specificity are biologically meaningful in crop research, even when a constitutively expressed protective gene performs well in a short laboratory test.

The distinction between short-term adjustment and longer-term acclimation can be evaluated with a repeated-stress design. Plants that experienced a mild initial drought are rewatered and later exposed to a second standardized drought alongside plants without the earlier exposure. Faster induction or improved function during the second treatment may indicate a persistent effect of the first exposure. But differences in leaf size, root architecture, stored reserves or tissue age can also affect the response. Evidence for a retained regulatory state requires controlling those alternatives. Persistence across a single recovery period should not be confused with heritable evolutionary adaptation or assumed to demonstrate transgenerational inheritance.

6. Osmotic adjustment and compatible solutes

A cell losing water normally experiences an increase in solute concentration even if it synthesizes or imports no new solute. Imagine a compartment containing a fixed amount of dissolved substance. Reducing its water volume from 1.0 to 0.8 arbitrary units raises its concentration by a factor of 1.25. Its osmotic potential becomes more negative. This passive concentration effect accompanies dehydration and should not be mistaken for a regulated accumulation of solutes. Active osmotic adjustment means that the cell accumulates additional osmotically active solutes beyond the concentration effect of water loss.

The pressure component of water potential explains the physiological benefit. Suppose a cell at Ψw = −1.20 MPa has Ψs = −1.40 MPa. Its turgor is +0.20 MPa. If an adjusted cell at the same Ψw has Ψs = −1.80 MPa, its turgor is +0.60 MPa. Provided the wall and membrane remain functional, the second cell can maintain greater turgor under the specified water potential. The plant has not created water or violated thermodynamics. It has changed its chemical potential relationships by accumulating solutes, allowing a different balance between water content and pressure.

The turgor loss point is the water potential at which a tissue's turgor approaches zero. It is often estimated with a pressure–volume curve. After establishing the fully hydrated mass and a reference water status, the researcher records water potential as the tissue dehydrates in steps. The nonlinear portion of a suitable transformed curve reflects both pressure and osmotic components, whereas the post-turgor-loss region permits estimation of osmotic properties under specified assumptions. Extrapolation and the apoplastic water fraction require care. Comparisons between treatments should use consistent rehydration procedures, leaf age and measurement conditions because these affect the derived parameters.

Osmotic potential measured at comparable full turgor is often more informative about adjustment than a measurement on a dehydrated sample alone. If a stressed sample is more concentrated only because it lost water, rehydration toward its original water volume should reduce that difference. A persistently lower osmotic potential at comparable hydration supports solute accumulation. It is not conclusive if rehydration leads to solute leakage, if tissues differ in development or if cell compartment volumes change. Measuring total solute amount per dry mass or per relevant compartment provides complementary information.

Compatible solutes are low-molecular-weight compounds that can accumulate to relatively high concentrations without severely disrupting ordinary biochemical function. Examples include proline, sucrose, raffinose-family sugars and glycine betaine in species capable of accumulating it. Inorganic ions can also contribute to osmotic potential, especially when stored in vacuoles, but excessive concentrations in the cytosol can inhibit enzymes. Compartmentation permits the plant to combine vacuolar osmotic contributions with cytoplasmic protection.

Proline, an amino acid, often accumulates during drought. Its production commonly involves glutamate conversion through pyrroline-5-carboxylate, with P5CS catalyzing early steps and P5CR reducing the intermediate to proline. Proline catabolism involves mitochondrial enzymes, including proline dehydrogenase, and can become important during recovery. Regulation of both synthesis and degradation determines the final pool. Proline may contribute to osmotic potential, redox metabolism and protection of macromolecules, but its quantitative osmotic contribution depends on its concentration in the relevant compartment. A high proline concentration can also be a marker of stress severity rather than evidence that a plant is better adapted.

Glycine betaine is a zwitterionic quaternary ammonium compound. In accumulating plants, choline can be oxidized through betaine aldehyde to glycine betaine. It can stabilize protein complexes and membranes and contribute to osmotic regulation. Many species do not naturally accumulate large quantities of it, so this response is not universal. Exogenous supply experiments require controls for uptake, vehicle and osmotic effects, and transgenic accumulation experiments must distinguish a protective effect from unrelated differences in growth.

Soluble sugars have multiple functions. Sucrose supplies carbon to sinks, while sugars released by starch breakdown can maintain metabolism during restricted photosynthesis. Their accumulation can lower osmotic potential and stabilize membranes or proteins during severe water limitation. However, a high sugar concentration may arise because growth and utilization have declined more than synthesis, not because synthesis increased. Measurements of photosynthetic carbon fixation, starch pools, soluble sugars and biomass over time are needed to discriminate among these possibilities.

Osmotic adjustment has costs. Carbon skeletons, reducing power and nitrogen invested in protective solutes cannot simultaneously be used for all other functions. Maintaining turgor may help a plant continue growing roots into moist soil or sustain photosynthesis during intermittent drought. In a shallow soil facing terminal drought, continued water use might deplete remaining supplies too early. Therefore a beneficial cellular mechanism does not automatically maximize seasonal crop yield. Its value depends on the timing of water shortage, root access, allocation and reproductive stage.

Quantifying solute accumulation and cellular elasticity

A concentration is an amount divided by a volume. This elementary relationship provides the simplest test of passive concentration. Suppose a cell initially contains 0.10 mmol of an ideal dissolved substance in 1.0 mL of water. Its concentration is 0.10 mol L⁻¹. If water volume decreases to 0.60 mL without solute gain or loss, the concentration becomes approximately 0.167 mol L⁻¹. A measured concentration of 0.20 mol L⁻¹ at the same final volume would correspond to 0.12 mmol, indicating a net increase of 0.02 mmol under the assumptions of a single uniform compartment and accurate volume measurement. Real plant tissues have apoplastic and symplastic water, so the same calculation on a whole leaf requires careful definition of the relevant volume.

The physiological importance of that additional solute can be estimated through the van't Hoff relationship. At 298 K, a difference of 0.033 mol L⁻¹ for a non-dissociating solute corresponds to an osmotic potential difference of about −0.082 MPa. This value might contribute to turgor maintenance but is smaller than a hypothetical whole-tissue osmotic adjustment of −0.40 MPa. Other solutes or compartmental effects would need to account for the remaining difference. Such estimates prevent the common error of assuming that a several-fold increase in a metabolite must dominate the osmotic potential. A compound initially present at a very low concentration can increase many times and still contribute little to bulk osmolality.

Pressure–volume behavior reflects cell walls as well as solutes. An elastic wall resists changes in cell volume, generating a relationship between volume and turgor. The bulk modulus of elasticity describes how strongly turgor changes with relative volume change under specified conditions. A tissue with a relatively rigid wall can lose substantial turgor after a small decrease in volume, whereas a more elastic tissue may undergo a larger volume change for the same pressure decline. The value can vary with tissue type, development and hydration. Thus a lower turgor loss point can reflect differences in osmotic potential, cell-wall elasticity or both.

Osmotic potential at full turgor is commonly estimated by analyzing the region of a pressure–volume curve in which turgor has been lost. After turgor loss, total potential is dominated by osmotic potential in the simplified model. If the solute amount remains constant and the osmotically active water volume decreases, the magnitude of osmotic potential is approximately inversely related to that volume. Taking the reciprocal of potential can produce an approximately linear relation with water content over the appropriate range. Extrapolating this relation to the chosen full-hydration reference permits an estimate of osmotic potential at full turgor. Deviations from model assumptions, including changes in solute content, apoplastic fraction and incomplete equilibration, must be considered.

The accumulation of compatible solutes also requires consideration of metabolic flux. A larger proline pool can result from an increased rate of synthesis, reduced oxidation, altered transport or several changes together. Pool size is the net outcome of input and output rates over time. If a plant is exposed to drought and its proline concentration rises, one cannot determine from this observation alone which enzyme changed activity. Measuring labeled precursor incorporation, enzyme activity or changes during recovery can provide more evidence. At the expected examination level, the key is understanding the distinction between a metabolite pool and a pathway flux rather than mastering isotope tracer protocols.

Proline metabolism also illustrates the connection between nitrogen and carbon physiology. Glutamate is a central product of nitrogen assimilation and an important amino-group donor. Diverting glutamate toward proline can change the allocation of nitrogen and carbon skeletons, while the reduction reactions consume reducing equivalents. During recovery, oxidation of accumulated proline can contribute to metabolism. These connections explain why the amount of proline available for protection depends on the plant's metabolic state. Drought and nitrogen limitation together may produce responses different from either treatment alone.

Sugars can provide osmotic support and alter the physical environment of macromolecules. In a well-hydrated tissue, their colligative contribution is related to the number of dissolved particles. During severe dehydration, stabilization can also depend on specific interactions and on the physical properties of concentrated sugar mixtures. These roles are not reducible to osmotic potential alone. A protective sugar may influence enzyme stability or membrane behavior at similar osmotic potential to another substance. Comparing two solutes at equal osmolality and measuring molecular protection is one way to investigate noncolligative effects.

The physiological consequences of active adjustment depend on where solutes accumulate. A highly negative vacuolar potential can support water entry and turgor provided the tonoplast and plasma membrane remain functional. Cytoplasmic solutes must be compatible with enzymes and other macromolecules. A bulk tissue extract blends these compartments and can obscure an important difference in distribution. The basic water-potential principle remains the same, but interpreting measured concentrations requires knowing the biological scale. This distinction will become especially important when studying salt stress, in which inorganic ion accumulation can be simultaneously beneficial for vacuolar osmotic balance and harmful in the cytosol.

7. Root architecture, aquaporins and whole-plant avoidance

The amount of water available to a plant depends partly on how roots explore soil. Deep roots may reach moist layers after the surface has dried, whereas a dense shallow root system can exploit small rainfall events before that water evaporates. Neither architecture is best in all environments. Root growth is constrained by mechanical impedance, soil oxygen, nutrient distribution, temperature and carbon supply. A genotype selected in a deep uniform soil may perform differently in a shallow stony soil.

The root-to-shoot dry mass ratio is often reported as an indicator of allocation. If shoots decline in growth more rapidly than roots, the ratio may increase even if absolute root biomass is smaller than in well-watered plants. For example, a control with 4 g roots and 12 g shoots has a ratio of 0.33; a stressed plant with 3 g roots and 6 g shoots has a ratio of 0.50. The second plant has a higher ratio but less root mass. Therefore a ratio must be interpreted together with absolute biomass, root length distribution and the water actually acquired.

Water crosses root tissues through apoplastic and cell-to-cell pathways. Aquaporins are membrane proteins that increase permeability to water and, for certain family members, additional small molecules. Plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs) and tonoplast intrinsic proteins (TIPs) are important plant classes. Aquaporins do not hydrolyze ATP to drive water uphill against water potential. Instead they increase passive water permeability. Their abundance, localization and gating influence how rapidly water moves when an appropriate driving force exists.

During drought, aquaporin expression and activity can increase or decrease depending on isoform, organ, species, stress intensity and duration. A decrease in some water permeabilities can restrict unwanted water loss, while increased permeability in other tissues may facilitate uptake or rehydration. Phosphorylation, pH, calcium and trafficking can regulate particular channels. An increase in messenger RNA for an aquaporin is therefore not sufficient evidence of increased root hydraulic conductance. Channel protein abundance, membrane localization, activity and other resistances in the root all influence the whole-organ result.

A root pressure probe or hydraulic flow measurement can help determine whether changes in membrane properties affect water transport. Researchers must control the driving force, temperature and root size. If a drought-treated root passes less water than a control at the same imposed pressure difference, its hydraulic conductance is lower under those conditions. This does not uniquely identify aquaporin closure because anatomical changes, root–soil contact and other pathways can also affect flow. Genetic comparisons and recovery measurements help attribute the cause more precisely.

Shoots contribute to avoidance through reduced leaf expansion, leaf rolling, altered leaf angle, increased cuticular resistance and stomatal control. Leaf rolling can reduce the exposed surface and alter the local boundary layer, but its effect depends on leaf anatomy and environmental conditions. Cuticular wax can decrease non-stomatal water loss, especially when stomata are mostly closed. A plant with an effective cuticle may conserve water late in drought when further stomatal closure provides little additional benefit.

Stomatal density is a developmental property distinct from aperture, which can change rapidly. A leaf that develops with fewer stomata may have a lower maximum conductance, but pore size, opening dynamics, boundary layer and total leaf area also matter. A smaller number of pores does not guarantee lower transpiration if the remaining pores are larger or remain more open. The whole-plant water budget combines conductance per area with the total transpiring surface and the duration of exposure.

Root architecture evidence and tradeoffs in hydraulic regulation

Root architecture can be changed without simply increasing total biomass. The angle of growth determines the vertical and horizontal distribution of a root system. The number and length of lateral roots affect the soil volume explored. Fine roots provide exchange surface, and their lifespan determines how long that surface remains functional. Root hairs can improve contact with the substrate, although their contribution to water acquisition depends on soil conditions and the characteristics of the surrounding rhizosphere. A description of a plant as deep-rooted should therefore be supported by measurements of root distribution rather than assumed from a large root mass alone.

An instructive example is the rice gene DEEPER ROOTING 1, studied by Uga and colleagues in 2013. A shallow-rooting rice cultivar was improved for root growth angle by introduction of a functional DRO1 allele through backcrossing. The resulting line explored deeper soil and performed better under drought than the recipient cultivar in the reported experiments. The biological reasoning is that altered architecture allowed better access to water rather than enabling leaf cells to survive complete desiccation. The study illustrates drought avoidance and shows why traits affecting root placement can be agriculturally important. The conclusion should not be generalized to every shallow soil or water regime; deep exploration can provide little advantage when the deeper layers contain no accessible water.

The genetic comparison is especially informative because a backcrossed line can be compared with the recipient background. To interpret such a study, ask whether root angle changed, whether roots occupied deeper layers, whether plant water status or yield differed under drought and whether a penalty appeared under adequate water. These are separate measurements along a causal sequence. A yield difference alone does not prove the root mechanism, while a change in root angle alone does not establish a yield benefit. The combination provides stronger evidence. In examination questions, the same reasoning applies to any proposed drought-resistance trait introduced into a crop genotype.

Hydraulic regulation can involve a tradeoff between preserving water and maintaining nutrient acquisition. Water movement toward roots and across tissues can help deliver dissolved nutrients to uptake surfaces. Reduced transpiration also changes the movement of some nutrients through xylem. During drought, root growth into moist soil can partly offset reductions in transport elsewhere, but carbon costs and soil nutrient distribution influence the outcome. A plant cannot optimize water acquisition independently of mineral nutrition and metabolic allocation. This is why biomass measurements should be interpreted alongside physiological measurements of supply.

The pathway through living roots can change in ways that are not captured by a single membrane permeability measurement. Endodermal barriers constrain apoplastic movement and influence the relative importance of transcellular and symplastic routes. During development or stress, the extent of suberization can change, reducing uncontrolled exchange in some regions. Such changes may improve control over radial transport but also add resistance to water entry. The effect depends on the age of the root segment and where active water uptake occurs. Measurements averaged across old and young roots can hide these differences.

Water uptake can also redistribute within a heterogeneous root system. If one soil layer is wetter than another, differences in local water potential and pathway conductance influence how much each root region supplies. Under low transpiration, in some systems water can move through roots from wetter to drier regions, a process called hydraulic redistribution. The direction depends on water-potential differences, not on an active root pump moving water arbitrarily against them. This behavior is not universal and does not create additional water in the soil. It illustrates the value of considering the entire network rather than treating each root as an independent one-way uptake device.

8. Protective proteins, membranes and desiccation tolerance

As cells dehydrate, their internal environment becomes increasingly crowded. Molecules that were separated by water may interact in unfavorable ways. Proteins can lose their normal conformations or aggregate; membranes can change their organization as hydration and lipid packing change. Severe drying imposes mechanical stresses because cell volume contracts while the wall constrains tissue geometry. These effects explain why maintaining turgor is only one aspect of drought resistance. A cell must also protect its molecular structures when its water content declines.

Late embryogenesis abundant proteins, or LEA proteins, were first associated with the accumulation of proteins during late seed development. Many are also induced in vegetative tissues during water limitation. Dehydrins are a group within the LEA family, commonly described as group 2 LEA proteins. Many dehydrins are hydrophilic and intrinsically disordered under hydrated conditions. Conserved motifs, including lysine-rich K segments, can contribute to interactions with membranes or other molecules. The lack of a single stable folded structure does not mean that a protein lacks function.

Proposed and experimentally supported LEA functions include reducing protein aggregation, stabilizing membranes and cooperating with protective sugars in dehydrated systems. Their effects vary among individual proteins and experimental conditions. A test in which a purified LEA protein preserves the activity of a reporter enzyme during drying supplies evidence of molecular protection in vitro. It does not alone demonstrate that the same protein increases crop yield. A plant-level hypothesis additionally requires appropriate expression, localization and physiological measurements.

The antioxidant systems introduced in the preceding node also matter during drought. When stomatal closure restricts carbon dioxide entry, the use of reducing equivalents in carbon fixation may decline while light absorption continues. This imbalance can favor reactive oxygen formation. Photoprotective energy dissipation, antioxidant enzymes and nonenzymatic antioxidants help limit damage. Their contribution should be interpreted alongside stomatal limitation and tissue water status, not treated as an alternative explanation for every decline in photosynthesis.

Desiccation tolerance is the exceptional ability to survive extensive loss of cellular water and resume function after rehydration. Many orthodox seeds acquire this capacity during maturation; recalcitrant seeds, in contrast, are sensitive to substantial drying. Vegetative tissues of most crop plants cannot survive comparable water loss. Resurrection plants such as some species of Craterostigma can withstand extreme dehydration of vegetative tissues and subsequently recover. Some retain much of their photosynthetic apparatus while others dismantle parts of it during drying and rebuild it during rehydration. These strategies illustrate the distinction between avoiding dehydration and surviving it.

Protective sugars and proteins can cooperate as water is removed. In very dry cells, suitable mixtures of solutes can form a glass-like amorphous matrix that greatly restricts molecular mobility. This vitrified state can reduce some damaging interactions, but it does not make metabolism continue normally without water. Extreme desiccation survival commonly involves metabolic quiescence and restoration of activity after rehydration. Glass formation also depends on composition, temperature and residual moisture.

Desiccation tolerance is not equivalent to the ability to withstand unlimited drought. A plant surviving one drying event must still have sufficient reserves and intact meristems to rehydrate and resume growth. Drying rate can determine whether protective responses develop in time. Rehydration itself imposes stress through rapid changes in volume, membrane organization and reactive oxygen metabolism. Successful survival therefore includes both preparation for drying and controlled restoration of physiological activity.

Mechanistic evidence for molecular protection during drying

A useful distinction between an osmolyte and a protective macromolecule emerges from their contribution to colligative properties. Osmotic potential depends on the number of dissolved particles. A large protein molecule contributes far fewer particles per gram than a small sugar or amino acid. A LEA protein can therefore provide substantial molecular protection without making a correspondingly large direct contribution to osmotic potential. Its value can arise from interactions with other proteins, membranes or a highly concentrated intracellular matrix. Treating all drought-induced compounds as interchangeable osmolytes obscures the different physical mechanisms.

Protein aggregation provides a concrete example. A folded enzyme normally keeps many hydrophobic side chains away from the surrounding water. During dehydration, changes in the molecular environment can expose regions that associate with other partially unfolded proteins. Aggregation may make activity difficult to restore when water returns. A hydrophilic protective protein can reduce undesirable associations under suitable conditions. A laboratory comparison might dry a reporter enzyme alone, with a candidate LEA protein and with an unrelated protein at comparable concentration. Enzyme activity after controlled rehydration is the measured outcome. The unrelated-protein control helps distinguish a specific protective effect from a general influence of added protein or crowding.

Membrane protection can be investigated with simplified lipid vesicles. If drying and rehydration cause a vesicle to release a trapped soluble marker, this is evidence that the barrier has become permeable or disrupted. Adding a candidate protective sugar or dehydrin and comparing marker retention can test whether it reduces damage in that system. Such an assay does not recreate the complete complexity of a plasma membrane, which contains many proteins, lipid species and cytoskeletal attachments. It provides a mechanistic model that can be connected to whole-tissue electrolyte leakage and survival experiments.

The behavior of intrinsically disordered proteins is particularly relevant during transitions in hydration. In abundant water, some LEA proteins lack a single compact structure; as their environment changes they can adopt more ordered conformations or interact with particular surfaces. This plasticity may contribute to their protective functions. It would be inaccurate to assert that every LEA protein undergoes the same structural transition or performs the same molecular role. The family contains distinct groups with different sequences and cellular locations. At NET level, the important concepts are their association with dehydration, protective rather than catalytic roles for many members, and the need to distinguish experimentally supported functions from broad hypotheses.

Orthodox seeds show that desiccation tolerance is often developmentally acquired. During maturation, reserves accumulate, metabolic activity is reduced and protective systems are established before the mature seed loses most of its free water. Recalcitrant seeds do not tolerate a comparable drying regime. Germination of an orthodox seed involves rehydration and reactivation of metabolism; as development progresses, the capacity for extreme drying survival can be lost. A trait expressed during one life stage should not be assumed to exist in every tissue of the same species. This connects the drought chapter with the hormone and seed-development chapters without requiring repetition of the entire seed maturation program.

Resurrection plants extend this developmental principle to vegetative tissues. A drying leaf can alter pigments, antioxidant metabolism, cell organization and protective protein abundance before entering a highly dehydrated state. During rehydration, repair and metabolic reactivation must proceed without excessive oxidative or mechanical damage. Different species retain or dismantle their photosynthetic apparatus to differing extents. Retaining apparatus can permit relatively rapid reactivation but may require protection during drying; dismantling it can limit certain kinds of photodamage while requiring rebuilding after hydration. These are alternative solutions, not successive obligatory stages for all resurrection plants.

A comparison of a succulent with a resurrection plant demonstrates two different endpoints. The succulent can maintain water reserves, reduce exposed surface relative to storage and regulate water loss, delaying extreme tissue dehydration. The resurrection plant can allow a much larger reduction in tissue water and survive the resulting cellular state. If both plants survive for a week without irrigation, survival alone cannot identify which strategy they used. Measuring water content through the dry period and observing recovery after rewatering reveals a more meaningful distinction.

9. Photosynthesis, growth and water-use efficiency

The conservation of water by stomatal closure has a direct cost because the same pores admit carbon dioxide. Net carbon assimilation A is commonly expressed in micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second. Stomatal conductance to water vapor gs is often expressed in moles of H₂O per square metre per second. An instantaneous intrinsic water-use efficiency can be defined as A/gs, with units such as micromoles of CO₂ per mole of H₂O. It should not be confused with the actual transpiration efficiency A/E, because transpiration also depends on VPD and boundary-layer conditions.

Consider a control leaf with A = 15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gs = 0.30 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Its intrinsic efficiency is 50 µmol mol⁻¹. A drought-treated leaf with A = 9 and gs = 0.10 has intrinsic efficiency 90 µmol mol⁻¹. The second leaf fixes less carbon per unit time even though the ratio has increased. If a student concludes that higher efficiency necessarily means higher crop yield, the conclusion ignores carbon assimilation, leaf area, seasonal water availability and reproduction.

The intercellular carbon dioxide concentration Ci can be informative when interpreted with gas-exchange data. During early stomatal limitation, decreasing conductance can lower Ci and A. If biochemical capacity is also depressed, Ci may remain stable or increase despite low A because carbon dioxide consumption has fallen. Calculated Ci can be misleading when stomatal closure is patchy or conductance is very low, and a stable Ci does not prove that mesophyll conductance is unchanged. Measurements across several carbon dioxide concentrations, together with fluorescence and physiological controls, provide stronger evidence.

Chlorophyll fluorescence offers additional information about photosystem II function. The maximum quantum efficiency of dark-adapted PSII is often expressed as Fv/Fm = (Fm − Fo)/Fm, where Fo is minimal fluorescence and Fm is maximal fluorescence in the dark-adapted state. An illustrative change from Fo = 200 and Fm = 1000 to Fo = 300 and Fm = 800 reduces Fv/Fm from 0.80 to 0.625. The lower ratio suggests impaired maximum PSII efficiency under the measurement conditions. An unchanged ratio during moderate drought does not prove that assimilation is normal; diffusion can limit carbon fixation even when this fluorescence measure remains high.

C4 photosynthesis concentrates carbon dioxide around Rubisco and can support relatively high carbon gain at lower stomatal conductance under favorable warm, bright conditions. CAM plants separate major carbon uptake and use in time, often opening stomata at night when evaporative demand is lower. These mechanisms can improve water-use efficiency relative to other strategies in appropriate environments, but they impose metabolic or storage costs and do not confer universal drought immunity. C3 species also differ widely in rooting, stomatal behavior and phenology.

Drought during reproduction can have particularly large consequences. Pollen development, fertilization, seed formation and grain filling depend on water relations and assimilate supply. A plant may survive a dry period while losing much of its potential seed production. The timing of a drought treatment relative to developmental stage is therefore essential information. A comparison based only on seedling survival is not sufficient to predict reproductive performance in the field.

Diagnosing photosynthetic limitation and evaluating yield

Photosynthetic limitation during drought can be studied by comparing responses to increased external CO₂. If carbon assimilation is low because stomatal diffusion restricts the supply of CO₂, raising the external concentration may partially restore assimilation, provided the diffusive pathway is not completely closed and biochemical capacity remains adequate. If the photosynthetic machinery has lost substantial capacity, the response to additional CO₂ can be smaller. A response curve of assimilation against estimated intercellular CO₂ helps examine these alternatives. Intercellular concentration is not identical to the concentration at Rubisco, because mesophyll resistance adds another diffusion step. This limitation must be remembered when interpreting such curves.

The immediate cost of closure can extend to leaf energy balance. Evaporation consumes latent heat, so lower transpiration often reduces evaporative cooling. The leaf may become warmer if absorbed radiation and convective heat exchange remain similar. Higher temperature raises saturation vapor pressure and can increase the leaf-to-air deficit. Thus stomatal closure reduces conductance to water vapor, while increased leaf temperature can partially oppose the resulting reduction in transpiration. Thermal imaging can detect changes in leaf temperature, but temperature also depends on wind, leaf angle, radiation and boundary-layer thickness. A warm leaf is consistent with reduced cooling, not a uniquely diagnostic measure of ABA signaling.

Instantaneous water-use efficiency has several definitions that should be kept separate. A/E relates the rate of net carbon gain to the actual rate of transpiration at the measurement time. A/gs removes some immediate variation associated with atmospheric demand and focuses on the carbon gain relative to stomatal conductance. At the crop scale, water productivity may be defined as grain or biomass produced per unit of water consumed or supplied over a growing period. Soil evaporation and nonproductive water loss can affect this last measure. Numerical values of these efficiencies should not be compared without specifying both the numerator and denominator.

Suppose an illustrative plant has a leaf area of 0.10 m² and a steady assimilation of 12 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for five hours. Its integrated net leaf carbon uptake over that interval is 12 × 0.10 × 18,000 = 21,600 µmol, or 0.0216 mol CO₂. Another plant has higher assimilation per area, 15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, but only 0.06 m² of active leaf area. Its uptake is 16,200 µmol during the same period. This simplified calculation neglects temporal variation, leaf heterogeneity and respiration outside the measured leaf. It demonstrates why a measurement normalized per leaf area cannot directly predict whole-plant biomass gain.

Allocation further complicates the relationship with yield. A stressed plant may maintain a substantial root system at the cost of reduced leaf expansion or reproductive growth. Another may remobilize stored carbohydrates into developing seeds as photosynthesis falls. These responses can be beneficial or costly depending on the timing of drought. A genotype that conserves soil water until flowering may maintain yield better under terminal drought than one that grows rapidly at the beginning of the season. Under frequent small rainfall events, however, an excessively conservative strategy could leave available water unused and limit productivity. The biological objective is adaptation to a water regime, not maximizing any one physiological ratio.

10. Hydraulic injury and recovery after rewatering

Xylem vessels and tracheids carry water under tension during transpiration. As drought progresses, water pressure in the xylem can become increasingly negative. Gas can enter water-filled conduits through pathways associated with pits or other vulnerabilities, producing embolism that obstructs flow. Cavitation is the formation or expansion of a gas phase in the liquid system; embolism describes the resulting gas blockage in a conduit. Loss of functional conduits reduces hydraulic conductance, making it harder to deliver water to leaves.

Hydraulic vulnerability is often described using percentage loss of conductivity (PLC). If Kinitial denotes conductivity measured in the native state and Kmax is an estimate of maximum conductivity after appropriate restoration, PLC = 100 × (1 − Kinitial/Kmax). For illustrative values of 2 and 5 in identical units, PLC is 60%. The parameter P50 denotes the water potential or corresponding xylem pressure at which a vulnerability curve reaches 50% loss of conductivity. A more negative P50 generally indicates greater embolism resistance under comparable methods, but values from different organs and techniques should not be combined without checking their assumptions.

Stomatal closure often precedes major hydraulic failure, thereby limiting the demand that creates tension. The sequence varies among species and organs. A loss of whole-leaf hydraulic conductance can arise from changes outside the xylem, including dehydration of living tissues, before extensive vessel embolism occurs. Consequently, a decline in conductance should not automatically be described as evidence of cavitation. Imaging of conduit gas, suitable hydraulic measurements and simultaneous water status measurements can distinguish mechanisms.

Rewatering does not instantly reverse every drought effect. Soil water potential may improve within minutes while stomatal conductance remains suppressed because ABA is still elevated, leaf hydraulic conductance is impaired or carbon metabolism has not recovered. Mildly dehydrated cells may rapidly regain turgor and resume growth. After severe injury, tissues may need repair or replacement. Dead cells cannot regain viability merely by absorbing water. A restored external supply is necessary for recovery but may not be sufficient.

Embolism recovery is a special case. Some plants can restore conductivity under conditions that allow gas dissolution or positive root pressure; others primarily recover by producing new conductive tissue. Whether particular conduits refill while the surrounding xylem remains under substantial tension is context-dependent and has been debated. It is inaccurate to claim that aquaporins universally pump water into blocked vessels after rainfall. Aquaporins facilitate passive transport, and restoration of conduit continuity requires appropriate physical conditions.

A recovery experiment should compare a well-watered control, a drought-treated group and a rewatered group at repeated time points. Record leaf water potential, relative water content, stomatal conductance, assimilation, and where possible hydraulic function. If leaf water content recovers but gas exchange does not, the difference suggests a limitation beyond bulk hydration. If assimilation recovers only when new leaves develop, the original leaves may have experienced irreversible injury. Recovery trajectories thus provide information that an endpoint drought measurement cannot supply.

Interpreting vulnerability curves and post-drought function

A vulnerability curve relates loss of hydraulic conductivity to increasingly negative water potential or xylem pressure. It is often sigmoid-like, although measured forms differ among species, organs and methods. The point P50 is one summary parameter, but it does not describe the entire curve. Two curves with identical P50 can differ in the pressure at which conductivity first declines or in the slope near P50. The consequence for the plant depends on the water potentials actually reached during drought and on the ability of stomata to regulate demand before substantial hydraulic loss occurs.

A simple illustrative hydraulic safety margin can be described as the difference between the minimum operating water potential and a chosen vulnerability threshold, with the sign convention stated explicitly. If minimum operating potential is −1.5 MPa and P50 is −3.0 MPa, then Ψmin − P50 equals +1.5 MPa. The positive difference in this convention indicates that the measured operating potential is less negative than P50. This does not prove safety from all hydraulic injury, since conductivity can begin to decline before P50 and roots or leaves may have different thresholds. A margin calculated from one organ should not automatically be assigned to the whole plant.

The decline in conductivity can feed back on water status. Suppose a pathway initially delivers 2 units of water at a gradient of 1 MPa and conductance of 2 units MPa⁻¹. If conductance falls to 0.5 while demand remains 2 units, the gradient required by the steady-state model would rise to 4 MPa. In a plant, this increased tension could cause further loss of hydraulic function or tissue dehydration. Stomatal closure reduces demand and can interrupt the feedback. If demand falls to 0.5 units, the required gradient at conductance 0.5 becomes 1 MPa again. The numbers are hypothetical; the model demonstrates the potential for a positive feedback between loss of conductivity and increasing tension.

Recovery trajectories can distinguish residual regulation from structural damage. In a mild-drought treatment, soil and leaf potential may recover promptly after watering, followed by stomatal reopening as hormone signaling changes. In a severe treatment, leaf potential may recover during a low-demand night but fall sharply the next day because the damaged pathway cannot sustain high daytime flow. Measurements at predawn alone might suggest complete recovery even though midday function remains compromised. A comparison at several times of day and across several days can therefore reveal limitations hidden by a single endpoint.

Hydraulic conductivity measurements require methodological care because cutting water-filled conduits under tension or introducing air during preparation can alter their state. The estimated maximum conductivity may also depend on how the sample is treated. Optical or imaging approaches can provide complementary evidence of gas in conduits, but the presence of gas and the functional contribution of individual vessels to total conductivity are not exactly the same measurement. At examination level, the important principle is that a technique can perturb the very system it is designed to measure. Appropriate controls and independent measurements strengthen the inference.

Drought-associated mortality can reflect interacting hydraulic and carbon constraints. Continued water loss can compromise conductive tissues; prolonged closure can limit carbon acquisition and affect maintenance and repair. These mechanisms can interact rather than compete as exclusive explanations. A plant with impaired carbon metabolism may be less able to replace damaged tissue, while hydraulic dysfunction can restrict photosynthesis further. A complete treatment of tree mortality would go beyond the assigned scope, but the interaction illustrates why physiological recovery requires more than a single favorable water-potential measurement.

11. Measurement, controls and analytical reasoning

Relative water content (RWC) compares the amount of water currently present in a tissue with the amount it contains at a defined fully hydrated state. Let FW be fresh mass measured promptly after collection, TW be turgid mass after appropriate rehydration and DW be dry mass after drying to constant mass. Then RWC = 100 × (FW − DW)/(TW − DW). In an illustrative leaf, FW = 0.82 g, TW = 1.00 g and DW = 0.20 g. Present water mass is 0.62 g; fully hydrated water mass is 0.80 g; RWC is 77.5%. The subtraction of dry mass is essential because dry matter is not water.

RWC and water potential measure different properties. RWC describes the water amount relative to a reference capacity; water potential describes the thermodynamic tendency of water to move. Leaves with distinct solute concentrations or cell wall elasticity can have different potentials at the same RWC. Conversely, two leaves can have similar potential and different hydration. Measuring both provides a stronger description of tissue status. Rehydration methods require care because prolonged immersion can alter solute pools or produce an inappropriate estimate of full hydration.

A pressure chamber estimates the balancing pressure needed to bring xylem sap to a cut surface. Under suitable equilibration conditions the measured positive balancing pressure approximates the magnitude of the negative water potential of the sampled tissue. The sign must be handled correctly. A reading of +1.5 MPa corresponds approximately to a tissue potential of −1.5 MPa under the usual assumptions, not +1.5 MPa. Predawn potential can sometimes approximate the potential of soil accessed by roots, but nocturnal transpiration, heterogeneous soil and incomplete equilibration weaken the assumption.

An osmometer estimates osmotic properties from colligative behavior, such as freezing-point depression or vapor pressure. Measurements on expressed sap may mix contents from distinct compartments and may be affected by dilution or extraction. They must not be equated automatically with intact-cell turgor. When osmotic potential and total water potential are measured under comparable conditions, the difference can provide an estimate of pressure potential using the simplified equation already developed.

Gas-exchange systems compare carbon dioxide and water vapor concentrations in air entering and leaving a chamber containing a leaf. With known flow and enclosed leaf area, net assimilation and transpiration can be estimated; conductance and intercellular carbon dioxide can then be calculated using model assumptions. Light, chamber temperature, external CO₂, humidity and boundary-layer behavior should be controlled or recorded. A single conductance value is not a whole-plant water-use measurement because the total leaf area and time course are missing.

Electrolyte leakage is used as an index of membrane injury. A common approach compares conductivity after tissue incubation with total conductivity after disruption of the tissue, reporting a percentage. Higher leakage can indicate loss of membrane integrity, but the assay also depends on tissue ion content, sample size, surface washing, incubation time and damage during cutting. It is stronger evidence when paired with viability or recovery assays. A difference in leakage does not establish which membrane protein or lipid was originally damaged.

Experimental drought severity should be defined independently of treatment labels. Giving the same small volume of water to plants of different sizes can cause unequal tissue stress. Withholding irrigation for seven days can generate different soil water potentials depending on pot size, substrate, temperature and initial leaf area. Weighing pots can estimate water loss if soil evaporation and changes in mass are accounted for. Measurements of soil potential, RWC or leaf potential establish more informative comparisons than duration alone.

A factorial design separates the effects of soil drought and atmospheric demand. Use well-watered and dry-soil treatments at both low and high VPD, with replicated plants and appropriate randomization. If high VPD reduces conductance even with wet soil, atmospheric demand is sufficient to elicit a response under those conditions. If the combined treatment reduces assimilation more than expected from the individual effects under a specified statistical model, an interaction may be present. A meaningful conclusion requires biological replication and variation estimates rather than four single values.

To distinguish avoidance from tolerance, compare genotypes in two ways. First expose both to a similar environment and record their water status and performance. Then compare physiological function at similar tissue water status, preferably with time and developmental stage controlled. A plant that performs better under the same external drought because its tissue remains wetter exhibits evidence of avoidance. A plant that retains greater function at a comparably low tissue water potential provides evidence of tolerance. A genotype can express both mechanisms; neither comparison is sufficient by itself to establish every causal trait.

The same discipline applies to omics and expression studies. A drought-associated increase in a transcript, metabolite or protein identifies a response, not necessarily its benefit. A loss-of-function phenotype, rescue, controlled expression or independent biochemical assay helps move from association toward causality. Measurements should address alternative explanations such as smaller plant size, slower soil drying, developmental delay or altered water demand. This reasoning links drought physiology with the general principles of experimental biology used throughout the NET and SET syllabi.

Integrating a drought dataset and testing alternative mechanisms

Imagine two genotypes, X and Y, grown in equal pots under the same light and atmospheric conditions. After six days without irrigation, X has RWC 82%, net assimilation 9 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and relatively low electrolyte leakage, while Y has RWC 62%, assimilation 5 and greater leakage. This illustrative result is consistent with X avoiding dehydration better, but it does not establish that X cells are intrinsically more tolerant. If Y is brought to RWC 82% by a milder treatment and has similar assimilation and leakage to X, the apparent difference at day six may be explained largely by the hydration difference. Conversely, if X retains greater function at RWC 62% than Y, the result supports an additional tolerance mechanism.

Even matching RWC has limitations. Two tissues with different osmotic properties can have distinct water potentials and turgor at the same RWC. Therefore, when the proposed mechanism concerns maintenance of turgor, measurements of total and osmotic potential are important. When the proposed mechanism concerns survival of cellular dehydration, water content and viability are directly relevant. The choice of a matching variable should follow the hypothesis. Matching one variable does not make every aspect of stress equivalent.

A water-potential time course can provide evidence about a mechanism of avoidance. Consider a genotype with smaller leaves that maintains predawn and midday water potential more effectively than a large-leaved genotype when irrigation is stopped. Measuring pot mass may reveal slower water depletion by the smaller plant. The result is consistent with reduced demand. If both plants are grown in a deep soil column and the larger plant instead maintains higher water potential through extensive deep roots, the ranking can reverse. This demonstrates why an avoidance trait is a relationship between plant characteristics and the environment, not simply an intrinsic label attached to a genotype.

A factorial experiment on osmotic adjustment could compare drought and control plants of a wild type with plants impaired in a candidate solute synthesis enzyme. Measure osmotic potential at full turgor, leaf RWC, turgor loss point, biomass and recovery. If the mutant has reduced solute accumulation and a less negative full-turgor osmotic potential under drought but similar values under control conditions, the pattern supports a drought-associated role for that enzyme. If the mutant differs greatly in growth even before stress, interpretation becomes more difficult. Complementing the mutation and measuring additional solutes helps evaluate whether the observed phenotype is specific to the proposed mechanism.

The experiment should include biological rather than merely technical replication. Repeated readings from the same leaf improve the estimate for that leaf, but they do not provide independent plants exposed to the treatment. If four leaves are sampled from each of three plants, the independent experimental units may be the three plants, depending on the design. Treating all twelve leaves as independent can exaggerate the certainty of the result. Randomizing treatment positions and using comparable sampling procedures reduces environmental and observer effects. These elementary statistical principles are particularly important in drought studies because pot position and evaporation can create substantial variation.

Data interpretation should also distinguish absolute and normalized quantities. Proline reported as micromoles per gram fresh mass can appear to increase as water is lost even without an increase in total proline. Reporting per dry mass avoids part of that artifact but does not fully solve differences in tissue composition or compartment volume. Chlorophyll reported per leaf area may change differently from chlorophyll reported per fresh mass because leaf thickness and hydration differ. An exam question may deliberately provide measurements on different bases; before comparing them, identify what the denominator represents.

12. Misconceptions and examination traps

Wet soil excludes drought
High VPD or limited root conductance can cause dehydration even when soil water is available. Students often equate water in soil with water available at a sufficient flux. In an exam, compare wet soil at low and high VPD; inspect the supply–demand balance rather than soil moisture alone.
Water flows toward the most concentrated solution regardless of pressure
Total water potential, not osmotic potential alone, determines net direction. The solute term is memorable but the pressure term can reverse its prediction. Add Ψs and Ψp for both cells before determining the direction of water movement.
Negative water potential means negative turgor
Positive cellular turgor can coexist with negative total potential. Negative values describe the total potential relative to a reference, not necessarily a negative pressure inside a living cell. Calculate Ψp = Ψw − Ψs before interpreting turgor.
Every decrease in osmotic potential is adjustment
Passive concentration accompanies water loss; compare at standardized hydration. Fresh sap from a wilted leaf is concentrated even without synthesis. A question giving full-turgor osmotic potential or solute amount at controlled hydration is testing active accumulation.
ABA is synthesized only in roots
Shoot tissues can synthesize ABA; transport and sensitivity also matter. The classic root-to-shoot hormone model is sometimes overgeneralized. Graft and split-root questions require distinguishing hormone origin, transport, perception and hydraulic signals.
ABA activates PP2C
ABA-bound PYL inhibits clade A PP2C, relieving kinase inhibition. An inhibitory phosphatase lies between the receptor and kinase. A gain-of-function negative regulator differs from its knockout; trace the signs of the regulatory edges.
SLAC1 is a potassium channel
SLAC1 mediates anion efflux; depolarization favors outward potassium flux through other channels. The two channels participate in the same closure process, but carry different ions. Anion efflux depolarizes the membrane and can favor K⁺ efflux through GORK.
Guard cells maintain turgor during closure
Guard-cell turgor normally decreases to close the pore. Maintaining turgor is useful for most growing cells, but guard cells regulate pore geometry. Evaluate the specialized cell function instead of applying a general tissue rule.
Aquaporins actively pump water
They facilitate passive movement according to the water-potential gradient. Water channels accelerate equilibration; they do not supply energy to transport water uphill. A permeability increase alone does not establish flow direction.
Greater root-to-shoot ratio proves root growth increased
Shoot biomass can decline more than root biomass. A ratio may increase when its denominator falls. Compare absolute root biomass, root depth and water uptake rather than inferring growth from relative allocation.
High proline proves drought tolerance
Proline can reflect stress intensity; assess protection at comparable dehydration. A stress marker may respond strongly because injury is severe. A causal tolerance claim needs perturbation, rescue or a comparison at matched tissue water status.
All LEA proteins are dehydrins
Dehydrins are one group of LEA proteins. LEA is a diverse family and dehydrin denotes a subgroup. Classifying every LEA as a dehydrin reverses the inclusion relationship.
High intrinsic WUE guarantees yield
Assimilation can decline while A/gs rises. The denominator can decline faster than the numerator. Compute A/gs, but assess assimilation, total leaf area and integrated yield separately.
Low assimilation proves PSII injury
Stomatal diffusion may limit assimilation while dark-adapted PSII efficiency remains high. Gas-exchange limitation can precede photochemical injury. Compare Ci, conductance and fluorescence under matched conditions before assigning a mechanism.
Rewatering immediately reverses injury
Persistent hydraulic or cellular injury can delay or prevent recovery. Rewatering removes an environmental limitation, not necessarily structural injury. Compare time courses of hydration, conductance, assimilation and growth.

13. Original examination practice

Part B — conceptual questions

1. Which component of water potential is lowered by dissolved solutes?

A. Pressure potential; B. Osmotic potential; C. Gravitational potential; D. Hydraulic conductance.

Answer: B. Osmotic potential.

Dissolved solutes lower water activity. Pressure and gravity are distinct potential terms; conductance is not a potential.

2. Which factor most directly distinguishes soil drought from atmospheric drought?

A. Soil-water supply versus atmospheric evaporative demand; B. Soil pH versus air pH; C. Root respiration versus photosynthesis; D. The occurrence of any water in the plant.

Answer: A. Soil-water supply versus atmospheric evaporative demand.

Both forms can reduce tissue hydration, but the limiting environmental factor differs. Soil moisture is not the same as VPD.

3. What is the major guard-cell SnRK2 in Arabidopsis?

A. SLAC1; B. ABI1; C. GORK; D. OST1 (SnRK2.6).

Answer: D. OST1 (SnRK2.6).

SLAC1 and GORK are ion channels; ABI1 is a PP2C phosphatase.

4. What is the role of clade A PP2C in the ABA core pathway?

A. Negative regulation of SnRK2; B. Direct ATP-dependent pumping of potassium; C. ABA biosynthesis; D. Anion transport.

Answer: A. Negative regulation of SnRK2.

The phosphatase restrains the kinase. It is neither a biosynthetic enzyme in this module nor an ion transporter.

5. What does SLAC1 conduct during stomatal closure?

A. Water; B. Potassium; C. Anions; D. ABA.

Answer: C. Anions.

Anion efflux favors depolarization; GORK conducts K⁺. Aquaporins facilitate water movement.

6. Which transcription factor family includes DREB proteins?

A. AP2/ERF; B. SnRK2; C. PP2C; D. PIP.

Answer: A. AP2/ERF.

SnRK2s are kinases, PP2Cs phosphatases and PIPs aquaporins.

7. What does an ABRE represent?

A. A potassium channel; B. An ABA biosynthetic enzyme; C. An ABA-responsive cis-regulatory element; D. An ABA transport vesicle.

Answer: C. An ABA-responsive cis-regulatory element.

ABREs are DNA regulatory sequences recognized by suitable AREB/ABF factors; the other options refer to proteins or structures.

8. What is a compatible solute?

A. A solute that must remain outside every cell; B. A substance that can accumulate with relatively little disruption of cellular biochemistry; C. A membrane channel; D. A solute that always produces toxicity.

Answer: B. A substance that can accumulate with relatively little disruption of cellular biochemistry.

Compatible solutes can support osmotic balance and molecular protection; they are not membrane transport proteins and their compatibility is concentration dependent.

9. Which amino acid commonly accumulates during water deficit?

A. Glycine exclusively; B. Methionine exclusively; C. Tryptophan exclusively; D. Proline.

Answer: D. Proline.

Free proline often accumulates in drought through altered synthesis and degradation; the other amino acids are not the characteristic answer.

10. To which protein family do dehydrins belong?

A. PYL receptors; B. Cellulose synthases; C. Late embryogenesis abundant proteins; D. Proton ATPases.

Answer: C. Late embryogenesis abundant proteins.

Dehydrins are a LEA subgroup; the other options have distinct transport, synthesis or signaling roles.

11. What does P50 on a xylem vulnerability curve represent?

A. The pressure of maximum turgor; B. The pressure or water potential at 50% conductivity loss; C. 50% relative humidity; D. A 50% reduction in photosynthesis.

Answer: B. The pressure or water potential at 50% conductivity loss.

P50 is a hydraulic parameter, not a direct measure of stomatal conductance, photosynthesis or humidity.

12. Which membranes contain PIP aquaporins?

A. Tonoplast exclusively; B. Outer mitochondrial membrane exclusively; C. Thylakoid exclusively; D. Plasma membrane.

Answer: D. Plasma membrane.

PIP denotes plasma membrane intrinsic protein; TIP denotes tonoplast intrinsic protein.

13. What is the denominator of the RWC formula before multiplication by 100?

A. FW − DW; B. TW − FW; C. TW − DW; D. DW alone.

Answer: C. TW − DW.

The denominator is water mass at the defined fully hydrated state; FW − DW is the numerator.

14. What does dehydration avoidance mean?

A. Maintaining relatively favorable tissue water status under shortage; B. Surviving severe drying at unchanged water content; C. Completing reproduction before a dry season; D. Restoring growth only after rewatering.

Answer: A. Maintaining relatively favorable tissue water status under shortage.

C describes escape; D is recovery. Tolerance is function or viability at low tissue water status, not unchanged water content.

15. Which tissues of many resurrection plants survive extensive drying?

A. Only pollen in every species; B. Only xylem vessel lumens; C. Vegetative tissues; D. Only the seed coat.

Answer: C. Vegetative tissues.

Resurrection plants are unusual because vegetative tissues tolerate extensive drying. Mature xylem conduits are already dead, so their drying does not demonstrate cellular survival.

16. What is the usual effect of stomatal closure on internal CO₂ supply?

A. It increases gaseous conductance; B. It restricts diffusion from the atmosphere; C. It increases Rubisco concentration instantly; D. It eliminates all respiration.

Answer: B. It restricts diffusion from the atmosphere.

The pore becomes more resistive. Closure does not instantaneously increase Rubisco or stop respiration.

17. What is the meaning of turgor loss point?

A. The point of maximal photosynthesis; B. The water potential at which tissue turgor approaches zero; C. The point at which all cells die; D. The humidity at which stomata open.

Answer: B. The water potential at which tissue turgor approaches zero.

Loss of turgor does not necessarily cause irreversible death and is distinct from atmospheric humidity and carbon fixation.

18. What does glycine betaine contribute in accumulating species?

A. Active water pumping; B. Xylem vessel formation exclusively; C. Osmotic and macromolecular protection; D. Direct hydrolysis of ATP in chloroplasts.

Answer: C. Osmotic and macromolecular protection.

Glycine betaine is a compatible solute in accumulating species; it is not a pump or an ATPase.

19. Which gas-exchange measure is used in the numerator of intrinsic WUE?

A. Net CO₂ assimilation A; B. Stomatal conductance; C. Transpiration; D. Relative water content.

Answer: A. Net CO₂ assimilation A.

Intrinsic WUE is A/gs. Actual transpiration occurs in the denominator of A/E, a different measure.

20. Why is leaf temperature needed when calculating leaf-to-air VPD?

A. It directly determines soil texture; B. It determines the number of stomata instantly; C. It fixes leaf osmotic potential; D. Saturation vapor pressure depends on temperature.

Answer: D. Saturation vapor pressure depends on temperature.

Leaf temperature influences the saturated vapor pressure at evaporation sites, even if the ambient relative humidity is unchanged.

Part C — analytical questions

21. A cell has Ψs = −1.1 MPa and Ψp = +0.3 MPa. An adjoining cell has Ψw = −0.5 MPa. Predict water movement.

Answer: Toward the first cell.

Its total potential is −0.8 MPa, lower than −0.5 MPa.

22. At 298 K, calculate Ψs of 0.10 M ideal sucrose.

Answer: −0.248 MPa.

Using i = 1 and R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ gives −1 × 0.10 × 0.008314 × 298.

23. A leaf has FW 0.7 g, TW 1.0 g, DW 0.25 g. Calculate RWC.

Answer: 60%.

Current water is 0.45 g and fully hydrated water is 0.75 g; their ratio is 0.60.

24. Conductance falls from 4 to 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ while the driving gradient rises from 0.5 to 1 MPa. Predict steady flow.

Answer: Unchanged at 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Both initial and final products KΔΨ equal 2; flow does not identify conductance alone.

25. A mutant fails to close stomata during drought, but application of ABA restores closure. Which defect is supported?

A. Loss of all guard-cell ABA receptors; B. Impaired endogenous ABA availability with retained response machinery; C. Complete loss of all downstream anion channels; D. Irreversible guard-cell death.

Answer: B. Impaired endogenous ABA availability with retained response machinery.

Exogenous ABA restoring closure supports an upstream hormone-availability defect. Complete loss of perception or essential effectors would not be expected to be rescued simply by applying the ligand. The experiment does not uniquely identify a biosynthetic gene.

26. A PP2C allele inhibits OST1 even in the presence of ABA-bound PYL. Predict the response.

A. Enhanced ABA-dependent closure; B. No effect on kinase activity; C. Direct activation of GORK by ABA without signaling; D. Reduced ABA-dependent closure.

Answer: D. Reduced ABA-dependent closure.

The active phosphatase remains a brake on OST1, reducing downstream channel phosphorylation. Removing a negative regulator would tend toward the opposite phenotype; this allele is not a simple knockout.

27. A knockout removes SLAC1 but other anion channels remain. Must ABA-induced closure be entirely abolished?

A. Yes, SLAC1 is the only possible route for solute release; B. No, parallel anion channels and kinases can preserve part of the response; C. Yes, all ABA biosynthesis stops; D. No, because SLAC1 normally transports potassium.

Answer: B. No, parallel anion channels and kinases can preserve part of the response.

The pathway includes alternative channels and signaling inputs, so the physiological phenotype must be measured. SLAC1 is an anion channel and its deletion does not logically eliminate ABA biosynthesis.

28. Two samples show equal Ψs when fully hydrated but different Ψs when dry. Is adjustment demonstrated?

A. Yes, because any lower Ψs proves synthesis; B. Yes, because equal full-turgor Ψs excludes passive concentration; C. No, unequal dehydration can explain the sampled values; D. No, because solutes never affect Ψs.

Answer: C. No, unequal dehydration can explain the sampled values.

The full-turgor comparison provides no evidence of persistent osmotic adjustment, although other measurements would be needed to rule out changes in solute compartmentation. The sampled values alone do not establish solute gain.

29. At Ψw −1.5 MPa, Ψs is −1.8 MPa in A and −2.1 MPa in B. Compare turgor.

Answer: A = 0.3 MPa; B = 0.6 MPa.

Subtract solute potential from total potential. B maintains higher pressure under the stated approximation.

30. A genotype has higher root-to-shoot ratio but both masses are lower than control. Does the ratio prove enhanced root growth?

A. Root mass must have increased; B. Shoot inhibition may be proportionally greater than root inhibition; C. Root water uptake must have doubled; D. Root angle must be steeper.

Answer: B. Shoot inhibition may be proportionally greater than root inhibition.

A higher ratio can arise while both absolute masses decrease. Root distribution and water uptake are independent measurements, not quantities recoverable from the ratio alone.

31. A line overexpressing a LEA protein survives longer in pots but grows much smaller. What comparison is needed?

A. Compare survival and injury at matched tissue dehydration, accounting for size and soil drying; B. Compare only survival after an identical number of days; C. Measure LEA transcript abundance alone; D. Compare only well-watered root mass.

Answer: A. Compare survival and injury at matched tissue dehydration, accounting for size and soil drying.

A smaller plant may avoid dehydration by consuming water more slowly. Matched tissue water status and functional injury data help distinguish protection from avoidance; a transcript or survival time alone cannot do so.

32. A falls from 18 to 12 while gs falls from 0.3 to 0.1. Compute intrinsic WUE before and after.

Answer: 60 and 120 µmol mol⁻¹.

A/gs doubles despite a decline in assimilation, so higher efficiency does not prove increased productivity.

33. After drought, A and Ci fall while dark-adapted Fv/Fm stays near control. Which limitation is initially consistent?

A. Complete PSII destruction; B. Increased Rubisco concentration as the sole cause; C. Initial stomatal diffusion limitation; D. No water-deficit response.

Answer: C. Initial stomatal diffusion limitation.

The combination of lower A and Ci with maintained maximum PSII efficiency is consistent with lower CO₂ supply. It does not exclude mesophyll or metabolic contributions, but it does not demonstrate PSII destruction.

34. After irrigation, RWC recovers but assimilation remains depressed. Give two plausible causes.

Answer: Persistent stomatal inhibition and nonstomatal metabolic or hydraulic impairment.

Hydration and functional recovery need not proceed at the same rate.

35. Native hydraulic conductivity is 3 units and restored maximum is 10. Calculate PLC.

Answer: 70%.

100 × (1 − 3/10) = 70.

36. At gs approximated by total conductance 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D = 2 kPa and P = 100 kPa, calculate E.

Answer: 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

0.15 × 2/100 = 0.003 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ under the simplified model.

37. A reporter promoter loses drought induction after DRE deletion but remains ABA responsive. Interpret.

A. DRE deletion removes every ABA receptor; B. DRE contributes to dehydration-induced regulation while another input preserves ABA responsiveness; C. DRE is a membrane ion channel; D. Drought cannot influence transcription.

Answer: B. DRE contributes to dehydration-induced regulation while another input preserves ABA responsiveness.

The reporter separates regulatory inputs. Other promoter elements may mediate ABA induction, and a reporter result does not establish the complete native-gene regulatory network.

38. High VPD reduces gs in wet soil. Is root drying necessary for this response?

A. Root drying is necessary in all cases; B. VPD can only change photosynthesis, not stomatal conductance; C. The wet soil proves that ABA cannot participate; D. Root drying is not necessary for the observed response.

Answer: D. Root drying is not necessary for the observed response.

High atmospheric demand can alter leaf water relations and ABA regulation without soil drying. The observation does not exclude a hydraulic or hormone contribution.

39. Proline doubles in the more severely injured genotype. Is the metabolite harmful?

A. Proline is proven toxic; B. Proline is proven protective; C. Causality is unresolved without controlling water status and manipulating proline; D. Proline concentration directly measures the amount of dead tissue.

Answer: C. Causality is unresolved without controlling water status and manipulating proline.

Severe stress can cause increased proline independently of its protective role. Comparing function at matched dehydration and perturbing proline metabolism are stronger causal tests.

40. A plant recovers through new xylem formation after severe drought. Does this prove embolized conduits refilled?

A. Yes, because all new xylem is derived from refilled vessels; B. No, newly formed conduits can restore transport while older vessels remain embolized; C. Yes, because water always enters embolized vessels under tension; D. No, because xylem does not conduct water.

Answer: B. No, newly formed conduits can restore transport while older vessels remain embolized.

Hydraulic recovery can occur by replacement rather than refilling. Establishing refilling requires evidence from the same formerly gas-filled conduits.

State SET and integrated practice

41. Assertion–reason: Drought avoidance can improve survival; because lower transpiration can preserve tissue hydration. Assess.

Answer: Both statements are correct and the reason explains the assertion.

Water conservation delays tissue dehydration when supply is restricted.

42. Assertion–reason: All plants accumulate glycine betaine during drought; because glycine betaine is a compatible solute. Assess.

Answer: Assertion false; reason true.

Ability to accumulate glycine betaine varies across species.

43. A pressure chamber balancing pressure is 1.25 MPa. Estimate leaf Ψw under standard assumptions.

Answer: Approximately −1.25 MPa.

The applied positive balancing pressure approximates the magnitude of the original negative potential.

44. A drought-sensitive crop survives a trial but seed yield falls sharply. Has the trial shown yield resistance?

Answer: No.

Survival and yield are distinct outcomes; reproduction can remain impaired.

45. Name an experiment that can test whether a drying root compartment affects the shoot.

Answer: A controlled split-root treatment.

Compare a half-dried root system with well-watered controls while measuring shoot hydration and signaling.

46. Why is dry mass subtracted when calculating RWC?

Answer: To isolate water mass.

Dry solids contribute to fresh and turgid mass but not tissue water.

47. A crop finishes flowering before terminal drought. Which strategy is illustrated?

Answer: Drought escape.

Phenological timing avoids the vulnerable stage during stress.

48. What control is important in testing exogenous glycine betaine?

Answer: A matched vehicle and appropriate osmotic control.

These distinguish the compound's effects from treatment medium and osmotic changes.

49. Why might a transcript increase without a corresponding increase in hydraulic conductance?

Answer: Translation, localization, channel gating or other resistances may limit flow.

Transcript abundance is an indirect measure of function.

50. Why is gradual drying sometimes more survivable than rapid drying?

Answer: It allows time for acclimatory gene expression and protective metabolite accumulation.

This benefit is context-dependent and does not remove the physical limits of water loss.

Mixed-format exam practice

51. Which observation most directly supports active osmotic adjustment? (A) Lower fresh mass (B) Lower Ψs in a wilted leaf (C) Lower Ψs at comparable full turgor (D) Reduced stomatal conductance.

Answer: C.

A and B can arise from passive water loss; D is a regulatory response, not evidence of additional solutes. C supports solute accumulation after controlling hydration.

52. Three hypothetical leaves have A and gs pairs of (12, 0.24), (8, 0.08), and (4, 0.02), with A in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gs in mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Which has highest intrinsic WUE and which has highest assimilation?

Answer: Leaf 3 has the highest A/gs (200 µmol mol⁻¹); leaf 1 has the highest assimilation (12).

The ratios are 50, 100 and 200 respectively. Greater intrinsic efficiency is not equivalent to higher carbon uptake.

53. Select all supported statements for the vulnerability figure: I. A more negative P50 indicates greater resistance under comparable conditions. II. P50 proves that no conductivity is lost before that threshold. III. Equal P50 values imply identical vulnerability curves. IV. Operating pressure is relevant to injury risk.

Answer: I and IV only.

A threshold summarizes the curve; conductivity may decline earlier and curves with the same midpoint can have different slopes.

54. A plant has higher proline concentration per gram fresh mass under drought but the same proline per gram dry mass. Which explanation is compatible? (A) Dehydration concentrated proline (B) Proline biosynthesis must have doubled (C) Proline oxidation ceased (D) Both measures prove tolerance.

Answer: A.

The changing fresh-mass denominator can explain the result; none of the other conclusions follows without flux or functional data.

55. In a hypothetical comparison, ABA alone does not inhibit purified PP2C, PYL alone has little effect, and ABA plus PYL strongly inhibits PP2C. Which model is supported?

Answer: ABA-bound PYL mediates inhibition of PP2C.

The combination is required in this simplified experiment. The assay supports receptor-dependent phosphatase inhibition but does not by itself establish the intact-plant phenotype.
