1. The biological problem: when water supply cannot match demand

A plant may be growing in soil that contains considerable water and nevertheless lose water faster than its roots can replace it. This observation provides a useful starting point for drought physiology: the presence of water is not equivalent to the availability of water at the rate required by living tissue. A leaf exposed to sunlight and moving dry air evaporates water through microscopic pores called stomata. The resulting tension draws liquid through the xylem, while roots obtain replacement water from the soil. The entire sequence can maintain a steady supply only when the pathways remain conductive and the soil can provide enough water. Water deficit develops when the balance between supply and loss becomes unfavorable.

Drought describes an environmental shortage of available water, whereas dehydration describes a reduction in the water content of an organism or tissue. The first is a cause or environmental condition; the second is a physiological state. A well-watered plant can become transiently dehydrated during an afternoon of intense evaporative demand. Conversely, a plant with effective stomatal control and access to deep moist soil may remain relatively hydrated during a period without rainfall. It is therefore important to ask whether a measurement describes the environment, the water status of the plant, its regulatory response, or the injury that has followed.

Water serves more functions than supplying electrons for photosynthesis. It is the solvent in which metabolites and ions move, the medium for enzymatic reactions, the source of turgor pressure and the means of long-distance transport. Water absorbed by a root ultimately supplies evaporative cooling as well as the small fraction incorporated into biomass. When cellular water content decreases, the concentration of dissolved compounds rises, the vacuole contracts and the plasma membrane experiences mechanical changes. These disturbances can initially be reversible. If dehydration proceeds too far, membrane organization, enzyme function and hydraulic integrity can be compromised. Stress does not necessarily imply damage; a regulated response can prevent damage even while growth is temporarily inhibited.

A young expanding leaf illustrates why growth is often among the first functions to decline. Its cells enlarge when water enters and the walls yield under turgor. If the pressure inside a cell falls below the threshold needed for irreversible wall extension, further expansion slows even though respiration and photosynthesis have not stopped. The leaf may therefore become smaller without experiencing lethal injury. The reduction in expanding surface area also decreases later evaporative demand. This immediate consequence of water limitation can contribute to longer-term conservation, but it must not automatically be interpreted as a genetically programmed adaptation. A controlled experiment must separate the physical constraint from regulated growth responses.

The meaning of drought resistance depends on the biological endpoint. A genotype that remains alive by closing stomata for weeks may have poor grain yield because the reproductive organs receive less carbon. Another genotype may use more water early, sustain assimilation and set seed before a terminal dry period. The latter can produce a better yield despite losing more water during part of the season. Drought escape refers to completing sensitive life-cycle stages before severe water shortage; avoidance refers to keeping tissue water status relatively favorable; tolerance refers to maintaining viable or functional tissue despite unfavorable water status. These mechanisms overlap in real plants. They are not interchangeable measures of a single universal trait.

Distinguishing environmental drought, tissue dehydration and irreversible injury becomes especially important when comparing species. A mesophytic herb, a desert succulent and a resurrection plant can experience the same atmospheric conditions while maintaining very different internal water contents. A succulent stores water and reduces the rate of depletion. A resurrection plant can tolerate much more extensive cellular drying. Both may survive, but the underlying mechanisms are fundamentally different. The following sections trace these mechanisms from the physics of water to molecular signaling, cellular protection and recovery.

2. Water potential, soil drying and hydraulic supply

Water potential expresses the chemical potential of water relative to pure water at a specified reference state, divided by the partial molar volume of water. It has dimensions of pressure and is commonly reported in megapascals (MPa). Under ordinary conditions the reference potential of pure water at atmospheric pressure is zero. Dissolved solutes lower the chemical potential of water, so the osmotic or solute potential is negative. Positive hydrostatic pressure raises water potential; tension can make the pressure component negative. Water moves spontaneously from a higher water potential to a lower water potential when a suitable pathway is available.

For a hydrated living cell in a simple laboratory situation, the approximation Ψw = Ψs + Ψp is useful. Here Ψw is total water potential, Ψs is osmotic potential and Ψp is pressure potential. The approximation neglects gravitational and matric contributions where they are small. Matric potential describes the effect of water binding to surfaces, which is especially important in unsaturated soil. Gravity contributes approximately 0.0098 MPa per metre of height relative to a reference level. In a short cell-level calculation that contribution is negligible, but it can matter in tall trees. Do not assume that the turgor pressure of a living mesophyll cell and the pressure in an adjacent xylem vessel must have the same sign.

Consider two living cells, one with Ψs = −0.80 MPa and Ψp = +0.40 MPa, and the other with Ψs = −0.65 MPa and Ψp = +0.10 MPa. Their total potentials are −0.40 and −0.55 MPa. If a permeable connection exists, water tends to move from the first to the second cell, despite the first cell having the more negative osmotic potential. This example shows why comparisons of solute concentration alone can give the wrong prediction. Solute potential and pressure potential must be considered together. If the water potentials become equal, net flow vanishes even though individual water molecules continue moving in both directions.

For dilute ideal solutions, the van't Hoff approximation is Ψs = −iCRT. The factor i represents the number of effective dissolved particles produced by each formula unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. When C is in mol L⁻¹, R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and T is in kelvin, the result is in MPa. At 298 K, a 0.20 M solution of a non-dissociating solute has Ψs ≈ −0.496 MPa. Real cytoplasm contains many solutes, ions and macromolecules; their activities and compartmentation limit the accuracy of the ideal approximation. The equation is a model for reasoning, not a direct assay of a leaf.

As a soil dries, its larger pores empty first. Water remains in smaller pores and films on mineral particles, and increasingly negative matric potential is needed to describe its state. At the same time, continuity and thickness of the water films decrease, so unsaturated hydraulic conductivity can fall steeply. Two soils with the same gravimetric water content may therefore supply roots very differently. A clay-rich soil can retain substantial water at a strongly negative potential, while a sandy soil loses readily drainable water quickly. Field capacity and permanent wilting point are useful operational descriptors, not universal thresholds for every species and soil.

A simplified steady-state hydraulic relationship is J = K(Ψsource − Ψsink), where J is the flow rate, K is the conductance of the specified pathway and Ψsource and Ψsink are water potentials at its boundaries. Units of conductance must be consistent with the units of flow and pressure; for example, mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ for a leaf-area-normalized pathway. If soil potential is −0.30 MPa, leaf potential is −1.10 MPa and K is 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹, the predicted flow is 1.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Halving conductance halves the predicted flow at the same gradient. In a real plant, conductance can change as dehydration advances; storage and time-dependent changes also violate the steady-state approximation.

Water passes through a series of resistances in soil, the root–soil interface, roots, xylem and leaves. If one component becomes very resistive, an increase in another component's conductance cannot fully compensate. Drying soil may shrink away from a root, producing a gap or poor contact. Fine roots may lose hydraulic function, while living cortical membranes can change their permeability. Within the leaf, water moves from veins through tissues toward evaporating surfaces. The resulting distribution of water potentials is not uniform. A bulk measurement of leaf water status cannot directly reveal every microscopic gradient.

Vapor pressure deficit (VPD) represents the difference between the vapor pressure at saturation and actual vapor pressure. For transpiration the relevant deficit is between the moist leaf interior and the surrounding air, using leaf temperature to estimate saturation vapor pressure. At 25 °C saturation vapor pressure is about 3.17 kPa. If air and leaf are both at that temperature and relative humidity is 50%, the deficit is about 1.59 kPa. At 30 °C, saturation vapor pressure is about 4.24 kPa, so the same relative humidity gives about 2.12 kPa. Thus unchanged relative humidity does not mean unchanged evaporative demand. Leaf warming during stomatal closure can further change the deficit.

In a simplified diffusion model, transpiration per unit area E ≈ gwD/P, where gw is total conductance to water vapor in mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D is leaf-to-air VPD and P is atmospheric pressure in the same units as D. At gw = 0.20 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D = 2 kPa and P = 100 kPa, E is approximately 0.004 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ or 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Stomatal and boundary-layer resistances both affect the effective conductance. If stomatal closure decreases gw to 0.10 while VPD doubles to 4 kPa, the model predicts the same E. An observation of unchanged transpiration does not prove that stomata failed to respond.

The distinction between soil and atmospheric drought is experimentally useful. Researchers can independently vary soil moisture and VPD while controlling light, temperature, carbon dioxide and plant age. A dry atmosphere may induce rapid changes in stomatal conductance without a major change in measured bulk soil moisture. Progressive soil drought can reduce water supply even when the atmosphere is relatively humid. Under field conditions these factors commonly occur together. Their interactions mean that measurements at one time of day should not be treated as a complete description of drought severity.

3. Hydraulic signals and abscisic acid

A plant cannot wait until every cell is severely dehydrated before responding. Because the root–shoot hydraulic pathway connects distant tissues, changing water availability can be transmitted through changes in water potential, tension and cellular hydration. A change in leaf hydration or root hydraulic conductance may occur faster than the transport of a newly synthesized regulatory molecule over the same distance. Such changes provide information that can influence stomata, growth and hormone metabolism. Chemical signaling supplements rather than necessarily replaces this hydraulic connection.

Abscisic acid, commonly abbreviated ABA, is a central regulator of drought responses in flowering plants. Its concentration depends on biosynthesis, catabolism, reversible conjugation, release from conjugated pools and transport between tissues. ABA is synthesized from carotenoid-derived precursors; the cleavage of 9-cis epoxycarotenoids by NCED enzymes is an important regulated step. Later reactions generate the active hormone. Under dehydration, ABA accumulation often results from increased synthesis together with other changes in metabolism. The occurrence of a drought-induced ABA signal should not be taken to mean that ABA is produced exclusively by roots. Roots, leaves, vascular tissues and guard cells can contribute, depending on species and conditions.

An early model emphasized ABA made in drying roots and transported through xylem to leaves. Split-root experiments remain informative: one root compartment can be dried while the other continues receiving water. If the shoot remains relatively hydrated yet stomatal conductance falls, the result suggests a root-associated signal beyond a simple measurement of bulk shoot water deficit. However, such an experiment does not by itself identify the signal as ABA. Local hydraulic changes, altered nutrient transport, xylem sap chemistry and shoot ABA synthesis may also contribute. Direct hormone measurements, genetically impaired ABA biosynthesis, controlled ABA application and carefully selected graft combinations help distinguish alternatives.

An ABA-deficient mutant and an ABA-insensitive mutant provide a classical logical contrast. The first has an impaired ability to produce sufficient active hormone but may retain the receptors and downstream machinery. If external ABA restores stomatal closure under suitable conditions, the result is consistent with a biosynthetic defect. The second may accumulate ABA but fail to respond normally because signaling is impaired. Exogenous ABA would not necessarily rescue that phenotype. Real mutants may have partial defects, redundant paralogs or additional developmental differences, so the comparison is strongest when the genetic lesion and physiological conditions are known.

The hormone signal is not simply a measure of total leaf ABA. The concentration experienced by specific receptors depends on tissue distribution and transport. In addition, response intensity depends on receptor abundance and downstream regulatory state. Two leaves containing similar amounts of ABA may exhibit different stomatal conductance if one differs in sensitivity or hydraulic limitation. Drought physiology therefore requires the distinction between amount of a hormone and responsiveness to that hormone. This same distinction is familiar from other hormone systems introduced in Unit 6.D.

Drought is accompanied by changes in calcium signaling, reactive oxygen species, membrane properties and protein phosphorylation. These are interconnected signals rather than isolated parallel pathways with identical effects in every tissue. The next section focuses on the ABA pathway because it supplies a clear causal example of how water deficit becomes a measurable physiological response, not because all drought responses require ABA.

4. From ABA perception to stomatal closure

A stoma consists of a pore bounded by two specialized guard cells. Their walls and shape convert changes in turgor into changes in pore aperture. In many dicots, hydrated guard cells become curved as their turgor rises and the pore opens; as turgor decreases, the pore narrows. Grasses have dumbbell-shaped guard cells and associated subsidiary cells that exchange ions and water with them. The mechanical organization differs, but regulated solute movement and water redistribution remain central.

In a favorable environment, activation of the plasma membrane proton ATPase promotes proton extrusion and makes the cell interior electrically more negative. This hyperpolarization favors inward potassium transport through suitable channels. Potassium, counterions such as chloride or malate, and organic osmolytes contribute to lowering guard-cell osmotic potential. Water then enters along a water-potential gradient and raises turgor. Light, carbon dioxide concentration, circadian state and water availability influence opening. A channel is not a pump: the ATPase uses ATP to establish an electrochemical gradient, while channels allow ions to move in directions determined by their electrochemical driving forces.

The ABA core module consists of soluble PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, clade A protein phosphatases of type 2C (PP2Cs) and SnRK2 protein kinases. In the absence of substantial ABA signaling, PP2Cs restrain the activity of relevant SnRK2 kinases by dephosphorylation. A major guard-cell kinase is OST1, also called SnRK2.6 in Arabidopsis. When ABA binds to its receptor, the resulting receptor complex interacts with a PP2C and inhibits the phosphatase. This releases inhibition of the kinase. The logic is a double negative: ABA inhibits an inhibitor through the receptor, permitting kinase-dependent phosphorylation.

Activated OST1 can phosphorylate the slow anion channel SLAC1, increasing anion efflux from the guard cell. Other anion channels and calcium-dependent kinases contribute in appropriate contexts. Efflux of negative charge depolarizes the plasma membrane, favoring outward potassium flux through channels such as GORK. Inward potassium transport and proton-pump-driven opening are also opposed during closure. As osmotically active solutes leave the guard cells or are redistributed, their osmotic potential becomes less negative. Water exits, turgor decreases and the aperture narrows. SLAC1 is an anion channel, not a potassium channel or a water pump.

Reactive oxygen production by NADPH oxidases can participate downstream of ABA signaling. Hydrogen peroxide and calcium signals can reinforce or modify channel activity through calcium-dependent kinases. The network is not a simple linear chain in every cell: parallel branches, feedback and partial redundancy mean that removal of one component may attenuate rather than completely eliminate closure. A biochemical demonstration that a kinase phosphorylates a channel is valuable, but determining whether the phosphorylation matters in the intact plant requires physiological measurements and appropriate genetic controls.

A drought-stressed guard cell presents an apparent paradox. Most plant cells benefit from maintaining turgor through osmotic adjustment, but closure requires guard cells to lose turgor relative to surrounding tissues. The paradox disappears when cell identity and function are considered. A mesophyll or growing root cell can benefit from retaining solutes; a guard cell must change its solute content dynamically to regulate the pore. The organism can therefore conserve water precisely because a specialized cell population relinquishes turgor in a controlled way.

The receptors were identified through complementary approaches involving chemical genetics and protein interactions in 2009. Park and colleagues used pyrabactin responsiveness to identify PYR1-related ABA signaling, while Ma and colleagues identified regulators of PP2C activity as ABA sensors. The key advance was to connect hormone perception with inhibition of a negative regulator. The proposed model predicts that an ABA-bound receptor should inhibit the phosphatase and that an uninhibited kinase should phosphorylate appropriate downstream substrates. Protein interaction and activity assays test these steps independently; stomatal responses in receptor, phosphatase and kinase mutants test their importance in living tissue. Receptor families show redundancy, so a single receptor knockout need not reproduce a severe hormone-insensitive phenotype.

A dominant ABA-insensitive PP2C allele can remain capable of inhibiting downstream signaling despite ABA exposure. Its phenotype differs from a simple loss of a negative regulator, which may instead enhance sensitivity. In a simplified epistasis experiment, removal of a positive kinase can block the effect of removing an upstream negative regulator if that kinase is indispensable for the measured response. Actual ABA networks contain multiple PP2Cs and SnRK2s, so predictions must specify which component and phenotype are being examined. This is a useful example of why mutation type is as important as pathway position.

Stomatal closure reduces transpirational water loss but also increases resistance to carbon dioxide entry. In moderate drought, photosynthesis may initially become limited by stomatal diffusion rather than a loss of the photosynthetic apparatus. During stronger or prolonged drought, mesophyll conductance, enzyme activity, photochemical function and carbon metabolism may also become limiting. A low assimilation rate alone cannot identify the causal limitation. The interpretation requires simultaneous measurements of conductance, carbon assimilation and other indicators of photosynthetic function.

5. Longer-term molecular acclimation

Ion-channel regulation can change a stomatal aperture within minutes. Longer drought exposures require changes in metabolism and gene expression that develop on different time scales. An existing enzyme can be activated rapidly by phosphorylation, whereas synthesizing a protective protein requires transcription, RNA processing, translation and accumulation of functional protein. Distinguishing these processes helps explain why pretreatment or gradual drying can alter the response to a later stress.

ABA-activated SnRK2 kinases can phosphorylate transcription factors of the AREB/ABF group. These basic leucine zipper proteins regulate genes through ABA-responsive elements, often abbreviated ABREs, in suitable promoters. The resulting expression changes can include proteins associated with water limitation, osmotic protection and signaling feedback. The presence of an ABRE-like sequence does not by itself establish that the associated gene is ABA responsive. Promoter context, transcription factor availability, chromatin state and additional cis elements influence actual transcription.

A complementary pathway involves dehydration-responsive element binding proteins (DREBs), members of the AP2/ERF transcription factor family. DREB2-related factors are especially associated with dehydration and heat responses in Arabidopsis, whereas DREB1/CBF factors are classically associated with cold acclimation. These are useful tendencies rather than mutually exclusive assignments. A DRE/CRT element in a drought-induced promoter can support transcriptional regulation that is not dependent solely on ABA. ABA-independent does not mean independent of all signaling, calcium, phosphorylation or other environmental inputs.

Promoter–reporter constructs provide a way to evaluate regulatory logic. Consider a promoter driving a reporter enzyme in otherwise similar plants. If deleting a DRE strongly reduces dehydration-induced reporter expression but leaves ABA-induced expression largely intact, a reasonable hypothesis is that the DRE contributes to the dehydration response while another element mediates the ABA response. A reciprocal mutation of an ABRE can test the alternative branch. A constitutive reporter or normalization to an unaffected reference helps control differences in sample amount. A reporter in an isolated promoter context does not necessarily reproduce every feature of native gene regulation.

Transcript accumulation should not be confused with protein accumulation or biological function. A transcript may rise substantially while translation is suppressed or the corresponding protein is unstable. To infer that a drought-induced gene improves survival, compare an appropriate loss-of-function line with a complemented line and the wild type at comparable tissue hydration. If an overexpressing line survives simply because it is smaller and uses less water, it may have acquired dehydration avoidance rather than greater cellular tolerance. Measuring soil water depletion and plant size helps expose that alternative.

Acclimation also involves feedback that limits the duration and cost of a response. Some ABA-responsive genes encode negative regulators of signaling, and the hormone can be inactivated when stress declines. Persistent activation of stress pathways under favorable conditions may restrict growth unnecessarily. A regulatory system that activates protection when needed and reverses it after recovery is consequently different from a constitutively activated system. This distinction matters when considering crop improvement, where stable yield under fluctuating conditions is often a more meaningful objective than survival under a single extreme challenge.

6. Osmotic adjustment and compatible solutes

A cell losing water normally experiences an increase in solute concentration even if it synthesizes or imports no new solute. Imagine a compartment containing a fixed amount of dissolved substance. Reducing its water volume from 1.0 to 0.8 arbitrary units raises its concentration by a factor of 1.25. Its osmotic potential becomes more negative. This passive concentration effect accompanies dehydration and should not be mistaken for a regulated accumulation of solutes. Active osmotic adjustment means that the cell accumulates additional osmotically active solutes beyond the concentration effect of water loss.

The pressure component of water potential explains the physiological benefit. Suppose a cell at Ψw = −1.20 MPa has Ψs = −1.40 MPa. Its turgor is +0.20 MPa. If an adjusted cell at the same Ψw has Ψs = −1.80 MPa, its turgor is +0.60 MPa. Provided the wall and membrane remain functional, the second cell can maintain greater turgor under the specified water potential. The plant has not created water or violated thermodynamics. It has changed its chemical potential relationships by accumulating solutes, allowing a different balance between water content and pressure.

The turgor loss point is the water potential at which a tissue's turgor approaches zero. It is often estimated with a pressure–volume curve. After establishing the fully hydrated mass and a reference water status, the researcher records water potential as the tissue dehydrates in steps. The nonlinear portion of a suitable transformed curve reflects both pressure and osmotic components, whereas the post-turgor-loss region permits estimation of osmotic properties under specified assumptions. Extrapolation and the apoplastic water fraction require care. Comparisons between treatments should use consistent rehydration procedures, leaf age and measurement conditions because these affect the derived parameters.

Osmotic potential measured at comparable full turgor is often more informative about adjustment than a measurement on a dehydrated sample alone. If a stressed sample is more concentrated only because it lost water, rehydration toward its original water volume should reduce that difference. A persistently lower osmotic potential at comparable hydration supports solute accumulation. It is not conclusive if rehydration leads to solute leakage, if tissues differ in development or if cell compartment volumes change. Measuring total solute amount per dry mass or per relevant compartment provides complementary information.

Compatible solutes are low-molecular-weight compounds that can accumulate to relatively high concentrations without severely disrupting ordinary biochemical function. Examples include proline, sucrose, raffinose-family sugars and glycine betaine in species capable of accumulating it. Inorganic ions can also contribute to osmotic potential, especially when stored in vacuoles, but excessive concentrations in the cytosol can inhibit enzymes. Compartmentation permits the plant to combine vacuolar osmotic contributions with cytoplasmic protection.

Proline, an amino acid, often accumulates during drought. Its production commonly involves glutamate conversion through pyrroline-5-carboxylate, with P5CS catalyzing early steps and P5CR reducing the intermediate to proline. Proline catabolism involves mitochondrial enzymes, including proline dehydrogenase, and can become important during recovery. Regulation of both synthesis and degradation determines the final pool. Proline may contribute to osmotic potential, redox metabolism and protection of macromolecules, but its quantitative osmotic contribution depends on its concentration in the relevant compartment. A high proline concentration can also be a marker of stress severity rather than evidence that a plant is better adapted.

Glycine betaine is a zwitterionic quaternary ammonium compound. In accumulating plants, choline can be oxidized through betaine aldehyde to glycine betaine. It can stabilize protein complexes and membranes and contribute to osmotic regulation. Many species do not naturally accumulate large quantities of it, so this response is not universal. Exogenous supply experiments require controls for uptake, vehicle and osmotic effects, and transgenic accumulation experiments must distinguish a protective effect from unrelated differences in growth.

Soluble sugars have multiple functions. Sucrose supplies carbon to sinks, while sugars released by starch breakdown can maintain metabolism during restricted photosynthesis. Their accumulation can lower osmotic potential and stabilize membranes or proteins during severe water limitation. However, a high sugar concentration may arise because growth and utilization have declined more than synthesis, not because synthesis increased. Measurements of photosynthetic carbon fixation, starch pools, soluble sugars and biomass over time are needed to discriminate among these possibilities.

Osmotic adjustment has costs. Carbon skeletons, reducing power and nitrogen invested in protective solutes cannot simultaneously be used for all other functions. Maintaining turgor may help a plant continue growing roots into moist soil or sustain photosynthesis during intermittent drought. In a shallow soil facing terminal drought, continued water use might deplete remaining supplies too early. Therefore a beneficial cellular mechanism does not automatically maximize seasonal crop yield. Its value depends on the timing of water shortage, root access, allocation and reproductive stage.

7. Root architecture, aquaporins and whole-plant avoidance

The amount of water available to a plant depends partly on how roots explore soil. Deep roots may reach moist layers after the surface has dried, whereas a dense shallow root system can exploit small rainfall events before that water evaporates. Neither architecture is best in all environments. Root growth is constrained by mechanical impedance, soil oxygen, nutrient distribution, temperature and carbon supply. A genotype selected in a deep uniform soil may perform differently in a shallow stony soil.

The root-to-shoot dry mass ratio is often reported as an indicator of allocation. If shoots decline in growth more rapidly than roots, the ratio may increase even if absolute root biomass is smaller than in well-watered plants. For example, a control with 4 g roots and 12 g shoots has a ratio of 0.33; a stressed plant with 3 g roots and 6 g shoots has a ratio of 0.50. The second plant has a higher ratio but less root mass. Therefore a ratio must be interpreted together with absolute biomass, root length distribution and the water actually acquired.

Water crosses root tissues through apoplastic and cell-to-cell pathways. Aquaporins are membrane proteins that increase permeability to water and, for certain family members, additional small molecules. Plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs) and tonoplast intrinsic proteins (TIPs) are important plant classes. Aquaporins do not hydrolyze ATP to drive water uphill against water potential. Instead they increase passive water permeability. Their abundance, localization and gating influence how rapidly water moves when an appropriate driving force exists.

During drought, aquaporin expression and activity can increase or decrease depending on isoform, organ, species, stress intensity and duration. A decrease in some water permeabilities can restrict unwanted water loss, while increased permeability in other tissues may facilitate uptake or rehydration. Phosphorylation, pH, calcium and trafficking can regulate particular channels. An increase in messenger RNA for an aquaporin is therefore not sufficient evidence of increased root hydraulic conductance. Channel protein abundance, membrane localization, activity and other resistances in the root all influence the whole-organ result.

A root pressure probe or hydraulic flow measurement can help determine whether changes in membrane properties affect water transport. Researchers must control the driving force, temperature and root size. If a drought-treated root passes less water than a control at the same imposed pressure difference, its hydraulic conductance is lower under those conditions. This does not uniquely identify aquaporin closure because anatomical changes, root–soil contact and other pathways can also affect flow. Genetic comparisons and recovery measurements help attribute the cause more precisely.

Shoots contribute to avoidance through reduced leaf expansion, leaf rolling, altered leaf angle, increased cuticular resistance and stomatal control. Leaf rolling can reduce the exposed surface and alter the local boundary layer, but its effect depends on leaf anatomy and environmental conditions. Cuticular wax can decrease non-stomatal water loss, especially when stomata are mostly closed. A plant with an effective cuticle may conserve water late in drought when further stomatal closure provides little additional benefit.

Stomatal density is a developmental property distinct from aperture, which can change rapidly. A leaf that develops with fewer stomata may have a lower maximum conductance, but pore size, opening dynamics, boundary layer and total leaf area also matter. A smaller number of pores does not guarantee lower transpiration if the remaining pores are larger or remain more open. The whole-plant water budget combines conductance per area with the total transpiring surface and the duration of exposure.

8. Protective proteins, membranes and desiccation tolerance

As cells dehydrate, their internal environment becomes increasingly crowded. Molecules that were separated by water may interact in unfavorable ways. Proteins can lose their normal conformations or aggregate; membranes can change their organization as hydration and lipid packing change. Severe drying imposes mechanical stresses because cell volume contracts while the wall constrains tissue geometry. These effects explain why maintaining turgor is only one aspect of drought resistance. A cell must also protect its molecular structures when its water content declines.

Late embryogenesis abundant proteins, or LEA proteins, were first associated with the accumulation of proteins during late seed development. Many are also induced in vegetative tissues during water limitation. Dehydrins are a group within the LEA family, commonly described as group 2 LEA proteins. Many dehydrins are hydrophilic and intrinsically disordered under hydrated conditions. Conserved motifs, including lysine-rich K segments, can contribute to interactions with membranes or other molecules. The lack of a single stable folded structure does not mean that a protein lacks function.

Proposed and experimentally supported LEA functions include reducing protein aggregation, stabilizing membranes and cooperating with protective sugars in dehydrated systems. Their effects vary among individual proteins and experimental conditions. A test in which a purified LEA protein preserves the activity of a reporter enzyme during drying supplies evidence of molecular protection in vitro. It does not alone demonstrate that the same protein increases crop yield. A plant-level hypothesis additionally requires appropriate expression, localization and physiological measurements.

The antioxidant systems introduced in the preceding node also matter during drought. When stomatal closure restricts carbon dioxide entry, the use of reducing equivalents in carbon fixation may decline while light absorption continues. This imbalance can favor reactive oxygen formation. Photoprotective energy dissipation, antioxidant enzymes and nonenzymatic antioxidants help limit damage. Their contribution should be interpreted alongside stomatal limitation and tissue water status, not treated as an alternative explanation for every decline in photosynthesis.

Desiccation tolerance is the exceptional ability to survive extensive loss of cellular water and resume function after rehydration. Many orthodox seeds acquire this capacity during maturation; recalcitrant seeds, in contrast, are sensitive to substantial drying. Vegetative tissues of most crop plants cannot survive comparable water loss. Resurrection plants such as some species of Craterostigma can withstand extreme dehydration of vegetative tissues and subsequently recover. Some retain much of their photosynthetic apparatus while others dismantle parts of it during drying and rebuild it during rehydration. These strategies illustrate the distinction between avoiding dehydration and surviving it.

Protective sugars and proteins can cooperate as water is removed. In very dry cells, suitable mixtures of solutes can form a glass-like amorphous matrix that greatly restricts molecular mobility. This vitrified state can reduce some damaging interactions, but it does not make metabolism continue normally without water. Extreme desiccation survival commonly involves metabolic quiescence and restoration of activity after rehydration. Glass formation also depends on composition, temperature and residual moisture.

Desiccation tolerance is not equivalent to the ability to withstand unlimited drought. A plant surviving one drying event must still have sufficient reserves and intact meristems to rehydrate and resume growth. Drying rate can determine whether protective responses develop in time. Rehydration itself imposes stress through rapid changes in volume, membrane organization and reactive oxygen metabolism. Successful survival therefore includes both preparation for drying and controlled restoration of physiological activity.

9. Photosynthesis, growth and water-use efficiency

The conservation of water by stomatal closure has a direct cost because the same pores admit carbon dioxide. Net carbon assimilation A is commonly expressed in micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second. Stomatal conductance to water vapor gs is often expressed in moles of H₂O per square metre per second. An instantaneous intrinsic water-use efficiency can be defined as A/gs, with units such as micromoles of CO₂ per mole of H₂O. It should not be confused with the actual transpiration efficiency A/E, because transpiration also depends on VPD and boundary-layer conditions.

Consider a control leaf with A = 15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gs = 0.30 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Its intrinsic efficiency is 50 µmol mol⁻¹. A drought-treated leaf with A = 9 and gs = 0.10 has intrinsic efficiency 90 µmol mol⁻¹. The second leaf fixes less carbon per unit time even though the ratio has increased. If a student concludes that higher efficiency necessarily means higher crop yield, the conclusion ignores carbon assimilation, leaf area, seasonal water availability and reproduction.

The intercellular carbon dioxide concentration Ci can be informative when interpreted with gas-exchange data. During early stomatal limitation, decreasing conductance can lower Ci and A. If biochemical capacity is also depressed, Ci may remain stable or increase despite low A because carbon dioxide consumption has fallen. Calculated Ci can be misleading when stomatal closure is patchy or conductance is very low, and a stable Ci does not prove that mesophyll conductance is unchanged. Measurements across several carbon dioxide concentrations, together with fluorescence and physiological controls, provide stronger evidence.

Chlorophyll fluorescence offers additional information about photosystem II function. The maximum quantum efficiency of dark-adapted PSII is often expressed as Fv/Fm = (Fm − Fo)/Fm, where Fo is minimal fluorescence and Fm is maximal fluorescence in the dark-adapted state. An illustrative change from Fo = 200 and Fm = 1000 to Fo = 300 and Fm = 800 reduces Fv/Fm from 0.80 to 0.625. The lower ratio suggests impaired maximum PSII efficiency under the measurement conditions. An unchanged ratio during moderate drought does not prove that assimilation is normal; diffusion can limit carbon fixation even when this fluorescence measure remains high.

C4 photosynthesis concentrates carbon dioxide around Rubisco and can support relatively high carbon gain at lower stomatal conductance under favorable warm, bright conditions. CAM plants separate major carbon uptake and use in time, often opening stomata at night when evaporative demand is lower. These mechanisms can improve water-use efficiency relative to other strategies in appropriate environments, but they impose metabolic or storage costs and do not confer universal drought immunity. C3 species also differ widely in rooting, stomatal behavior and phenology.

Drought during reproduction can have particularly large consequences. Pollen development, fertilization, seed formation and grain filling depend on water relations and assimilate supply. A plant may survive a dry period while losing much of its potential seed production. The timing of a drought treatment relative to developmental stage is therefore essential information. A comparison based only on seedling survival is not sufficient to predict reproductive performance in the field.

10. Hydraulic injury and recovery after rewatering

Xylem vessels and tracheids carry water under tension during transpiration. As drought progresses, water pressure in the xylem can become increasingly negative. Gas can enter water-filled conduits through pathways associated with pits or other vulnerabilities, producing embolism that obstructs flow. Cavitation is the formation or expansion of a gas phase in the liquid system; embolism describes the resulting gas blockage in a conduit. Loss of functional conduits reduces hydraulic conductance, making it harder to deliver water to leaves.

Hydraulic vulnerability is often described using percentage loss of conductivity (PLC). If Kinitial denotes conductivity measured in the native state and Kmax is an estimate of maximum conductivity after appropriate restoration, PLC = 100 × (1 − Kinitial/Kmax). For illustrative values of 2 and 5 in identical units, PLC is 60%. The parameter P50 denotes the water potential or corresponding xylem pressure at which a vulnerability curve reaches 50% loss of conductivity. A more negative P50 generally indicates greater embolism resistance under comparable methods, but values from different organs and techniques should not be combined without checking their assumptions.

Stomatal closure often precedes major hydraulic failure, thereby limiting the demand that creates tension. The sequence varies among species and organs. A loss of whole-leaf hydraulic conductance can arise from changes outside the xylem, including dehydration of living tissues, before extensive vessel embolism occurs. Consequently, a decline in conductance should not automatically be described as evidence of cavitation. Imaging of conduit gas, suitable hydraulic measurements and simultaneous water status measurements can distinguish mechanisms.

Rewatering does not instantly reverse every drought effect. Soil water potential may improve within minutes while stomatal conductance remains suppressed because ABA is still elevated, leaf hydraulic conductance is impaired or carbon metabolism has not recovered. Mildly dehydrated cells may rapidly regain turgor and resume growth. After severe injury, tissues may need repair or replacement. Dead cells cannot regain viability merely by absorbing water. A restored external supply is necessary for recovery but may not be sufficient.

Embolism recovery is a special case. Some plants can restore conductivity under conditions that allow gas dissolution or positive root pressure; others primarily recover by producing new conductive tissue. Whether particular conduits refill while the surrounding xylem remains under substantial tension is context-dependent and has been debated. It is inaccurate to claim that aquaporins universally pump water into blocked vessels after rainfall. Aquaporins facilitate passive transport, and restoration of conduit continuity requires appropriate physical conditions.

A recovery experiment should compare a well-watered control, a drought-treated group and a rewatered group at repeated time points. Record leaf water potential, relative water content, stomatal conductance, assimilation, and where possible hydraulic function. If leaf water content recovers but gas exchange does not, the difference suggests a limitation beyond bulk hydration. If assimilation recovers only when new leaves develop, the original leaves may have experienced irreversible injury. Recovery trajectories thus provide information that an endpoint drought measurement cannot supply.

11. Measurement, controls and analytical reasoning

Relative water content (RWC) compares the amount of water currently present in a tissue with the amount it contains at a defined fully hydrated state. Let FW be fresh mass measured promptly after collection, TW be turgid mass after appropriate rehydration and DW be dry mass after drying to constant mass. Then RWC = 100 × (FW − DW)/(TW − DW). In an illustrative leaf, FW = 0.82 g, TW = 1.00 g and DW = 0.20 g. Present water mass is 0.62 g; fully hydrated water mass is 0.80 g; RWC is 77.5%. The subtraction of dry mass is essential because dry matter is not water.

RWC and water potential measure different properties. RWC describes the water amount relative to a reference capacity; water potential describes the thermodynamic tendency of water to move. Leaves with distinct solute concentrations or cell wall elasticity can have different potentials at the same RWC. Conversely, two leaves can have similar potential and different hydration. Measuring both provides a stronger description of tissue status. Rehydration methods require care because prolonged immersion can alter solute pools or produce an inappropriate estimate of full hydration.

A pressure chamber estimates the balancing pressure needed to bring xylem sap to a cut surface. Under suitable equilibration conditions the measured positive balancing pressure approximates the magnitude of the negative water potential of the sampled tissue. The sign must be handled correctly. A reading of +1.5 MPa corresponds approximately to a tissue potential of −1.5 MPa under the usual assumptions, not +1.5 MPa. Predawn potential can sometimes approximate the potential of soil accessed by roots, but nocturnal transpiration, heterogeneous soil and incomplete equilibration weaken the assumption.

An osmometer estimates osmotic properties from colligative behavior, such as freezing-point depression or vapor pressure. Measurements on expressed sap may mix contents from distinct compartments and may be affected by dilution or extraction. They must not be equated automatically with intact-cell turgor. When osmotic potential and total water potential are measured under comparable conditions, the difference can provide an estimate of pressure potential using the simplified equation already developed.

Gas-exchange systems compare carbon dioxide and water vapor concentrations in air entering and leaving a chamber containing a leaf. With known flow and enclosed leaf area, net assimilation and transpiration can be estimated; conductance and intercellular carbon dioxide can then be calculated using model assumptions. Light, chamber temperature, external CO₂, humidity and boundary-layer behavior should be controlled or recorded. A single conductance value is not a whole-plant water-use measurement because the total leaf area and time course are missing.

Electrolyte leakage is used as an index of membrane injury. A common approach compares conductivity after tissue incubation with total conductivity after disruption of the tissue, reporting a percentage. Higher leakage can indicate loss of membrane integrity, but the assay also depends on tissue ion content, sample size, surface washing, incubation time and damage during cutting. It is stronger evidence when paired with viability or recovery assays. A difference in leakage does not establish which membrane protein or lipid was originally damaged.

Experimental drought severity should be defined independently of treatment labels. Giving the same small volume of water to plants of different sizes can cause unequal tissue stress. Withholding irrigation for seven days can generate different soil water potentials depending on pot size, substrate, temperature and initial leaf area. Weighing pots can estimate water loss if soil evaporation and changes in mass are accounted for. Measurements of soil potential, RWC or leaf potential establish more informative comparisons than duration alone.

A factorial design separates the effects of soil drought and atmospheric demand. Use well-watered and dry-soil treatments at both low and high VPD, with replicated plants and appropriate randomization. If high VPD reduces conductance even with wet soil, atmospheric demand is sufficient to elicit a response under those conditions. If the combined treatment reduces assimilation more than expected from the individual effects under a specified statistical model, an interaction may be present. A meaningful conclusion requires biological replication and variation estimates rather than four single values.

To distinguish avoidance from tolerance, compare genotypes in two ways. First expose both to a similar environment and record their water status and performance. Then compare physiological function at similar tissue water status, preferably with time and developmental stage controlled. A plant that performs better under the same external drought because its tissue remains wetter exhibits evidence of avoidance. A plant that retains greater function at a comparably low tissue water potential provides evidence of tolerance. A genotype can express both mechanisms; neither comparison is sufficient by itself to establish every causal trait.

The same discipline applies to omics and expression studies. A drought-associated increase in a transcript, metabolite or protein identifies a response, not necessarily its benefit. A loss-of-function phenotype, rescue, controlled expression or independent biochemical assay helps move from association toward causality. Measurements should address alternative explanations such as smaller plant size, slower soil drying, developmental delay or altered water demand. This reasoning links drought physiology with the general principles of experimental biology used throughout the NET and SET syllabi.

11. Misconceptions and examination traps

Wet soil excludes drought
High VPD or limited root conductance can cause dehydration even when soil water is available.
Water flows toward the most concentrated solution regardless of pressure
Total water potential, not osmotic potential alone, determines net direction.
Negative water potential means negative turgor
Positive cellular turgor can coexist with negative total potential.
Every decrease in osmotic potential is adjustment
Passive concentration accompanies water loss; compare at standardized hydration.
ABA is synthesized only in roots
Shoot tissues can synthesize ABA; transport and sensitivity also matter.
ABA activates PP2C
ABA-bound PYL inhibits clade A PP2C, relieving kinase inhibition.
SLAC1 is a potassium channel
SLAC1 mediates anion efflux; depolarization favors outward potassium flux through other channels.
Guard cells maintain turgor during closure
Guard-cell turgor normally decreases to close the pore.
Aquaporins actively pump water
They facilitate passive movement according to the water-potential gradient.
Greater root-to-shoot ratio proves root growth increased
Shoot biomass can decline more than root biomass.
High proline proves drought tolerance
Proline can reflect stress intensity; assess protection at comparable dehydration.
All LEA proteins are dehydrins
Dehydrins are one group of LEA proteins.
High intrinsic WUE guarantees yield
Assimilation can decline while A/gs rises.
Low assimilation proves PSII injury
Stomatal diffusion may limit assimilation while dark-adapted PSII efficiency remains high.
Rewatering immediately reverses injury
Persistent hydraulic or cellular injury can delay or prevent recovery.

12. Original examination practice

Part B — conceptual short-response questions

1. Which component of water potential is lowered by dissolved solutes?

Answer: Osmotic potential.

Hydrostatic pressure is distinct; dissolved particles decrease water chemical potential.

2. Which factor most directly distinguishes soil drought from atmospheric drought?

Answer: Water supply versus evaporative demand.

Both may lower tissue hydration but arise through different environmental drivers.

3. What is the major guard-cell SnRK2 in Arabidopsis?

Answer: OST1 (SnRK2.6).

It participates in ABA-dependent activation of downstream targets including SLAC1.

4. What is the role of clade A PP2C in the ABA core pathway?

Answer: Negative regulation of SnRK2.

Inhibiting this phosphatase allows kinase signaling to proceed.

5. What does SLAC1 conduct during stomatal closure?

Answer: Anions.

Loss of negative charge promotes depolarization and potassium efflux through other channels.

6. Which transcription factor family includes DREB proteins?

Answer: AP2/ERF.

DREB factors regulate genes containing suitable dehydration-responsive elements.

7. What does an ABRE represent?

Answer: An ABA-responsive cis-regulatory element.

It can be recognized by suitable AREB/ABF transcription factors in a promoter.

8. What is a compatible solute?

Answer: A compound that can accumulate with relatively little disruption of cellular biochemistry.

Its osmotic and protective contributions depend on concentration and localization.

9. Which amino acid commonly accumulates during water deficit?

Answer: Proline.

Its pool depends on synthesis and degradation; accumulation alone does not prove tolerance.

10. To which protein family do dehydrins belong?

Answer: Late embryogenesis abundant proteins.

They are commonly classified as group 2 LEA proteins.

11. What does P50 on a xylem vulnerability curve represent?

Answer: The pressure or water potential at 50% loss of conductivity.

Under comparable measurements a more negative P50 indicates greater resistance to embolism.

12. Which membranes contain PIP aquaporins?

Answer: Plasma membranes.

TIP proteins are associated with the tonoplast; both classes can influence water permeability.

13. What is the denominator of the RWC formula before multiplication by 100?

Answer: TW − DW.

This is the water mass at the defined fully hydrated state.

14. What does dehydration avoidance mean?

Answer: Maintaining relatively favorable tissue water status under water shortage.

Tolerance instead concerns function or survival despite low water status.

15. Which tissues of many resurrection plants survive extensive drying?

Answer: Vegetative tissues.

Unlike most crop leaves, these tissues can resume activity after rehydration.

16. What is the usual effect of stomatal closure on internal CO₂ supply?

Answer: It restricts diffusion from the atmosphere.

Carbon assimilation may decline even when PSII remains functional.

17. What is the meaning of turgor loss point?

Answer: Water potential at which tissue turgor approaches zero.

It is not necessarily the point of irreversible tissue death.

18. What does glycine betaine contribute in accumulating species?

Answer: Osmotic and macromolecular protection.

Not all plants accumulate physiologically substantial concentrations.

19. Which gas-exchange measure is used in the numerator of intrinsic WUE?

Answer: Net CO₂ assimilation A.

The denominator is stomatal conductance to water vapor, not actual transpiration.

20. Why is leaf temperature needed when calculating leaf-to-air VPD?

Answer: Saturation vapor pressure depends on temperature.

Air relative humidity alone cannot describe the actual evaporative gradient.

Part C — analytical short-response questions

1. A cell has Ψs = −1.1 MPa and Ψp = +0.3 MPa. An adjoining cell has Ψw = −0.5 MPa. Predict water movement.

Answer: Toward the first cell.

Its total potential is −0.8 MPa, lower than −0.5 MPa.

2. At 298 K, calculate Ψs of 0.10 M ideal sucrose.

Answer: −0.248 MPa.

Using i = 1 and R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ gives −1 × 0.10 × 0.008314 × 298.

3. A leaf has FW 0.7 g, TW 1.0 g, DW 0.25 g. Calculate RWC.

Answer: 60%.

Current water is 0.45 g and fully hydrated water is 0.75 g; their ratio is 0.60.

4. Conductance falls from 4 to 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ while the driving gradient rises from 0.5 to 1 MPa. Predict steady flow.

Answer: Unchanged at 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Both initial and final products KΔΨ equal 2; flow does not identify conductance alone.

5. A mutant fails to close stomata during drought, but application of ABA restores closure. Which defect is supported?

Answer: Impaired endogenous ABA availability.

The downstream response remains functional; genetic or biochemical evidence is needed to localize the defect.

6. A PP2C allele inhibits OST1 even in the presence of ABA-bound PYL. Predict the response.

Answer: Reduced ABA-dependent closure.

Persistent negative regulation prevents the normal activation of OST1 and downstream anion channels.

7. A knockout removes SLAC1 but other anion channels remain. Must ABA-induced closure be entirely abolished?

Answer: No.

Parallel channels and calcium-dependent pathways can contribute; the phenotype requires measurement.

8. Two samples show equal Ψs when fully hydrated but different Ψs when dry. Is adjustment demonstrated?

Answer: No.

The difference can reflect passive concentration from unequal water loss.

9. At Ψw −1.5 MPa, Ψs is −1.8 MPa in A and −2.1 MPa in B. Compare turgor.

Answer: A = 0.3 MPa; B = 0.6 MPa.

Subtract solute potential from total potential. B maintains higher pressure under the stated approximation.

10. A genotype has higher root-to-shoot ratio but both masses are lower than control. Does the ratio prove enhanced root growth?

Answer: No.

The ratio can rise solely because the shoot is inhibited more strongly.

11. A line overexpressing a LEA protein survives longer in pots but grows much smaller. What comparison is needed?

Answer: Compare survival and injury at matched tissue dehydration.

Smaller leaves may deplete soil water more slowly; identical days without watering do not equal identical tissue stress.

12. A falls from 18 to 12 while gs falls from 0.3 to 0.1. Compute intrinsic WUE before and after.

Answer: 60 and 120 µmol mol⁻¹.

A/gs doubles despite a decline in assimilation, so higher efficiency does not prove increased productivity.

13. After drought, A and Ci fall while dark-adapted Fv/Fm stays near control. Which limitation is initially consistent?

Answer: Stomatal diffusion limitation.

Restricted CO₂ supply can lower A without detectable decline in maximum PSII efficiency; other measurements are still needed.

14. After irrigation, RWC recovers but assimilation remains depressed. Give two plausible causes.

Answer: Persistent stomatal inhibition and nonstomatal metabolic or hydraulic impairment.

Hydration and functional recovery need not proceed at the same rate.

15. Native hydraulic conductivity is 3 units and restored maximum is 10. Calculate PLC.

Answer: 70%.

100 × (1 − 3/10) = 70.

16. At gs approximated by total conductance 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, D = 2 kPa and P = 100 kPa, calculate E.

Answer: 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

0.15 × 2/100 = 0.003 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ under the simplified model.

17. A reporter promoter loses drought induction after DRE deletion but remains ABA responsive. Interpret.

Answer: The deleted DRE contributes to dehydration-induced regulation.

A separate regulatory input can preserve ABA responsiveness; reporter context limits generalization.

18. High VPD reduces gs in wet soil. Is root drying necessary for this response?

Answer: No, not in that experiment.

Atmospheric demand can alter leaf hydration and stomatal signaling independently of soil drying.

19. Proline doubles in the more severely injured genotype. Is the metabolite harmful?

Answer: The data cannot establish causation.

Injury can drive accumulation; manipulate proline independently and compare at matched water status.

20. A plant recovers through new xylem formation after severe drought. Does this prove embolized conduits refilled?

Answer: No.

New conductive tissue can restore transport without refilling older gas-blocked conduits.

State SET and integrated practice

1. Assertion–reason: Drought avoidance can improve survival; because lower transpiration can preserve tissue hydration. Assess.

Answer: Both statements are correct and the reason explains the assertion.

Water conservation delays tissue dehydration when supply is restricted.

2. Assertion–reason: All plants accumulate glycine betaine during drought; because glycine betaine is a compatible solute. Assess.

Answer: Assertion false; reason true.

Ability to accumulate glycine betaine varies across species.

3. A pressure chamber balancing pressure is 1.25 MPa. Estimate leaf Ψw under standard assumptions.

Answer: Approximately −1.25 MPa.

The applied positive balancing pressure approximates the magnitude of the original negative potential.

4. A drought-sensitive crop survives a trial but seed yield falls sharply. Has the trial shown yield resistance?

Answer: No.

Survival and yield are distinct outcomes; reproduction can remain impaired.

5. Name an experiment that can test whether a drying root compartment affects the shoot.

Answer: A controlled split-root treatment.

Compare a half-dried root system with well-watered controls while measuring shoot hydration and signaling.

6. Why is dry mass subtracted when calculating RWC?

Answer: To isolate water mass.

Dry solids contribute to fresh and turgid mass but not tissue water.

7. A crop finishes flowering before terminal drought. Which strategy is illustrated?

Answer: Drought escape.

Phenological timing avoids the vulnerable stage during stress.

8. What control is important in testing exogenous glycine betaine?

Answer: A matched vehicle and appropriate osmotic control.

These distinguish the compound's effects from treatment medium and osmotic changes.

9. Why might a transcript increase without a corresponding increase in hydraulic conductance?

Answer: Translation, localization, channel gating or other resistances may limit flow.

Transcript abundance is an indirect measure of function.

10. Why is gradual drying sometimes more survivable than rapid drying?

Answer: It allows time for acclimatory gene expression and protective metabolite accumulation.

This benefit is context-dependent and does not remove the physical limits of water loss.

13. Revision and mastery

Follow the causal sequence from water supply and evaporative demand to hydraulic and hormonal responses, stomatal conductance, osmotic adjustment, cellular protection and post-drought recovery. Distinguish physical water relations from regulated cellular responses and compare genotypes at matched tissue hydration when testing tolerance.

Active recall

  1. Distinguish soil drought from dehydration.
  2. Explain why total water potential includes pressure.
  3. Predict flow between −0.4 and −0.6 MPa.
  4. Describe how VPD changes with temperature at fixed RH.
  5. Trace ABA–PYL–PP2C–OST1 signaling.
  6. Explain why SLAC1 activity leads to K⁺ efflux.
  7. Contrast ABRE and DRE regulation.
  8. Distinguish osmotic adjustment from passive concentration.
  9. Explain why aquaporins cannot pump water uphill.
  10. Compare LEA proteins and compatible solutes.
  11. Distinguish avoidance from desiccation tolerance.
  12. Explain why rewatering need not restore photosynthesis immediately.

Mastery checklist

  • Calculate Ψw, Ψs, RWC, intrinsic water-use efficiency and PLC with correct signs and units.
  • Predict ABA pathway mutant behavior while accounting for redundancy.
  • Interpret data that distinguish active osmotic adjustment and passive concentration.
  • Compare drought escape, avoidance, cellular tolerance and recovery without treating them as equivalent.
