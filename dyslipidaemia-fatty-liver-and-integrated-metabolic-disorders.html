1. The physiological problem of transporting fat

Every cell requires cholesterol to maintain the structural and physical properties of its membranes, yet no cell can obtain its supply simply by dissolving a large quantity of cholesterol in water. The blood plasma that connects the organs is largely aqueous. Triacylglycerol, which is an efficient form of energy storage, has the same fundamental transport difficulty. A vertebrate therefore needs a mechanism for moving chemically water-insoluble material through a water-rich extracellular environment. An understanding of metabolic disorders must begin with this successful physiological arrangement rather than with a list of abnormal laboratory measurements.

The term lipid encompasses molecules that differ greatly in function. A triacylglycerol consists of glycerol esterified with three fatty acids and contains concentrated chemical energy. Free cholesterol is an amphipathic sterol that helps regulate membrane organisation and supplies material for steroid and bile acid synthesis. A cholesteryl ester has a fatty acid attached to the hydroxyl group of cholesterol and is considerably more hydrophobic than free cholesterol. Phospholipids contain polar head groups and hydrophobic portions, making them suitable components of interfaces. These different chemical properties explain the arrangement of molecules within circulating lipid carriers.

A lipoprotein is a supramolecular particle rather than a single protein. Its surface consists largely of phospholipids, unesterified cholesterol and proteins, while the nonpolar core contains triacylglycerol and cholesteryl esters. The particle has a single surface layer with polar groups facing the surrounding plasma; it does not possess an aqueous lumen enclosed by a phospholipid bilayer like an intracellular vesicle. This is a particularly useful distinction when interpreting structural drawings. Cholesterol in the surface and cholesterol esterified within the core are chemically related but occupy different physical environments.

The proteins on lipoproteins are called apolipoproteins. Some form an essential structural framework, while others are exchanged between particles or regulate reactions at the particle surface. A receptor can recognise a protein ligand on the carrier rather than having to recognise every molecule of fat hidden within its core. Consequently, the distribution of lipid between tissues depends on the identity and accessibility of proteins as well as the quantity of lipid. This establishes a central principle of this chapter: an abnormal concentration may arise from changes in delivery, processing, uptake or clearance, not merely from the amount of fat entering the diet.

Not every fatty acid travels inside a lipoprotein. During fasting, hydrolysis of adipocyte triacylglycerol releases nonesterified fatty acids into extracellular fluid, where many bind reversibly to albumin. Their concentration and rate of appearance respond rapidly to hormonal conditions. This route should be distinguished from the transport of esterified fatty acids in the cores of VLDL and chylomicrons. In an experiment that measures only plasma triacylglycerol, the albumin-bound fatty acid pool is not directly quantified. The distinction is essential because liver fat may increase when adipose fatty acid delivery is excessive even in the absence of an immediate increase in circulating triacylglycerol.

The lipoprotein classes are defined by physical and compositional properties, not by an absolute separation of metabolic functions. A relatively large, triglyceride-rich particle has a lower density than a small particle that contains a higher proportion of protein. Chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) are especially important for carrying triglyceride; intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL) are products of VLDL processing; low-density lipoproteins (LDL) carry substantial cholesterol; and high-density lipoproteins (HDL) participate in the redistribution of cholesterol. Remnant particles exist between the conventional classes, so the labels describe useful ranges within a dynamic system.

A student may imagine the particles as delivery vehicles carrying cargo, with apolipoproteins serving as address labels and regulators. This analogy helps distinguish the particle from the lipid it carries. It fails if a vehicle is thought to retain its original structure throughout its journey. Lipoproteins are remodelled in circulation, lose or gain lipid, exchange proteins and can become a particle of another density class. Some of their cargo leaves before the entire particle is taken up. The metabolism of the carrier is therefore inseparable from the metabolism of its cargo.

2. The intestinal route and postprandial lipid delivery

After a mixed meal, digested fatty acids and monoacylglycerols enter intestinal epithelial cells. The majority of long-chain dietary fatty acids are re-esterified within enterocytes and assembled into lipoprotein particles containing apolipoprotein B-48. Assembly requires the transfer of lipid onto the developing apolipoprotein by microsomal triglyceride transfer protein. Chylomicrons leave the intestine through lymphatic vessels before reaching systemic venous blood. The lymphatic route differs from the immediate portal transport of most absorbed monosaccharides and amino acids, which is why the liver is not the obligatory first recipient of every newly absorbed long-chain fatty acid.

This difference in route has an important experimental implication. An intestinally derived tracer incorporated into a circulating chylomicron may initially appear in systemic blood, while labelled glucose can reach the liver through the portal vein. Some shorter-chain fatty acids can enter portal blood more directly; therefore, the rule of chylomicron-mediated transport should not be applied to all fatty acids without qualification. The chemical structure of the absorbed molecule influences both its initial route and its later tissue distribution.

A newly secreted chylomicron undergoes surface remodelling in circulation and can acquire apolipoproteins such as apoC-II and apoE. Its triglyceride core is progressively hydrolysed by lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which functions at the luminal surface of capillaries in tissues including adipose tissue, skeletal muscle and heart. The enzyme is produced mainly by nearby parenchymal cells and is transported to its endothelial site of action by GPIHBP1. This spatial arrangement allows lipolysis to occur close to the tissues that take up the liberated fatty acids. An enzyme operating inside an adipocyte lipid droplet would not have equivalent access to particles suspended in the capillary lumen.

The immediate products of LPL-mediated hydrolysis include fatty acids that may enter surrounding cells, where they can be oxidised or converted into storage lipids. Not all released fatty acids are necessarily captured by the adjacent tissue. Some escape into the circulating nonesterified fatty acid pool, a process often described as spillover. The remaining lipoprotein loses much of its original triglyceride and changes its relative composition. Hepatic clearance of these chylomicron remnants requires recognition of surface ligands, particularly apoE, by hepatic receptors and associated pathways.

There is a physiological reason why the adipose tissue of a fed animal should receive much of the newly available energy. The increase in insulin following a meal promotes lipid storage in adipocytes by encouraging glucose utilisation and favouring esterification, while suppressing intracellular triglyceride mobilisation. In many settings adipose LPL activity rises in the fed state, whereas fasting favours lipid utilisation by oxidative tissues. These changes are not universal identical switches in every species or tissue: enzyme activity also depends on local nutrient signals, exercise, hormonal status and regulatory proteins. The relevant principle is the coordinated allocation of circulating fuel according to physiological demand.

ApoC-II is an important activator of LPL. ApoC-III can oppose triglyceride-rich particle clearance by inhibiting lipolysis and remnant removal. The direction of the change in circulating triglyceride following altered activity of these apolipoproteins can be predicted using the balance between entry and removal. If the arrival of triglyceride-rich particles stays constant but their hydrolysis and clearance slow, the circulating triglyceride pool increases. The increase does not demonstrate that the intestine has increased lipid absorption; it may indicate a longer residence time.

The distinction between intracellular adipocyte lipolysis and extracellular lipoprotein lipolysis is one of the most frequently confused mechanisms in metabolic physiology. Intracellular adipose triglyceride lipase and hormone-sensitive lipase mobilise stored lipid. In contrast, endothelial LPL acts on circulating particles and provides fatty acids for uptake by tissues. Insulin can therefore suppress release of fatty acids from adipocytes while favouring storage of fatty acids supplied by LPL after a meal. The two effects are complementary rather than contradictory.

3. The liver-derived route: VLDL, IDL and LDL

The liver has the dual responsibility of using fatty acids for its own metabolism and redistributing lipid to other organs. It receives nonesterified fatty acids from blood, obtains lipids during remnant clearance and can synthesise fatty acids from nonlipid precursors. Some of the resulting triglyceride is packaged with apolipoprotein B-100 in VLDL. Whereas apoB-48 is the structural apolipoprotein of intestinal chylomicrons, apoB-100 is the structural apolipoprotein of the liver-derived VLDL particle in humans. ApoB-100 remains associated with the particle during its transformation into IDL and LDL.

The fate of VLDL illustrates the difference between particle secretion and lipid secretion. A liver may release a larger number of particles, each with approximately the same triglyceride load. Alternatively, it may release particles containing a larger amount of triglyceride per particle. Both changes can increase triglyceride delivery, but their effects on the number of circulating apoB-containing particles need not be identical. An assay of cholesterol mass cannot, by itself, resolve those alternatives. Measurements of lipoprotein composition or particle kinetics provide additional information.

Like chylomicrons, VLDL particles lose triglyceride through LPL-mediated hydrolysis. Their remnants, including IDL, are either cleared by the liver or remodelled further. Hepatic lipase participates in remodelling some particles, and a portion of IDL becomes LDL. These are not independent categories produced in fixed proportions at a single location. The abundance of LDL depends partly on the rate of VLDL secretion and conversion but also on the rate at which LDL particles leave the circulation. Some particles may be cleared before reaching the LDL stage.

LDL delivers cholesterol to cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis. ApoB-100 supplies a ligand for the LDL receptor. Once bound, the receptor-particle complex enters a clathrin-associated endocytic pathway. Endosomal acidification promotes separation of LDL from its receptor, permitting many receptors to recycle to the plasma membrane while the particle proceeds toward lysosomal degradation. Hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters liberates cholesterol that can enter the cellular regulatory pool or be re-esterified for storage. The uptake process is regulated rather than being a simple passive transfer of all cholesterol across a membrane.

The classical LDL receptor studies of Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein established the relationship between cellular cholesterol regulation and the clearance of circulating LDL. The scientific question was whether cells from individuals with familial hypercholesterolaemia failed to respond normally to extracellular lipoproteins because the cells could not recognise or process the carrier appropriately. Comparisons of cultured cells and their LDL binding, uptake and metabolic responses provided a mechanism linking inherited defects to systemic cholesterol accumulation. The 1986 account of this receptor-mediated pathway is a useful historical source because it connects biochemical feedback with a physiological phenotype.

Intracellular cholesterol can restrain further endogenous cholesterol synthesis and reduce expression of the LDL receptor through feedback involving sterol regulatory element-binding proteins. When the accessible sterol pool is depleted, activation of the appropriate sterol-sensitive transcriptional programme can increase both synthesis and receptor-mediated acquisition. These responses maintain cellular cholesterol within functional limits. Importantly, a cell experiencing relative cholesterol depletion can coexist with a high extracellular LDL concentration if receptor-dependent uptake is impaired. The concentration in the blood and the regulatory pool inside the cell are not the same variable.

A decrease in hepatic LDL receptor function usually increases the residence time of LDL. In contrast, reduced VLDL secretion may reduce delivery of particles into the LDL-producing pathway. If an experiment changes both processes simultaneously, the final LDL level depends on their relative magnitude. This is why a single measured concentration, even when accurately measured, cannot prove the biochemical mechanism that changed it. Investigators may measure turnover, receptor expression or tracer disappearance to distinguish alternative explanations.

4. HDL and the redistribution of cholesterol

Cells need to avoid indefinite accumulation of cholesterol because they have limited ability to destroy its sterol ring. Redistribution and eventual elimination are therefore important features of lipid homeostasis. HDL particles participate in moving cholesterol away from peripheral tissues and delivering it to other lipoproteins or the liver. The term reverse cholesterol transport refers to the overall physiological pathway, rather than to a single concentration measured in plasma.

ApoA-I is a major structural and functional protein of HDL. Relatively lipid-poor apoA-I can receive phospholipids and free cholesterol exported from cells through the ATP-binding cassette transporter ABCA1. Additional cholesterol export may occur through mechanisms involving ABCG1 and mature HDL particles. Lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), activated by apoA-I, converts free cholesterol on the HDL surface into cholesteryl ester. The ester moves into the hydrophobic core, contributing to changes in particle shape and composition. Esterification helps accommodate cholesterol, but it is not synonymous with its final removal from the body.

The liver can take up HDL-associated cholesteryl ester through scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI). Selective uptake of lipid need not require endocytosis and destruction of the entire HDL particle. In humans, cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) can also exchange HDL cholesteryl ester for triglyceride carried by apoB-containing particles. The transferred cholesterol may subsequently reach the liver when the recipient particle is cleared. These parallel routes explain why a molecule of cholesterol that initially left a peripheral cell through HDL does not necessarily remain in HDL until it reaches the liver.

Hepatic cholesterol can be secreted into bile or metabolised to bile acids. Some secreted material is reabsorbed from the intestine, so secretion into bile is not equivalent to net faecal elimination. A full account of reverse cholesterol transport must distinguish cellular efflux, intravascular transfer, hepatic uptake and elimination. An intervention increasing the cholesterol content of HDL may act at any one of these stages, including by slowing departure of cholesterol from HDL.

HDL cholesterol (HDL-C) is the concentration of cholesterol assigned to the HDL fraction, not the number of HDL particles, their capacity to accept cholesterol, or the net rate of cholesterol transport from peripheral cells to faeces. Two samples can contain equal amounts of HDL cholesterol while having different particle compositions and different abilities to promote efflux in a laboratory assay. Conversely, greater measured efflux in a cell-based assay does not establish that all later stages of whole-body transport are accelerated. Low HDL-C frequently accompanies insulin resistance, but its interpretation must account for the wider changes in particle metabolism.

Calling HDL good cholesterol and LDL bad cholesterol obscures the underlying physiology. The cholesterol molecule is chemically the same in both fractions. Different carrier particles differ in their behaviour, their residence times and their ability to enter or become retained in particular tissues. LDL receptor uptake is a normal, necessary process, while prolonged exposure of the arterial wall to excess atherogenic particles is a pathological process. Distinguishing function from pathological exposure avoids the false inference that every LDL particle is an intrinsically poisonous substance.

5. Hepatic fatty acid balance and the origin of steatosis

Hepatic steatosis means an abnormal accumulation of lipid, principally triglyceride, within hepatocytes. The quantity of triglyceride present at one moment is a pool. The rates of entry, synthesis, oxidation and secretion are fluxes. A pool grows when its net input exceeds net output, even if both input and output are high. This elementary observation explains why fatty liver may coexist with increased lipid export from the liver: export may have risen, but not sufficiently to match the greater substrate supply.

A simple organ-scale model tracks fatty acid equivalents in the hepatic esterifiable lipid pool. Let U represent uptake of exogenous fatty acids, D the contribution of newly synthesised fatty acids, O net removal through oxidation, and E net export of esterified fatty acids. All fluxes must use the same units, for example millimoles of fatty acid equivalents per hour. Under a simplified boundary in which other exchange terms are negligible, the change in the pool is U plus D minus O minus E. The assumption must be stated because actual hepatocytes contain multiple interconnected lipid pools, and free fatty acids can be oxidised before ever being esterified.

Suppose an illustrative liver takes up 12 millimoles of fatty acid equivalents per hour and synthesises 4, while removing 9 by oxidation and exporting 5. The net balance is 12 plus 4 minus 9 minus 5, or positive 2 millimoles per hour. If export increases to 6 but uptake increases to 16 and the other terms stay constant, the balance becomes positive 5. Thus the liver has increased its export rate but its accumulation rate has also increased. Neither an elevated export rate nor an elevated plasma triglyceride measurement proves protection against steatosis.

During ordinary fasting, falling insulin permits adipose lipolysis, supplying nonesterified fatty acids to the liver. Oxidation supports hepatic ATP production and can supply acetyl-CoA for ketone body formation. In the fed state insulin normally suppresses adipose release, while carbon from carbohydrate can support fatty acid synthesis through the coordinated actions of lipogenic enzymes. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase forms malonyl-CoA, which supplies fatty acid synthesis and inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase I. This provides an example of regulation coupling synthesis with mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acids. It does not imply that oxidation ceases completely whenever synthesis increases.

In insulin-resistant states, suppression of adipose lipolysis may become less effective. The liver can then receive excess fatty acid substrate even when circulating insulin is elevated. At the same time, lipogenesis can be supported by carbohydrate availability and insulin-responsive pathways such as SREBP-1c and glucose-responsive pathways involving ChREBP. Selective insulin resistance is a useful description of a situation in which particular insulin-regulated outcomes become impaired while other responses are relatively preserved. The phrase should not be interpreted as proof that every hepatocyte retains one perfectly functioning branch and loses another completely. Substrate availability, indirect hormonal actions and tissue-level heterogeneity also contribute.

Hepatic triglyceride storage has both protective and potentially pathological aspects. Esterifying fatty acids into neutral triglyceride can reduce exposure of membranes and signalling systems to certain more reactive lipid intermediates. However, persistent oversupply may alter lipid composition, induce organelle stress and eventually accompany cell injury. The presence of a large triglyceride droplet is therefore not sufficient evidence that triglyceride itself is the direct toxic species. An experiment that lowers hepatic triglyceride by blocking esterification could increase the abundance of harmful intermediates unless the remaining fatty acids are adequately removed or utilised.

An important human tracer investigation by Donnelly and colleagues in 2005 examined fatty acid sources in liver tissue and secreted lipoproteins of nine individuals undergoing medically indicated liver biopsy. Stable isotope labelling allowed the investigators to distinguish contributions from circulating nonesterified fatty acids, dietary fat and new fatty acid synthesis. The study established that the stored hepatic lipid could derive from multiple sources, including substantial contributions from peripheral fatty acid delivery and endogenous synthesis. Its small selected cohort and particular feeding and labelling conditions mean that source fractions cannot be assumed identical for every individual or disease stage. The broader inference is that hepatic lipid origin cannot be determined simply by measuring the fat content of a meal.
