1. The physiological problem of transporting fat

Every cell requires cholesterol to maintain the structural and physical properties of its membranes, yet no cell can obtain its supply simply by dissolving a large quantity of cholesterol in water. The blood plasma that connects the organs is largely aqueous. Triacylglycerol, which is an efficient form of energy storage, has the same fundamental transport difficulty. A vertebrate therefore needs a mechanism for moving chemically water-insoluble material through a water-rich extracellular environment. An understanding of metabolic disorders must begin with this successful physiological arrangement rather than with a list of abnormal laboratory measurements.

The term lipid encompasses molecules that differ greatly in function. A triacylglycerol consists of glycerol esterified with three fatty acids and contains concentrated chemical energy. Free cholesterol is an amphipathic sterol that helps regulate membrane organisation and supplies material for steroid and bile acid synthesis. A cholesteryl ester has a fatty acid attached to the hydroxyl group of cholesterol and is considerably more hydrophobic than free cholesterol. Phospholipids contain polar head groups and hydrophobic portions, making them suitable components of interfaces. These different chemical properties explain the arrangement of molecules within circulating lipid carriers.

A lipoprotein is a supramolecular particle rather than a single protein. Its surface consists largely of phospholipids, unesterified cholesterol and proteins, while the nonpolar core contains triacylglycerol and cholesteryl esters. The particle has a single surface layer with polar groups facing the surrounding plasma; it does not possess an aqueous lumen enclosed by a phospholipid bilayer like an intracellular vesicle. This is a particularly useful distinction when interpreting structural drawings. Cholesterol in the surface and cholesterol esterified within the core are chemically related but occupy different physical environments.

The proteins on lipoproteins are called apolipoproteins. Some form an essential structural framework, while others are exchanged between particles or regulate reactions at the particle surface. A receptor can recognise a protein ligand on the carrier rather than having to recognise every molecule of fat hidden within its core. Consequently, the distribution of lipid between tissues depends on the identity and accessibility of proteins as well as the quantity of lipid. This establishes a central principle of this chapter: an abnormal concentration may arise from changes in delivery, processing, uptake or clearance, not merely from the amount of fat entering the diet.

Not every fatty acid travels inside a lipoprotein. During fasting, hydrolysis of adipocyte triacylglycerol releases nonesterified fatty acids into extracellular fluid, where many bind reversibly to albumin. Their concentration and rate of appearance respond rapidly to hormonal conditions. This route should be distinguished from the transport of esterified fatty acids in the cores of VLDL and chylomicrons. In an experiment that measures only plasma triacylglycerol, the albumin-bound fatty acid pool is not directly quantified. The distinction is essential because liver fat may increase when adipose fatty acid delivery is excessive even in the absence of an immediate increase in circulating triacylglycerol.

The lipoprotein classes are defined by physical and compositional properties, not by an absolute separation of metabolic functions. A relatively large, triglyceride-rich particle has a lower density than a small particle that contains a higher proportion of protein. Chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) are especially important for carrying triglyceride; intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL) are products of VLDL processing; low-density lipoproteins (LDL) carry substantial cholesterol; and high-density lipoproteins (HDL) participate in the redistribution of cholesterol. Remnant particles exist between the conventional classes, so the labels describe useful ranges within a dynamic system.

A student may imagine the particles as delivery vehicles carrying cargo, with apolipoproteins serving as address labels and regulators. This analogy helps distinguish the particle from the lipid it carries. It fails if a vehicle is thought to retain its original structure throughout its journey. Lipoproteins are remodelled in circulation, lose or gain lipid, exchange proteins and can become a particle of another density class. Some of their cargo leaves before the entire particle is taken up. The metabolism of the carrier is therefore inseparable from the metabolism of its cargo.

The classification of lipoproteins arose in part from the ability to separate plasma constituents according to density. Ultracentrifugation places particles in a medium under a large centrifugal field, and their movement depends on properties including their density relative to the medium. Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins contain a comparatively high proportion of hydrophobic cargo, whereas HDL contains a larger mass fraction of protein. Separation by density is therefore a useful operational definition. It is not proof that every particle assigned to one fraction has identical size, composition or metabolic history. Investigators using size-based methods, electrophoretic separation or measurements of individual apolipoproteins can describe the same population in different but complementary ways.

The relationship between particle radius and lipid capacity can be understood qualitatively from geometry. A spherical object's volume is proportional to the cube of its radius, while its surface area is proportional to the square. Increasing radius therefore increases core capacity more rapidly than surface area. A lipoprotein is not an ideal homogeneous sphere, but this relationship explains why a large triglyceride-rich particle can carry a considerable volume of hydrophobic material relative to its surrounding interface. When the core loses lipid during lipolysis, there may be excess surface components relative to the remaining core. Some of these components are redistributed to other particles. The conversion of a particle into a remnant is thus a physical as well as biochemical process.

The distinction between a structural and exchangeable apolipoprotein helps predict what can happen during remodelling. ApoB remains attached to the particle through its metabolic lifetime and provides a convenient way to follow that particle's origin. ApoC family proteins and apoE are relatively exchangeable, allowing a carrier to acquire or lose functional properties as it moves through plasma. The presence of an apolipoprotein in more than one lipoprotein class does not imply that the classes are identical. Their properties depend on the combination of constituents, their organisation and the local environment in which a receptor or enzyme encounters them.

Plasma lipoproteins must be distinguished from bile salt micelles. Mixed micelles participate in the delivery of digested lipid to the intestinal absorptive surface, using amphipathic bile salts to maintain hydrophobic material in the intestinal fluid. A chylomicron is assembled within the enterocyte after absorption and delivers lipid to tissues through the lymph and blood. The two structures solve transport problems at different anatomical stages. Failure of lipid digestion or micellar solubilisation therefore has a different primary effect from failure of chylomicron assembly, and neither is identical to failure of LPL in the systemic circulation.

2. The intestinal route and postprandial lipid delivery

After a mixed meal, digested fatty acids and monoacylglycerols enter intestinal epithelial cells. The majority of long-chain dietary fatty acids are re-esterified within enterocytes and assembled into lipoprotein particles containing apolipoprotein B-48. Assembly requires the transfer of lipid onto the developing apolipoprotein by microsomal triglyceride transfer protein. Chylomicrons leave the intestine through lymphatic vessels before reaching systemic venous blood. The lymphatic route differs from the immediate portal transport of most absorbed monosaccharides and amino acids, which is why the liver is not the obligatory first recipient of every newly absorbed long-chain fatty acid.

This difference in route has an important experimental implication. An intestinally derived tracer incorporated into a circulating chylomicron may initially appear in systemic blood, while labelled glucose can reach the liver through the portal vein. Some shorter-chain fatty acids can enter portal blood more directly; therefore, the rule of chylomicron-mediated transport should not be applied to all fatty acids without qualification. The chemical structure of the absorbed molecule influences both its initial route and its later tissue distribution.

A newly secreted chylomicron undergoes surface remodelling in circulation and can acquire apolipoproteins such as apoC-II and apoE. Its triglyceride core is progressively hydrolysed by lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which functions at the luminal surface of capillaries in tissues including adipose tissue, skeletal muscle and heart. The enzyme is produced mainly by nearby parenchymal cells and is transported to its endothelial site of action by GPIHBP1. This spatial arrangement allows lipolysis to occur close to the tissues that take up the liberated fatty acids. An enzyme operating inside an adipocyte lipid droplet would not have equivalent access to particles suspended in the capillary lumen.

The immediate products of LPL-mediated hydrolysis include fatty acids that may enter surrounding cells, where they can be oxidised or converted into storage lipids. Not all released fatty acids are necessarily captured by the adjacent tissue. Some escape into the circulating nonesterified fatty acid pool, a process often described as spillover. The remaining lipoprotein loses much of its original triglyceride and changes its relative composition. Hepatic clearance of these chylomicron remnants requires recognition of surface ligands, particularly apoE, by hepatic receptors and associated pathways.

There is a physiological reason why the adipose tissue of a fed animal should receive much of the newly available energy. The increase in insulin following a meal promotes lipid storage in adipocytes by encouraging glucose utilisation and favouring esterification, while suppressing intracellular triglyceride mobilisation. In many settings adipose LPL activity rises in the fed state, whereas fasting favours lipid utilisation by oxidative tissues. These changes are not universal identical switches in every species or tissue: enzyme activity also depends on local nutrient signals, exercise, hormonal status and regulatory proteins. The relevant principle is the coordinated allocation of circulating fuel according to physiological demand.

ApoC-II is an important activator of LPL. ApoC-III can oppose triglyceride-rich particle clearance by inhibiting lipolysis and remnant removal. The direction of the change in circulating triglyceride following altered activity of these apolipoproteins can be predicted using the balance between entry and removal. If the arrival of triglyceride-rich particles stays constant but their hydrolysis and clearance slow, the circulating triglyceride pool increases. The increase does not demonstrate that the intestine has increased lipid absorption; it may indicate a longer residence time.

The distinction between intracellular adipocyte lipolysis and extracellular lipoprotein lipolysis is one of the most frequently confused mechanisms in metabolic physiology. Intracellular adipose triglyceride lipase and hormone-sensitive lipase mobilise stored lipid. In contrast, endothelial LPL acts on circulating particles and provides fatty acids for uptake by tissues. Insulin can therefore suppress release of fatty acids from adipocytes while favouring storage of fatty acids supplied by LPL after a meal. The two effects are complementary rather than contradictory.

The supply of fatty acids from capillary lipolysis should be considered in relation to the energy demands of the receiving tissue. Cardiac muscle maintains continuous mechanical activity and may oxidise substantial quantities of fatty acid. Skeletal muscle varies its substrate requirements with contraction, intensity and training status. White adipose tissue is particularly adapted to retaining energy in lipid droplets during times of abundance. The same extracellular reaction can consequently supply fuel for oxidation in one organ and substrate for esterification in another. An arrow leading from lipoprotein triglyceride to tissue fatty acids is not an arrow directly to ATP production; further intracellular handling determines their fate.

The regulation of lipolysis involves more than simply changing the quantity of LPL protein present. Angiopoietin-like proteins provide examples of extracellular regulators that alter LPL activity. Differences in such regulation across tissues and nutritional states can contribute to fuel redistribution. Protein abundance measured by an immunoassay is therefore not necessarily proportional to effective catalytic flux in vivo. An LPL molecule must reach its appropriate capillary location, interact with the substrate and retain catalytic competence. A disturbance in localisation or inhibition can produce a functional phenotype even when expression of the enzyme appears relatively preserved.

Insulin-dependent storage also depends on glycerol backbone availability. An adipocyte requires glycerol-3-phosphate to esterify fatty acyl groups into glycerolipids. Glucose metabolism supplies glycerol-3-phosphate through dihydroxyacetone phosphate, and glyceroneogenesis can contribute under suitable conditions. Many white adipocytes have relatively limited glycerol kinase activity, so glycerol liberated by intracellular lipolysis is not necessarily recycled at the same rate as fatty acids. This creates different fates for the products of triglyceride hydrolysis. Glycerol released into blood can serve as a hepatic gluconeogenic precursor, illustrating that the adipose contribution to liver metabolism includes both fatty acid and glycerol flux.

After an oral fat load, the increase and eventual decrease of circulating triglyceride reflect both the intestinal appearance of chylomicrons and the handling of endogenous VLDL. The two classes can compete for processing capacity at capillaries. A meal tolerance experiment measuring total triglyceride at successive time points therefore describes the combined response of multiple pathways. A prolonged curve could reflect slow intestinal appearance, sustained hepatic secretion, reduced lipolysis or slow remnant uptake. Labelling dietary lipid or measuring an intestinal particle marker such as apoB-48 can help distinguish these alternatives. Equal fasting values do not guarantee equal postprandial curves.

Suppose an isolated tissue preparation is supplied with equal concentrations of labelled fatty acid in two forms: albumin-bound nonesterified fatty acid or triglyceride contained within intact lipoproteins. An intervention that inhibits extracellular LPL should strongly affect acquisition of fatty acids from triglyceride-rich particles, but need not directly prevent entry of albumin-delivered fatty acid. This prediction depends on maintaining comparable tissue viability, substrate delivery and extracellular conditions. If both forms show equally impaired uptake, an additional defect in downstream membrane transport or intracellular metabolism may be present. A route-specific control thus separates an extracellular processing defect from a general failure of tissue metabolism.

3. The liver-derived route: VLDL, IDL and LDL

The liver has the dual responsibility of using fatty acids for its own metabolism and redistributing lipid to other organs. It receives nonesterified fatty acids from blood, obtains lipids during remnant clearance and can synthesise fatty acids from nonlipid precursors. Some of the resulting triglyceride is packaged with apolipoprotein B-100 in VLDL. Whereas apoB-48 is the structural apolipoprotein of intestinal chylomicrons, apoB-100 is the structural apolipoprotein of the liver-derived VLDL particle in humans. ApoB-100 remains associated with the particle during its transformation into IDL and LDL.

The fate of VLDL illustrates the difference between particle secretion and lipid secretion. A liver may release a larger number of particles, each with approximately the same triglyceride load. Alternatively, it may release particles containing a larger amount of triglyceride per particle. Both changes can increase triglyceride delivery, but their effects on the number of circulating apoB-containing particles need not be identical. An assay of cholesterol mass cannot, by itself, resolve those alternatives. Measurements of lipoprotein composition or particle kinetics provide additional information.

Like chylomicrons, VLDL particles lose triglyceride through LPL-mediated hydrolysis. Their remnants, including IDL, are either cleared by the liver or remodelled further. Hepatic lipase participates in remodelling some particles, and a portion of IDL becomes LDL. These are not independent categories produced in fixed proportions at a single location. The abundance of LDL depends partly on the rate of VLDL secretion and conversion but also on the rate at which LDL particles leave the circulation. Some particles may be cleared before reaching the LDL stage.

LDL delivers cholesterol to cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis. ApoB-100 supplies a ligand for the LDL receptor. Once bound, the receptor-particle complex enters a clathrin-associated endocytic pathway. Endosomal acidification promotes separation of LDL from its receptor, permitting many receptors to recycle to the plasma membrane while the particle proceeds toward lysosomal degradation. Hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters liberates cholesterol that can enter the cellular regulatory pool or be re-esterified for storage. The uptake process is regulated rather than being a simple passive transfer of all cholesterol across a membrane.

The classical LDL receptor studies of Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein established the relationship between cellular cholesterol regulation and the clearance of circulating LDL. The scientific question was whether cells from individuals with familial hypercholesterolaemia failed to respond normally to extracellular lipoproteins because the cells could not recognise or process the carrier appropriately. Comparisons of cultured cells and their LDL binding, uptake and metabolic responses provided a mechanism linking inherited defects to systemic cholesterol accumulation. The 1986 account of this receptor-mediated pathway is a useful historical source because it connects biochemical feedback with a physiological phenotype.

Intracellular cholesterol can restrain further endogenous cholesterol synthesis and reduce expression of the LDL receptor through feedback involving sterol regulatory element-binding proteins. When the accessible sterol pool is depleted, activation of the appropriate sterol-sensitive transcriptional programme can increase both synthesis and receptor-mediated acquisition. These responses maintain cellular cholesterol within functional limits. Importantly, a cell experiencing relative cholesterol depletion can coexist with a high extracellular LDL concentration if receptor-dependent uptake is impaired. The concentration in the blood and the regulatory pool inside the cell are not the same variable.

A decrease in hepatic LDL receptor function usually increases the residence time of LDL. In contrast, reduced VLDL secretion may reduce delivery of particles into the LDL-producing pathway. If an experiment changes both processes simultaneously, the final LDL level depends on their relative magnitude. This is why a single measured concentration, even when accurately measured, cannot prove the biochemical mechanism that changed it. Investigators may measure turnover, receptor expression or tracer disappearance to distinguish alternative explanations.

Cholesterol regulation must accommodate the requirement for membrane synthesis during cell growth without allowing unrestricted accumulation. Consider a dividing cell in a culture medium deprived of lipoproteins. If the cell is capable of synthesising cholesterol, deprivation can increase the activity of its own synthetic pathway. Providing receptor-recognised LDL can then supply extracellular cholesterol and restrain endogenous synthesis. The comparison provides a way to observe feedback: changing extracellular availability produces a change in an intracellular reaction. If the receptor is defective, addition of LDL may fail to deliver the regulatory sterol signal efficiently, even though the medium contains plenty of cholesterol.

The experiments that clarified receptor-mediated uptake also distinguished several logically separate operations: surface binding, endocytosis, delivery to degradative compartments and the response to liberated sterol. Radiolabelled LDL can be used to monitor binding at conditions that restrict internalisation, followed by an incubation permitting uptake. A cell population showing weak binding differs from one that binds ligand normally but does not internalise it efficiently. Both may show reduced delivery of cholesterol to the regulatory pool. To identify which step failed, investigators need measurements specific to each step rather than only the final cellular cholesterol concentration.

Receptor abundance is not controlled only by transcription. Receptors may be recycled or directed toward degradation after internalisation. PCSK9 provides a representative example of a protein that can favour degradation of LDL receptors rather than their return to the cell surface. A change in receptor turnover therefore alters the surface population without requiring the initial rate of receptor gene transcription to change in the same direction. This principle is broadly applicable to membrane receptor physiology: the number of functional receptors at the surface depends on synthesis, trafficking, recycling and removal.

ApoB-containing particle assembly in the liver also requires coordination between translation and the availability of lipid. ApoB is a large protein that must acquire sufficient lipid during formation of a secreted particle. If this process fails, newly made apoB can be degraded rather than successfully exported. It follows that an increased amount of hepatic lipid does not necessarily produce an increased number of circulating VLDL particles; assembly capacity may constrain the response. Conversely, enhanced availability of triglyceride can favour packaging into secreted particles when assembly machinery is functional. A steady concentration of circulating apoB is compatible with dynamic synthesis and degradation.

One way to distinguish a defect in VLDL formation from a defect in clearance is to measure lipid appearance in medium surrounding cultured hepatocytes under controlled substrate conditions, then independently examine the disappearance of particles in an intact circulation. A hepatocyte preparation cannot reproduce all the endocrine, neural and interorgan inputs operating in the living animal. Nevertheless, it can establish whether a change in secretion can occur in the absence of altered systemic clearance. When both preparations give consistent results, the mechanistic inference is stronger than when either observation is interpreted alone.

4. HDL and the redistribution of cholesterol

Cells need to avoid indefinite accumulation of cholesterol because they have limited ability to destroy its sterol ring. Redistribution and eventual elimination are therefore important features of lipid homeostasis. HDL particles participate in moving cholesterol away from peripheral tissues and delivering it to other lipoproteins or the liver. The term reverse cholesterol transport refers to the overall physiological pathway, rather than to a single concentration measured in plasma.

ApoA-I is a major structural and functional protein of HDL. Relatively lipid-poor apoA-I can receive phospholipids and free cholesterol exported from cells through the ATP-binding cassette transporter ABCA1. Additional cholesterol export may occur through mechanisms involving ABCG1 and mature HDL particles. Lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), activated by apoA-I, converts free cholesterol on the HDL surface into cholesteryl ester. The ester moves into the hydrophobic core, contributing to changes in particle shape and composition. Esterification helps accommodate cholesterol, but it is not synonymous with its final removal from the body.

The liver can take up HDL-associated cholesteryl ester through scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI). Selective uptake of lipid need not require endocytosis and destruction of the entire HDL particle. In humans, cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) can also exchange HDL cholesteryl ester for triglyceride carried by apoB-containing particles. The transferred cholesterol may subsequently reach the liver when the recipient particle is cleared. These parallel routes explain why a molecule of cholesterol that initially left a peripheral cell through HDL does not necessarily remain in HDL until it reaches the liver.

Hepatic cholesterol can be secreted into bile or metabolised to bile acids. Some secreted material is reabsorbed from the intestine, so secretion into bile is not equivalent to net faecal elimination. A full account of reverse cholesterol transport must distinguish cellular efflux, intravascular transfer, hepatic uptake and elimination. An intervention increasing the cholesterol content of HDL may act at any one of these stages, including by slowing departure of cholesterol from HDL.

HDL cholesterol (HDL-C) is the concentration of cholesterol assigned to the HDL fraction, not the number of HDL particles, their capacity to accept cholesterol, or the net rate of cholesterol transport from peripheral cells to faeces. Two samples can contain equal amounts of HDL cholesterol while having different particle compositions and different abilities to promote efflux in a laboratory assay. Conversely, greater measured efflux in a cell-based assay does not establish that all later stages of whole-body transport are accelerated. Low HDL-C frequently accompanies insulin resistance, but its interpretation must account for the wider changes in particle metabolism.

Calling HDL good cholesterol and LDL bad cholesterol obscures the underlying physiology. The cholesterol molecule is chemically the same in both fractions. Different carrier particles differ in their behaviour, their residence times and their ability to enter or become retained in particular tissues. LDL receptor uptake is a normal, necessary process, while prolonged exposure of the arterial wall to excess atherogenic particles is a pathological process. Distinguishing function from pathological exposure avoids the false inference that every LDL particle is an intrinsically poisonous substance.

Cholesterol efflux is particularly relevant in macrophages that have encountered excess lipid in an arterial lesion. A cell can transfer cholesterol to extracellular acceptors when appropriate membrane transport machinery and acceptors are present. The rate measured in a culture dish depends on the donor cell's cholesterol availability, the expression of transporters, the concentration and composition of the acceptor and the incubation conditions. If equal volumes of plasma are used as acceptors, a difference might reflect concentration, particle quality or other constituents of the sample. Normalising HDL-related variables can answer a different question, such as the efflux capacity per unit of acceptor rather than the total capacity of the original sample.

The HDL pathway is also shaped by species differences. Mice used in metabolic studies do not naturally express CETP in the same way as humans and ordinarily distribute a larger fraction of plasma cholesterol in HDL. A manipulation that substantially changes HDL and apoB particle metabolism in humans may consequently produce a different lipoprotein response in an ordinary mouse. Genetically modified models may reproduce selected aspects of human physiology, but a model's suitability depends on the specific mechanism being tested. Before translating a result, students should ask whether the enzyme, transporter and particle distribution central to the proposed mechanism are represented in the experimental system.

The term reverse cholesterol transport is often used as though it describes a single reaction. It is more useful to draw it as a sequence of compartments with alternative routes. In a simplified model, an increase in transfer from peripheral cells to HDL is followed by either direct transfer of lipid to liver or transfer through other carriers. The liver must subsequently handle the delivered cholesterol, and the fraction ultimately eliminated depends on biliary and intestinal processes. If an intervention accelerates the first step while slowing a later step, the steady-state mass of cholesterol in intermediate compartments can rise. This can occur without an equivalent rise in net removal from the organism.

Consider the interpretation of an experiment in which HDL-C doubles after genetic alteration of a hepatic receptor. A plausible explanation is that cholesterol-rich HDL particles are no longer unloading lipid efficiently. A different explanation is that peripheral cells have increased delivery of cholesterol into HDL. Both generate an increase in measured HDL-C, but the predicted changes in tracer arrival at the liver differ. Measuring only the final HDL-C concentration would not distinguish the alternatives. This example demonstrates why the apparent desirability of a concentration change cannot substitute for an understanding of its mechanism.

5. Hepatic fatty acid balance and the origin of steatosis

Hepatic steatosis means an abnormal accumulation of lipid, principally triglyceride, within hepatocytes. The quantity of triglyceride present at one moment is a pool. The rates of entry, synthesis, oxidation and secretion are fluxes. A pool grows when its net input exceeds net output, even if both input and output are high. This elementary observation explains why fatty liver may coexist with increased lipid export from the liver: export may have risen, but not sufficiently to match the greater substrate supply.

A simple organ-scale model tracks fatty acid equivalents in the hepatic esterifiable lipid pool. Let U represent uptake of exogenous fatty acids, D the contribution of newly synthesised fatty acids, O net removal through oxidation, and E net export of esterified fatty acids. All fluxes must use the same units, for example millimoles of fatty acid equivalents per hour. Under a simplified boundary in which other exchange terms are negligible, the change in the pool is U plus D minus O minus E. The assumption must be stated because actual hepatocytes contain multiple interconnected lipid pools, and free fatty acids can be oxidised before ever being esterified.

Suppose an illustrative liver takes up 12 millimoles of fatty acid equivalents per hour and synthesises 4, while removing 9 by oxidation and exporting 5. The net balance is 12 plus 4 minus 9 minus 5, or positive 2 millimoles per hour. If export increases to 6 but uptake increases to 16 and the other terms stay constant, the balance becomes positive 5. Thus the liver has increased its export rate but its accumulation rate has also increased. Neither an elevated export rate nor an elevated plasma triglyceride measurement proves protection against steatosis.

During ordinary fasting, falling insulin permits adipose lipolysis, supplying nonesterified fatty acids to the liver. Oxidation supports hepatic ATP production and can supply acetyl-CoA for ketone body formation. In the fed state insulin normally suppresses adipose release, while carbon from carbohydrate can support fatty acid synthesis through the coordinated actions of lipogenic enzymes. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase forms malonyl-CoA, which supplies fatty acid synthesis and inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase I. This provides an example of regulation coupling synthesis with mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acids. It does not imply that oxidation ceases completely whenever synthesis increases.

In insulin-resistant states, suppression of adipose lipolysis may become less effective. The liver can then receive excess fatty acid substrate even when circulating insulin is elevated. At the same time, lipogenesis can be supported by carbohydrate availability and insulin-responsive pathways such as SREBP-1c and glucose-responsive pathways involving ChREBP. Selective insulin resistance is a useful description of a situation in which particular insulin-regulated outcomes become impaired while other responses are relatively preserved. The phrase should not be interpreted as proof that every hepatocyte retains one perfectly functioning branch and loses another completely. Substrate availability, indirect hormonal actions and tissue-level heterogeneity also contribute.

Hepatic triglyceride storage has both protective and potentially pathological aspects. Esterifying fatty acids into neutral triglyceride can reduce exposure of membranes and signalling systems to certain more reactive lipid intermediates. However, persistent oversupply may alter lipid composition, induce organelle stress and eventually accompany cell injury. The presence of a large triglyceride droplet is therefore not sufficient evidence that triglyceride itself is the direct toxic species. An experiment that lowers hepatic triglyceride by blocking esterification could increase the abundance of harmful intermediates unless the remaining fatty acids are adequately removed or utilised.

An important human tracer investigation by Donnelly and colleagues in 2005 examined fatty acid sources in liver tissue and secreted lipoproteins of nine individuals undergoing medically indicated liver biopsy. Stable isotope labelling allowed the investigators to distinguish contributions from circulating nonesterified fatty acids, dietary fat and new fatty acid synthesis. The study established that the stored hepatic lipid could derive from multiple sources, including substantial contributions from peripheral fatty acid delivery and endogenous synthesis. Its small selected cohort and particular feeding and labelling conditions mean that source fractions cannot be assumed identical for every individual or disease stage. The broader inference is that hepatic lipid origin cannot be determined simply by measuring the fat content of a meal.

The liver's position in the circulation creates an unusual combination of substrate sources. Its portal input reflects products released by the gastrointestinal tract and other organs drained by the portal circulation. Its arterial input reflects systemic metabolism. Hepatocytes can therefore encounter changes in carbohydrate supply and fatty acid delivery on different timescales after a meal. Local concentrations also vary within the hepatic lobule as blood moves from portal regions towards central veins. Oxygen, substrate and signalling gradients contribute to metabolic zonation, so measurements averaged across the entire liver may conceal regional differences. Detailed liver zonation is not needed to understand the balance equation, but it is an important limitation when relating a whole-organ average to the behaviour of an individual cell.

De novo lipogenesis transforms carbon from nonlipid precursors into fatty acids. Carbohydrate-derived pyruvate can supply mitochondrial acetyl-CoA; citrate export permits transfer of carbon to the cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool. Formation of malonyl-CoA commits material to fatty acid synthesis, and fatty acid synthase uses reducing power to construct fatty acid chains. The precise reaction sequence was introduced in biomolecule metabolism. Here its physiological significance is that excess substrate entering liver as carbohydrate can contribute to stored lipid even when dietary triglyceride intake is unchanged. The conversion requires energy and reducing equivalents, so it is not a spontaneous chemical conversion driven solely by the presence of glucose.

Regulation of fatty acid synthesis includes acute changes in enzyme activity and slower changes in the abundance of enzymes. AMPK activation during energy stress can phosphorylate and inhibit acetyl-CoA carboxylase, reducing malonyl-CoA formation and potentially relieving inhibition of mitochondrial fatty acid entry. Transcriptional responses to nutrient availability alter synthetic capacity on longer timescales. These mechanisms operate within a network of substrate, energy and hormonal signals. The direction of a particular enzyme's regulatory change does not automatically establish the measured whole-organ flux: substrate concentration, alternative enzyme regulation and availability of other required reactants may alter the final rate.

The relationship between lipid oxidation and ketogenesis deserves particular attention. Beta oxidation produces acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents. During prolonged fasting, the liver can convert acetyl-CoA into ketone bodies when hormonal and substrate conditions favour that route. Ketones released into circulation provide fuel for tissues with the enzymes needed to utilise them. The liver itself lacks the usual ketone utilisation enzyme SCOT and therefore exports ketones rather than using them as a major fuel. A change in circulating ketone concentration may reflect production, utilisation or both; it is not automatically a direct measurement of hepatic beta oxidation. Similarly, fatty liver does not imply that oxidation or ketogenesis is absent.

An organ may simultaneously store lipid and oxidise fatty acids because different molecules move through different reactions at the same time. At the scale of a whole liver, spatial heterogeneity and temporal variation further weaken a binary interpretation of synthesis versus oxidation. A labelled fatty acid appearing in liver triglyceride does not imply that all incoming fatty acids were stored. Some may be oxidised, incorporated into phospholipid, exported in VLDL or retained in intermediates. To construct a meaningful mass balance, investigators must define which pool is being followed and which fates are counted as input, output or exchange.

Hepatic VLDL export presents a useful counterexample to the assumption that all metabolic outcomes move together. A reduction in lipid export may lower the triglyceride load entering circulation while increasing retention in hepatocytes. Human genetic variation affecting TM6SF2 is an example in which liver fat and circulating lipids can change in opposite directions, consistent with altered hepatic lipoprotein handling. Variants affecting lipid droplet metabolism, including PNPLA3, provide another illustration of heterogeneity. Such examples do not imply that every carrier has the same phenotype; background genetics and environment modify outcomes. Their value is to show that hepatic fat accumulation and atherogenic particle exposure are separate endpoints connected by particular mechanisms.

6. Dyslipidaemia as disturbed particle turnover

Dyslipidaemia describes an abnormal pattern of lipid or lipoprotein concentrations in the circulation. The word does not specify a single molecular lesion. The pattern can reflect excess cholesterol carried in LDL, excess triglyceride in triglyceride-rich particles, reduced HDL-C, abnormal remnant clearance, or a combination. A useful mechanistic classification asks whether the primary disturbance lies in particle formation, enzymatic remodelling or receptor-mediated removal. This classification is more informative for physiological reasoning than treating every lipid abnormality as an identical manifestation of energy surplus.

Consider a plasma compartment containing a particular class of particles. If the number of particles entering the compartment per hour is P and the fractional removal rate is k per hour, the simplest first-order approximation gives a removal flux kN, where N is the number of particles present. At steady state, the equality P = kN leads to N = P/k. The model assumes constant production, homogeneous particles and a removal process proportional to the current pool size. Real lipoprotein metabolism involves multiple compartments and saturable receptor processes, but the approximation reveals why concentration alone cannot identify production and clearance separately.

In a hypothetical plasma compartment a class of particles enters at 120 arbitrary particle units per hour and is removed with a fractional rate constant of 0.2 per hour. Its steady-state pool is 600 units. Halving the fractional removal constant while keeping input unchanged raises the predicted pool to 1200 units. Doubling production without altering clearance produces the same pool. In both cases the resulting concentration would be identical if plasma volume were fixed. A tracer measurement of the rate of disappearance of particles would distinguish the two models, whereas a static concentration measurement would not.

A common metabolic pattern in insulin resistance consists of increased circulating triglyceride, increased representation of triglyceride-rich remnants, reduced HDL-C and changes in LDL size and composition. The liver receives substrate from adipose tissue and can increase VLDL triglyceride export. If the clearance of triglyceride-rich particles cannot keep pace, their residence time increases. The increase in plasma triglyceride in this setting need not be attributable exclusively to hepatic secretion or to a defect in LPL; both input and removal may be altered. The relative importance of the processes varies between individuals and physiological conditions.

An increase in triglyceride-rich particles also affects HDL and LDL indirectly. CETP-mediated exchange can transfer triglyceride into HDL and LDL in exchange for cholesteryl ester. Subsequent lipase activity may remodel triglyceride-enriched particles into smaller forms. Smaller HDL particles and lipid-poor apolipoprotein may be cleared differently from their larger counterparts. LDL may become relatively small and dense. These changes explain why concentrations of the three conventionally measured lipid fractions can change together even though each fraction participates in a different physiological route.

The concentration of LDL-C need not rise substantially in an insulin-resistant individual whose apoB-containing particle concentration increases. If particles carry less cholesterol on average, a higher number of particles can transport the same total cholesterol mass. Thus normal-looking LDL-C relative to a comparison group does not necessarily imply an identical exposure of tissues to apoB particles. Conversely, an elevated LDL-C concentration can reflect an increased cholesterol content per particle as well as increased particle number. In an exam problem, a statement about the amount of cholesterol must not be silently replaced by a statement about particle count.

Familial defects help separate mechanisms that are difficult to distinguish in a complex metabolic phenotype. Impaired LDL receptor function increases circulating LDL principally by reducing removal. Severe loss of LPL activity, by contrast, directly interferes with hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich particles. Altered ligand recognition on remnants changes their hepatic clearance. These mechanisms show how different molecular failures can produce distinct lipid profiles, even though all are classified broadly as dyslipidaemia. They also explain why a disorder of cholesterol transport is not necessarily equivalent to a disorder of triglyceride delivery.

A physiological disturbance can also originate outside lipid metabolism. Thyroid hormone status affects expression and activity of proteins involved in cholesterol turnover, while endocrine disorders can alter adipose lipolysis and hepatic substrate availability. Renal disease and impaired hepatic function can influence lipoprotein synthesis, remodelling and clearance. These examples are useful because they demonstrate cross-organ dependence; detailed diagnosis and clinical management are separate topics. A lipid profile should be treated as an observation of a physiological network rather than as a complete description of the network itself.

A steady-state equation must be distinguished from a transient response. If particle production changes suddenly, the plasma pool does not immediately reach the value predicted by the new steady-state equation. Instead, the difference between input and removal determines the instantaneous direction of change. Suppose a compartment contains 600 particle units and receives 120 units per hour. If its removal constant falls from 0.2 to 0.1 per hour, removal immediately becomes 60 units per hour. The initial accumulation rate is therefore 60 units per hour. As the pool grows, the removal flux increases despite the reduced fractional clearance. Eventually, in the idealised model, the pool reaches 1200 units and removal again matches production. This example explains why a new steady state can have the same total removal flux as the old state yet require a larger circulating pool.

Fractional clearance and absolute clearance are easy to confuse. Fractional clearance describes the fraction of a pool removed per unit time. Absolute clearance in the preceding particle model is the number of particles removed per unit time. If two steady-state groups have identical input rates but different fractional removal rates, the absolute removal rates are identical under the assumed model. A student should not conclude that the group with impaired fractional clearance necessarily has fewer particles removed each hour at its established steady state. Instead, the larger pool is required to sustain an equal total rate of disposal. This distinction appears repeatedly in physiological models involving hormone turnover, renal handling and circulating metabolites.

Tracer disappearance offers a way to investigate fractional turnover. For an ideal single compartment receiving no additional labelled particles, the labelled pool decreases exponentially when removal is first order. If the fractional removal constant is 0.2 per hour, the half-life is the natural logarithm of two divided by 0.2, approximately 3.47 hours. If the constant falls to 0.1 per hour, the half-life doubles to approximately 6.93 hours. The estimate assumes negligible recycling of label, rapid mixing within the measured compartment and a constant rate constant. In an actual experiment, conversion between lipoprotein classes, loss of labelled cargo before removal of the carrier, and delayed mixing can invalidate a single exponential description.

A tracer attached to triglyceride cargo answers a different question from a tracer attached to the structural apolipoprotein. During lipolysis, labelled fatty acids can leave the particle while apoB remains with the remnant. If only the disappearance of triglyceride label is measured, rapid loss could reflect delivery of fatty acids to tissue without removal of the remnant particle from circulation. An apoB-based tracer can instead follow particle conversion and disposal, subject to appropriate modelling of new secretion and label incorporation. The same initial lipoprotein can thus yield two different turnover curves, neither of which should automatically be called wrong. They represent different objects moving through the system.

There are two particularly instructive genetic perturbations of triglyceride handling. A loss of catalytic LPL activity prevents efficient removal of triglyceride from susceptible circulating particles. A loss of a protein required to deliver LPL to its endothelial location can generate a related functional phenotype even when the LPL gene itself is intact. Conversely, defects that diminish production of apoB-containing particles can limit lipid export from the tissues that assemble them. These examples reveal why the phenotype of an enzyme pathway need not be caused by mutation of the enzyme's own coding sequence. Proper transport, cofactors and assembly factors are part of the mechanism.

ApoE-mediated remnant clearance further illustrates the importance of molecular recognition. A remnant has lost much of the triglyceride it originally carried but retains cholesterol and proteins that determine its later fate. If recognition by hepatic uptake pathways is reduced, remnants may accumulate even when the initial hydrolysis step remains functional. This differs from the mechanism of impaired LPL activity, where particles may retain an unusually large triglyceride load. Comparisons of particle composition can therefore suggest which step is disturbed. Such comparisons must be interpreted with caution because hepatic secretion, intestinal appearance and competing clearance routes can also alter the distribution.

A researcher testing an association between insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia should distinguish a difference in mean lipid concentrations between groups from an experimentally demonstrated change in turnover. Matching groups for meal timing and relevant biological characteristics improves the interpretation but does not establish causality. A controlled intervention that alters insulin responsiveness, accompanied by measurement of fatty acid flux and VLDL kinetics, addresses the mechanism more directly. Even then, an intervention may alter multiple metabolic processes simultaneously. An appropriately narrow conclusion might state that a change in substrate availability accompanied a change in VLDL export, rather than asserting that one molecular pathway was the sole cause of the entire lipid phenotype.

7. From hepatic steatosis to metabolic liver injury

Steatotic liver disease is an umbrella term for disorders characterised by excess hepatic fat. An important subset is associated with metabolic dysfunction. A multisociety consensus published in 2023 introduced the name metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) to replace the former NAFLD terminology, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) to replace NASH. The older terminology remains frequent in foundational research articles. When comparing older and newer studies, a reader should recognise that the diagnostic frameworks overlap substantially but are not exactly interchangeable, rather than mechanically changing an author's original study population.

Steatosis and steatohepatitis describe different biological features. Steatosis refers to lipid accumulation; steatohepatitis additionally involves hepatic inflammatory injury, including characteristic hepatocellular damage. A person with steatosis does not necessarily progress to steatohepatitis, and the latter does not inevitably progress to fibrosis or cirrhosis. Fibrosis is the accumulation of extracellular matrix following persistent injury and altered repair responses. Cirrhosis denotes advanced architectural remodelling with widespread fibrotic changes and regenerative nodules. The distinctions matter because fat accumulation, cellular damage, matrix deposition and altered organ architecture are separate outcomes that can vary partly independently.

The existence of a large hepatic triglyceride pool raises a question: which aspect of lipid metabolism damages cells? Neutral triglyceride is stored inside lipid droplets and can act as a buffer against excess fatty acids. When the availability of particular fatty acids, sterols or lipid intermediates exceeds the capacity to process or compartmentalise them, membranes and organelles can become stressed. Saturated lipid exposure, unesterified cholesterol and some signalling intermediates are among the factors implicated in lipotoxicity. Their importance depends on chemical species, location, metabolic context and duration. An experiment detecting increased triglyceride cannot alone establish the abundance of any one harmful lipid species.

Mitochondria link fuel availability with energy metabolism. Increased hepatic delivery of fatty acids may initially stimulate oxidation as an adaptation to increased demand. The relationship between oxidation and injury is not necessarily linear: an increased oxidative flux does not prove healthy mitochondrial function, and a decrease in measured respiratory activity can reflect several mechanisms. If oxidative metabolism and electron transfer are poorly matched to available substrates, reducing equivalents, oxygen supply and ATP demand, reactive oxygen species may increase. Antioxidant defences and cellular repair systems determine whether the resulting stress is transient or damaging.

The endoplasmic reticulum is also closely involved in lipid synthesis, membrane organisation and lipoprotein assembly. Altered membrane lipid composition or excessive metabolic demand may interfere with its functions. An unfolded protein response is an adaptive attempt to restore protein-folding homeostasis; sustained or excessive stress can activate signalling pathways that favour inflammation or cell death. The same type of response may initially protect cells and later participate in injury under different conditions. This illustrates a general principle encountered in the stress physiology subunit: the biological significance of a response depends on intensity, duration and the capacity for recovery.

Injured hepatocytes communicate with neighbouring cells. Resident liver macrophages, including Kupffer cells, respond to local signals and can contribute to inflammatory regulation. Hepatic stellate cells normally have functions distinct from the production of extensive scar tissue. In the context of sustained injury, their activation can lead to a matrix-producing phenotype. Profibrotic signals such as TGF-beta can favour extracellular matrix deposition. This links a metabolic disturbance inside hepatocytes to changes in the multicellular organisation and mechanical properties of the organ. Not all inflammatory signalling causes fibrosis, and fibrosis depends also on the rate at which extracellular matrix is degraded.

A liver with excess fat can retain substantial functional reserve. Blood markers released during hepatocyte injury and measurements of hepatic fat reflect different processes. A marker of cellular injury is not a direct measure of fat content or of fibrotic architecture. An imaging method estimating lipid fraction cannot, by that measurement alone, distinguish all histological patterns of inflammation and injury. In scientific reasoning, each observation must be mapped to the property it actually measures before it is used to support a conclusion about a disease mechanism.

The older model of sequential hits, in which fat accumulated first and a second insult produced injury, offered a simple conceptual separation of steatosis and inflammation. A more integrated account allows multiple processes to interact over time: substrate excess, impaired signalling, oxidative stress, immune responses, genetics and environmental influences may operate in parallel. The essential lesson is not to memorise a universal chronological sequence but to distinguish causal mechanisms from correlated features. A time-course experiment showing that an intermediate rises before an injury marker is informative, but necessity and sufficiency require additional perturbations and appropriate controls.

The observation that triglyceride storage can be protective in some contexts is best understood by comparing two theoretical cells with the same incoming fatty acid load. Cell A esterifies a considerable fraction of the fatty acids into neutral storage lipid, while cell B has restricted esterification capacity. If neither cell can increase oxidation or export, cell B may accumulate more free fatty acids and other intermediates despite forming fewer large droplets. Measuring droplets alone would make cell B appear less steatotic. Measuring viability and membrane stress could reveal a different result. This comparison clarifies why an endpoint that is easy to image is not necessarily the endpoint responsible for cell injury.

A lipid droplet has a surface monolayer enclosing a neutral lipid core. It interacts dynamically with enzymes responsible for lipid synthesis and mobilisation and with other organelles. The droplet is not an inert deposit. Its formation and turnover influence substrate access to oxidation and export pathways. Altered droplet-associated proteins can change the partitioning of fatty acids without changing the amount of fatty acid entering the cell. In mechanistic studies, measurements of droplet size, number and total neutral lipid may therefore be accompanied by measurements of lipid composition and flux. A change in droplet number alone cannot reveal the direction of fatty acid turnover.

Lipotoxicity should be regarded as a description of harmful consequences of particular lipid exposures, not as a single universal chemical mechanism. An excess of fatty acid can influence signalling by altering the abundance or location of lipid-derived intermediates. In some models, diacylglycerol contributes to activation of protein kinase C isoforms, which may interfere with aspects of insulin signalling. Ceramides can also influence insulin-responsive pathways under particular conditions. Neither class should be treated as uniformly harmful in every cellular location: many lipid intermediates have essential physiological roles, and their molecular species and compartmental distribution matter. The evidence for a proposed mechanism is strongest when perturbation of the implicated lipid species changes the signalling outcome without simply changing every other metabolic variable.

Fibrosis represents a change in the balance between synthesis and degradation of extracellular matrix. A sustained signal favouring matrix deposition can increase collagen content over time even if the rate of synthesis at a later measurement has returned towards its earlier value. This is analogous to the pool and flux distinction used for triglyceride. In a simplified matrix compartment, an increase in synthesis above degradation results in net deposition. If injury subsides and degradation exceeds synthesis, the matrix pool may decrease. Tissue architecture and the properties of the deposited material influence the extent to which this mathematical possibility translates into structural recovery. The model should not be interpreted as a guarantee of reversibility in every disease stage.

Liver inflammation is also not a homogeneous phenomenon. Resident macrophages can respond to damage-associated signals, and circulating immune cells can enter the tissue under appropriate conditions. Different cell populations may participate in injury, clearance of cellular debris or repair. Measuring one inflammatory mediator in a liver homogenate averages the contributions of many cells. A decrease in that mediator could reflect reduced production per cell, a change in the number of producing cells or a change in the distribution of cells within the tissue. A cell-specific experiment or spatial observation is needed to distinguish these possibilities.

An experimental comparison of animals with hepatic steatosis but different levels of fibrosis illustrates the need for independent endpoints. If the animals have similar hepatic triglyceride concentrations and different collagen content, the result suggests that fat accumulation alone does not fully determine matrix deposition. It does not establish that hepatic lipid is unrelated to fibrosis. Differences in lipid composition, duration of exposure, susceptibility to injury and repair responses could explain the divergence. A time-matched comparison and a direct perturbation of a candidate injury pathway would provide more specific information than a single cross-sectional observation.

Human biopsy studies provide information about tissue architecture and cell-level injury that cannot be inferred reliably from circulating lipid levels. However, biopsy samples represent a limited region of a heterogeneous organ, and medically selected participants may differ from a broader population. Imaging can assess a larger proportion of the liver but may not provide the same cellular detail. These strengths and limitations explain why different experimental methods are often used together. For exam reasoning, a proposed measurement should be selected to match the biological variable in the hypothesis: lipid fraction, cell injury, inflammation and fibrotic architecture require distinct evidence.

8. Metabolic syndrome and the loss of interorgan coordination

Metabolic syndrome is a term for a recurring cluster of cardiometabolic abnormalities rather than a single disease with one invariant cause. Its familiar components concern central adiposity, glucose regulation, blood pressure, triglyceride and HDL cholesterol. Historical definitions differ in their requirements and how component measurements are interpreted. The 2009 joint interim statement attempted to harmonise major definitions. For physiology, the cluster is valuable because it points to interacting regulatory systems. It should not be treated as evidence that every component necessarily appears together or that a single laboratory variable proves all the others.

Adipose tissue is an important buffer between dietary energy supply and the needs of other organs. When energy intake exceeds immediate utilisation, adipocytes can store fatty acids as triglyceride. Expansion through enlargement of existing cells and recruitment of new adipocytes differs between depots and individuals. If storage capacity and metabolic regulation are impaired, fatty acid release and delivery to nonadipose tissues may rise. The consequence is not simply more fat everywhere: tissue-specific uptake, oxidative demand and lipid export determine where substrate accumulates and how local cells respond.

Insulin-resistant skeletal muscle may take up less glucose in response to insulin, especially under conditions in which insulin normally stimulates GLUT4 recruitment to the cell surface. Reduced muscle glucose disposal changes the distribution of meal-derived substrate. The pancreas may compensate by increasing insulin secretion, maintaining glucose homeostasis for a time. The liver may continue to receive substrates for lipid synthesis and may not suppress glucose output appropriately. Adipose tissue may release fatty acids despite increased circulating insulin. Each change modifies the conditions experienced by the other organs, creating a network in which the apparent effect of one hormone differs by tissue and physiological state.

The liver is not simply a passive recipient of these altered fluxes. It can release glucose, ketones, lipid carriers and signalling molecules. Increases in hepatic lipid export influence skeletal muscle, adipose tissue and the vascular system. Liver-derived signals such as FGF21 may respond to particular nutritional or stress conditions, but the concentration of such a signal alone does not establish its efficacy in a target tissue. Hormone concentration, receptor responsiveness and downstream physiological effects are distinct variables. An elevated concentration can represent a compensatory response rather than the initiating cause of dysfunction.

Adipose-derived signals also have context-dependent effects. Adiponectin is associated with aspects of fatty acid oxidation and insulin sensitivity; altered secretion or responsiveness may accompany adipose dysfunction. Inflammatory cytokines produced by immune cells and adipose-associated cells can influence insulin signalling, while many of the same molecules serve useful functions under other conditions. It is important not to equate every elevation in a cytokine with a harmful chronic inflammatory response. Duration, tissue source, concentration, receptor signalling and local environment determine the net effect.

Blood pressure provides an additional connection between metabolic and circulatory regulation. The sympathetic nervous system influences vascular resistance and cardiac output, while renal regulation of sodium and water influences extracellular fluid volume. Hormones affecting renal sodium handling and vascular function can be altered in some insulin-resistant states. Endothelial signalling affects the ability of vessels to dilate in response to physiological stimuli. The presence of metabolic disturbances does not imply one obligatory mechanism of hypertension, but these pathways explain why disturbances in fuel regulation can coexist with altered cardiovascular homeostasis.

The cluster of metabolic findings must also be interpreted in the context of genetic and environmental diversity. Individuals with similar body mass can differ in visceral fat distribution, adipose storage capacity, physical fitness and insulin sensitivity. Some people with little excess overall adiposity have hepatic steatosis or other disturbances, while others with substantial adiposity do not exhibit the same severity of metabolic impairment. Body mass and waist measurements can be useful population-level indicators, but neither is equivalent to a direct assessment of tissue-specific metabolic flux.

A useful way to integrate the syndrome is to construct a causal diagram with separate variables for insulin responsiveness, adipose fatty acid release, hepatic lipid and glucose output, particle clearance and vascular response. An arrow should represent a specified influence, not merely two features that coexist. Feedback arrows may be needed because changes in one organ can amplify or compensate for disturbances elsewhere. Alternative causal diagrams can explain the same cross-sectional observation. Longitudinal or intervention data are therefore needed to distinguish a common cause from a chain of consequences.

Compensation is a defining feature of homeostatic regulation. If insulin becomes less effective at stimulating a physiological response, increasing secretion may partially restore that response. A compensated animal can therefore have a near-normal measured glucose concentration despite substantial changes in hormone secretion and tissue responsiveness. However, the compensation may not correct all organ-specific disturbances equally. The insulin concentration needed to maintain muscle glucose disposal may not produce the same degree of suppression of adipose lipolysis or hepatic glucose output as before. A normal value of a regulated variable does not establish that the underlying controller is operating normally.

The pancreas and liver communicate through a route that creates a gradient between the hormone concentration encountered by liver and that measured in systemic blood. Insulin secreted into the portal circulation undergoes hepatic extraction before the remaining hormone reaches other tissues. Changes in secretion and extraction can both alter systemic insulin concentration. In an experiment reporting higher peripheral insulin, it is therefore insufficient to infer an identical proportional rise in pancreatic secretion without information about clearance. C-peptide measurements, interpreted with suitable kinetic assumptions, can provide complementary information about endogenous insulin secretion. This example illustrates how interorgan regulation includes hormone disposal as well as hormone production.

The relationship between skeletal muscle and adipose metabolism is especially apparent during exercise. Contraction increases energy demand and can stimulate glucose uptake by mechanisms that are not wholly dependent on the insulin signalling pathway. Thus an insulin-resistant muscle may retain a capacity to increase substrate utilisation when activated by contraction, although the magnitude of response can vary. Increased substrate demand also alters the circulating availability of fuels and can influence adipose release and hepatic output. When interpreting metabolic measurements taken before and after an activity challenge, the timing relative to contraction and recovery is essential. A fasting observation made many hours later is not a direct measurement of the acute contraction response.

A change in body fat distribution can be metabolically significant even if the total amount of stored fat remains unchanged. Different adipose depots have distinct anatomical relationships to the circulation, differences in cellular properties and patterns of hormonal responsiveness. Visceral adipose drainage can influence the portal environment, whereas much subcutaneous adipose release enters systemic circulation. The absolute contribution of each depot to circulating fatty acids also depends on its size and release rate. A depot that releases more fatty acid per gram need not be the largest whole-body source if its total mass is much smaller than that of another depot. The distinction between rates per unit mass and total organ output is another example of the need to specify the denominator in a physiological measurement.

The connection between metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular physiology can be expressed in terms of feedback from blood flow to substrate delivery. Tissue uptake depends partly on the arrival of substrate at capillaries, which is influenced by perfusion and microvascular recruitment. Changes in endothelial function may modify local delivery of hormones and nutrients. However, increased blood flow does not by itself ensure increased uptake if membrane transport or intracellular metabolism is limiting. Similarly, impaired cellular insulin action can occur despite normal bulk perfusion. A tissue-level measurement of uptake should therefore be interpreted alongside the factors governing delivery and extraction.

A useful integrated comparison concerns two hypothetical animals with the same whole-body energy intake and expenditure over a measured period. One has greater capacity for adipose expansion and appropriate suppression of lipid release after meals. The other has impaired adipose storage and higher delivery of fatty acids to the liver. Energy balance alone does not predict that the two animals have identical liver lipid content. The location and chemical form of energy storage can differ even when the net whole-body balance is the same. The comparison does not violate conservation of energy; it shows that conservation at one system boundary does not specify the partitioning of material among internal compartments.

The metabolic syndrome is thus best used as an organising framework for analysing patterns, not as a replacement for mechanism. For each observation, identify a candidate source organ, a transport route, a recipient tissue and an expected functional response. Then ask what measurement would distinguish failure of signal production from failure of signal recognition or a downstream limitation in substrate processing. This approach also helps connect previous Unit 7 topics: endocrine feedback sets regulatory signals, circulation carries them, digestion changes substrate appearance, renal and vascular physiology influence pressure and perfusion, and neural control coordinates acute behavioural and autonomic adjustments.

9. Lipoprotein exposure, endothelium and atherosclerosis

The vascular endothelium is a metabolically active interface between blood and the tissues. Endothelial cells regulate permeability, coagulation-related responses, interactions with circulating leukocytes and the release of mediators influencing vascular tone. Nitric oxide generated by endothelial nitric oxide synthase can diffuse to neighbouring smooth muscle and promote relaxation through cyclic GMP signalling. This pathway connects metabolic signals to blood flow and nutrient delivery. Altered endothelial function is therefore relevant to both cardiovascular and metabolic physiology.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic disease of the arterial wall, not a simple deposition of dietary fat inside a blood vessel lumen. Atherogenic apoB-containing lipoproteins can enter the arterial intima and be retained through interactions with extracellular matrix components. Retention increases the time available for particle modification and for interactions with cells within the wall. LDL and certain remnant particles are important in this process; the largest newly formed chylomicrons are generally too large to participate in intimal entry in the same way. The rate of entry, probability of retention, particle residence and local vascular conditions together influence lesion development.

Retained lipid can stimulate responses involving endothelial cells, recruited monocytes and resident arterial cells. Monocytes can differentiate into macrophages, which take up modified LDL through scavenger receptor pathways. Unlike the tightly feedback-regulated classical LDL receptor pathway, uptake of modified lipoprotein through some scavenger receptors can continue despite intracellular lipid accumulation. Cholesteryl ester-rich lipid droplets produce the characteristic appearance of foam cells. Vascular smooth muscle cells can also contribute to foam-cell populations and other lesion components, so a foam cell should not automatically be identified as a macrophage solely from its lipid-rich appearance.

A developing lesion includes changes in extracellular matrix and in the cellular composition of the arterial wall. Smooth muscle cells can migrate and contribute matrix material, forming part of a fibrous cap over lipid-rich regions. Cell death, inefficient removal of dead cells, inflammatory activity and matrix turnover influence lesion structure. The biological consequences of a plaque depend not only on its size but on its composition, location and interaction with the overlying endothelium. This is why the disease cannot be reduced to a simple proportional relationship between lumen diameter and a single circulating lipid concentration.

If a plaque's surface is disrupted or eroded, circulating blood can encounter material that promotes platelet activation and coagulation. A thrombus may form, compromising blood flow. The acute event is mechanistically distinct from the slow accumulation of lipid and matrix within the arterial wall. The preceding subunits on haemostasis and cardiovascular physiology supply the relevant framework: vascular injury, platelet responses and the coagulation system act together. A chronic metabolic exposure can thereby contribute to an acute disturbance of organ perfusion.

Evidence for a causal role of LDL in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease extends beyond correlation. Inherited alterations affecting LDL levels, prospective observations and controlled interventions provide different kinds of evidence. Their agreement strengthens the inference that exposure to LDL contributes causally to disease. The 2017 European Atherosclerosis Society consensus synthesises these lines of evidence. This does not imply that LDL alone determines every event, that all carriers are equivalent, or that the risk of a specific individual can be inferred from one measurement. The time spent exposed to atherogenic particles and the other features of the arterial environment remain important.

HDL-C, by comparison, is not a simple causal inverse of LDL-C. A negative association between HDL-C and disease in a population could reflect accompanying changes in triglyceride metabolism, insulin sensitivity or other variables. A manipulation that increases the concentration of cholesterol carried by HDL without improving relevant cholesterol fluxes need not reduce arterial lipid retention or disease. When reasoning about causal claims, compare what was manipulated, what was measured, and whether changes in particle concentration represent the mechanism proposed to alter the outcome.

An illustrative experiment might compare two animal groups with similar plasma LDL-C but different arterial extracellular matrix properties. If one group retains more apoB-containing particles in the intima, it may develop more lipid accumulation despite equivalent measured cholesterol concentrations. To attribute the difference specifically to retention, investigators would need to examine other possibilities, including particle number, composition, arterial permeability and immune activation. This example connects cell adhesion and extracellular matrix principles to whole-animal metabolic pathology without requiring a specialised clinical account of cardiovascular disease.

To understand why duration matters in atherosclerosis, imagine that an artery encounters a constant concentration of atherogenic particles over a series of years. The probability of a particle entering and remaining in susceptible regions of the arterial wall depends on local properties, but repeated exposure supplies repeated opportunities for retention. If exposure doubles while all other factors are fixed, the cumulative opportunities for retention over the same interval may increase. This reasoning supports the qualitative distinction between a single concentration and cumulative exposure. It is not a precise equation for predicting an individual lesion because arterial properties, particle composition and repair mechanisms can change with time.

The geometry of blood flow helps explain why atherosclerotic lesions do not develop uniformly throughout the arterial tree. Local flow patterns can influence endothelial gene expression, barrier properties and signalling. Arterial branches and curved regions may encounter different haemodynamic forces than relatively straight segments. A circulating particle concentration is shared widely through the circulation, yet the local arterial environment differs by site. This distinction reconciles a systemic exposure with spatially heterogeneous disease. An experiment comparing lesion distribution should therefore consider anatomical location and local flow conditions rather than simply averaging the entire vascular system.

Nitric oxide production illustrates how metabolic signalling and vascular function can intersect. Endothelial insulin signalling can promote nitric oxide-related responses through pathways involving phosphoinositide 3-kinase and Akt. Oxidative conditions can influence the availability of nitric oxide, including through reactions that reduce its effective signalling. Impaired vasodilation is not proof of a single defect in nitric oxide synthesis, because altered smooth muscle responsiveness and other mediators can also influence the observed vascular response. A vessel preparation can be challenged with stimuli that depend on intact endothelium and with agents acting directly on smooth muscle to distinguish some of these possibilities.

Macrophage uptake and cholesterol efflux form another balance problem. Let uptake of lipoprotein-derived cholesterol be I and net removal by export be R, in identical mass-per-time units. If the intracellular cholesterol pool is not being diluted by growth or altered substantially by other reactions, accumulation occurs when I exceeds R. A large pool can persist even when the two rates are temporarily equal. In an illustrative cellular comparison, increasing efflux from 4 to 6 units per hour may reduce accumulation if uptake stays at 8, but the pool still grows at 2 units per hour. A cell-based efflux assay demonstrating improvement is therefore not sufficient evidence that the cell's total cholesterol pool is declining.

The arterial wall contains smooth muscle cells that exhibit substantial phenotypic plasticity. Under particular stimuli, cells may alter expression of proteins associated with contractile identity and extracellular matrix production. Such changes complicate the interpretation of markers used to assign cell origin in lesions. A marker expressed by a cell at the time of sampling does not necessarily reveal the lineage from which the cell developed. Lineage tracing in suitable animal models can clarify the origin of cells contributing to a lesion. This is an example of how developmental and cell biological methods inform the study of adult physiological disorders.

An important distinction in causal reasoning is between a biomarker, a mediator and an outcome. A biomarker is a measured feature that carries information about a biological condition. A mediator participates in the mechanism linking a cause to an outcome. The same variable can be both, but this cannot be assumed from correlation alone. For example, circulating triglyceride can mark changes in triglyceride-rich particle metabolism, whereas retained apoB-containing remnants and their cholesterol cargo are more directly relevant to arterial lipid deposition. The measurement of plasma triglyceride does not identify exactly how many remnant particles reached or remained in a particular arterial region.

The strength of causal evidence for LDL comes from the convergence of methods with different limitations. An inherited difference can influence exposure over long periods but may affect more than one biological pathway. A prospective observational study can establish temporal order but remains susceptible to confounding. A controlled intervention can test whether altering a proposed cause changes outcomes, although the intervention may have additional effects and the duration of observation is limited. When these lines of evidence agree with a plausible cellular mechanism of particle retention and lesion formation, the causal conclusion becomes more robust. A single experiment need not reproduce every stage of disease to contribute meaningfully to that conclusion.

10. Quantitative interpretation of metabolic measurements

A standard lipid profile reports concentrations of total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglyceride, together with an estimated or measured LDL cholesterol value. The word cholesterol in each fraction refers to the mass concentration of cholesterol assigned to particular particles. Total cholesterol is not the sum of cholesterol and triglyceride; these are different chemical classes and their measurements are reported separately. A common exam error is to subtract triglyceride directly from total cholesterol as if the two measurements quantified the same molecular pool.

Non-HDL cholesterol is calculated by subtracting HDL-C from total cholesterol. Both quantities must be expressed in the same units, such as milligrams per decilitre. The difference represents cholesterol associated with particles outside the HDL fraction, including LDL, IDL, VLDL remnants and other apoB-containing lipoproteins. In an illustrative sample with total cholesterol of 205 mg/dL and HDL-C of 45 mg/dL, non-HDL cholesterol is 160 mg/dL. This calculation does not require an assumption about the ratio of triglyceride to cholesterol in VLDL. It is nevertheless a cholesterol mass measurement rather than a direct particle count.

The historical Friedewald estimate uses total cholesterol minus HDL-C minus triglyceride divided by five when all concentrations are given in mg/dL. The triglyceride term estimates cholesterol carried in VLDL using an assumed average mass ratio. For a teaching sample with total cholesterol 205, HDL-C 45 and triglyceride 150 mg/dL, estimated VLDL-C is 30 mg/dL and estimated LDL-C is 130 mg/dL. The calculation is not an identity derived from conservation of cholesterol alone, because the ratio is an empirical approximation. The ratio varies with particle composition and metabolic state, and the estimate is unsuitable in some situations, particularly marked hypertriglyceridaemia. Modern laboratory methods may use different approaches.

Consider two theoretical samples with the same LDL-C of 100 arbitrary cholesterol units per volume. In sample A, each LDL particle carries an average of two such units, requiring 50 particles per volume. In sample B, each carries one unit, requiring 100 particles per volume. The cholesterol concentration is identical while the particle count differs twofold. This simplified arithmetic demonstrates why an apoB measurement may provide information not obtainable from LDL-C alone. In actual plasma, apoB is associated with several lipoprotein classes, so measured total apoB cannot automatically be equated with LDL particle count.

Arteriovenous balance experiments can help establish net substrate exchange by an organ. If flow is Q litres per minute, arterial substrate concentration is Ca millimoles per litre and venous concentration is Cv millimoles per litre, then Q times (Ca minus Cv) gives the net removal from blood across that organ in millimoles per minute. A hypothetical skeletal muscle bed receiving 0.4 litres of blood per minute, with arterial fatty acid concentration 0.6 mmol/L and venous concentration 0.4 mmol/L, has a net uptake of 0.08 mmol/min. This simple example assumes comparable flow and sampling bases, no significant changes in fluid volume and that measured concentrations and flow refer to the same compartment. Plasma concentrations should be paired with plasma flow unless appropriate corrections are made.

Net uptake can conceal simultaneous uptake and release. If a tissue extracts 0.12 mmol/min of labelled or unlabelled fatty acids and releases 0.04 mmol/min into the same circulation, the net removal is 0.08 mmol/min. An organ that extracts only 0.08 mmol/min and releases none produces the same net difference. A tracer can help separate unidirectional fluxes, provided labelling and compartmental assumptions are justified. This distinction is fundamental in metabolism because a measured concentration difference is often the result of several competing transport and reaction pathways.

The liver has two principal blood inputs, from the hepatic artery and portal vein. A hepatic arteriovenous calculation must therefore account for the weighted input concentrations and flow from both sources, as well as hepatic venous outflow. Multiplying a peripheral arterial-to-venous concentration difference by total hepatic flow while ignoring portal delivery could give a misleading estimate of liver exchange. This illustrates how anatomical knowledge constrains quantitative physiological reasoning: a mathematical equation is only useful if its boundaries correspond to the actual biological system.

A stable isotope tracer introduces a distinguishable form of a substrate or precursor. Measurement of enrichment over time can inform estimates of appearance, disappearance, source contribution or conversion between pools. A tracer is not a direct count of all molecules undergoing a reaction. Accurate inference requires an appropriate model, sufficient sampling and consideration of label recycling or dilution by unlabelled substrate. For example, labelled acetate incorporated into newly synthesised fatty acids can help estimate de novo lipogenesis, but the enrichment of the relevant precursor pool and the relation of measured lipid to the tissue of interest matter.

Finally, a tissue's lipid content can be estimated through imaging, chemical extraction or histological observation, with different trade-offs. An imaging-derived fat fraction provides an organ-scale property; histology reveals the localisation and appearance of material in sampled tissue; biochemical analysis measures the amount of selected molecules extracted from a specimen. None measures all aspects of metabolic function. A study seeking to establish whether increased adipose release causes liver fat accumulation should therefore combine appropriate flux measurements with independent information about hepatic lipid pools and injury rather than treating a single endpoint as proof of the entire proposed mechanism.

A simple comparison of lipid measurements should always retain their units. Cholesterol expressed in milligrams per decilitre can be converted to millimoles per litre using the molecular mass of cholesterol. Multiplication by approximately 0.02586 converts mg/dL to mmol/L. Triglyceride conversion uses a different approximate factor, conventionally about 0.01129, because triglycerides have a different assumed molecular mass. For illustration, 200 mg/dL cholesterol corresponds to approximately 5.17 mmol/L, while 150 mg/dL triglyceride corresponds to approximately 1.69 mmol/L using the conventional approximation. The numerical factor five in the historical Friedewald equation applies to mass units and must not be carried unchanged into a calculation using molar units.

A kinetic study may compare groups that have equal triglyceride concentrations but different rates of secretion and disappearance. Suppose a defined circulating triglyceride pool contains 300 arbitrary mass units in both groups. In group A, 30 units enter and leave each hour at steady state. In group B, 60 units enter and leave each hour. Although the pool sizes are identical, group B has twice the turnover. If the circulating pool is suddenly depleted by the same fraction in both groups and input remains unchanged, group B may restore its pool more quickly under the simple first-order model. A concentration measurement after equilibrium has been restored would conceal this difference in dynamics.

An integrated hepatic balance can be calculated from separate vascular inputs. Suppose hepatic arterial plasma flow is 0.3 L/min with a fatty acid concentration of 0.4 mmol/L. Portal plasma flow is 0.7 L/min with a concentration of 0.8 mmol/L. The total input is 0.12 plus 0.56, or 0.68 mmol/min. If hepatic venous plasma flow is 1.0 L/min with a concentration of 0.5 mmol/L, output is 0.50 mmol/min. The net removal from plasma is 0.18 mmol/min. This illustrative calculation does not establish whether the removed fatty acids were oxidised, stored or packaged into secreted lipoproteins. It also assumes steady flows, consistent concentration bases and negligible additional routes for the particular measured pool.

When source contributions are expressed as percentages, the reference total must be specified. Suppose labelled-source analysis attributes 60 percent of newly deposited hepatic fatty acids to circulating nonesterified fatty acids, 25 percent to new synthesis and 15 percent to meal-derived lipid in an illustrative experiment. If the total deposition flux is 20 mmol/h, these percentages correspond to 12, 5 and 3 mmol/h. If a later experiment observes a 30 percent contribution from synthesis but only 10 mmol/h total deposition, the absolute synthesis contribution is 3 mmol/h. An increased percentage is thus compatible with a decreased absolute rate. These numbers are hypothetical and are not presented as results of the Donnelly study.

A common error in comparing tracer experiments is to assume that the fraction of a lipid pool carrying label equals the fraction made through the pathway under investigation. Labelled precursors may be diluted by unlabelled precursors; newly labelled molecules may mix with a large pre-existing pool; different molecules may have different times available for incorporation. The relation between enrichment and pathway flux must account for these factors. If two groups receive the same amount of tracer but differ in endogenous precursor production, the precursor enrichment may differ. Normalising product enrichment to an appropriate estimate of precursor enrichment is therefore essential when making quantitative comparisons.

A well-designed tissue-specific perturbation provides a different type of evidence. For example, an investigator could alter adipocyte lipid mobilisation and examine the effects on hepatic fatty acid delivery and liver lipid accumulation. Suitable controls would include animals of comparable genetic background and physiological state, matched conditions of feeding and sampling, and verification that the intended perturbation affects adipose tissue. Measuring both circulating fatty acid flux and hepatic lipid content addresses separate links in the proposed pathway. If liver fat changes without the predicted change in fatty acid delivery, another mechanism may be involved. If delivery changes but liver fat does not, compensatory changes in oxidation or export may have balanced the altered input.

An observational association between hepatic fat and vascular disease could arise because both outcomes share determinants, such as altered adipose function and insulin responsiveness. It could also arise because liver metabolism changes circulating particle exposure, contributing to arterial disease. To distinguish those possibilities, the researcher should measure candidate mediating variables and consider genetic or experimental conditions in which liver fat and circulating lipid levels diverge. The TM6SF2 example illustrates why such counterexamples matter: the relationship observed across a broad population need not hold in the same way for every mechanism leading to steatosis. A causal network should be able to accommodate meaningful exceptions rather than dismissing them as inconvenient observations.

The final stage of Unit 7 reasoning is to connect a measurement to the level of organisation at which a prediction is made. A receptor phosphorylation assay concerns molecular signalling; a cellular uptake assay concerns transport and metabolism under defined conditions; an arteriovenous difference concerns net tissue exchange; a blood lipid profile concerns circulating pools; and an imaging or histological observation concerns tissue composition. A strong scientific explanation links these levels without conflating them. When an examination presents a dataset, first identify the variable actually measured, then infer the mechanisms consistent with it, and finally specify what extra information would distinguish those mechanisms. This procedure allows a student to apply the same principles to previously unfamiliar metabolic scenarios.
