1. Why plants need effector surveillance

A leaf that remains green in a field containing many microorganisms is not necessarily free from infection attempts. Its cuticle, cell wall and cell-surface immune receptors limit access to nutrients and prevent many microbes from multiplying. Yet some microorganisms are able to colonize particular plants despite these defenses. The decisive biological question is how a pathogen turns contact with a living plant into a successful association, and how the plant detects the molecular activities that make such an association possible. The previous chapter introduced the perception of microbial patterns. We now examine the additional problem created when a pathogen interferes with the very signaling system that should restrict its growth.

A plant cell possesses a plasma membrane that separates the cytoplasm from the apoplast, the extracellular continuum of walls and intercellular spaces. Pattern-recognition receptors generally recognize molecular information at the cell surface. Bacteria in the apoplast can nevertheless deliver certain proteins across the host plasma membrane. Other pathogens produce proteins at the interface between their feeding structures and the plant, and some of these molecules enter host cells. Pathogens therefore do not need to place their entire body inside a host cell in order to alter its intracellular physiology. This distinction between the position of the pathogen and the position of its secreted molecule is essential for understanding intracellular immunity.

An effector is a pathogen-derived molecule that changes a host process or the host–pathogen interface in a way relevant to the interaction. Many experimentally characterized effectors are proteins. Some alter signaling, some change transcription or protein turnover, and others modify the accessibility of nutrients. The term describes an activity in a biological interaction, not a particular chemical structure or a guaranteed direction of effect. A molecule that promotes the pathogen's growth in one host genotype may cause strong immune recognition in another. It is therefore misleading to equate the term effector with toxin, or to assume that all effectors kill plant cells.

Suppose a microbe activates a cell-surface receptor through a conserved structural pattern. A kinase associated with that receptor normally passes information to downstream proteins. An effector that decreases the kinase's activity may lower the speed or strength of the defense response. This could allow the microorganism to reproduce before antimicrobial compounds accumulate. However, the plant benefits from knowing that its signaling machinery has been disturbed. An intracellular surveillance protein may recognize the interfering molecule itself or detect a biochemical modification of a host target. Such recognition initiates an immune response referred to as effector-triggered immunity, or ETI.

The distinction between recognition and suppression is especially useful when a single effector has opposing consequences in different plant lines. In a susceptible line lacking an appropriate surveillance receptor, the effector's virulence function can dominate. In a resistant line, recognition may instead outweigh the virulence benefit. This is not a contradiction in the molecule's identity. The outcome depends on both genomes and on the rest of the cellular signaling machinery. A disease phenotype is a relationship between organisms rather than an unconditional property of the effector gene.

Immunity here means the capacity to perceive and respond to biologically relevant threats. Plants do not produce antibodies or rearrange lymphocyte antigen receptor genes. Their genetically encoded cell-surface and intracellular receptors operate in individual living cells and participate in tissue-wide communication. The diversity of resistance genes is generated over evolutionary and reproductive timescales, not by the animal adaptive immune mechanism. A plant that responds more effectively after an initial challenge may have been primed; priming does not imply the acquisition of a new rearranged immune receptor.

Three measurements must be distinguished from the beginning. Pathogen multiplication quantifies whether the invading organism can increase its population. Tissue damage records injury to the host, whether caused by pathogen activity, host defense or both. Host performance measures the functional consequences, such as growth, reproduction or yield. A plant that reduces pathogen multiplication is resistant in the relevant sense. A plant that sustains its performance despite a comparable pathogen burden is tolerant. A plant that exhibits conspicuous local cell death is not automatically more resistant or less tolerant than another plant. These outcomes can be experimentally separated.

The biological challenge involves both sensitivity and restraint. A receptor that activates too late may permit disease; a receptor that activates spontaneously may cause unnecessary defense and reduced growth. A highly specific receptor could detect a particular pathogen allele very efficiently yet fail against a different allele. A receptor that monitors a frequently targeted host process may detect more than one effector but must distinguish harmful changes from normal regulation. Thus plant immunity is a regulated surveillance problem, not merely an attempt to maximize cell death or the amount of antimicrobial chemicals produced.

To reason about ETI, trace the causal sequence from pathogen genotype to effector delivery, host target or receptor recognition, intracellular signaling and disease outcome. At every step, identify the observations that would establish the next arrow. An increase in a defense transcript indicates an altered host response but does not prove that an intracellular receptor directly binds an effector. A physical interaction between two purified proteins establishes biochemical association under the tested conditions but does not establish the resistance phenotype in a whole plant. A strong lesion may demonstrate local injury without proving that the pathogen has been restricted. Keeping these distinctions separate is the foundation of analytical examination questions.

2. How pathogens interfere with defense

A pathogen faces successive physiological obstacles. It must encounter a suitable host, reach an appropriate tissue, obtain nutrients and reproduce despite host defenses. For many bacterial pathogens, secretion of effectors is an important component of this process. The type III secretion system of several Gram-negative plant-pathogenic bacteria can translocate proteins into plant cells. The bacterium often remains extracellular in the apoplast while the delivered protein acts on intracellular targets. Secretion into the external medium and successful translocation into the host cytoplasm are distinct processes; demonstration of one does not automatically prove the other.

Fungal and oomycete pathogens use different mechanisms to interact with their hosts. Some form haustoria, specialized feeding interfaces surrounded by a host-derived membrane. Molecules released at these interfaces may act outside the host plasma membrane or, for particular effectors, inside the host cell. Not every pathogen forms haustoria, and not every effector crosses a membrane. Plant viruses already replicate within host cells and encode proteins that alter defense and host processes in that context. The diversity of these interactions explains why a definition based exclusively on bacterial injection would be too narrow.

Consider the early defense pathway activated by a microbial pattern. A surface receptor associates with signaling partners, kinases change phosphorylation states, calcium concentration can increase in the cytosol, and reactive oxygen species are produced. Many of these steps are attractive points for pathogen interference because inhibiting a shared signaling component can reduce several defensive outputs. A pathogen need not destroy every receptor individually if it can reduce a downstream activity used by several receptor systems. The result can be weaker cell-wall reinforcement, altered transcription or delayed closure of a natural opening. These are mechanistic consequences, not necessarily universal responses to every effector.

In the model bacterium Pseudomonas syringae, AvrPto and AvrPtoB illustrate the targeting of receptor-associated signaling. Their interference with host immune components helps explain why surface pattern recognition alone may not prevent multiplication of a compatible pathogen. The precise biochemical target and effect depend on the effector, receptor system and host background. A student should understand the causal idea: receptor recognition can be intact while transmission of the information is impaired. A ligand can bind its receptor normally even when downstream defensive outputs are reduced. Distinguish receptor abundance, ligand perception and kinase activity in an experimental interpretation.

An effector can also alter the state of a regulatory host protein. Protein phosphorylation, cleavage, acetylation and degradation are ordinary cellular regulatory processes. Pathogen proteins that influence these reactions can redirect the host's machinery. A protease may change the abundance of its target, whereas a protein that modifies a kinase may change its activity without causing degradation. Measuring total protein abundance is therefore not enough to establish activity. One can have an unchanged band on a conventional immunoblot but a profound change in a phosphorylation-dependent signaling response.

The presence of an effector does not prove that it is required for virulence. A pathogen may contain many effectors with overlapping functions. Deletion of one effector gene may produce little change in growth because other effectors compensate, or because the assay conditions do not require that particular function. In another host genotype, the same deletion may improve pathogen growth by removing a signal that a resistance receptor recognizes. This is one reason why a simple comparison of a wild-type pathogen with a deletion strain should be conducted in more than one host background.

A useful conceptual experiment uses two pathogen strains that differ only in a candidate effector gene and two host lines that differ only in a candidate recognition receptor. The effector-positive strain may multiply better than the deletion strain in a receptor-deficient host, while multiplying less well in a receptor-competent host. Restoring the effector gene in the deletion strain can test whether the observed change is attributable to the deleted locus. The experiment requires comparable starting pathogen populations and equivalent sampling conditions. A complemented strain that expresses the protein at an excessive level may produce an unusual phenotype, so restoration of the native expression context is preferable when interpreting normal virulence functions.

A catalytically inactive effector is a particularly informative comparison for an enzyme-like effector. If the inactive variant is delivered normally but does not modify its host target, its loss of virulence suggests that catalytic activity is important. If the same variant fails to trigger immunity, the result is consistent with recognition of the biochemical change rather than the mere presence of the protein. However, the inactive substitution might also disrupt protein stability or conformation. Expression, delivery and target modification should therefore be examined independently. A missing immune response is not proof that a catalytic residue is the direct contact site for the host receptor.

Timing is equally important. An effector that inhibits an early response may cause substantial differences minutes after perception, yet the final pathogen burden will be determined over a much longer interval. Conversely, an effector that primarily changes nutrient availability may affect multiplication without causing a conspicuous reduction in the earliest calcium or ROS burst. Thus the absence of an effect on one readout does not establish the absence of a virulence function. An experiment must match its measurements to the hypothesized mechanism.

The term effector-triggered susceptibility describes a situation in which pathogen interference contributes to successful infection. It is not a new receptor family, nor does it imply that every aspect of host immunity is switched off. A compatible interaction can contain measurable defense signaling that is insufficient to prevent disease. Susceptibility represents the balance of many processes, including virulence activity, host physiology and environmental conditions. A response that reduces pathogen multiplication relative to a complete absence of defense can still be classified as susceptible in a particular diagnostic assay.

Finally, avoid assuming that a pathogen always benefits from losing a recognized effector. If the effector contributes importantly to growth or survival in hosts without the recognition gene, deletion may carry a cost. An effector sequence change that preserves its virulence function while eliminating recognition can be favored under selection imposed by resistant host populations. Such evolutionary constraints make particular conserved targets attractive in resistance breeding, although conservation alone is not evidence that a resistance mechanism will be durable. The next section translates these molecular possibilities into a genetic model.

3. Flor's gene-for-gene model and compatibility

Long before molecular biologists could identify the structures of immune receptors, plant geneticists noticed that resistance could be exceptionally specific. A flax variety resistant to one physiological race of flax rust might be susceptible to another, while another flax variety showed the opposite pattern. These observations could not be explained by a single universal property such as leaf thickness or total antimicrobial compound concentration. They suggested a genetic correspondence between the host and pathogen. Harold Henry Flor's work on flax and its rust pathogen established the classical gene-for-gene framework, summarized in his 1971 review.

The key experimental approach involved comparing inherited host resistance with inherited pathogen avirulence. Host lines were crossed, progeny were challenged with characterized pathogen races, and the resulting patterns of resistance and susceptibility were compared with genetic segregation. Pathogen crosses and analysis of their offspring provided the complementary information. When a particular host resistance determinant and a corresponding pathogen avirulence determinant were present together, the interaction frequently became incompatible. The important discovery was the correspondence, not an assertion that every plant disease is controlled by one dominant resistance locus.

In the simplest model, use R for a functional host recognition allele and r for the absence of its recognition function. Use Avr for a pathogen allele producing a determinant recognized in the presence of R, and avr for a pathogen variant lacking that recognized determinant. Nomenclature is not universal in publications; the meaning of each symbol must be established from the question. In this chapter Avr denotes the recognized form, not a universal absence of virulence activity. The combination R–Avr generates resistance in the simplified model. The combinations R–avr, r–Avr and r–avr lack that particular recognition event and are classified as compatible, assuming no other resistance mechanism prevents infection.

The word incompatible refers to the failure of the pathogen to establish normal disease in the tested interaction. It does not imply that the plant and pathogen cannot physically touch, that every pathogen cell is killed or that every infected plant cell dies. A compatible interaction permits sufficient colonization or disease in the assay, though many defenses may remain functional. The same isolate may be compatible with one cultivar and incompatible with another. Therefore one should never assign compatibility to the pathogen alone without specifying the host.

The name avirulence gene can confuse a beginner because its product may be a useful virulence effector. It was originally identified through its association with inability to cause disease on a host carrying a corresponding resistance gene. In a host without R, the molecule may facilitate infection rather than inhibit it. The historical term reflects the genetic phenotype of recognition. This dual meaning is central to the evolutionary relationship between pathogens and resistant plants. When reading a question, ask which genetic background was used to define avirulence.

Under a simple dominant host resistance model, a cross between RR and rr gives Rr offspring, all of which possess the functional recognition allele. Selfing an Rr plant gives expected genotypes RR, Rr and rr in the proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter, respectively. If challenged with an Avr strain and assuming complete penetrance, the first two genotypes are resistant and the third susceptible, producing a three-to-one phenotype ratio. These are expected probabilities across many independent segregants, not guaranteed counts in a small family. A challenge with an avr strain will not reveal this segregation if no other effective resistance mechanism is present.

For 160 independent F2 plants from this cross, the expected counts with an Avr isolate are 120 resistant and 40 susceptible. If an investigator observes 118 resistant and 42 susceptible plants, these values are consistent with the expected proportions subject to sampling variation. A chi-square goodness-of-fit calculation uses the sum of squared differences between observed and expected counts divided by expected counts. Here the contributions are 4/120 and 4/40, giving approximately 0.133. With one degree of freedom under the prespecified model, this is not strong evidence against the expected ratio. It does not prove that R encodes an NLR or that the receptor directly contacts the effector; many molecular mechanisms could produce the same segregation.

The pathogen genotype must be kept constant in a test of host segregation. If each seedling is challenged with a mixture of isolates, some seedlings may receive an isolate that lacks the recognized effector, and the observed phenotype ratio may not reflect host inheritance. Conversely, to examine pathogen segregation, use a fixed host line carrying the relevant recognition allele. The ploidy, reproductive system and genetic structure of the pathogen determine the expected segregation ratios. It is not legitimate to apply a diploid host F2 ratio to every pathogen cross.

When two independently segregating dominant host resistance genes, R1 and R2, recognize distinct effectors that are both present in an isolate, recognition by either gene may suffice in a simplified model. In the F2 of a double heterozygote, the probability of losing both recognition functions is 1/4 multiplied by 1/4, or 1/16, assuming independent assortment and complete penetrance. Expected resistance is therefore 15/16 and susceptibility 1/16. If instead the two genes encode indispensable cooperating components of one recognition pathway, both functions may be required. Under the same genetic assumptions, resistance then requires R1– and R2–, with probability 3/4 multiplied by 3/4, or 9/16. Different biological relationships thus produce different segregation patterns.

A matrix of differential plant lines and pathogen isolates helps identify specificity. If line A carrying R1 resists isolates X and Y but not Z, while line B carrying R2 resists Y and Z but not X, the pattern is consistent with X possessing Avr1, Z possessing Avr2 and Y possessing both. However, alternative explanations are possible if the lines differ at more loci or if isolate Z actively suppresses recognition. A genetic compatibility matrix supports a model; it does not identify the biochemical molecules without additional experiments. Isogenic lines and targeted gene complementation strengthen the inference.

The classical model has useful limits. Not all resistance genes are dominant; recessive resistance can arise when a pathogen requires a functional host susceptibility factor. Quantitative resistance often involves contributions from multiple loci, and the effects of those loci may vary with temperature, host development and pathogen isolate. Some R proteins detect extracellular effectors or pathogen-induced changes at the cell surface rather than through an intracellular NLR. Thus gene-for-gene describes a correspondence between determinants, whereas ETI commonly refers to the immune process initiated by effector surveillance. These two concepts overlap strongly but should not be treated as identical definitions.

Flor's experiments were powerful because they turned complex symptoms into testable predictions about the interaction between two genomes. Molecular studies later asked how the correspondence was physically implemented. A gene encoding a receptor could interact directly with the pathogen protein, or the correspondence might arise because the receptor monitors a host protein that the effector changes. The inheritance model alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. That distinction becomes clear once we examine the molecular organization of resistance receptors.

4. Molecular architecture of intracellular NLR receptors

Many plant resistance genes encode proteins that belong to the nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptor family, abbreviated NLR. The older abbreviation NB-LRR emphasizes two characteristic portions of the protein: a nucleotide-binding region and leucine-rich repeats. These are not two independent proteins; they are regions of a single polypeptide in a typical receptor. The term domain refers to a region of a protein that has a recognizable structural or functional organization. The receptor's architecture helps explain how recognition can be linked to activation without requiring a separate cell-surface transmembrane receptor kinase.

A typical plant NLR has an amino-terminal signaling region, a central nucleotide-binding region commonly called NB-ARC, and a carboxyl-terminal LRR region. The leucine-rich repeats form an ordered structure that can participate in recognition and regulation. The NB-ARC region binds nucleotides and acts as a conformational switch. The amino-terminal region helps communicate activation to the rest of the cell. Domain functions are context dependent, and individual NLRs may possess additional regions or noncanonical architectures. A schematic showing three regions is a useful starting point, not a universal structural definition without exceptions.

The central nucleotide-binding switch is often described in terms of an inactive ADP-bound state and an active ATP-associated state. This model is well supported for representative receptors but should not be interpreted as an identical biochemical cycle for every NLR. Binding and exchange of nucleotide are linked to changes in protein conformation. In an inactive conformation, interactions between domains can restrain the signaling region. Recognition can release that restraint and permit an activated arrangement of domains, sometimes accompanied by oligomerization. The energy-bearing nucleotide is part of a regulatory switch rather than a simple fuel that directly kills a microorganism.

A mutation in the conserved nucleotide-binding region can impair receptor function even when the protein remains present and can associate with a recognition partner. In contrast, a mutation that destabilizes the inactive conformation may permit signaling in the absence of the intended pathogen input. The latter phenotype is termed autoactivity. Autoactive receptors can cause defense gene expression, local or widespread cell death and reduced plant growth. The observation that an altered receptor is constitutively active supports the idea that immune activation is normally subject to molecular restraint.

Most introductory classifications distinguish coiled-coil NLRs, or CNLs, from TIR-domain NLRs, or TNLs. The CC designation refers to a coiled-coil region near the amino terminus. The TIR designation derives from structural homology to Toll and interleukin-1 receptor domains. Similar domain names across organisms do not mean that the entire plant pathway reproduces an animal Toll receptor cascade. Plant TNLs are typically intracellular receptors, and activated TIR domains can generate biochemical signals that are decoded by plant-specific immune proteins. A third broad group, RPW8-like coiled-coil NLRs or RNLs, contains important helper receptors. Not every CNL acts independently and not every NLR has a single role.

The location of a receptor should be inferred from its actual architecture and experimental localization rather than from its family name alone. Some NLRs act in association with the plasma membrane, others occur in the cytoplasm or nucleus, and some redistribute during activation. Membrane association does not turn an intracellular NLR into a classical extracellular pattern-recognition receptor. A receptor that recognizes an intracellular event at the cytoplasmic face of a membrane remains distinct from a receptor whose extracellular domain detects an apoplastic ligand. Location, ligand accessibility and mechanism are separate features.

An NLR may operate as a sensor, an executor, or both. A sensor recognizes an effector directly or indirectly but may depend on another receptor to produce effective signaling. A helper receptor receives or transmits information from one or more sensors. Paired NLRs can divide recognition and execution between distinct proteins. In other cases a single receptor carries both functions. A mutation in a helper can therefore affect several recognition specificities while the corresponding sensors remain intact. Conversely, loss of one sensor may selectively abolish resistance to one effector even if the shared helper retains its function.

Imagine a host that contains sensors S1 and S2 and helper H. S1 recognizes effector E1, S2 recognizes E2, and H is necessary for both responses. A sensor S1 loss-of-function plant retains immunity against E2 but loses the specific E1 response. An H-deficient plant loses both responses. Restoring S1 cannot restore the E1 response in an H-deficient background because the downstream requirement remains unsatisfied. Such an epistasis pattern is consistent with shared dependence, although further experiments are needed to determine whether H acts immediately after the sensors or through an indirect signaling function. Genetic logic provides a map of functional dependencies, not a complete physical interaction map.

Protein abundance is especially important when interpreting receptor mutants. A domain deletion that abolishes signaling may remove a required biochemical function, but it may also destabilize the entire protein. An appropriate analysis compares receptor expression, localization and, where feasible, interaction with partners. If a mutant receptor accumulates at the same level as wild type yet fails to respond to its effector, loss of the tested function becomes a stronger interpretation. Complementation using a functional receptor is useful for verifying that the observed immune defect is linked to the intended genetic locus.

The NLR family contains substantial sequence diversity, particularly in regions involved in specificity. Changes that permit recognition of a new effector can be beneficial, while changes that cause chronic activation can be harmful. The functional receptor must maintain the balance between recognition, conformational regulation and signaling competence. This explains why swapping a domain or increasing receptor expression does not automatically improve resistance. The next step is to distinguish molecular recognition mechanisms that allow the receptor to respond to an effector.
