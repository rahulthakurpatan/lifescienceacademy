1. Why plants need effector surveillance

A leaf that remains green in a field containing many microorganisms is not necessarily free from infection attempts. Its cuticle, cell wall and cell-surface immune receptors limit access to nutrients and prevent many microbes from multiplying. Yet some microorganisms are able to colonize particular plants despite these defenses. The decisive biological question is how a pathogen turns contact with a living plant into a successful association, and how the plant detects the molecular activities that make such an association possible. The previous chapter introduced the perception of microbial patterns. We now examine the additional problem created when a pathogen interferes with the very signaling system that should restrict its growth.

A plant cell possesses a plasma membrane that separates the cytoplasm from the apoplast, the extracellular continuum of walls and intercellular spaces. Pattern-recognition receptors generally recognize molecular information at the cell surface. Bacteria in the apoplast can nevertheless deliver certain proteins across the host plasma membrane. Other pathogens produce proteins at the interface between their feeding structures and the plant, and some of these molecules enter host cells. Pathogens therefore do not need to place their entire body inside a host cell in order to alter its intracellular physiology. This distinction between the position of the pathogen and the position of its secreted molecule is essential for understanding intracellular immunity.

An effector is a pathogen-derived molecule that changes a host process or the host–pathogen interface in a way relevant to the interaction. Many experimentally characterized effectors are proteins. Some alter signaling, some change transcription or protein turnover, and others modify the accessibility of nutrients. The term describes an activity in a biological interaction, not a particular chemical structure or a guaranteed direction of effect. A molecule that promotes the pathogen's growth in one host genotype may cause strong immune recognition in another. It is therefore misleading to equate the term effector with toxin, or to assume that all effectors kill plant cells.

Suppose a microbe activates a cell-surface receptor through a conserved structural pattern. A kinase associated with that receptor normally passes information to downstream proteins. An effector that decreases the kinase's activity may lower the speed or strength of the defense response. This could allow the microorganism to reproduce before antimicrobial compounds accumulate. However, the plant benefits from knowing that its signaling machinery has been disturbed. An intracellular surveillance protein may recognize the interfering molecule itself or detect a biochemical modification of a host target. Such recognition initiates an immune response referred to as effector-triggered immunity, or ETI.

The distinction between recognition and suppression is especially useful when a single effector has opposing consequences in different plant lines. In a susceptible line lacking an appropriate surveillance receptor, the effector's virulence function can dominate. In a resistant line, recognition may instead outweigh the virulence benefit. This is not a contradiction in the molecule's identity. The outcome depends on both genomes and on the rest of the cellular signaling machinery. A disease phenotype is a relationship between organisms rather than an unconditional property of the effector gene.

Immunity here means the capacity to perceive and respond to biologically relevant threats. Plants do not produce antibodies or rearrange lymphocyte antigen receptor genes. Their genetically encoded cell-surface and intracellular receptors operate in individual living cells and participate in tissue-wide communication. The diversity of resistance genes is generated over evolutionary and reproductive timescales, not by the animal adaptive immune mechanism. A plant that responds more effectively after an initial challenge may have been primed; priming does not imply the acquisition of a new rearranged immune receptor.

Three measurements must be distinguished from the beginning. Pathogen multiplication quantifies whether the invading organism can increase its population. Tissue damage records injury to the host, whether caused by pathogen activity, host defense or both. Host performance measures the functional consequences, such as growth, reproduction or yield. A plant that reduces pathogen multiplication is resistant in the relevant sense. A plant that sustains its performance despite a comparable pathogen burden is tolerant. A plant that exhibits conspicuous local cell death is not automatically more resistant or less tolerant than another plant. These outcomes can be experimentally separated.

The biological challenge involves both sensitivity and restraint. A receptor that activates too late may permit disease; a receptor that activates spontaneously may cause unnecessary defense and reduced growth. A highly specific receptor could detect a particular pathogen allele very efficiently yet fail against a different allele. A receptor that monitors a frequently targeted host process may detect more than one effector but must distinguish harmful changes from normal regulation. Thus plant immunity is a regulated surveillance problem, not merely an attempt to maximize cell death or the amount of antimicrobial chemicals produced.

To reason about ETI, trace the causal sequence from pathogen genotype to effector delivery, host target or receptor recognition, intracellular signaling and disease outcome. At every step, identify the observations that would establish the next arrow. An increase in a defense transcript indicates an altered host response but does not prove that an intracellular receptor directly binds an effector. A physical interaction between two purified proteins establishes biochemical association under the tested conditions but does not establish the resistance phenotype in a whole plant. A strong lesion may demonstrate local injury without proving that the pathogen has been restricted. Keeping these distinctions separate is the foundation of analytical examination questions.

2. How pathogens interfere with defense

A pathogen faces successive physiological obstacles. It must encounter a suitable host, reach an appropriate tissue, obtain nutrients and reproduce despite host defenses. For many bacterial pathogens, secretion of effectors is an important component of this process. The type III secretion system of several Gram-negative plant-pathogenic bacteria can translocate proteins into plant cells. The bacterium often remains extracellular in the apoplast while the delivered protein acts on intracellular targets. Secretion into the external medium and successful translocation into the host cytoplasm are distinct processes; demonstration of one does not automatically prove the other.

Fungal and oomycete pathogens use different mechanisms to interact with their hosts. Some form haustoria, specialized feeding interfaces surrounded by a host-derived membrane. Molecules released at these interfaces may act outside the host plasma membrane or, for particular effectors, inside the host cell. Not every pathogen forms haustoria, and not every effector crosses a membrane. Plant viruses already replicate within host cells and encode proteins that alter defense and host processes in that context. The diversity of these interactions explains why a definition based exclusively on bacterial injection would be too narrow.

Consider the early defense pathway activated by a microbial pattern. A surface receptor associates with signaling partners, kinases change phosphorylation states, calcium concentration can increase in the cytosol, and reactive oxygen species are produced. Many of these steps are attractive points for pathogen interference because inhibiting a shared signaling component can reduce several defensive outputs. A pathogen need not destroy every receptor individually if it can reduce a downstream activity used by several receptor systems. The result can be weaker cell-wall reinforcement, altered transcription or delayed closure of a natural opening. These are mechanistic consequences, not necessarily universal responses to every effector.

In the model bacterium Pseudomonas syringae, AvrPto and AvrPtoB illustrate the targeting of receptor-associated signaling. Their interference with host immune components helps explain why surface pattern recognition alone may not prevent multiplication of a compatible pathogen. The precise biochemical target and effect depend on the effector, receptor system and host background. A student should understand the causal idea: receptor recognition can be intact while transmission of the information is impaired. A ligand can bind its receptor normally even when downstream defensive outputs are reduced. Distinguish receptor abundance, ligand perception and kinase activity in an experimental interpretation.

An effector can also alter the state of a regulatory host protein. Protein phosphorylation, cleavage, acetylation and degradation are ordinary cellular regulatory processes. Pathogen proteins that influence these reactions can redirect the host's machinery. A protease may change the abundance of its target, whereas a protein that modifies a kinase may change its activity without causing degradation. Measuring total protein abundance is therefore not enough to establish activity. One can have an unchanged band on a conventional immunoblot but a profound change in a phosphorylation-dependent signaling response.

The presence of an effector does not prove that it is required for virulence. A pathogen may contain many effectors with overlapping functions. Deletion of one effector gene may produce little change in growth because other effectors compensate, or because the assay conditions do not require that particular function. In another host genotype, the same deletion may improve pathogen growth by removing a signal that a resistance receptor recognizes. This is one reason why a simple comparison of a wild-type pathogen with a deletion strain should be conducted in more than one host background.

A useful conceptual experiment uses two pathogen strains that differ only in a candidate effector gene and two host lines that differ only in a candidate recognition receptor. The effector-positive strain may multiply better than the deletion strain in a receptor-deficient host, while multiplying less well in a receptor-competent host. Restoring the effector gene in the deletion strain can test whether the observed change is attributable to the deleted locus. The experiment requires comparable starting pathogen populations and equivalent sampling conditions. A complemented strain that expresses the protein at an excessive level may produce an unusual phenotype, so restoration of the native expression context is preferable when interpreting normal virulence functions.

A catalytically inactive effector is a particularly informative comparison for an enzyme-like effector. If the inactive variant is delivered normally but does not modify its host target, its loss of virulence suggests that catalytic activity is important. If the same variant fails to trigger immunity, the result is consistent with recognition of the biochemical change rather than the mere presence of the protein. However, the inactive substitution might also disrupt protein stability or conformation. Expression, delivery and target modification should therefore be examined independently. A missing immune response is not proof that a catalytic residue is the direct contact site for the host receptor.

Timing is equally important. An effector that inhibits an early response may cause substantial differences minutes after perception, yet the final pathogen burden will be determined over a much longer interval. Conversely, an effector that primarily changes nutrient availability may affect multiplication without causing a conspicuous reduction in the earliest calcium or ROS burst. Thus the absence of an effect on one readout does not establish the absence of a virulence function. An experiment must match its measurements to the hypothesized mechanism.

The term effector-triggered susceptibility describes a situation in which pathogen interference contributes to successful infection. It is not a new receptor family, nor does it imply that every aspect of host immunity is switched off. A compatible interaction can contain measurable defense signaling that is insufficient to prevent disease. Susceptibility represents the balance of many processes, including virulence activity, host physiology and environmental conditions. A response that reduces pathogen multiplication relative to a complete absence of defense can still be classified as susceptible in a particular diagnostic assay.

Finally, avoid assuming that a pathogen always benefits from losing a recognized effector. If the effector contributes importantly to growth or survival in hosts without the recognition gene, deletion may carry a cost. An effector sequence change that preserves its virulence function while eliminating recognition can be favored under selection imposed by resistant host populations. Such evolutionary constraints make particular conserved targets attractive in resistance breeding, although conservation alone is not evidence that a resistance mechanism will be durable. The next section translates these molecular possibilities into a genetic model.

3. Flor's gene-for-gene model and compatibility

Long before molecular biologists could identify the structures of immune receptors, plant geneticists noticed that resistance could be exceptionally specific. A flax variety resistant to one physiological race of flax rust might be susceptible to another, while another flax variety showed the opposite pattern. These observations could not be explained by a single universal property such as leaf thickness or total antimicrobial compound concentration. They suggested a genetic correspondence between the host and pathogen. Harold Henry Flor's work on flax and its rust pathogen established the classical gene-for-gene framework, summarized in his 1971 review.

The key experimental approach involved comparing inherited host resistance with inherited pathogen avirulence. Host lines were crossed, progeny were challenged with characterized pathogen races, and the resulting patterns of resistance and susceptibility were compared with genetic segregation. Pathogen crosses and analysis of their offspring provided the complementary information. When a particular host resistance determinant and a corresponding pathogen avirulence determinant were present together, the interaction frequently became incompatible. The important discovery was the correspondence, not an assertion that every plant disease is controlled by one dominant resistance locus.

In the simplest model, use R for a functional host recognition allele and r for the absence of its recognition function. Use Avr for a pathogen allele producing a determinant recognized in the presence of R, and avr for a pathogen variant lacking that recognized determinant. Nomenclature is not universal in publications; the meaning of each symbol must be established from the question. In this chapter Avr denotes the recognized form, not a universal absence of virulence activity. The combination R–Avr generates resistance in the simplified model. The combinations R–avr, r–Avr and r–avr lack that particular recognition event and are classified as compatible, assuming no other resistance mechanism prevents infection.

The word incompatible refers to the failure of the pathogen to establish normal disease in the tested interaction. It does not imply that the plant and pathogen cannot physically touch, that every pathogen cell is killed or that every infected plant cell dies. A compatible interaction permits sufficient colonization or disease in the assay, though many defenses may remain functional. The same isolate may be compatible with one cultivar and incompatible with another. Therefore one should never assign compatibility to the pathogen alone without specifying the host.

The name avirulence gene can confuse a beginner because its product may be a useful virulence effector. It was originally identified through its association with inability to cause disease on a host carrying a corresponding resistance gene. In a host without R, the molecule may facilitate infection rather than inhibit it. The historical term reflects the genetic phenotype of recognition. This dual meaning is central to the evolutionary relationship between pathogens and resistant plants. When reading a question, ask which genetic background was used to define avirulence.

Under a simple dominant host resistance model, a cross between RR and rr gives Rr offspring, all of which possess the functional recognition allele. Selfing an Rr plant gives expected genotypes RR, Rr and rr in the proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter, respectively. If challenged with an Avr strain and assuming complete penetrance, the first two genotypes are resistant and the third susceptible, producing a three-to-one phenotype ratio. These are expected probabilities across many independent segregants, not guaranteed counts in a small family. A challenge with an avr strain will not reveal this segregation if no other effective resistance mechanism is present.

For 160 independent F2 plants from this cross, the expected counts with an Avr isolate are 120 resistant and 40 susceptible. If an investigator observes 118 resistant and 42 susceptible plants, these values are consistent with the expected proportions subject to sampling variation. A chi-square goodness-of-fit calculation uses the sum of squared differences between observed and expected counts divided by expected counts. Here the contributions are 4/120 and 4/40, giving approximately 0.133. With one degree of freedom under the prespecified model, this is not strong evidence against the expected ratio. It does not prove that R encodes an NLR or that the receptor directly contacts the effector; many molecular mechanisms could produce the same segregation.

The pathogen genotype must be kept constant in a test of host segregation. If each seedling is challenged with a mixture of isolates, some seedlings may receive an isolate that lacks the recognized effector, and the observed phenotype ratio may not reflect host inheritance. Conversely, to examine pathogen segregation, use a fixed host line carrying the relevant recognition allele. The ploidy, reproductive system and genetic structure of the pathogen determine the expected segregation ratios. It is not legitimate to apply a diploid host F2 ratio to every pathogen cross.

When two independently segregating dominant host resistance genes, R1 and R2, recognize distinct effectors that are both present in an isolate, recognition by either gene may suffice in a simplified model. In the F2 of a double heterozygote, the probability of losing both recognition functions is 1/4 multiplied by 1/4, or 1/16, assuming independent assortment and complete penetrance. Expected resistance is therefore 15/16 and susceptibility 1/16. If instead the two genes encode indispensable cooperating components of one recognition pathway, both functions may be required. Under the same genetic assumptions, resistance then requires R1– and R2–, with probability 3/4 multiplied by 3/4, or 9/16. Different biological relationships thus produce different segregation patterns.

A matrix of differential plant lines and pathogen isolates helps identify specificity. If line A carrying R1 resists isolates X and Y but not Z, while line B carrying R2 resists Y and Z but not X, the pattern is consistent with X possessing Avr1, Z possessing Avr2 and Y possessing both. However, alternative explanations are possible if the lines differ at more loci or if isolate Z actively suppresses recognition. A genetic compatibility matrix supports a model; it does not identify the biochemical molecules without additional experiments. Isogenic lines and targeted gene complementation strengthen the inference.

The classical model has useful limits. Not all resistance genes are dominant; recessive resistance can arise when a pathogen requires a functional host susceptibility factor. Quantitative resistance often involves contributions from multiple loci, and the effects of those loci may vary with temperature, host development and pathogen isolate. Some R proteins detect extracellular effectors or pathogen-induced changes at the cell surface rather than through an intracellular NLR. Thus gene-for-gene describes a correspondence between determinants, whereas ETI commonly refers to the immune process initiated by effector surveillance. These two concepts overlap strongly but should not be treated as identical definitions.

Flor's experiments were powerful because they turned complex symptoms into testable predictions about the interaction between two genomes. Molecular studies later asked how the correspondence was physically implemented. A gene encoding a receptor could interact directly with the pathogen protein, or the correspondence might arise because the receptor monitors a host protein that the effector changes. The inheritance model alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. That distinction becomes clear once we examine the molecular organization of resistance receptors.

4. Molecular architecture of intracellular NLR receptors

Many plant resistance genes encode proteins that belong to the nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptor family, abbreviated NLR. The older abbreviation NB-LRR emphasizes two characteristic portions of the protein: a nucleotide-binding region and leucine-rich repeats. These are not two independent proteins; they are regions of a single polypeptide in a typical receptor. The term domain refers to a region of a protein that has a recognizable structural or functional organization. The receptor's architecture helps explain how recognition can be linked to activation without requiring a separate cell-surface transmembrane receptor kinase.

A typical plant NLR has an amino-terminal signaling region, a central nucleotide-binding region commonly called NB-ARC, and a carboxyl-terminal LRR region. The leucine-rich repeats form an ordered structure that can participate in recognition and regulation. The NB-ARC region binds nucleotides and acts as a conformational switch. The amino-terminal region helps communicate activation to the rest of the cell. Domain functions are context dependent, and individual NLRs may possess additional regions or noncanonical architectures. A schematic showing three regions is a useful starting point, not a universal structural definition without exceptions.

The central nucleotide-binding switch is often described in terms of an inactive ADP-bound state and an active ATP-associated state. This model is well supported for representative receptors but should not be interpreted as an identical biochemical cycle for every NLR. Binding and exchange of nucleotide are linked to changes in protein conformation. In an inactive conformation, interactions between domains can restrain the signaling region. Recognition can release that restraint and permit an activated arrangement of domains, sometimes accompanied by oligomerization. The energy-bearing nucleotide is part of a regulatory switch rather than a simple fuel that directly kills a microorganism.

A mutation in the conserved nucleotide-binding region can impair receptor function even when the protein remains present and can associate with a recognition partner. In contrast, a mutation that destabilizes the inactive conformation may permit signaling in the absence of the intended pathogen input. The latter phenotype is termed autoactivity. Autoactive receptors can cause defense gene expression, local or widespread cell death and reduced plant growth. The observation that an altered receptor is constitutively active supports the idea that immune activation is normally subject to molecular restraint.

Most introductory classifications distinguish coiled-coil NLRs, or CNLs, from TIR-domain NLRs, or TNLs. The CC designation refers to a coiled-coil region near the amino terminus. The TIR designation derives from structural homology to Toll and interleukin-1 receptor domains. Similar domain names across organisms do not mean that the entire plant pathway reproduces an animal Toll receptor cascade. Plant TNLs are typically intracellular receptors, and activated TIR domains can generate biochemical signals that are decoded by plant-specific immune proteins. A third broad group, RPW8-like coiled-coil NLRs or RNLs, contains important helper receptors. Not every CNL acts independently and not every NLR has a single role.

The location of a receptor should be inferred from its actual architecture and experimental localization rather than from its family name alone. Some NLRs act in association with the plasma membrane, others occur in the cytoplasm or nucleus, and some redistribute during activation. Membrane association does not turn an intracellular NLR into a classical extracellular pattern-recognition receptor. A receptor that recognizes an intracellular event at the cytoplasmic face of a membrane remains distinct from a receptor whose extracellular domain detects an apoplastic ligand. Location, ligand accessibility and mechanism are separate features.

An NLR may operate as a sensor, an executor, or both. A sensor recognizes an effector directly or indirectly but may depend on another receptor to produce effective signaling. A helper receptor receives or transmits information from one or more sensors. Paired NLRs can divide recognition and execution between distinct proteins. In other cases a single receptor carries both functions. A mutation in a helper can therefore affect several recognition specificities while the corresponding sensors remain intact. Conversely, loss of one sensor may selectively abolish resistance to one effector even if the shared helper retains its function.

Imagine a host that contains sensors S1 and S2 and helper H. S1 recognizes effector E1, S2 recognizes E2, and H is necessary for both responses. A sensor S1 loss-of-function plant retains immunity against E2 but loses the specific E1 response. An H-deficient plant loses both responses. Restoring S1 cannot restore the E1 response in an H-deficient background because the downstream requirement remains unsatisfied. Such an epistasis pattern is consistent with shared dependence, although further experiments are needed to determine whether H acts immediately after the sensors or through an indirect signaling function. Genetic logic provides a map of functional dependencies, not a complete physical interaction map.

Protein abundance is especially important when interpreting receptor mutants. A domain deletion that abolishes signaling may remove a required biochemical function, but it may also destabilize the entire protein. An appropriate analysis compares receptor expression, localization and, where feasible, interaction with partners. If a mutant receptor accumulates at the same level as wild type yet fails to respond to its effector, loss of the tested function becomes a stronger interpretation. Complementation using a functional receptor is useful for verifying that the observed immune defect is linked to the intended genetic locus.

The NLR family contains substantial sequence diversity, particularly in regions involved in specificity. Changes that permit recognition of a new effector can be beneficial, while changes that cause chronic activation can be harmful. The functional receptor must maintain the balance between recognition, conformational regulation and signaling competence. This explains why swapping a domain or increasing receptor expression does not automatically improve resistance. The next step is to distinguish molecular recognition mechanisms that allow the receptor to respond to an effector.

5. Direct recognition, guard and decoy models

The gene-for-gene relationship describes which combinations of host and pathogen determinants produce resistance. To explain that relationship at molecular scale, distinguish the signal that activates a receptor from the process by which the signal becomes accessible. In direct recognition, the effector associates with the receptor or a recognition domain of a receptor complex. In indirect recognition, the relevant signal is a host protein or other host component whose state has been altered by the effector. Both arrangements can generate very specific immunity. It is incorrect to infer direct physical binding solely because the pathogen and plant genes show a simple genetic correspondence.

Direct recognition has an intuitive molecular basis. A receptor surface and an effector surface can associate through noncovalent interactions that depend on the chemical properties and three-dimensional arrangement of their residues. A substitution that changes the interface can reduce binding without necessarily destroying the effector's virulence function. In the rice blast system, the fungal effector AVR-Pita and the rice resistance protein Pi-ta have been studied as a classical example of effector–receptor interaction. Other rice NLRs contain an additional heavy-metal-associated domain that can interact with fungal effectors. Different recognition arrangements demonstrate that the LRR region is not the only possible binding surface.

A binding experiment tests association under the conditions of the assay. If an effector associates with an isolated receptor fragment, it supports a candidate interface but does not establish that the fragment alone is sufficient to trigger resistance. The full receptor may require another domain for signaling, appropriate nucleotide binding, or a cooperating NLR. Conversely, failure to detect association does not establish indirect recognition, because the proteins may require a modification or cellular environment missing from the assay. A sound conclusion relies on several complementary lines of evidence, including association, recognition-dependent signaling and resistance in the relevant genetic background.

The guard model provides an alternative solution to the recognition problem. Rather than detecting the pathogen protein itself, an NLR monitors a host target, often termed a guardee. An effector that modifies this target may generate an altered state recognized by the surveillance machinery. A particular host protein could be targeted by more than one pathogen effector, so monitoring it may broaden detection. The NLR still requires a selective mechanism: normal changes in the guardee during growth should not routinely produce destructive immune activation. Recognition depends on the nature, location and physiological consequences of the altered state.

Consider a host protein that normally participates in regulating membrane-associated signaling. An effector enzyme changes its phosphorylation status. If the surveillance receptor recognizes the resulting state, the host can detect the consequence even when it has no direct affinity for the effector. A pathogen could therefore escape recognition by changing the enzymatic target, not only by changing a receptor-binding surface. If the target is important for virulence, however, switching targets may reduce pathogenic fitness. The guard model thus links immune specificity to the functional constraints acting on pathogen proteins.

A well-established example involves the Arabidopsis protein RIN4, which participates in the regulation of immune responses and is associated with the plasma membrane. The bacterial effectors AvrB and AvrRpm1 induce changes associated with RIN4 phosphorylation; the NLR RPM1 responds to the modified state. Another bacterial effector, AvrRpt2, is a protease that cleaves RIN4, and the NLR RPS2 responds to the loss or alteration of RIN4. The two NLRs therefore monitor different consequences of pathogen activity at a common host component. The distinction is important: AvrRpt2-mediated cleavage is associated with RPS2 activation, whereas phosphorylation associated with AvrB or AvrRpm1 activates RPM1.

This example illustrates why the same host component should not be casually described as a universal positive regulator of immunity. RIN4 has regulatory roles, and its presence, absence and modification can affect different pathways in different ways. If a student observes that RPS2-dependent signaling increases after loss of RIN4, this does not imply that all immune functions require RIN4 degradation. Nor does observing RIN4 phosphorylation establish that the relevant effector itself is the kinase responsible for the modification; host enzymes can contribute to the altered state. The appropriate conclusion concerns the molecular consequence monitored by the receptor.

The decoy model adds an evolutionary refinement. A decoy resembles an effector's usual target sufficiently to attract or respond to the effector but is specialized for recognition. Unlike a genuine virulence target, its principal biological function in the proposed model is immune surveillance. By offering a molecular mimic, the plant can convert an effector activity that would normally assist infection into a recognition event. In practice, distinguishing a guardee from a decoy requires evidence about the host protein's contribution to normal physiology and to pathogen virulence in the absence of the monitoring receptor. Structural resemblance alone is insufficient.

An informative Arabidopsis example is the RPS5 pathway, in which the bacterial protease AvrPphB cleaves the host kinase PBS1. RPS5 activation depends on the cleavage-associated change. PBS1 has structural relatives that participate in immune signaling, so it has often been discussed in relation to the guard and decoy concepts. The mechanistic fact that cleavage of PBS1 is monitored is more secure than a categorical claim about whether PBS1 has no other physiological function. In examination questions, pay attention to whether the requested answer concerns the established recognition mechanism or the broader evolutionary model.

A decoy may even be integrated into a receptor rather than existing as a separate host protein. In Arabidopsis, the paired TNLs RRS1 and RPS4 provide a useful example: RRS1 contains a WRKY-like integrated domain that can be targeted by pathogen effectors such as PopP2 and AvrRps4. WRKY proteins ordinarily participate in transcriptional regulation, so a domain resembling a host target can provide a recognition interface within the immune receptor complex. The paired receptor arrangement allows the recognition event to be linked to signaling. The key lesson is that a domain's evolutionary origin and its current role within a receptor may differ.

How could an experiment discriminate direct recognition from recognition of target modification? Compare a functional effector with a variant that is unable to carry out its relevant enzymatic activity, verify both variants accumulate and reach the relevant compartment, and test their association with the NLR and the candidate host target. Next examine immune signaling in hosts that contain the NLR but lack the target, where loss of that target is compatible with viability. Finally, restore the target in the mutant and compare both a wild-type form and a modification-resistant form. A response dependent on target modification despite retained effector delivery supports the indirect model. Physical association between the receptor and target may strengthen that interpretation, but association alone is not decisive.

A critical limitation is that removal of a host target may alter the basal state of the receptor. If a surveillance protein normally restrains an NLR, removing it can activate the NLR even without an effector. In that case an investigator cannot interpret the knockout merely as removal of a necessary intermediate. The baseline phenotype, including spontaneous cell death and defense gene expression, must be established before pathogen challenge. Genetic suppression of the receptor-dependent baseline phenotype can help reveal whether the altered state is caused by the surveillance receptor rather than nonspecific injury.

Direct, guard and decoy models are therefore working explanations for how the host obtains information. They need not be mutually exclusive across all domains of a receptor or across all effectors recognized by a particular immune system. The same pathogen population may encounter several forms of recognition. The examination-level task is to infer the best-supported model from the evidence, distinguish direct association from functional dependence, and understand why monitoring a host process can be advantageous. The molecular signal still has to be converted into an active immune state, which is the subject of the next section.

6. From NLR activation to immune signaling

Recognition is not itself a complete defense response. A plant cell must translate the molecular event into coordinated changes in its physiology. In an inactive NLR, contacts between protein domains and the state of its nucleotide-binding pocket help restrict signaling. Recognition can change these contacts, allowing the receptor to adopt a signaling-competent conformation. For several structurally characterized NLRs, activation leads to the assembly of multiple receptor molecules into a higher-order complex called a resistosome. This is a molecular assembly, not a membrane-bound organelle or a universal structure shared in identical form by every plant immune receptor.

The Arabidopsis CNL ZAR1 provides an instructive example of indirect surveillance coupled to a physical signaling mechanism. ZAR1 functions with host kinase-related partner proteins. The bacterial effector AvrAC modifies a host kinase, PBL2, by uridylylation, creating a modified host protein that participates in activation of the ZAR1 complex through the pseudokinase RKS1. The important causal order is effector activity, generation of an altered host component, recognition by a receptor complex and receptor activation. The NLR does not need to bind the bacterial effector directly for specificity to arise.

Structural studies of ZAR1 revealed a dramatic difference between inactive and activated receptor assemblies. Activated ZAR1 forms a fivefold complex, with amino-terminal regions rearranged to form a funnel-like structure. Subsequent functional evidence established that activated ZAR1 can form a calcium-permeable cation channel associated with the plasma membrane. This provides a direct link from a protein conformational switch to a change in cellular ion flux. The structural observation alone suggested a membrane-related function; ion conductance and physiological assays were needed to support channel activity. For NET and SET, the central principle is that an activated immune receptor can participate in producing an intracellular signal through an oligomeric assembly.

Calcium is a messenger because its free concentration in the cytosol is tightly regulated. A change in membrane permeability to calcium can allow movement down an electrochemical gradient. The gradient depends on both concentration and electrical potential. Calcium entering the cytoplasm may alter the activity of calcium-binding proteins and calcium-dependent kinases, which then influence other defense processes. The ionic signal is dynamic: transporters and binding proteins shape its amplitude, duration and spatial distribution. A rise in cytosolic calcium does not imply that total calcium in the entire cell has increased by the same proportion, because most cellular calcium is not freely dissolved in the cytosol.

It is tempting to generalize from ZAR1 and conclude that every NLR is a calcium channel. This would be incorrect. Some CNLs and helper NLRs form signaling complexes with channel activity, but receptor families differ in their downstream mechanisms. The biochemical role of activated TIR-domain receptors is especially important. A TNL can assemble into an enzymatically active complex, in which associated TIR domains generate nucleotide-derived signaling molecules. TIR catalytic activity is associated with cleavage of NAD-related substrates and production of signals that are interpreted by plant immune machinery. The exact chemical repertoire is beyond the necessary examination scope; the distinction between an enzymatic signaling complex and a directly channel-forming complex is the essential concept.

In Arabidopsis and related model systems, EDS1-family proteins are central to the relay of TNL signaling. EDS1 can form complexes with PAD4 or SAG101, which cooperate with helper NLRs such as ADR1 or NRG1, respectively. These modules help connect TIR-derived signals to defense outputs including pathogen restriction and cell death. The two modules are not absolute independent pipelines that produce only one phenotype each in every species. Their contributions overlap, and dependency can vary with the pathogen, receptor and plant lineage. It is safer to learn their representative associations and their role in transmitting TNL signaling than to memorize a universal one-output-per-protein rule.

The division between sensor and helper explains why some NLRs are not sufficient to confer a recognition phenotype when moved into another species. A transferred sensor might retain the ability to recognize its effector but fail to communicate with the new host's signaling partners. Differences in partner availability, receptor regulation or expression can all affect the result. In a genetic experiment, the failure of a sensor to confer resistance should be interpreted in the context of the complete signaling network rather than immediately attributed to a failure of ligand binding.

A pathway diagram can distinguish several kinds of evidence. Receptor–effector association suggests physical recognition. A mutation in the receptor nucleotide-binding site that abolishes downstream responses supports the requirement for receptor activation. A mutant lacking a helper that fails to produce defense despite intact sensor expression supports a downstream requirement. An assay showing a calcium current in a suitable membrane preparation provides evidence for channel activity under those conditions. Finally, lower pathogen growth in a receptor-competent plant demonstrates a functional resistance phenotype. These observations address separate questions and together establish a stronger mechanism than any one assay.

Suppose a candidate NLR forms an oligomer after effector exposure. Oligomerization might be necessary for signaling, but it could also be a consequence of another activation event or an artifact of protein overexpression. To test necessity, compare a receptor variant that disrupts the proposed oligomer interface with a functional receptor at similar abundance. If recognition remains detectable but oligomerization and immune output are lost, the result supports a functional assembly requirement. However, the substitution may have broader effects on folding. Structural information, receptor stability and rescue experiments make the inference more convincing.

NLR signaling can converge on pathways also used by cell-surface receptors. Calcium-dependent processes, reactive oxygen production, transcriptional changes and hormonal responses are not unique markers of ETI. These common outputs help explain why activation of distinct receptors can produce partially similar defense phenotypes. The initiating recognition event remains important for classification. An experiment that measures ROS after an infection cannot classify the response as PTI or ETI without knowing which receptors or inputs are required. The downstream output should be interpreted as a component of a network in which several inputs may be active.

The strength of the immune response depends on its timing and distribution. A sharp early calcium signal may activate existing proteins; transcriptional changes may increase the abundance of signaling components over a longer interval. If the pathogen continues to deliver effectors, continued surveillance and feedback may alter the dynamics. Signaling may be locally intense while neighboring cells respond to diffusible or transmitted signals. The spatial organization matters because a plant has to restrict infection without destroying an excessive amount of functional tissue. The local response is the immediate topic here; the coordinated response of distant tissues is developed in node 6.H.12.

A useful causal sequence can therefore be described as recognition, relief of receptor inhibition, activation of an assembly or relay, generation and interpretation of intracellular signals, and coordinated defense. This sequence should not be read as a universal linear chain: receptor networks can branch and feed back into one another. The quantitative magnitude of one readout is not a universal scale of immune success. A receptor-dependent signal has biological significance only when interpreted alongside the relevant pathogen and host outcomes.

7. Physiological defense outputs and their regulation

After intracellular recognition, the cell must redirect physiological processes that normally serve growth and maintenance. Many defense responses involve proteins already present in the cell, followed by altered expression of genes encoding additional components. These timescales are important. A rapid ion flux can occur before a measurable change in transcript abundance, whereas the accumulation of defense proteins and antimicrobial metabolites may take longer. Failure to detect an early response in a late sample does not prove that it never occurred. Likewise, increased transcription at one time point does not establish that sufficient functional protein has accumulated.

Reactive oxygen species, including hydrogen peroxide and superoxide, are important examples of shared immune and stress signals. The plasma-membrane NADPH oxidase RBOHD contributes to extracellular ROS production in many defense contexts. Regulation by phosphorylation and calcium-associated mechanisms connects receptor activation to this output. ROS can influence redox-sensitive proteins and cell-wall chemistry, as well as contribute to damage at excessive levels. The physiological significance depends on location, concentration and duration. A strong bulk ROS measurement is not automatically equivalent to more effective antimicrobial action; the source and localization can be crucial.

The cell wall is more than a mechanical barrier. Defense signaling can increase deposition of materials that make penetration or movement more difficult at particular sites. Callose, a beta-1,3-glucan, may accumulate in response to immune stimulation. Lignification and related phenolic changes can strengthen or alter wall properties. Such outputs can limit aspects of colonization, but their effectiveness depends on the pathogen's infection strategy. Callose deposition is also involved in nonimmune processes, so its presence alone is not a specific indicator of ETI. Controlled comparisons with untreated and receptor-deficient plants are necessary to assign it to the particular recognition event.

Plants also produce antimicrobial proteins and specialized metabolites. Pathogenesis-related proteins include groups with different biochemical functions; some chitinases and glucanases can act on substrates associated with microbial cell walls. Their presence is not a guarantee of protection against all pathogen types, because not all microbes expose the same substrates or have the same sensitivity. Induced antimicrobial compounds, including species-specific phytoalexins, can contribute to restriction of pathogen growth. The relevant metabolic pathways have been developed in Unit 6.G. Here the connection is from immune recognition to the redirection of resources toward local defensive chemistry.

Salicylic acid is frequently associated with resistance to pathogens that depend on living host tissue, and with the expression of particular defense genes. Its accumulation and signaling can amplify aspects of the local immune response. However, a plant hormone does not have a single fixed action independent of tissue, developmental stage and pathogen lifestyle. The effect of salicylic acid depends on interactions with other regulatory pathways, and some resistance responses persist even when particular salicylic acid-dependent outputs are impaired. Jasmonate and ethylene often participate in responses to tissue damage and necrotrophic pathogens, but simplified categories are not universal rules. The full systemic integration is addressed later.

The distinction between transcription and physiological activity can be illustrated using a defense marker such as PR1. Increased PR1 transcript can indicate activation of a salicylic acid-associated response in suitable contexts. It does not by itself measure the concentration of salicylic acid, the activity of all defense branches or the eventual number of surviving pathogen cells. If a mutant has high PR1 expression but also high pathogen multiplication, the marker has reported one component of the response, not successful resistance. Conversely, effective restriction with a comparatively modest marker response suggests that other defense mechanisms may contribute.

Some of the strongest immune phenotypes result from feedback. A receptor-initiated signal can increase the abundance of proteins that enhance subsequent responses, and altered cellular conditions can reinforce activation of existing components. Feedback is useful because a small recognition event may require a much larger physiological response to stop microbial multiplication. Yet a feedback loop that continues in the absence of an appropriate input could impose costs. Plants use several forms of negative regulation, including protein turnover, changes in phosphorylation state and redox buffering. The balance is not simply between an immune response that is on or off; it involves thresholds and dynamic regulation.

A direct test of an output's role in disease restriction compares plants that differ in the capacity to generate that output while preserving the initial recognition machinery as far as possible. For example, a plant lacking a functional ROS-producing enzyme may still recognize an effector but show altered downstream responses. If pathogen burden is increased, this supports a contribution of the enzyme-dependent process to resistance. It does not prove that ROS kills the pathogen directly, because ROS might regulate other host functions that are responsible for the phenotype. To discriminate these alternatives, one would need evidence linking the output to the relevant biochemical or cellular mechanism.

The local immune response is spatially heterogeneous. Cells near an infection site can receive different amounts of pathogen-derived material. Some cells may have already been damaged by invasion, while neighboring cells retain enough metabolic function to synthesize defense compounds and reinforce barriers. A measurement from a whole leaf averages these states. An apparent intermediate level of transcript or calcium may represent a uniform modest response or a strong response confined to a small fraction of cells. The distinction matters when interpreting microscopy, reporter assays and bulk biochemical measurements.

Immunity uses resources that are also needed for growth. Synthesis of defense proteins consumes amino acids and energy, production of metabolites redirects carbon and nitrogen, and changes in stomatal behavior can affect gas exchange. The physiological cost of an induced response should be distinguished from the effect of pathogen damage. A small plant after infection may be small because its immunity was inadequate, because its defense was costly, or because both processes contributed. To assess the cost of immunity itself, compare genotypes under conditions without infection and under matched infection burdens where feasible. Defense activation can be beneficial in a challenging environment even if it reduces growth in a benign environment.

The response discussed so far preserves or modifies living tissues. A particularly striking phenotype in some resistant interactions is instead rapid local cell death. Understanding when that phenotype contributes to defense, when it merely accompanies recognition, and when it can benefit the attacker requires separating cell death from pathogen restriction.

8. Hypersensitive response: local death versus resistance

A hypersensitive response, abbreviated HR, is a rapid, localized cell-death response that can accompany plant immune recognition. In some resistant interactions the affected tissue develops a visible necrotic lesion. Because classical gene-for-gene resistance often coincided with such lesions, it is easy to equate the lesion with immunity itself. However, the lesion is a phenotype of the plant tissue, whereas resistance is a reduction in the ability of the pathogen to establish infection or multiply. These are different observables and must be assessed independently.

The possible adaptive value of local death is most intuitive for a pathogen that depends on living host cells. A biotroph obtains resources from living tissue, and early loss of a suitably restricted group of host cells may disrupt its access to a compatible physiological environment. Local death can occur alongside cell-wall reinforcement and antimicrobial responses in surrounding tissue. The combined effect can prevent further establishment of the pathogen. Nevertheless, the precise contribution of death varies across interactions. A plant might restrict a pathogen through other outputs before a visible lesion develops, or it might achieve resistance without macroscopic necrosis.

Necrotrophs exploit dead or dying host tissue, making the relationship between cell death and resistance more complicated. In such interactions, host cell death can create conditions that favor pathogen access to nutrients. Some necrotrophic pathogens produce molecules that induce death in sensitive plant genotypes. A dramatic lesion may therefore indicate susceptibility rather than successful defense. Hemibiotrophs may initially depend on living tissue and later shift toward destructive colonization. The timing of death relative to pathogen development becomes important. The pathogen's lifestyle provides a hypothesis about the value of a response, not a rule that eliminates the need to measure disease and growth.

The hypersensitive response is not synonymous with the animal immunological hypersensitivity classifications. Although both terms describe particular responses to biological stimuli, plants do not possess the antibody and lymphocyte systems used to classify mammalian hypersensitivity reactions. Nor should all visible necrosis be called HR. Heat injury, mechanical damage, toxic compounds and successful pathogen attack can all cause cell death. Assigning a lesion to a receptor-dependent HR requires suitable evidence, including dependence on the recognition machinery, appropriate kinetics and relevant controls.

Membrane integrity is commonly assessed through leakage of electrolytes from tissue. Living cells maintain gradients of ions across their membranes; injury and loss of membrane integrity can allow ions to escape into the external medium. Increased conductivity can therefore provide a quantitative readout of tissue injury. It is not an exclusive marker of programmed cell death or of ETI, and it may be influenced by the amount of tissue, initial ion content and sample handling. Comparisons require matching the sample size and using appropriate baseline and maximum-leakage references. A large difference in conductivity should be interpreted as a difference in the measured leakage, not automatically a proportional difference in pathogen restriction.

For a hypothetical experiment, suppose equally sized leaf samples give background-corrected conductivities of 30 and 12 microsiemens per centimeter before complete disruption, with maximum conductivities of 60 and 48 microsiemens per centimeter after complete disruption. The normalized leakage estimates are 50 percent and 25 percent, respectively, calculated by dividing the initial corrected measurement by the corresponding maximum and multiplying by 100. The first sample has a larger fractional leakage in this illustrative assay. The calculation assumes comparable volumes and valid background correction, and that the maximum measurement represents the releasable electrolyte pool for that sample. It provides no direct estimate of viable pathogen numbers.

To determine whether HR contributes to resistance, a researcher can compare plants with similar recognition capacity but differences in the execution of cell death. If a mutant loses visible death yet maintains low pathogen burden, visible death is dispensable for that resistance phenotype under the tested conditions. If a mutant retains death but becomes susceptible, death is insufficient for resistance. If both death and resistance are lost, the simplest conclusion is that the affected gene is required for both in that context; the result does not establish that loss of death caused the loss of resistance. A shared upstream signaling requirement could account for both.

The logic is analogous to separating a signal from its downstream branches. Two outputs that usually occur together may be triggered by the same upstream receptor but depend on different intermediate processes. Genetic or physiological perturbations that separate the outputs are informative precisely because they test whether one is necessary for the other. A cell-death inhibitor that also affects pathogen growth directly would be difficult to interpret without additional controls. A genetic perturbation with pleiotropic effects may likewise change resistance independently of its effect on death. Evidence for necessity is stronger when the perturbation selectively alters the proposed causal process.

Local death can also affect surrounding living cells. Damage-associated signals released or exposed during tissue injury may stimulate defense in neighboring cells, contributing to spatial coordination. At the same time, destruction of too much tissue reduces photosynthetic area and can alter water relations. A lesion confined to a small region and a spreading lesion have different physiological consequences even if both originate with immune activation. Thus the relevant outcome is not maximal death but a regulated balance between restricting the pathogen and retaining sufficient viable tissue.

An examination question may describe a plant line with normal recognition, normal defense transcription, reduced electrolyte leakage and low bacterial growth. The best interpretation is that the measured cell-death phenotype has been reduced without abolishing effective resistance in the experiment. It would be unjustified to conclude that HR never contributes to resistance in any species, or that the affected gene plays no role in immunity. The finding establishes a conditional relationship in the particular interaction. This careful interpretation is a recurring principle of Part C reasoning.

The historical zig-zag model placed ETI after pathogen suppression of surface-receptor-mediated immunity and emphasized the stronger defense that may accompany ETI. That framework is valuable, but the overlapping outputs and variable relationship between cell death and resistance already suggest that a single linear amplitude scale is insufficient. Modern experiments reveal a cooperative network in which the two receptor systems can enhance one another.

9. From the zig-zag model to cooperating immune receptors

The historical zig-zag model introduced by Jones and Dangl in 2006 organized several decades of plant pathology into an intelligible sequence. The plant initially recognizes conserved microbial features through surface receptors, giving rise to pattern-triggered immunity. Pathogens capable of colonizing the host deploy effectors that can reduce defense, creating conditions for susceptibility. Plants possessing appropriate resistance genes detect effector activity and mount ETI. Evolution can then favor pathogen variants that avoid or suppress recognition, while plants evolve new resistance specificities. The repeated changes in the balance of defense and susceptibility give the model its name.

The zig-zag representation is especially useful for explaining why recognition of a pathogen-associated molecular pattern may not suffice to stop an adapted pathogen. It connects the acquisition of virulence functions to the selection of plant resistance genes. It also emphasizes that effector recognition can restore or increase defense after interference. However, the graph is a conceptual model, not a quantitative law describing the time course of every individual infection. Its vertical axis represents a qualitative effectiveness of defense, not a defined universal unit such as concentration of ROS or number of dead cells.

A strict interpretation in which PTI switches off completely before an independent ETI pathway switches on would overlook the biological context of an infection. A living bacterium can contain microbial patterns and deliver effectors during overlapping intervals. The plant may perceive extracellular patterns while also monitoring intracellular changes. Some surface-receptor outputs may be suppressed rather than abolished, and the strength of suppression may change over time. This makes it possible for the receptor systems to cooperate even when the pathogen is actively interfering with particular components.

An important mechanistic issue is the difference between the abundance of a signaling protein and its activation. Suppose a kinase or oxidase contributes to pattern-triggered signaling. Effector recognition may increase the synthesis or stability of that protein, while activation of a cell-surface receptor changes its activity through phosphorylation or another regulatory event. A large amount of inactive protein may produce little immediate output; a strong activation signal may have a limited effect when the amount of available protein is small. When both receptor systems operate, the abundance and activation of shared components can jointly support a greater response.

A simple illustrative model expresses an output rate v as k times E times a, where E is the abundance of an enzyme in arbitrary abundance units, a is the fraction of that enzyme in an active state, and k is its effective activity per abundance unit per time under fixed assay conditions. The model assumes output is approximately proportional to active enzyme and that substrate supply and other factors are not limiting. If E equals 2 and a equals 0.2, then active abundance is 0.4 units. If intracellular receptor signaling increases E to 5 while surface signaling raises a to 0.8, active abundance becomes 4 units. The resulting tenfold increase illustrates how abundance and activation can cooperate. These are hypothetical quantities, not kinetic parameters measured for a particular immune enzyme.

This simple multiplication also exposes the limitation of labeling two responses as stronger or weaker without identifying the biological variable. Increasing abundance alone from 2 to 5 at active fraction 0.2 produces 1 active unit. Raising activation alone to 0.8 at abundance 2 produces 1.6 active units. Applying both changes yields 4 units, which is greater than either single treatment. The calculation does not establish that a real immune network behaves multiplicatively; it demonstrates why a mechanism that alters protein abundance can interact with another mechanism that alters activation. Saturation, feedback, substrate depletion and negative regulation could all change the relationship.

In 2021, Yuan and colleagues investigated whether cell-surface pattern-recognition receptor pathways were required for NLR-mediated immunity in Arabidopsis. The study addressed a causal question: could an intact intracellular receptor system produce its normal resistance phenotype when selected surface-receptor signaling components were defective? Genetic comparisons and infection assays revealed that surface-receptor-associated signaling made an important contribution to effective NLR-mediated bacterial resistance. Their analysis identified the NADPH oxidase RBOHD and the kinase BIK1 as important elements connecting the two recognition systems in the tested context.

The result is conceptually important because a plant may possess a functional NLR, recognize the relevant effector and still fail to produce full resistance when shared or surface-associated signaling machinery is impaired. A student should not conclude that the NLR is absent simply because the pathogen multiplies in such a mutant. The receptor can be present and capable of initiating part of its response while downstream output is inadequate. Experimental evidence that a PRR-associated component contributes to ETI establishes functional cooperation in the relevant system, not a universal requirement for every individual PRR in every form of NLR-mediated immunity.

A complementary study by Ngou and colleagues, also published in Nature in 2021, examined interactions between surface and intracellular receptor activation. The investigators separated aspects of intracellular recognition from surface stimulation to test the contribution of each. They found that intracellular receptor signaling increased the abundance of components involved in surface-receptor signaling and that cell-surface activation potentiated intracellular receptor-associated responses. Together these studies support mutual reinforcement rather than a model of completely autonomous pathways. The interpretation is particularly informative because it relates the functional interaction to measurable changes in shared signaling proteins.

The two studies are not simply repetitions of the same experiment. One emphasizes the requirement for surface-associated machinery in effective NLR-mediated responses, while the other illuminates how activation of intracellular receptors can prepare or strengthen the machinery used by cell-surface receptors. Read together, they establish a more complete picture: the presence of both forms of recognition can change the strength, duration and efficacy of defense outputs. They do not show that every signaling event is identical between PTI and ETI, nor do they eliminate the meaningful distinction between the initiating receptors and the molecules perceived by them.

A hypothetical factorial experiment makes this reasoning accessible. Treat genetically matched plant tissue with neither input, a pattern alone, an inducible effector-recognition signal alone, or both. Suppose normalized ROS output is 2, 12, 7 and 35 units, respectively. The pattern-only effect relative to baseline is 10 units; the effector-only effect is 5 units. On an additive output scale, the predicted combined value is baseline 2 plus 10 plus 5, or 17. The observed combined value of 35 exceeds that expectation by 18 units. This is an illustrative positive interaction on the specified measurement scale. It does not demonstrate which molecular components mediate the interaction.

A quantitative interaction depends on the measurement scale. Additive effects on a linear fluorescence scale differ from additive effects on a logarithmic scale. A bounded percentage cannot increase indefinitely, and measurements close to saturation can obscure interactions. Thus experimental reasoning should specify both the measured quantity and the null expectation for independence. The four-treatment design allows a statistical test of interaction, provided appropriate biological replication and uncertainty estimates are available. Without replicates, the four numbers can illustrate a possible effect but cannot establish its reproducibility or statistical significance.

The integrated model also helps clarify the use of the terms PAMP, DAMP and effector. The classification is based on how the molecule or event is perceived and its role in the interaction. An extracellular microbial pattern activates a suitable surface receptor; a damage-associated molecular pattern can originate from the plant itself; an effector is a molecule that changes an interaction-relevant process. Some extracellular effectors are recognized by surface receptors, and intracellular receptor activation can lead to release of damage signals that engage surface systems. Consequently, location and molecular origin should be interpreted carefully rather than treated as exceptionless shortcuts.

The modern view does not require discarding the historical zig-zag model. For an examination asking who proposed it or how it accounts for selection of new resistance specificities, the classical framework remains useful. For an experimental question showing incomplete NLR-mediated resistance in a PRR-signaling mutant, the cooperative framework is more informative. The two descriptions answer different levels of question: one explains a broad evolutionary sequence, while the other explains the functional integration of receptor systems during actual responses. Good scientific models remain valuable when their assumptions and boundaries are made explicit.

10. Experimental tests of recognition and resistance

Plant disease resistance is experimentally challenging because several causal steps occur between molecular perception and a visible outcome. A pathogen may fail to cause a lesion because it cannot enter the tissue, because it cannot multiply once inside, or because the plant maintains function despite colonization. Conversely, a lesion may arise from host immune death rather than successful pathogen multiplication. To distinguish these possibilities, select assays that correspond to distinct stages of the proposed mechanism. An appropriate study typically combines pathogen growth, host physiological readouts, receptor or target genotypes and relevant controls.

Consider a bacterial growth assay expressed in colony-forming units per unit leaf area. A colony-forming unit, or CFU, is an operational estimate of a viable bacterium or group capable of producing a colony under the chosen culture conditions. It is not necessarily an exact count of all living cells, since some cells may fail to grow on the medium and clumps may yield one colony. Normalizing by leaf area helps account for differences in sampled tissue size. Such a growth assay is informative for culturable bacterial pathogens but may not be directly applicable to obligate biotrophs, viruses or pathogens measured through other methods.

In an illustrative data set, both a receptor-competent and a receptor-deficient plant contain 10,000 CFU per square centimeter immediately after inoculation. After three days the first contains 100,000 CFU per square centimeter, whereas the second contains 10,000,000. The starting population is the same, making the subsequent difference consistent with altered multiplication or survival rather than a difference in initial access. The receptor-competent line shows a tenfold net increase, while the deficient line shows a thousandfold increase. At day three, pathogen burden in the receptor-competent line is one hundredth of the deficient line's burden.

Biologists often present bacterial counts on a base-ten logarithmic scale because growth spans several orders of magnitude. In the preceding example, day-zero counts have log10 values of 4; the day-three values are 5 and 7. Their difference of 2 log units corresponds to a ratio of 10 squared, or 100. The log difference is dimensionless after expressing the count relative to a common unit. The plotted values are not the actual cell counts; a difference from 5 to 7 represents a multiplicative hundredfold change rather than an increase of two bacteria. A decrease of one log unit corresponds to a tenfold lower count.

One can calculate a descriptive average net growth rate using natural logarithms. Let N0 and Nt be pathogen population estimates at the beginning and end of a time interval t. Under an exponential approximation, r equals ln(Nt/N0) divided by t. With t equal to three days, the receptor-competent line has r approximately 0.768 per day, while the deficient line has r approximately 2.303 per day. These are average net rates over the interval, not necessarily instantaneous division rates. The calculation combines multiplication and losses, and assumes exponential behavior only as an approximation. Plant infection commonly involves lag periods, nutrient limitation and changing immune responses, so a multi-time-point measurement would better reveal the actual growth dynamics.

To investigate resistance specificity, compare a pathogen carrying a recognized effector with a matched variant lacking it. In the receptor-deficient line, the recognized effector might contribute to growth; in the receptor-competent line, the same effector could reduce growth by provoking immune recognition. If the effector-positive strain shows poor multiplication in all host genotypes, its poor growth might instead reflect a general fitness defect. Testing the strain in a suitable permissive host or measuring its growth under appropriate nonhost conditions can help separate these explanations. However, normal growth outside the plant does not prove that the strain has no fitness defect within the plant environment.

Complementation is central to causal genetics. If a loss-of-function host mutant is susceptible, restoring a functional version of the affected gene can test whether the mutation is responsible for the phenotype. A restored response supports the locus-specific interpretation, particularly when several independent lines show similar behavior. Nevertheless, excessive expression can activate defense artificially. A complemented line should be compared with a suitable control and assessed for basal immune activity. A line that is small and constitutively defensive before inoculation may resist through a mechanism different from the normally regulated recognition response.

A strong experimental design may also distinguish pathogen entry from internal multiplication. A surface challenge requires successful passage through external barriers, whereas an approach that introduces a matched inoculum directly into the apoplast bypasses some entry barriers. If the resistance difference appears only after surface exposure and not after matched internal exposure, altered entry may contribute to the phenotype. If the difference persists after equivalent initial internal loads, post-entry restriction is implicated. The latter observation does not exclude an additional contribution from surface defense in natural infections. The comparison is a conceptual tool for separating processes, not an instruction to reproduce a specialist infection procedure.

A measurement of immune transcription is frequently normalized to an internal reference. The reference should be stable across the relevant treatments; if the reference gene itself changes during infection, relative expression values can be misleading. In the idealized case of equal and near-perfect amplification efficiencies, relative expression can be represented by two raised to the negative delta-delta Ct. Here Ct denotes the amplification cycle at which a signal passes the chosen threshold. A lower Ct generally indicates a larger initial amount of the target sequence under matched conditions. The expression is a relative fold change rather than an absolute transcript concentration.

Suppose a control sample has a target Ct of 28 and reference Ct of 22, giving delta Ct equal to 6. An effector-recognition treatment has target Ct of 24 and reference Ct of 22, giving delta Ct equal to 2. The difference between treatment and control delta Ct is minus 4. Two raised to the fourth power equals 16, suggesting a sixteenfold increase in relative target transcript under the stated assumptions. If the target is a defense marker, this is evidence for transcriptional response. It does not show that the encoded protein is active or that the receptor directly regulates the gene. Protein abundance, functional assays and pathogen counts would address those additional questions.

Physical interaction assays likewise need appropriate interpretation. A receptor and an effector found together in a cellular protein complex may contact one another directly, or they may be joined through another host protein. An association observed in a cell does not by itself distinguish these possibilities. Binding of well-characterized purified components under controlled conditions provides stronger evidence for direct association, although in vitro binding may differ from the native intracellular context. A negative result may reflect inappropriate conditions or loss of a needed modification. A sequence of orthogonal assays is therefore more informative than a single interaction test.

Genetic epistasis can establish dependencies without requiring every physical interaction to be known. Suppose resistance to effector E requires host genes A and B. Plants lacking either are susceptible, and the double mutant is also susceptible. This result is compatible with a shared pathway but cannot determine which component acts first. If an independently activated form of B restores defense in the absence of A, while an activated form of A does not restore defense in the absence of B, the observations support B acting downstream of A or bypassing a requirement fulfilled by A. Overexpression or artificial activation could create a nonphysiological route, so such an interpretation remains conditional on the constructs and assay.

A rescue of pathogen restriction need not rescue every early readout. If B restores late defense without recovering an early calcium transient, it may bypass a specific signaling step or activate a parallel route. Conversely, restoration of the calcium signal without restoration of pathogen restriction shows that the rescued readout is not sufficient for the final outcome. These distinctions matter because diagrams often suggest a simple chain from receptor to disease phenotype, but real immune networks contain branching mechanisms and overlapping functions. The best interpretation is the narrowest causal conclusion justified by the observed dependencies.

When comparing mutant responses, include biological replication rather than treating several measurements from the same leaf as independent plants. Multiple readings of one sample can estimate technical variability, while independent plants capture biological variability. A difference between mean values should be assessed alongside the distribution of measurements. Environmental variation can influence plant immune phenotypes, so matching light, temperature, developmental stage and treatment timing helps isolate the genetic effect. Randomization and blinded phenotype scoring, where appropriate, can reduce bias. These general principles apply to experiments across life sciences and are central to Part C examination reasoning.

The experimental conclusion should state exactly what has been demonstrated: dependence on a receptor, modification of a target, activation of a downstream output, or restriction of pathogen growth. A complete mechanistic model may require evidence from several such experiments. For example, a compatibility matrix plus a receptor complementation test supports recognition specificity, a target-modification assay supports an indirect recognition mechanism, and a pathogen growth comparison establishes functional resistance. When these observations agree, they form a coherent causal account. A single correlation between a lesion and a gene expression change cannot substitute for this integrated evidence.

11. Evolution, resistance trade-offs and crop protection

Plant resistance genes are subject to selection in changing ecological environments. A plant that recognizes a common pathogenic effector may leave more offspring in a population where the corresponding pathogen is prevalent. A pathogen variant that escapes recognition may then have a selective advantage on hosts carrying the resistance gene. The selective effects depend on the frequency of the host and pathogen genotypes, the consequences of recognition, and the contribution of the effector to pathogen fitness. This helps explain why plant resistance specificity and pathogen effector diversity can change together.

The fitness cost of a pathogen escaping recognition can vary. If an effector is dispensable because other molecules perform similar virulence functions, its deletion may allow evasion with little immediate loss of pathogen fitness. If the effector contributes strongly to colonization, losing it may be disadvantageous in hosts that lack the corresponding receptor. An amino-acid change that prevents detection but preserves the relevant virulence function may then be favored. The consequences of escape therefore depend on functional constraints, and the most common escape mechanism cannot be inferred merely from the presence of a resistance gene.

A simplified numerical fitness model illustrates these trade-offs. Assume a pathogen population encounters a fraction p of host plants carrying R. Let the recognized effector-positive variant have relative multiplication 1.0 on susceptible hosts and 0.1 on resistant hosts. Let an escape variant have relative multiplication 0.8 on both, reflecting a hypothetical cost of losing effector function. Average relative multiplication of the recognized variant equals (1 − p) times 1.0 plus p times 0.1, or 1 − 0.9p. The escape variant has value 0.8 under the assumptions. Equating the values gives p equal to 0.2/0.9, approximately 0.222. Above this fraction, the escape variant is favored in the illustrative model.

The model assumes random encounter with host types, fixed relative multiplication values, and no effects of population density, migration, coinfection or temporal environmental changes. Real epidemiological and evolutionary dynamics are more complicated. The purpose is to show that the selective advantage of escape may depend on resistance-gene frequency even when escape carries a cost. It is not a forecast of how quickly a crop resistance gene will fail. In an examination problem, follow the assumptions supplied in the question and avoid transferring the calculated threshold to an actual crop.

The host faces an analogous trade-off. Maintaining receptor genes and immune readiness can be beneficial when pathogen pressure is high, but chronically active defense can reduce growth or reproduction in the absence of disease. A receptor allele that responds strongly may be advantageous against a pathogen yet impose a greater cost if it is prone to inappropriate activation. Conversely, a tightly regulated receptor may have little measurable cost when inactive. Thus the existence of a growth–defense trade-off does not imply that every functional resistance allele reduces yield in an uninfected plant. The size and nature of the cost must be measured.

Autoimmunity is a useful mechanistic example of such costs. A plant with an altered receptor or regulatory protein may express defense-associated genes without pathogen challenge and may exhibit dwarfing or necrosis. If removal of the activated immune receptor restores normal growth, the result supports a role for inappropriate immunity in the growth phenotype. Not every small plant with high defense markers is autoimmune; a primary metabolic defect may cause both poor growth and stress signaling. Genetic rescue and the order of observed events are important for distinguishing these explanations.

Crop improvement often uses resistance genes that recognize important pathogen determinants. A resistant cultivar can reduce disease and protect yield without requiring continuous external treatment. However, resistance based on a single specificity can be vulnerable when pathogen populations evolve or when an already existing variant escapes recognition. The probability of breakdown depends on pathogen genetic diversity, dispersal, reproductive biology, virulence costs, host deployment and other factors. Resistance should be described as effective against tested isolates and conditions rather than universally permanent.

Gene pyramiding brings several resistance determinants into one cultivar. If each gene recognizes a distinct effector and each recognition event is sufficient for restriction, a pathogen may need to evade several recognition functions to establish compatibility. This can increase the barrier to escape. Yet the strategy is not automatically durable: the pathogen could already carry several escape alleles, suppress a shared downstream component, or alter a common virulence process. Resistance genes may also interact in ways that change expression or cause fitness costs. The effectiveness of a pyramid depends on the actual biological mechanisms and the diversity of pathogen populations.

Quantitative resistance provides a different form of protection. Rather than completely preventing normal infection through one strong recognition determinant, multiple host loci may each reduce pathogen development, colonization, reproduction or disease severity. Such resistance can be partial and influenced by environment. It can complement specific resistance genes and may sometimes be less readily overcome, but it is not intrinsically indestructible. An exam question comparing major-gene and quantitative resistance should distinguish the underlying inheritance and phenotype, while recognizing that real cultivars can combine both.

Recessive resistance illustrates that effective plant protection does not always require the activation of an intracellular immune receptor. A pathogen may depend on a host factor for entry, replication or manipulation of cellular processes. An allele that removes the needed susceptibility function can reduce infection, provided the plant remains viable and functional. Such resistance may segregate recessively if a single functional copy supplies the pathogen with the required host activity. This differs from a dominant recognition allele even though both can lead to low pathogen burden. Genetic ratios and molecular experiments can help distinguish the two mechanisms.

Assessing crop resistance also requires distinguishing resistance from tolerance. A cultivar that has a similar pathogen load to another cultivar but shows less yield reduction may be tolerant rather than more resistant. Tolerance can be agriculturally valuable, but a tolerant cultivar may still support pathogen multiplication and contribute to inoculum in a population. Conversely, a highly resistant cultivar may preserve yield by reducing the pathogen burden. Measurements of both infection and crop performance help clarify the basis of protection. The disease triangle introduced earlier reminds us that host genotype, pathogen properties and environment all contribute to the observed outcome.

An illustrative tolerance comparison uses two genotypes that both lose yield as pathogen burden increases. At a common burden, genotype A retains 80 percent of its uninfected yield while genotype B retains 50 percent. These relative yields suggest that A maintains performance better under the tested burden. To establish a general difference in tolerance, measure performance across a range of comparable burdens and consider genotype-specific baseline performance. If genotype A has a much lower burden under identical exposure, that additional finding suggests greater resistance as well. Both properties can occur together, but one should not be inferred solely from the other.

The practical significance of ETI is therefore inseparable from evolution and whole-plant physiology. Molecular recognition establishes specificity, intracellular signaling coordinates defense, interacting receptors strengthen selected outputs, and the phenotype depends on the balance between protection and cost. Selection acts on the resulting performance of both organisms, not merely on the amount of a signaling protein or the size of a lesion. This perspective connects molecular biology to genetics, ecology and crop physiology without requiring specialist breeding protocols.

12. Integrating the local immune response

A single infection illustrates the continuity between the preceding nodes. A microbial pattern activates a surface receptor, producing early signals and local defenses. A compatible pathogen can deploy effectors that modify selected host processes. If a suitable intracellular receptor detects an effector or the altered state of a host target, NLR-dependent signaling contributes additional information. Some activated receptors form molecular assemblies; some generate nucleotide-derived signals and recruit helper pathways. The resulting immune outputs overlap with pattern-triggered responses and can reinforce the availability and activity of shared components.

The sequence is not a rigid timetable. Pattern recognition, effector delivery, transcription and changes in ion flux can overlap. The pathogen's activity may vary spatially across an infected leaf, and cells differ in their capacity to respond. A neighboring cell that has not received an effector may nevertheless sense a microbial pattern or a damage-associated signal. A local immune outcome is therefore produced by a network operating across molecular and tissue scales. The next stages of the curriculum examine other biological threats and the mechanisms that communicate defense beyond the original site.

When a question asks for the strongest evidence of disease resistance, select a measurement that demonstrates reduced successful infection or pathogen multiplication under comparable exposure. If the question asks for recognition specificity, examine matched host and pathogen genotypes. If it asks for direct binding, consider an appropriate biochemical interaction test. If the question asks whether a response is necessary for resistance, seek a selective perturbation that removes that response while retaining other relevant functions. These are not interchangeable forms of evidence, even when each addresses some aspect of plant immunity.

It is also useful to separate genotype from molecular activity. An R gene can be present while its protein product is unstable, inactive or unable to communicate with a downstream helper. An Avr gene can be present while its protein is not expressed, not delivered, or modified in a way that prevents recognition. Compatibility matrices are therefore simplified descriptions of functional determinants under stated assumptions, not proof that the mere detection of a gene sequence establishes the disease phenotype. In real interactions, gene expression and biological context determine whether the predicted recognition event occurs.

Finally, immunity should be connected to the broad objective of stress physiology: the maintenance of viable plant function under challenge. Effective defense can involve local cell sacrifice, but it must be considered in relation to pathogen restriction and preservation of the organism's remaining tissues. Resistance is not synonymous with an absence of measurable stress, and susceptibility is not synonymous with complete failure of perception. A susceptible plant may initiate several appropriate responses that are insufficient to control a particular pathogen. The most meaningful explanation traces the causes of that insufficiency and identifies which processes could alter the final outcome.
