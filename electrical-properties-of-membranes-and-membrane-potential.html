1. Why a living membrane has a voltage

Place two recording electrodes in the same salt solution and their potential difference should be close to zero after instrumental offsets have been corrected. Now move the tip of one fine electrode into an intact animal cell while keeping the reference electrode outside. The measured voltage often becomes negative. Withdraw the electrode and the difference disappears. The observation is remarkable because the cell has no metal battery hidden inside it. Instead, its thin lipid boundary, selectively permeable proteins and unequal distributions of ions together create an electrical state. How can these components produce a voltage while most of the cytoplasm remains electrically neutral?

The answer starts with ideas from membrane architecture and transport. Water dissolves ions, but the hydrophobic interior of a lipid bilayer strongly opposes the entry of hydrated charged particles. Ion channels provide selective routes through this barrier, and pumps maintain concentration differences using metabolic energy. The previous transport chapters explain how these routes and gradients arise. Here we ask a different question: given the gradients and the open pathways, what voltage will the membrane adopt, how quickly will it change, and what can an electrical recording reveal about the underlying transport processes?

A membrane potential is not peculiar to neurons. Many cells possess a voltage across their plasma membrane even when they never produce a classical nerve impulse. A voltage helps regulate uptake of nutrients, cellular pH, calcium entry and the storage of electrochemical energy. In bacteria and plant cells proton transport frequently contributes strongly to the voltage. In animal cells the potassium gradient and its resting permeability often make major contributions. This variety is precisely why a universal numerical resting potential cannot be assigned to all cells.

By the end of the chapter a student should be able to establish a consistent voltage and current convention, derive the equilibrium potential of an ion, calculate the potential produced by several permeant ions, distinguish conductance from permeability, use an electrical equivalent circuit to predict time-dependent behaviour, and interpret current-clamp and voltage-clamp records. These tasks build toward understanding electrical excitability at the level of an individual membrane rather than memorising the behaviour of an entire nervous system.

A useful scientific principle will recur throughout: concentrations describe chemical distributions, voltages describe electrical energy differences, and current describes the movement of charge per unit time. None of these quantities is interchangeable with another. Their connection can be developed from basic physics, and the resulting equations can be tested experimentally. A cell can have a substantial voltage with no net transmembrane current at that moment, and it can have no voltage despite unequal concentrations of certain ions.

2. Charge, potential and the thin insulating boundary

An ion carries an integral multiple of the elementary charge e, approximately 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb. The valence z gives both sign and magnitude: sodium and potassium have z = +1, calcium has z = +2, and chloride has z = −1. A coulomb is a unit of charge, not of energy. One mole of singly charged ions carries a charge magnitude F ≈ 96,485 C mol⁻¹, the Faraday constant. When ions move, their charge moves with them; in a biological saline solution, ions rather than free electrons are the main mobile charge carriers.

Electrical potential φ at a position represents electrical potential energy per unit positive test charge. Only a difference in potential is needed for the usual membrane calculations. Define Vₘ = φᵢₙ − φₒᵤₜ, and assign the extracellular solution zero as a convenient reference. If Vₘ = −70 mV, the intracellular side is 0.070 V below the outside. A positive charge moved from outside to inside would therefore decrease its electrical potential energy; a negative charge moved the same way would increase its electrical energy. The direction of this electrical preference does not by itself settle the direction of net ion movement because concentration can favour the opposite movement.

Why does a membrane sustain a potential? The water-rich solutions on each side conduct ionic charge reasonably well. The lipid interior is a much poorer conductor because a charged particle must give up favourable interactions with water to enter it. The membrane therefore separates two conducting regions with a thin dielectric layer between them. Opposite excess charges can collect close to the membrane surfaces without immediate neutralisation through the lipid core. At a negative inside potential, the inner interfacial region bears an excess negative charge relative to its uncharged state and the outer region a compensating positive excess, in the simplest capacitor picture. Actual membranes also contain fixed surface charges and ionic screening layers.

Do not picture the entire cytoplasm as a large reservoir of uncompensated negative charge. Nearly all positive charges are balanced by negative charges in the bulk. A very small imbalance close to the boundary is sufficient to produce a measurable voltage because the distance across the membrane is so small. If an ion crosses, the electrical distribution adjusts rapidly compared with the time needed to alter the bulk concentrations substantially in many cells. This separation of timescales is fundamental to the electrical model.

A membrane potential is also different from a membrane surface potential. Charged lipid headgroups or protein groups can attract counterions near a single membrane surface and change the local potential relative to the nearby bulk solution. Conventional intracellular recording measures the difference between the two bulk phases, not the entire microscopic potential profile. For elementary Nernst and Goldman calculations we use bulk concentrations or activities and a defined transmembrane voltage. Surface effects can alter local ion activity, but a detailed treatment is not necessary for the standard models.

3. Capacitance: how much charge is needed?

Capacitance measures how much charge must be separated to establish a given voltage. For an ideal capacitor the charge on the inner plate relative to its uncharged state is Q = CₘVₘ, where Cₘ is total membrane capacitance in farads. The opposite plate has charge −Q. A farad equals a coulomb per volt. A large capacitance means that more charge movement is required for the same voltage change; it does not mean that charge crosses the dielectric freely.

For a simple parallel-plate capacitor, a uniform electric field between plates has magnitude E = σ/ε, where σ is charge per unit area and ε is dielectric permittivity. The voltage magnitude is E d for plate separation d. Substitution of σ = |Q|/A gives |V| = |Q|d/(εA), so Cₘ = εA/d. Real biological membranes are not uniform planar capacitors, but this relation explains the main geometry dependence. Doubling the membrane area doubles total capacitance if composition and thickness are unchanged; increasing the effective dielectric thickness lowers capacitance. Specific capacitance cₘ = Cₘ/A is approximately 1 μF cm⁻² for many unmyelinated plasma membranes, with variation among preparations.

An electrical field can be large even when the potential difference seems small. A 70 mV drop across 5 nm, if treated as uniform, corresponds to 0.070/(5 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.4 × 10⁷ V m⁻¹. This is a model estimate rather than a claim that the local field is constant throughout the molecular membrane. Charged regions of voltage-sensitive proteins can respond to such fields, explaining how an electrical change can alter the probability of a channel opening.

For a worked example, consider a spherical cell of radius 10 μm. Its membrane area is 4πr² = 1,257 μm² = 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ cm². With cₘ = 1 μF cm⁻², Cₘ = 1.257 × 10⁻¹¹ F, or 12.57 pF. Charging it from zero to −70 mV requires Q = −8.80 × 10⁻¹³ C, equivalent in magnitude to about 5.49 × 10⁶ elementary charges. This is the net separated charge, not the total number of potassium ions that may have crossed over the cell's lifetime.

The same sphere has a volume of 4πr³/3 = 4,189 μm³ = 4.189 × 10⁻¹² L. At 140 mM K⁺ it contains approximately 0.140 × 4.189 × 10⁻¹² × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.53 × 10¹¹ potassium ions. The charge equivalent calculated above is only 0.00156% of that number. The estimate explains why a single electrical transient can be modelled using effectively constant bulk concentrations in a typical cell. It would be less safe to assume fixed concentrations in an extremely small compartment or during prolonged repeated transport.

The energy stored by a capacitor is U = ½CₘVₘ², obtained by integrating the incremental charging work V dQ from zero to Q. For the example cell, it is about 3.08 × 10⁻¹⁴ J. This stored electrical energy is not the whole energy in the unequal concentrations of ions. Chemical gradients store additional free energy and require metabolic work to maintain against leaks.

Cₘ = εA/d; Q = CₘVₘ; cₘ = Cₘ/A; U = ½CₘVₘ².

4. Chemical potential and electrochemical work

The passive movement of ions is a thermodynamic process. For a dilute ideal solution the chemical potential of a species can be expressed as μ = μ° + RT ln a, where a is dimensionless activity relative to a standard state, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and T is absolute temperature. Higher activity gives higher chemical potential. Concentration ratios approximate activity ratios when non-ideal interactions can be neglected or the activity coefficients cancel. A concentration gradient is therefore a source of chemical free energy; random microscopic motion gives a net movement down this gradient when a permeation route is available.

An ion has an additional electrical contribution to its molar potential. Since one mole carries charge zF, the electrical energy at potential φ is zFφ. The sum is called electrochemical potential, conventionally written μ̃ = μ° + RT ln a + zFφ. This expression unifies diffusion and electrical attraction without treating either as a separate mysterious force.

To decide whether an ion moves inward spontaneously, calculate the change in its electrochemical potential when one mole is transferred from outside to inside. Subtract the outside expression from the inside one. Assuming equivalent chemical standard states, the standard terms cancel. The chemical term becomes RT ln(aᵢ/aₒ) and the electrical term becomes zFVₘ. The inward free-energy change is therefore ΔGᵢₙ = RT ln(aᵢ/aₒ) + zFVₘ. If it is negative, inward transfer is favourable; if positive, outward transfer is favourable. A closed channel may prevent either favourable transfer from occurring. The equation predicts direction and energy per mole, not rate.

The logarithm can be positive or negative. For a potassium concentration of 140 mM inside and 5 mM outside, the chemical term for inward movement is positive: bringing more potassium into the already concentrated cytoplasm is chemically uphill. At −70 mV, the electrical term for K⁺ is negative and favours inward movement. Which preference dominates? At 310 K the chemical cost is 8.314 × 310 × ln(28) ≈ 8.59 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical contribution is 96,485 × (−0.070) ≈ −6.75 kJ mol⁻¹. Their sum is +1.84 kJ mol⁻¹. Hence potassium has a net outward tendency through an open potassium channel despite the negative intracellular voltage.

Contrast chloride with potassium. A negative intracellular voltage makes inward movement electrically unfavourable for Cl⁻, since multiplying a negative z by a negative Vₘ gives a positive energy. An outward chloride movement transfers negative charge outward and is electrically equivalent to positive current inward. This is why anion flux and conventional electrical current require separate sign reasoning. The free-energy expression avoids the common error of assuming that a negative voltage attracts every ion.

Electrochemical potentials provide the same bookkeeping used for energy-coupled transport in the preceding chapter. For several transported species, sum each molar free-energy change multiplied by its stoichiometric coefficient in the direction of transport. A coupled process may run when the overall free-energy change is negative even if one constituent moves uphill. Here the special case in which a single permeant ion has no net free-energy difference leads directly to the Nernst equation.

For outside → inside: ΔG = RT ln(aᵢ/aₒ) + zFVₘ. Negative ΔG favours inward transfer.

5. Deriving the Nernst equation from equilibrium

Imagine two compartments containing different concentrations of KCl, separated by a membrane permeable to K⁺ but not to Cl⁻. Initially set the voltage to zero. Potassium begins to diffuse from the concentrated side to the dilute side. Chloride cannot follow. A tiny excess of negative charge remains near the concentrated side and positive charge appears near the dilute side. The developing electrical potential increasingly opposes further net potassium loss. There is a moment when the chemical tendency outward is balanced exactly by the electrical tendency inward. The net potassium flux then vanishes, although potassium ions may continue to cross in both directions at equal rates.

At this equilibrium, μ̃ᵢ = μ̃ₒ, so the free energy for moving potassium inward is zero. For a general ion X, set 0 = RT ln(aᵢ/aₒ) + zFEₓ. Rearranging, Eₓ = −(RT/zF) ln(aᵢ/aₒ) = (RT/zF) ln(aₒ/aᵢ). Eₓ is the membrane potential, inside relative to outside, that would balance the electrochemical gradient of ion X. It is not the voltage that must exist in every membrane containing that ion. The formula can be written with base-ten logarithms by using ln x = 2.302585 log₁₀ x.

At 25°C (298.15 K) the factor 2.303RT/F is 59.16 mV. At 37°C (310.15 K) it is 61.54 mV. At 20°C (293.15 K) it is 58.17 mV. These differences are modest but relevant when temperature is specified in an examination. Always convert Celsius to kelvin before substituting into an expression containing R. For divalent ions divide the coefficient by two. For anions divide by a negative valence, which is equivalent to reversing the activity ratio while using the magnitude of the valence.

Consider a tenfold gradient with the ion concentrated inside. For a monovalent cation at 37°C, log₁₀(0.1) = −1, so Eₓ = −61.54 mV. For a monovalent anion with exactly the same gradient, Eₓ = +61.54 mV. For a divalent cation the result is −30.77 mV. If both sides have equal activity, the equilibrium potential is zero for every nonzero valence. The Nernst equation is undefined for an uncharged solute because there is no electrical potential that can balance a nonzero chemical gradient for z = 0.

This equation makes explicit what an ion-selective membrane does: it converts a chemical potential difference into a voltage. If an initially uncharged compartment has finite volume, redistribution slightly changes the concentrations as it charges. The elementary calculation assumes that the stated concentrations are the equilibrium values or that the changes required for charging are negligible. Ion binding, chemical reactions and activity coefficients can alter the available free ion concentration, so total cellular content is not automatically the correct input. Calcium is an especially clear example because much of cellular calcium is buffered or sequestered.

A crucial distinction is that equilibrium for potassium says nothing about sodium, chloride or any other ion. At Eₖ, sodium may still have an enormous inward driving force. If a sodium pathway opens, the potential will change. The Nernst equation gives an individual ion's reference voltage, which will become the natural battery voltage of that ionic pathway in the electrical circuit.

Eₓ = (RT/zF) ln(aₒ/aᵢ) = (2.303RT/zF) log₁₀(aₒ/aᵢ). At 37°C: Eₓ ≈ (61.54 mV/z) log₁₀([X]ₒ/[X]ᵢ).

6. Calculate equilibrium potentials and interpret signs

A useful set of illustrative mammalian-cell concentrations is K⁺ 140 mM inside and 5 outside, Na⁺ 15 inside and 145 outside, Cl⁻ 10 inside and 110 outside, and free Ca²⁺ 0.0001 mM inside and 1.2 outside. These numbers are not a universal recipe for cytoplasm or plasma; the listed ions are only a subset of the species required for bulk electroneutrality. We use them as inputs for calculations at 37°C.

For K⁺, the ratio is 5/140 = 0.035714; its common logarithm is −1.44716. Multiplying by 61.54 mV gives Eₖ ≈ −89.1 mV. For Na⁺, 145/15 = 9.667 and log₁₀(9.667) ≈ 0.9853; Eₙₐ ≈ +60.6 mV. These results have very different signs because K⁺ is concentrated inside and Na⁺ is concentrated outside.

For Cl⁻, use z = −1. The ratio outside/inside is 110/10 = 11; log₁₀(11) = 1.0414, multiplied by −61.54 mV gives E꜀ₗ ≈ −64.1 mV. An alternative calculation is +61.54 log₁₀(10/110). Both are identical. Never reverse the ratio and also introduce a negative valence, which would incorrectly change the sign twice.

For Ca²⁺, the free intracellular concentration of 100 nM must be converted to the same unit as 1.2 mM outside. It is 0.0001 mM, not 0.1 mM. The ratio is 12,000 and its log is 4.07918; multiplying by 30.77 mV gives E꜀ₐ ≈ +125.5 mV. This unusually positive reference voltage is a consequence of the enormous free calcium gradient, partly moderated by the divalent charge. It predicts a strong tendency for calcium entry when its channels open at an ordinary negative voltage.

Now imagine raising extracellular K⁺ from 5 to 10 mM while intracellular K⁺ remains fixed. Because Eₖ depends logarithmically on concentration ratio, the increase in Eₖ is 61.54 log₁₀(2) ≈ +18.53 mV. Its new value is about −70.5 mV. Doubling extracellular potassium does not double its equilibrium potential or halve the original negative value. A tenfold increase gives a +61.54 mV shift at this temperature regardless of the starting concentration as long as the inside concentration is held fixed.

If equilibrium potential and extracellular concentration are known, rearrange to find the intracellular concentration. For chloride at 25°C with E꜀ₗ = −59.16 mV and 100 mM chloride outside, −59.16 = −59.16 log₁₀(100/[Cl⁻]ᵢ). Therefore the logarithm is one and the ratio is ten, giving 10 mM inside. This inverse reasoning is frequently more useful than memorising typical values. In a real experiment, a reversal measurement can provide an estimate of free intracellular concentration if the channel is sufficiently selective and activity and recording corrections are appropriate.

7. Driving force, ionic current and reversal

To translate the equilibrium potential into a prediction at any actual membrane voltage, replace the chemical term in the inward free-energy expression with −zFEₓ. The result is ΔGᵢₙ = zF(Vₘ − Eₓ). The difference Vₘ − Eₓ is called the electrical driving force in the conventional current expression. The quantity is a voltage. For cations, a negative difference favours inward flux; for anions, a positive difference favours inward flux. This follows directly from the sign of z in the energy equation.

Electrophysiologists commonly define positive transmembrane ionic current as outward movement of positive charge. A positive ion moving out and a negative ion moving in therefore both produce positive current. Conversely, inward sodium and outward chloride both produce negative current. If Jₒ is the outward molar flux of an ion per second, its conventional outward current is I = zFJₒ. The formula refers to total flux and current; if J is per area, I is current density per area. For the rest of this chapter, outward ionic current is positive and inward injected current is positive in the membrane charging equation.

For an approximately ohmic open pathway, Iₓ = gₓ(Vₘ − Eₓ), where gₓ is conductance in siemens. A siemens is an ampere per volt. The current depends on both conductance and driving force. One nanosiemens multiplied by one millivolt gives one picoampere because 10⁻⁹ S × 10⁻³ V = 10⁻¹² A. An equilibrium potential is a property of the ionic gradient and temperature, not the number of channels. Changing conductance alters the slope of an ideal current-voltage relation but not the zero-current voltage of a perfectly selective channel with fixed concentrations.

For the example concentrations, at Vₘ = −70 mV the potassium driving force is +19.1 mV. If gₖ = 4 nS, the outward potassium current is +76.4 pA. Sodium's driving force is −130.6 mV; with gₙₐ = 0.5 nS, its current is −65.3 pA (inward). Chloride's driving force is −5.9 mV; with g꜀ₗ = 2 nS, its current is −11.8 pA, corresponding to outward movement of chloride. Calcium's driving force is −195.5 mV; any open calcium pathway would tend to carry inward positive charge. Note that potassium and sodium currents can partially cancel electrically although both ionic gradients are being dissipated.

If voltage is varied while a selective pathway remains open, its current reverses direction at Eₓ. The observed reversal potential is the voltage at which the current through the pathway is zero. For an ideally selective passive channel this equals the Nernst potential of its permeant ion. For a channel permeable to multiple ions, its reversal potential depends on the competing permeabilities and concentrations; individual ions may still pass in opposite electrical directions. For a coupled transporter, zero net transport may depend on the sum of the electrochemical terms and any energy source, not just one ion's Nernst equation.

Real current-voltage curves need not be straight. Conductance can vary with voltage, and the concentration dependence of ionic movement can make an open pore non-ohmic. Rectification means current is not symmetric on either side of the reversal potential. Inward rectifier potassium channels, for example, can permit greater inward than outward current at equal magnitudes of driving force under appropriate conditions. Their behaviour does not negate the thermodynamic equilibrium condition. A zero current can also occur because every channel is closed; in this case the observation is not evidence that the membrane is at the ion's reversal potential.

8. Resistance, conductance and the equivalent circuit

Electrical resistance Rₘ describes the opposition to current in a given pathway; the reciprocal is conductance Gₘ = 1/Rₘ. Resistance is measured in ohms and conductance in siemens. Lipid membranes with few open channels can have large resistance. Opening channels provides parallel conductive paths, reducing the membrane resistance even when the lipid itself is unchanged. Total membrane conductance depends on area, channel density, open probability and the individual channel conductances.

To see why the pathways are parallel, note that each crosses between the same intracellular and extracellular compartments. All share the membrane voltage. Each selective pathway has a distinct reference voltage, represented as a battery with voltage Eₓ in series with a conductance or resistor. The battery symbol represents stored electrochemical free energy in a gradient, not an ATP-consuming molecular pump inside the channel. In parallel with these paths is the membrane capacitor. An active electrogenic pump may be represented separately as a current source in a model where its current is specified.

For independent identical channels of single-channel conductance γ and open probability Pₒ, the expected macroscopic conductance is g = NγPₒ. For 500 channels with γ = 20 pS and Pₒ = 0.1, the mean conductance is 1,000 pS = 1 nS. At a driving force of −50 mV, the mean current is −50 pA. A single open channel would carry −1 pA. In a population of channels the instantaneous open number fluctuates, but the ensemble average follows this calculation. A reduction in current could arise from fewer channels, smaller single-channel conductance, lower opening probability or reduced driving force; a single current amplitude cannot distinguish all these causes.

At a steady voltage, an ideal capacitor carries no charging current. For the parallel ohmic pathways and a specified outward pump current Iₚ, zero net outward current gives Σgₓ(Vₘ − Eₓ) + Iₚ = 0 when no external current is injected. Expanding gives VₘΣgₓ − ΣgₓEₓ + Iₚ = 0. Hence Vₘ = (ΣgₓEₓ − Iₚ)/Σgₓ. In the absence of electrogenic pump current, the result is a conductance-weighted mean of the equilibrium potentials. Increasing one conductance pulls the voltage toward that pathway's equilibrium potential. This form is exact only for the assumed linear conductances and fixed gradients. If conductances depend on voltage, solving the balance requires their values at the operating voltage and may require a nonlinear calculation.

For gₖ = 8 nS, gₙₐ = 1 nS and g꜀ₗ = 3 nS with the earlier potentials, the voltage is [8(−89.1) + 1(60.6) + 3(−64.1)]/12 ≈ −70.38 mV. Adding a constant outward pump current of 24 pA changes the result by −24 pA/12 nS = −2 mV, to −72.38 mV. The sign is physically reasonable: a current that removes positive charge makes the interior more negative until passive currents balance it.

A high resistance makes a given injected current produce a larger steady voltage displacement, but the same high resistance also makes capacitor discharge slower if capacitance is unchanged. Total conductance is the sum of the conductances in parallel; total resistance is not the sum of their resistances. The electrical model explains why opening channels can simultaneously change the resting voltage, lower resistance and alter the rate of the membrane response.

Vsteady = (ΣgₓEₓ − Iₚ)/Σgₓ; Rₘ = 1/Σgₓ (fixed ohmic pathways).

9. Membrane charging and the RC time constant

A change in membrane voltage requires redistribution of charge, so a voltage response cannot generally jump instantaneously under a finite current. Differentiate Q = CₘVₘ at constant capacitance to obtain the capacitive current Cₘ dVₘ/dt. Charge conservation says that the rate of increase in inner charge equals inward injected current minus total outward ionic current. Therefore Cₘ dVₘ/dt = Iinj − Σgₓ(Vₘ − Eₓ) − Iₚ. This equation connects the circuit directly to voltage dynamics. It is valid for the lumped, isopotential membrane model; spatially extended cells may have different local voltages.

Consider a membrane initially at its resting value Vrest, with fixed conductances and fixed pump current. Write ΔV = Vₘ − Vrest. Subtracting the resting current balance gives Cₘ dΔV/dt = Iinj − GₘΔV. For a constant current step, rearrange to dΔV/dt = (Iinj/Gₘ − ΔV)/(Cₘ/Gₘ). Define Rₘ = 1/Gₘ and τ = RₘCₘ. Then dΔV/dt = (IinjRₘ − ΔV)/τ. This is a first-order relaxation equation: as ΔV increases, the opposing leak current increases, reducing the fraction of injected current available to charge the capacitor.

With ΔV(0) = 0, integrate dΔV/(IinjRₘ − ΔV) = dt/τ. The integral gives −ln[(IinjRₘ − ΔV)/(IinjRₘ)] = t/τ. Rearranging gives ΔV(t) = IinjRₘ(1 − e⁻ᵗ/τ). When the injection ends, the capacitor discharges through the ionic pathways, and ΔV(t) = ΔV(0)e⁻ᵗ/τ measured from the end of the pulse. At t = τ, the charging displacement reaches about 63.2% of its final value and a discharging displacement retains about 36.8%. After three time constants the charging response is approximately 95% complete.

For a 100 pF cell with 100 MΩ input resistance, τ = 100 × 10⁻¹² × 100 × 10⁶ s = 0.010 s = 10 ms. A +50 pA inward injection produces a steady change IinjRₘ = 50 × 10⁻¹² × 100 × 10⁶ V = 5 mV. After 10 ms the membrane has depolarized by 3.16 mV, and after 20 ms by 4.32 mV. When the pulse ends at steady state, after another 10 ms the displacement is 1.84 mV. If conductance doubles at the same capacitance and the resting voltage is treated separately, resistance and τ halve, and the steady displacement for the same current is 2.5 mV.

The initial charging rate just after the pulse begins is Iinj/Cₘ, since ΔV is still zero. For this example it is 0.5 V s⁻¹ = 0.5 mV ms⁻¹. An abrupt change in leak conductance without a change in capacitance does not change this incremental initial slope when starting from the corresponding steady state. It does change the later curvature and plateau. This distinction allows capacitance and resistance to be inferred from different parts of a passive response.

Capacitance and resistance must both refer to the same membrane area. Specific resistance in Ω cm² multiplied by specific capacitance in F cm⁻² also gives seconds. If area doubles without changing specific conductance or capacitance, total capacitance doubles, total resistance halves, and τ is unchanged. A larger cell nevertheless experiences a smaller voltage change for the same total current injection. In an actual elongated neuron, current may also travel along the cytoplasm and membrane voltage need not be uniform. The lumped RC result is a local foundation rather than a full cable-theory description.

τ = RₘCₘ; charging ΔV = IinjRₘ(1 − e⁻ᵗ/τ); discharge ΔV = ΔV₀e⁻ᵗ/τ.

10. Why a real resting potential requires several ions: Goldman

A membrane permeable only to potassium eventually approaches Eₖ. Actual cells usually have some resting permeability to sodium, chloride and other ions as well. A sodium leak tends to carry positive charge inward at negative membrane potentials, whereas potassium loss tends to carry positive charge out. The actual voltage is the value at which their electrical effects balance together with other currents. A weighted average of equilibrium potentials using conductances is useful for linear pathways, but a different model is needed when the given quantities are permeabilities and concentrations.

Permeability P expresses the ease with which a species traverses a membrane under a concentration or electrochemical gradient. In a simple homogeneous barrier model it has units of length per time and can be represented by Dβ/d, where D is a diffusion coefficient within the barrier, β is a partition factor and d is thickness. Effective ion permeabilities may be fitted from experiments and depend on available pathways. A conductance in siemens measures electrical current per voltage difference in an appropriate approximation. There is no general equality between a permeability ratio and a conductance ratio; conductance also depends on ionic concentrations and voltage.

Goldman's constant-field model provides an expression for ion flux that includes both diffusion and electrical migration. Hodgkin and Katz applied the framework to biological membranes. The membrane is approximated as a homogeneous barrier with a linear electrical potential profile, independent movement of ionic species, constant effective permeabilities for the conditions considered, and fixed boundary concentrations. The familiar voltage equation is derived by setting the net passive ionic current to zero and using only monovalent species. It is not a statement that every species is individually in equilibrium.

The derivation begins with the one-dimensional Nernst–Planck expression for outward molar flux J = −D(dc/dx + zFc/RT · dφ/dx), where x increases from inside to outside. The first term is diffusion; the second is migration in the electric field. Let the inside be x = 0, the outside x = d and φₒ − φᵢ = −Vₘ. Then dφ/dx = −Vₘ/d under the constant-field approximation. Set u = zFVₘ/RT. The flux equation becomes dc/dx − (u/d)c = −J/D.

Multiplying by the integrating factor e⁻ᵘˣ/ᵈ converts the left side into the derivative of c e⁻ᵘˣ/ᵈ. Integrating from 0 to d gives c(d)e⁻ᵘ − c(0) = −(J/D)d(1 − e⁻ᵘ)/u. With boundary partition factors absorbed into P, rearrange to obtain outward flux J = Pu(cᵢ − cₒe⁻ᵘ)/(1 − e⁻ᵘ). Multiplying by zF gives current density. The expression has a well-defined limit as V approaches zero: J tends to P(cᵢ − cₒ), ordinary outward diffusion. It also vanishes when cᵢ = cₒe⁻ᵘ, which rearranges to the Nernst equation, an important consistency check.

For monovalent cations put q = FVₘ/RT, so u = q. Their currents have a common factor Fq/(1 − e⁻ᑫ) times P(cᵢ − cₒe⁻ᑫ). For a monovalent anion u = −q, and the multiplication by zF reverses flux sign; expressing the denominator in terms of e⁻ᑫ gives the same common factor times P(cₒ − cᵢe⁻ᑫ). Summing K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ currents and setting the result to zero therefore yields A − Be⁻ᑫ = 0, with A = Pₖ[K]ᵢ + Pₙₐ[Na]ᵢ + P꜀ₗ[Cl]ₒ and B = Pₖ[K]ₒ + Pₙₐ[Na]ₒ + P꜀ₗ[Cl]ᵢ. Thus eᑫ = B/A. Taking the logarithm produces the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation. At V = 0 the result is obtained by continuity, not by treating the common prefactor as zero.

Notice that chloride appears with inside and outside interchanged relative to sodium and potassium. This is a direct consequence of its charge sign, not an arbitrary memorisation rule. If only K⁺ permeability is nonzero, the expression reduces to Eₖ. If only chloride is permeant, it reduces to its correctly signed Nernst potential. These limiting cases are quick ways to detect a transposed ratio in an examination. The ordinary logarithmic voltage form cannot simply accept calcium as another monovalent term: a divalent ion changes the exponential dependence, and the zero-current equation must be solved with its appropriate current expression.

The idealised GHK flux and voltage expressions are not identical. The flux expression describes individual ionic movement at a specified voltage; the voltage expression identifies a zero-net-current condition under its particular assumptions. The latter does not explicitly include pump current. If an electrogenic pump is appreciable, the full passive current expressions and pump current must be balanced, or a suitable linearised circuit can be used. GHK is particularly valuable for predicting how a change in relative permeability or ion concentration changes the membrane potential, but it is not a complete description of every real membrane.

11. Goldman calculations and permeability changes

Use the earlier concentrations and relative permeabilities Pₖ:Pₙₐ:P꜀ₗ = 1:0.04:0.45 at 37°C. Relative permeabilities can be used because a common multiplier cancels from the numerator and denominator. In the numerator, potassium contributes 5, sodium contributes 0.04 × 145 = 5.8, and chloride contributes 0.45 × 10 = 4.5. Their sum is 15.3. In the denominator potassium contributes 140, sodium 0.04 × 15 = 0.6, and chloride 0.45 × 110 = 49.5, totalling 190.1. The ratio is 0.08048. The voltage is 61.54 log₁₀(15.3/190.1) ≈ −67.34 mV.

This value is less negative than Eₖ because sodium and chloride have more positive individual reference voltages with the stated concentrations. Potassium has the largest relative permeability but the sodium term in the numerator is appreciable even though the sodium permeability is small, because outside sodium concentration is high. The expression therefore depends on products of permeabilities and concentrations rather than permeability alone. If all permeabilities are multiplied by ten, the predicted zero-current voltage is unchanged even though the fluxes at a given voltage and the membrane's electrical response can be very different.

Now increase extracellular potassium to 10 mM with other concentrations and relative permeabilities fixed. The numerator rises to 20.3 and the denominator stays 190.1, so Vₘ ≈ −59.79 mV. This depolarization is smaller than the 18.53 mV shift of the potassium Nernst potential because other permeant species buffer the voltage change. Raising external K⁺ to 50 mM gives a predicted Vₘ ≈ −30.69 mV. These are hypothetical changes to isolate the parameter; real bathing solutions must preserve suitable osmolarity and charge balance by replacing ions appropriately.

Next increase sodium permeability to 0.4 while restoring extracellular K⁺ to 5 mM. The numerator is 5 + 58 + 4.5 = 67.5 and the denominator is 140 + 6 + 49.5 = 195.5. Vₘ becomes −28.42 mV. Increasing Pₙₐ further to 20 gives 47.64 mV, moving toward Eₙₐ without necessarily reaching it. This simple calculation captures the central idea of the action-potential rising phase, although a real action potential is a dynamic process governed by changing conductances and capacitance rather than a sequence of exact instantaneous GHK steady states.

The Goldman equation can also be rearranged to infer a relative permeability. Suppose only sodium and potassium are permeant. Set Pₖ = 1 and Pₙₐ = r. With a measured steady Vₘ = −60 mV, q = 10^(−60/61.54) ≈ 0.106. Then q(140 + 15r) = 5 + 145r. Rearrange r = (140q − 5)/(145 − 15q) ≈ 0.069. The inference is valid only within the assumptions of the two-ion model. If an unknown chloride permeability or pump current is present, the same single measured voltage cannot uniquely identify both sodium and potassium permeability. Additional measurements or interventions are needed.

One can reason about an individual permeability change without repeated arithmetic. The ratio inside the logarithm is a positive weighted average of the individual numerator/denominator concentration ratios, with weights proportional to the permeability times that ion's denominator concentration. Increasing the weight for one ion moves the ratio toward its own ratio, and because the logarithm is increasing, the voltage moves toward its own Nernst potential. This is why increasing chloride permeability can depolarize or hyperpolarize depending on the chloride gradient. No ion is intrinsically assigned a fixed voltage-changing direction.

12. Resting potential: a dynamic steady state maintained by metabolism

The resting membrane potential describes the baseline voltage of a cell in a defined physiological condition, without a deliberately applied stimulus. Rest is not synonymous with inactivity. Cells continually exchange ions and consume energy while maintaining a roughly stable voltage and internal composition. In many animal cells, potassium-selective resting pathways provide relatively high K⁺ permeability. Sodium leaks inward, potassium tends to leave, and chloride behaviour depends on its gradient. The voltage settles where the sum of currents, including active transport, is zero if no external current is applied.

A useful distinction is between electrical steady state and ionic steady state. Electrical steady state means dVₘ/dt = 0, or no net charging of the membrane capacitor. It is possible even while a large inward sodium current cancels an equal outward potassium current. Ionic steady state requires that the amount of each species in the cell remain constant over the chosen interval. To maintain this second condition, passive leaks must be balanced by active or coupled transport and any relevant cellular reactions. A cell with only leaks and no energy supply may temporarily show a steady voltage while its gradients slowly decay.

In animal plasma membranes, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase uses one ATP to export three sodium ions and import two potassium ions. The cycle moves one net elementary positive charge outward. Hence it is electrogenic and has a direct hyperpolarizing contribution. More importantly, it maintains the sodium and potassium gradients that selective channels convert into electrical potential. The direct contribution is not necessarily large compared with the diffusion potential; it varies with pump activity and membrane resistance. It is therefore inaccurate to say that the pump alone directly produces the entire resting potential or that potassium leaks alone can maintain a living cell's gradients indefinitely.

Imagine selectively inhibiting the pump while channel behaviour and external solutions are initially unchanged. The immediate effect is loss of its outward current, so the membrane tends to become less negative. At first Eₖ and Eₙₐ are almost unchanged because the stored gradients contain many ions relative to the small charge required to change voltage. Later, as sodium accumulates and potassium is lost through available pathways, the gradients weaken and their Nernst potentials move toward zero. Secondary active transport and osmotic balance can also be disturbed. The immediate electrical response and the slow chemical response are different consequences of the same inhibition.

For example, a 24 pA outward pump current corresponds to 24 × 10⁻¹²/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 1.50 × 10⁸ net elementary charges per second. Under ideal 3 Na⁺/2 K⁺ coupling this is about 1.50 × 10⁸ pump cycles and ATP molecules per second across the entire example membrane. The molecular sodium efflux is three times this cycle rate and potassium influx is twice it. The net electrical current is therefore not a direct count of all transported ions. The example is an assumed aggregate current and not a universal metabolic rate.

Fixed intracellular anions, including charged proteins, help explain why ionic distributions and osmotic conditions cannot be understood by considering only freely permeant ions. In an ideal Donnan system with a membrane permeable to monovalent cations and anions but impermeable to a charged macromolecule, the permeant ions can achieve electrochemical equilibrium simultaneously. Equating their Nernst potentials implies [cation]ᵢ[anion]ᵢ = [cation]ₒ[anion]ₒ under ideal conditions. If the outside is a large 100 mM salt reservoir and inside contains 50 mM fixed negative charge equivalents, electroneutrality gives cᵢ − aᵢ = 50 and the product cᵢaᵢ = 10,000 mM². Solving yields cᵢ ≈ 128.08 mM and aᵢ ≈ 78.08 mM, and the voltage at 37°C is about −6.6 mV. The example assumes fixed volume or an appropriate mechanical constraint; freely suspended animal cells also face osmotic water entry. Living cells are generally not simple passive Donnan equilibria because pumps, selective pathways and osmotic regulation maintain a nonequilibrium state.

Plants and many microorganisms provide a valuable contrast. In plant plasma membranes, outward proton pumping can make the interior strongly negative, supplying electrical energy for nutrient uptake. In bacterial membranes, electron transport and proton translocation can establish an electrochemical proton gradient. The basic charge and energy equations are unchanged, even though the ions, transport proteins and quantitative resting potentials differ. These examples show why the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is not a universal prerequisite for membrane voltage.

13. Depolarization, hyperpolarization and the special role of chloride

Depolarization means movement of the membrane voltage toward a less negative value from a negative resting baseline, for example from −70 to −50 mV. A voltage that becomes positive is also depolarized relative to that baseline; the portion above zero is called overshoot in an action potential. Hyperpolarization means movement to a more negative value than the baseline, such as −70 to −90 mV. Repolarization means returning toward the original resting value after a displacement, especially after depolarization. These terms describe voltage changes, not the movement of one named ion.

Opening a sodium-selective channel at −70 mV usually causes depolarization because Eₙₐ is far more positive, making sodium entry favourable. Opening a potassium channel at the same voltage usually causes hyperpolarization because Eₖ is more negative. But at −110 mV the same potassium-selective channel has Vₘ − Eₖ negative; positive charge enters and the membrane moves toward −89 mV, a depolarization. The direction depends on voltage relative to the equilibrium potential. The ion name is not sufficient.

Chloride provides an especially important application. If E꜀ₗ is −75 mV and the cell is at −60 mV, opening chloride channels drives chloride inward, a positive outward conventional current, pulling the voltage toward −75 mV. If E꜀ₗ is −40 mV because intracellular chloride is elevated, opening chloride channels at −60 mV drives chloride outward and depolarizes the membrane. Even when E꜀ₗ equals the current voltage, opening chloride channels can have a major electrical effect: it increases total conductance and reduces input resistance. Subsequent depolarizing currents then produce smaller voltage changes. This is called shunting. No initial net chloride current is required for the shunt.

In a simple model a cell at −65 mV has a leak conductance of 5 nS and leak reversal at −65 mV. An injected inward current of 50 pA would produce a steady displacement of 10 mV. If an additional 5 nS chloride conductance is opened at E꜀ₗ = −65 mV, the initial baseline does not change. The same current now produces 5 mV instead of 10 mV. The time constant also halves for unchanged capacitance. Thus a channel can reduce excitation without noticeably hyperpolarizing the cell. Whether it inhibits an excitable cell depends on its reversal potential, threshold, timing and conductance, not just the sign of its initial voltage effect.

A permeability increase pulls a passive membrane voltage toward the pathway's reversal potential, but it need not reach that value because other pathways continue to conduct. The weighted-conductance formula demonstrates the same principle for linear pathways. When channel gating is voltage dependent, the change can recruit additional channels and trigger nonlinear responses. This sets the stage for the action potential.

14. From graded voltage change to a regenerative action potential

A passive membrane produces graded responses: a small current makes a small voltage change and a larger current makes a larger change, subject to the RC time constant. Some cells possess channels that respond to voltage in a way that produces positive feedback. In a typical sodium-based action potential, a depolarizing perturbation raises the probability of opening voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Sodium entry further depolarizes the membrane, which opens more channels. This regenerative process can turn a modest initial stimulus into a rapid voltage excursion.

The threshold for an action potential is the condition at which the increase in inward current becomes sufficient to overcome opposing outward currents and capacitive requirements so that depolarization becomes self-amplifying. Threshold is not a fixed universal voltage. It depends on channel availability, prior voltage, rate of stimulation and the competing conductances. Below threshold, leak and other outward currents usually restore the voltage after a perturbation; above it, positive feedback dominates for part of the response. In a given preparation under similar conditions, the resulting action potential is relatively stereotyped, giving rise to the familiar all-or-none description.

During the rising phase, inward sodium current charges the membrane toward more positive voltages. The peak need not reach Eₙₐ because the sodium driving force decreases as Vₘ approaches Eₙₐ, sodium channels progressively inactivate, and delayed outward potassium conductance increases. At the exact peak dVₘ/dt = 0, which means net charging current is zero; sodium entry need not be zero. An overshoot above zero indicates an interior temporarily positive relative to outside, not that the bulk ionic concentrations have reversed.

Repolarization follows because sodium channel inactivation decreases inward current while delayed voltage-gated K⁺ channels increase outward current. The resulting net outward positive current makes the voltage fall. Potassium conductance may remain above its resting level for a time, producing an afterhyperpolarization in cells where Eₖ is more negative than the resting potential. As potassium channels close and sodium channels recover from inactivation, the baseline and excitability are restored. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the gradients over longer intervals but does not need to reverse millions of channel openings or directly perform the rapid falling phase of each spike.

During the absolute refractory period, a substantial fraction of sodium channels is unavailable because of inactivation, making a second normal sodium-based spike impossible under ordinary conditions. Recovery requires time and a suitable repolarized voltage. During the relative refractory period, some sodium channels have recovered but elevated potassium conductance or incomplete recovery means a stronger stimulus may be needed. These states illustrate why the history of voltage affects current and threshold.

The action-potential mechanism is not universal in its ionic details. Some excitable cells rely substantially on Ca²⁺ entry for depolarization; plant electrical events can involve anion efflux and changes in proton and potassium transport. The common physics is that voltage-dependent pathways change net current, which charges or discharges membrane capacitance. Details of propagation along axons, synapses, cardiac pacemaking and muscle contraction require tissue-specific physiology and are separate from this membrane-level explanation.

15. How membrane voltage and ionic mechanisms were discovered

The existence of resting voltages became experimentally clear when intracellular electrodes could measure the voltage difference between the cell interior and bathing solution. A recording must have a reference electrode outside the cell; an electrode placed inside alone cannot determine an absolute membrane voltage. When the tip penetrates an intact membrane, a stable negative shift supports the existence of a transmembrane voltage. If the voltage drifts toward zero as the cell is damaged, the result may reflect loss of the barrier rather than a normal biological response.

The potassium hypothesis of resting potential predicts that when a membrane is mainly permeable to K⁺, changing external K⁺ should shift the voltage in a logarithmic manner. Classical studies with large nerve fibres, including the work of Hodgkin and Katz, examined how external ionic concentrations influence resting and action potentials. A near-Nernstian potassium dependence over suitable ranges supports a dominant potassium permeability. Deviations at low external potassium can reveal contributions from other ions. A failure to fit a pure potassium curve does not disprove electrochemical theory; it suggests that the one-ion model is incomplete.

Sodium substitution experiments provided a second crucial test. If the action potential were simply the loss of resting potassium selectivity with no inward sodium current, it would be difficult to explain a large positive overshoot and its dependence on external sodium. Reducing external Na⁺ decreases Eₙₐ and, other conditions being similar, reduces the attainable peak of a sodium-based spike. The response supports a transient increase in sodium permeability during depolarization. Such substitutions must be designed to preserve osmotic conditions and avoid introducing other permeant ions, and a changed spike amplitude alone does not identify every current involved.

Hodgkin and Huxley's voltage-clamp experiments on the squid giant axon in 1952 provided a stronger mechanistic test. Instead of allowing voltage and channel opening to alter each other freely, they controlled the membrane voltage and measured the time course of current. Depolarizing voltage steps revealed an early transient inward component and a delayed outward component. Ionic substitution and reversal behaviour helped identify the former with sodium and the latter with potassium. The central achievement was to show that conductances vary with time and voltage, quantitatively explaining how positive feedback and delayed negative feedback create an action potential.

An experiment can distinguish cause from consequence when voltage is controlled. In a free-running action potential, conductances change because the voltage changes, while voltage changes because conductances change. A voltage step interrupts that loop, allowing the effect of voltage on ionic currents to be examined. The detailed Hodgkin–Huxley kinetic equations and spatial propagation models are not needed to understand the experimental logic at this level. The key result is that resting and active membrane behaviour can be understood using selective ionic pathways, electrical driving force, capacitance and time-dependent gating.

16. Current clamp and voltage clamp: reading the signals

In current clamp, the investigator specifies the current injected into a cell and records the resulting voltage. With zero injected current the electrode measures the cell's resting potential, subject to recording conditions. A small constant current pulse can reveal passive input resistance from the steady voltage displacement and the time constant from the exponential approach to the plateau. A larger inward pulse can reveal threshold, action potentials and refractory behaviour in excitable cells. The experiment answers how the membrane voltage responds to an imposed current.

Voltage clamp reverses the controlled and measured variables. A feedback amplifier compares the measured membrane voltage with a command voltage and injects current to reduce the difference. Once voltage is held constant, the capacitive contribution is zero; the required injected current balances the net outward ionic current under the conventions used here. At the onset of a voltage step there is also a capacitive transient, because charge must be redistributed to reach the new voltage. In a real recording the clamp cannot change voltage infinitely fast, so the transient lasts for a finite interval. Software and amplifier conventions may display clamp current with different signs; check the stated sign before interpreting an inward or outward deflection.

Suppose a cell with Cₘ = 50 pF is commanded from −80 to −40 mV. The charge required for the voltage change is ΔQ = CₘΔV = 50 × 10⁻¹² F × 0.040 V = 2.0 × 10⁻¹² C = 2 pC. The area under the isolated charging-current transient equals this charge, after appropriate leak and baseline correction. If the voltage is subsequently constant, a maintained inward or outward current is not a capacitive charging current; it reflects ionic transport or experimental leakage. This gives a simple way to distinguish the contributions to an electrical trace.

For an ideal potassium-selective channel, a series of command voltages produces currents that cross zero at Eₖ. If the slope of the I–V curve is 2 nS, the current at −40 mV with Eₖ = −90 mV is +100 pA. At −110 mV the current is −40 pA. Changing external potassium shifts the zero-current voltage, whereas reducing the number of identical functional channels changes the slope under the linear approximation. An apparent current reversal in a whole-cell trace may reflect the balance of several ion species. To identify the reversal of a particular channel, isolate its contribution using appropriate comparisons, blockers, ionic substitutions or channel-specific recordings.

Voltage clamp can be performed in a whole-cell patch configuration, in which the recorded cell membrane is electrically accessed through a micropipette, or related configurations can measure smaller membrane patches. A high-resistance seal helps reduce leakage and permits small current measurements. Single-channel records show discrete openings and closings; the current amplitude of a single open channel depends on voltage and selectivity. The ensemble current depends additionally on the fraction of channels open. Patch clamp is a recording technique, not a synonym for voltage clamp: the instrument can be used in either current- or voltage-clamp mode depending on configuration.

Both modes have limitations. Series or access resistance between the electrode and the cell creates a voltage error proportional to current; large currents are particularly problematic. In a spatially extended cell, distant membrane regions may not follow the commanded voltage exactly, a problem known as imperfect space clamp. Electrode junction potentials, leak currents and capacitance can shift apparent reversal or current size. These issues do not invalidate the principles, but remind the reader that a graph is a measurement under specified conditions, not a direct unfiltered picture of molecular events.

17. Experimental data interpretation and model limitations

Consider a hypothetical voltage-clamp experiment with an initially negative holding voltage. A depolarizing step produces an early negative current and then a sustained positive current. When extracellular sodium is reduced while osmotic conditions are maintained, the early inward current is smaller. When a selective potassium-channel blocker is used in a separate trial, the late outward current is smaller. These observations support the interpretation that the early component is largely sodium current and the late component largely potassium current. They are not proof that no other ion contributes; one would strengthen the interpretation by measuring reversal shifts and using suitable controls.

Now consider three current-clamp measurements of a passive cell. With a +20 pA current pulse the plateau displacement is +4 mV; with +40 pA it is +8 mV; with −20 pA it is −4 mV. These observations fit a linear input resistance of 200 MΩ. If each response reaches 63% of its plateau after 8 ms, the capacitance is τ/R = 0.008/(200 × 10⁶) F = 40 pF. After a ligand opens channels with a reversal near the original baseline, suppose the +40 pA displacement falls to 4 mV and the time constant to 4 ms. The resistance has halved to 100 MΩ and the inferred capacitance remains 40 pF. The evidence supports increased conductance rather than a change in membrane area or capacitance. An unchanged baseline does not contradict channel opening.

In a second hypothetical experiment an isolated current is measured at −100, −80, −60 and −40 mV, giving −40, 0, +40 and +80 pA respectively. The slope is 2 pA mV⁻¹ = 2 nS and reversal is −80 mV. After an intervention the slope becomes 1 nS but reversal remains −80 mV. A reduction in functional conductance is supported; an altered ionic gradient is not required. When external K⁺ is doubled at 37°C, if this current is purely potassium selective, the reversal should shift by approximately +18.5 mV. A failure to shift may mean the current is not potassium selective, the assumed boundary concentrations did not change as intended, or additional measurement errors are involved.

A model should be selected from the question's inputs rather than from a preference for a familiar formula. A single-ion equilibrium question calls for Nernst. Relative permeabilities of several monovalent species with zero net passive current call for Goldman under its assumptions. Given linear conductances and reversal potentials, current balance gives a conductance-weighted voltage and can incorporate a specified pump current. Given capacitance, resistance and a current step, use the RC charging model. A time-dependent voltage-clamp trace requires recognizing that conductance varies with time as well as voltage. Mixing these models without checking assumptions creates errors even when arithmetic is correct.

Several qualifications matter when extending calculations to real biological membranes. Concentration is an approximation to thermodynamic activity; ionic strength and interactions can cause differences. Calcium calculations require free calcium rather than total calcium. The constant-field assumption can fail in narrow selective channels or strongly interacting pores, and effective permeabilities can depend on voltage and ionic composition. The ohmic conductance expression is a local or ideal linear approximation. A membrane with active pumps, nonzero injected current or transient capacitive current does not satisfy the unmodified zero-net-passive-current GHK voltage condition.

Finally, ion gradients can evolve on longer timescales. In very small compartments the movement of a relatively small number of ions can significantly change their concentrations. During repetitive activity or metabolic inhibition, a fixed-gradient approximation can become inaccurate. An equilibrium voltage is not a permanent property of an ion or channel; it changes with temperature and activity ratio. Understanding which variables are fixed, measured, or evolving is the most important habit for interpreting membrane-electrical problems.

Select the best answer. Open each question to review the options and reasoning.

1. A membrane has Vₘ = −60 mV. What does this mean?

A. Inside is 60 mV above outside; B. Inside is 60 mV below outside; C. Cytoplasm contains no cations; D. All channels are at equilibrium.

Answer and reasoning: B. Vₘ is inside minus outside. A reverses the convention; C contradicts bulk electroneutrality; D is not implied by voltage.

Answer and reasoning: C. Capacitance is Q/V. Conductance is I/V in an ohmic pathway, resistance its inverse, and permeability concerns transport.

3. A selective K⁺ channel is open at Vₘ = Eₖ. Which statement is correct?

A. All K⁺ motion stops; B. Net K⁺ flux is zero; C. ATP must be hydrolyzed by the channel; D. Na⁺ is also necessarily at equilibrium.

Answer and reasoning: B. Opposite K⁺ fluxes balance. A confuses dynamic equilibrium with no motion; C confuses channel and pump; D ignores distinct gradients.

A. Cl⁻ is attracted inward; B. Na⁺ is electrically driven inward; C. All ions move inward; D. K⁺ necessarily has inward net flux.

Answer and reasoning: B. The electrical term favours positive charge inward. A has wrong sign; C ignores charge; D ignores the chemical gradient.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, reason unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

7. For the preceding two-pathway cell, a constant outward pump current of 20 pA is introduced. New voltage?

A. −47.5 mV; B. −57.5 mV; C. −72.5 mV; D. unchanged.

Answer and reasoning: B. Shift = −20/4=−5 mV. A has wrong sign, C neglects total conductance, D ignores the current.

8. For [K]i=140, [K]o=5, [Na]i=15, [Na]o=145, [Cl]i=10 and [Cl]o=110 mM, relative P=1:0.04:0.45 at 37°C, choose Vₘ.

A. about −67.3 mV; B. −89.1 mV; C. +67.3 mV; D. +60.6 mV.

Answer and reasoning: A. 61.54 log₁₀(15.3/190.1)≈−67.3 mV. B and D are single-ion reference voltages; C reverses the ratio.

9. A cell's membrane area doubles, with constant specific capacitance and specific leak conductance. What happens to total capacitance, input resistance and τ?

A. C doubles, R halves, τ unchanged; B. C doubles, R doubles, τ quadruples; C. all unchanged; D. C halves, R doubles.

Answer and reasoning: A. More area adds capacitance and parallel leak pathways. B treats parallel paths as series; C ignores total area; D reverses the geometry.

10. A channel has γ=25 pS, N=400, Pₒ=0.2 and driving force −80 mV. Find mean current.

A. −160 pA; B. −2 pA; C. −800 pA; D. +160 pA.

Answer and reasoning: A. g=400×25×0.2 pS=2 nS, I=2×−80=−160 pA. B is single-open-channel current; C assumes all channels open; D reverses sign.

11. A 30 pF membrane undergoes a voltage-clamp step from −70 to +10 mV. What isolated capacitive charge is delivered inward?

A. 2.4 pC; B. 0.24 pC; C. 240 pC; D. −2.4 pC.

Answer and reasoning: A. ΔV=0.080 V; Q=30×10⁻¹²×0.080=2.4×10⁻¹² C. B and C are unit errors, D reverses direction.

12. A passive cell at −65 mV has g=4 nS. A channel with Erev=−65 mV adds 6 nS. What is the plateau response to +50 pA?

A. +12.5 mV; B. +5 mV; C. −5 mV; D. zero.

Answer and reasoning: B. Total conductance=10 nS; ΔV=50/10=5 mV. A ignores added channels, C reverses current, D mistakes zero baseline current for zero response.

13. An ion-selective current reverses at −85 mV. Doubling extracellular K⁺ shifts reversal to −66.5 mV at 37°C, with fixed inside K⁺. Best interpretation?

A. Consistent with K⁺ selectivity; B. proves Ca²⁺ selectivity; C. proves Cl⁻ selectivity; D. no dependence on gradient.

Answer and reasoning: A. The positive shift matches 61.54 log₁₀2. This supports but does not uniquely prove selectivity; B and C have incorrect predictions for the specified K⁺ intervention, D contradicts data.

14. In a voltage-clamp recording, a current is −30 pA at −100 mV and +90 pA at −40 mV. Assuming ohmic behaviour, find conductance and reversal.

A. 2 nS, −85 mV; B. 0.5 nS, −85 mV; C. 2 nS, −70 mV; D. 2 nS, +85 mV.

Answer and reasoning: A. Slope=(90−(−30))/60=2 nS; −30=2(−100−E), so E=−85 mV. B inverts slope, C assumes midpoint, D reverses sign.

15. After pump inhibition, voltage immediately depolarizes by 2 mV but the potassium concentration does not measurably change until later. Which model fits?

A. Loss of direct outward pump current precedes gradient loss; B. K⁺ gradients must vanish instantly; C. pump is electrically neutral; D. capacitor no longer exists.

Answer and reasoning: A. Small charge rearrangements precede slow changes in bulk concentration. B ignores the charge scale; C conflicts with 3:2 stoichiometry; D has no basis.

16. A cell initially at −70 mV has 140 mM K⁺ inside and 5 mM outside at 310 K. Which inward electrochemical work is closest?

A. +1.84 kJ/mol; B. −1.84 kJ/mol; C. +15.34 kJ/mol; D. zero.

Answer and reasoning: A. RT ln28≈8.59 kJ/mol; FV=−6.75 kJ/mol; sum positive so outward favoured. B reverses direction, C adds absolute magnitudes, D assumes equilibrium.

20. Consolidation and next conceptual step

The lipid bilayer separates charge like a capacitor while ion-selective proteins provide conductive paths. Pumps and coupled transport establish gradients, which are converted into voltage by selective permeabilities. The Nernst equation identifies the voltage at which an individual ion's chemical and electrical preferences balance. The Goldman equation generalizes the zero-net-passive-current condition for several monovalent ions in the constant-field model. In the linear equivalent circuit, conductances weight individual equilibrium potentials, and capacitance together with input resistance determines the time course of voltage changes.

Before solving an unfamiliar problem, write the voltage and current conventions, convert temperatures and concentrations to consistent units, and ask which quantities are held constant. If the question concerns equilibrium of one ion, use Nernst. If a channel is open away from equilibrium, use driving force to infer current and remember that anion particle movement has the opposite sign to conventional current. If several permeabilities are given, use the appropriate Goldman assumptions. If conductances are given, balance currents. If voltage changes with time, include capacitance. Finally, separate the direct current generated by an active pump from its longer-term maintenance of concentrations.

A resting voltage is a dynamic electrical steady state rather than universal ionic equilibrium. Opening a channel draws the membrane toward its reversal potential and can reduce resistance even if the baseline voltage does not change. When channel gating creates positive feedback, graded depolarization can become an action potential. Current clamp and voltage clamp separate different aspects of the resulting behaviour. These principles connect the channels and pumps already studied with membrane function throughout the cell.

Membranes also differ from one intracellular compartment to another. Their electrical and chemical identity depends on the particular proteins and lipids they contain and on the transport pathways that establish gradients. The next curriculum node turns to how cells preserve that identity through sorting, vesicle budding, targeting, fusion and recycling.
