1. The problem of observing cardiac excitation

A heartbeat can be detected in many ways. A hand placed on an artery detects a pressure pulse, a stethoscope detects vibrations produced during mechanical events, and an ultrasound image reveals moving walls and valves. None of these observations directly identifies the sequence of electrical excitation that initiates contraction. To understand why a heart beats regularly, why the atria usually contract before the ventricles and why some beats fail to produce a normal pulse, physiology requires an independent record of excitation. The electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) provides this record by measuring differences in electric potential at the body surface as cardiac muscle depolarises and repolarises. The electrocardiograph is the instrument; the electrocardiogram is its output. Electrocardiography is the measurement technique.

Consider two hearts producing the same number of palpable pulses per minute. In the first, the sinoatrial node activates the atria before conducting through the atrioventricular junction; in the second, some atrial impulses fail to reach the ventricles and a subsidiary pacemaker initiates ventricular beats. Counting pulses alone cannot distinguish the two patterns. An electrical recording can display the atrial and ventricular rhythms separately, revealing whether they maintain a stable temporal relationship. Conversely, two hearts can exhibit similar electrical sequences but generate very different stroke volumes because of differences in filling, resistance to ejection or muscle contractility. Electrical organisation is an important prerequisite for efficient pumping, not a measurement of pumping itself.

The essential distinction is between the membrane potential of a single cardiac cell, the distribution of extracellular potentials near active tissue and the potential difference recorded between two body-surface measurement points. A membrane potential compares the inside of a cell with its immediate exterior. An extracellular recording compares two positions outside cells. The body-surface measurement sums contributions from many regions of the myocardium after propagation through a conducting medium. These three quantities have different reference points, characteristic amplitudes and waveform shapes. A ventricular cell may have a prolonged action-potential plateau even though the surface ECG during much of the same period is approximately isoelectric. There is no contradiction: the first quantity describes a cell's electrical state, whereas the second depends on spatial electrical differences across tissue.

This chapter uses the adult human ECG as a standard example because its lead geometry and calibration are widely established. Other animals also generate measurable surface electrical signals, but differences in heart size, orientation, conduction architecture, body composition and resting rate affect their recordings. A human timing reference must not automatically be transferred to a mouse, fish or amphibian. The common physiological principles are excitation, recovery, extracellular current flow and measurement along a chosen lead. The particular wave shapes and reference values depend on the organism and recording conditions.

A productive way to interpret an ECG is to ask four questions in order. What cardiac tissue is changing its electrical state? Where is that change propagating? What component of the resulting electrical activity does the lead detect? How does the measured timing constrain the possible sequence of physiological events? These questions avoid the mistake of treating the trace as a memorised sequence of labelled peaks. They also provide a framework for experimental prediction: if conduction slows in one part of the heart, which interval changes, and which other intervals could remain unchanged?

2. Historical evidence and the emergence of a quantitative recording

The observation that muscle generates electrical activity preceded the development of electrocardiography. The important experimental advance was to detect the heart's electrical activity outside the organ without relying solely on its visible contraction. Augustus Waller recorded human cardiac electrical activity using a capillary electrometer in the late nineteenth century. The instrument responded too slowly and imperfectly to reproduce rapid changes faithfully. A recording device has its own physical response characteristics, and a waveform can be distorted even if the biological source is perfectly reproducible. This limitation is central to understanding why improved instrumentation changed what investigators could infer about cardiac physiology.

Willem Einthoven developed the string galvanometer as a sensitive instrument capable of producing a more useful record of the small electrical differences generated by the beating heart. A fine conducting filament placed in a magnetic field moved when current passed through it. Optical enlargement enabled the movement to be registered. By relating the deflection to electrical input and recording time, investigators could compare successive beats and different recording configurations. Einthoven established a widely used system of limb leads and the nomenclature underlying modern P, Q, R, S and T deflections. He received the 1924 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his discovery of the mechanism of the electrocardiogram.

The scientific question addressed by these early studies was not merely whether cardiac activity produced electricity. It was whether the electrical activity could be measured reproducibly, assigned temporal meaning and related to the position of electrodes. A suitable experimental comparison uses simultaneously recorded electrical and mechanical events. Reproducible electrical signals that precede mechanical changes support a causal sequence in which excitation initiates contraction. To distinguish this from an instrument responding to chest movement, electrical and mechanical signals can be compared when their normal relationship changes or when motion contamination is controlled. Electrical signals have characteristic relations across lead orientations that are not simply explained by external displacement.

A calibration signal is another type of control. If the instrument is exposed to a known potential difference and produces the expected deflection, differences among biological recordings can be interpreted relative to that scale. Without calibration, an increase in deflection might represent a more intense biological signal or merely increased instrument gain. Similarly, a faster moving recording medium stretches the horizontal appearance of the same electrical event. Reproducibility requires separating the biological variable of interest from the response of the measuring system.

The historical limb-lead model uses a simplified geometric representation of the body to make vector reasoning accessible. Its success does not require the torso to be a perfect equilateral triangle. In particular, the algebraic relationship among three bipolar limb voltages follows from subtraction of electrode potentials; it remains true even when the physical geometry is not ideal, provided the signals are derived consistently at the same instant. The geometric model used to estimate electrical direction is a different matter and has additional assumptions.

Modern digital electrocardiographs replace photographic or paper registration with sampled electrical signals. Their increased speed and storage capacity permit comparison of long recordings, automatic timing measurements and simultaneous display of many leads. However, digital measurement does not eliminate physiological uncertainty. Automatic labels are interpretations of data, not direct observations of a mechanism. The examiner may deliberately present a trace with a calibration change or altered reference to test whether the student analyses the measurement before interpreting the biology.

3. From a membrane potential to extracellular current

A cardiac myocyte is surrounded by a membrane that separates intracellular and extracellular solutions. Selective permeability and maintained ionic gradients produce a transmembrane electrical potential. In a resting ventricular cell the interior is negative relative to the exterior. During rapid depolarisation, inward current changes the membrane potential toward positive values; subsequent calcium and potassium currents contribute to the prolonged action potential and recovery. The detailed channel sequence belongs to cardiac electrophysiology. For electrocardiography the crucial point is that transmembrane currents do not remain confined to an electrically isolated cell. Current flows through extracellular fluid and neighbouring coupled cells, establishing spatial patterns of potential.

Imagine a narrow strand of myocardium lying horizontally. Its left region has just been activated but its right region remains resting. In the simplified extracellular dipole model, the activated side near the advancing boundary is relatively negative and the resting side relatively positive. An electrode near the resting side can therefore be positive relative to an electrode near the activated side. As the wavefront advances, the spatial location of these source and sink regions changes. A lead whose positive electrode lies ahead of this activation front records a positive deflection in the usual idealised convention. Reversing the electrode order reverses the measured difference even though cellular activation proceeds unchanged.

The terms positive and negative require care. An activated cell's interior may become positive relative to its extracellular solution while the extracellular region near the depolarisation front becomes relatively negative compared with neighbouring resting tissue. Both statements are consistent because they compare different pairs of locations. It is incorrect to assign a positive extracellular voltage to every depolarised cell simply because its intracellular action potential rises. At a distance, the recorded signal represents the superposition of source and sink contributions from many cells.

Gap junctions allow electrical current to pass between connected cardiomyocytes. Local current from excited tissue brings neighbouring tissue closer to threshold, propagating a wave rather than activating the whole chamber instantaneously. The speed and direction of propagation depend on excitability, intercellular coupling, tissue architecture and the specialised conduction pathways. As a result, a moving boundary separates regions in different electrical states. This spatial nonuniformity is necessary for a substantial surface signal. If every point in the heart changed identically at the same instant in a perfectly symmetric model, external differential signals would be greatly diminished.

After most ventricular myocardium has depolarised, the main activation front is no longer present. Cells are still electrically active in the sense that their membranes maintain an action potential, calcium can enter and contraction can continue. Nevertheless, relatively similar electrical states across the ventricular myocardium lead to small net extracellular gradients in many leads. This explains why an almost flat ST segment can coexist with vigorous ventricular mechanical activity. Uniformity, not electrical inactivity, is the key distinction.

Recovery also propagates or varies spatially. Different regions have different activation times and action-potential durations. The first region activated need not be the first region to repolarise. If region A activates at time zero and has an action potential of 300 ms, while region B activates 20 ms later and has an action potential lasting 250 ms, region B recovers at 270 ms, before region A at 300 ms. Thus the sequence of recovery cannot be inferred from activation order without considering action-potential duration. The relation between activation and recovery direction becomes essential when explaining the T wave.

The simple strand model is useful but it does not reproduce every detail of an intact heart. Real myocardium is three-dimensional, fibre orientation varies, conduction is anisotropic and multiple wavefronts coexist. Tissue boundaries and conductivity also affect the distribution of potential. A dipole model is therefore a concise description of the net electrical effect seen at a distance, not a claim that the heart literally consists of one moving pair of charges.

4. Electrical vectors and the lead as a projection

A scalar quantity has magnitude alone, while a vector has magnitude and direction. Cardiac electrical activity may be represented at a particular instant by an equivalent electrical vector. The vector summarises the contributions of many active regions. Its direction follows the convention appropriate to the net extracellular electrical dipole. Its magnitude depends on the strength and spatial arrangement of contributing sources. A lead is sensitive to a component of this activity along its measurement direction. The same cardiac vector can therefore give a large positive signal in one lead, a small signal in another and a negative signal in a third.

In an idealised two-dimensional model, the measured lead voltage can be written V = kM cos θ, where M is the magnitude of an equivalent cardiac vector, θ is the angle between the vector and the positive lead axis, and k is a proportionality factor reflecting the measurement geometry and units. If M is already expressed as an equivalent aligned voltage, k can be absorbed into M. The cosine is dimensionless. When the vector is parallel to the positive axis, cos 0° equals one; when it is perpendicular, cos 90° equals zero; and when it is opposite to the positive axis, cos 180° equals minus one. The equation is an ideal projection, not a complete model of volume conduction.

For example, assume an equivalent aligned voltage of 1.2 mV and an angle of 60°. The predicted lead voltage is 1.2 × 0.5 = 0.6 mV. Rotating the lead so that the angle becomes 120° gives −0.6 mV. The amplitude is the same but the polarity differs. If a third lead is perpendicular, the ideal voltage is zero. These changes require no change in heart rate, cellular action-potential size or strength of contraction. Geometry alone changes the recording.

The instantaneous cardiac vector changes throughout a beat. Early ventricular activation has a different direction from the dominant activation of the ventricular free walls; late basal activation contributes a different vector again. Consequently a QRS complex may contain both negative and positive deflections within a single lead. The lead axis itself has not rotated during those few milliseconds. Instead the source vector has changed as different myocardial regions become active. A biphasic complex can occur when the vector crosses the direction perpendicular to a lead, but near cancellation of separate contributions can also produce a small net signal.

A useful test of the projection concept is to imagine two equal source vectors pointing in opposite directions. Their vector sum is zero even though each individual region generates current. If one vector is slightly larger, the net vector points in that direction and has the difference in magnitude. If the vectors instead point at right angles, the resultant lies between them and must be found by vector addition, not simple addition of their magnitudes. The larger ventricular contribution to the normal ECG is related to the amount and arrangement of activated tissue, but opposing currents partly cancel.

A lead is not identical to an electrode. An electrode is a physical interface that detects a potential relative to the instrument's reference. A lead is a defined difference or derived combination of electrode potentials. A single electrode cannot specify a unique potential difference without another reference. A standard twelve-lead ECG uses ten physical electrodes, with multiple mathematical combinations generating different views of the same underlying cardiac activity. Thinking in terms of lead vectors rather than counting electrodes helps explain why twelve traces need not correspond to twelve independent physical sources.

In the real body, conductivity is neither homogeneous nor isotropic. Blood, lung, muscle, fat and other tissues differ in their electrical properties. A lead's sensitivity is better represented by a spatial lead field than by a rigid line through a point source. The simple cosine model nevertheless captures polarity, approximate magnitude and the geometric logic required for basic ECG interpretation. An examination question may specify the ideal model; its assumptions must be preserved when solving the problem.

5. Bipolar limb leads and Einthoven's relation

Let R, L and F represent the instantaneous electric potentials at the right-arm, left-arm and left-leg electrodes, respectively, relative to an arbitrary shared reference. The three standard bipolar limb leads are I = L − R, II = F − R and III = F − L. In lead I the positive direction is toward the left arm, in lead II toward the left leg from the right arm, and in lead III toward the left leg from the left arm. The right-leg electrode generally serves a circuit reference or noise-reduction function rather than forming one of these standard bipolar recording differences.

Adding leads I and III eliminates the left-arm potential: (L − R) + (F − L) = F − R. The result is lead II. Thus II = I + III. This is Einthoven's relation. It is true at each instant for consistently derived voltages. It does not require the potentials to be positive, the heart to be normal or the body's electrode positions to define a mathematically perfect triangle. Nor does it mean the peak of lead II must equal the sum of peak heights measured at different moments in leads I and III. Addition must use simultaneous signed values.

Suppose R = −0.3 mV, L = 0.2 mV and F = 0.6 mV at a particular instant. Lead I equals 0.5 mV, lead II equals 0.9 mV and lead III equals 0.4 mV. The sum 0.5 + 0.4 = 0.9 mV confirms the relation. If a common 0.4 mV is added to all three electrode potentials, their individual numbers change but the differences do not. This invariance shows that an arbitrary common reference does not determine the measured bipolar voltage.

A second example requires signed arithmetic. If lead I is −0.2 mV and lead II is +0.7 mV, lead III equals II − I = 0.7 − (−0.2) = +0.9 mV. A student who treats all voltages as positive heights would obtain 0.5 mV, an incorrect result. Voltage polarity is a measured property, not a decoration on the diagram. When the problem gives grid divisions rather than millivolts, the values must first share a common voltage calibration.

The conventional frontal-plane axes for leads I, II and III are approximately 0°, +60° and +120°, respectively. These axes provide a convenient geometric framework for interpreting a net cardiac vector. They are not the reason Einthoven's algebraic relation holds. Separating the voltage identity from the geometric approximation is an important conceptual achievement. The identity remains correct for any simultaneous values from the three electrode locations; the geometric interpretation becomes less exact when the equivalent dipole or body geometry assumptions fail.

If a recording system swaps the right-arm and left-arm channels, the measured lead I reverses because the instrument now calculates R − L. The new lead II becomes the original lead III, and the new lead III becomes the original lead II. Einthoven's identity still holds for the newly labelled combinations, so satisfaction of the identity alone cannot prove electrode placement was correct. To identify such an error one needs information about electrode assignment, expected morphology or independent recordings. This is a valuable example of a mathematical consistency check that cannot establish all aspects of experimental validity.

Because only two independent differences are available from three electrode potentials, one bipolar limb lead can be calculated from the other two. The standard display remains useful because each lead orientation offers a different visual projection. Redundancy can help with interpretation and quality control, but it must not be mistaken for independent replication of the same biological event.

6. Augmented limb leads and precordial views

The standard bipolar leads compare pairs of limb electrode potentials. Additional frontal-plane views can be obtained by treating one limb as the exploring electrode and comparing it with the average potential of the other two. The augmented right-arm lead is aVR = R − (L + F)/2; aVL = L − (R + F)/2; and aVF = F − (R + L)/2. The letter a denotes augmentation, V denotes voltage, and the final letter identifies the exploring limb. These derived leads are commonly described as unipolar, but they still measure a difference between an electrode and a reference combination. No measurement is an absolute voltage of the body independent of a reference.

Using the illustrative electrode values R = −0.3 mV, L = 0.2 mV and F = 0.6 mV gives aVR = −0.7 mV, aVL = +0.05 mV and aVF = +0.65 mV. Their sum is zero. More generally, adding the three formulas cancels each electrode potential. The zero-sum identity is a property of the chosen combinations, not evidence of absent myocardial activity. The same signals can be expressed using the bipolar leads: aVR = −(I + II)/2, aVL = I − II/2 and aVF = II − I/2. This confirms that the six frontal leads derive from the same underlying electrode differences.

The conventional augmented axes are approximately −150° for aVR, −30° for aVL and +90° for aVF. Combining these with the three standard bipolar axes produces the hexaxial reference system used for introductory frontal-plane axis reasoning. A dominant ventricular activation vector directed leftward and inferiorly commonly projects positively onto leads I and II, while projecting negatively onto aVR. However, normal variation exists and the geometry of a given phase cannot be inferred from a single expected polarity alone.

The six precordial leads, V1 through V6, sample chest-surface potentials relative to the Wilson central terminal, an average of the three standard limb potentials in the conventional formulation. Their positions span the anterior and lateral chest. V1 and V2 are placed in the fourth intercostal spaces beside the sternum, V4 in the fifth intercostal space at the left midclavicular line, V3 between V2 and V4, and V5 and V6 at the horizontal level of V4 along anterior and midaxillary lines. These are standard human recording landmarks, not transferable anatomical coordinates for other animal species. Their purpose in this chapter is to explain how viewing the same cardiac source from different locations changes waveform morphology.

Unlike the limb leads, precordial recordings sample perspectives closer to the heart and mainly inform horizontal-plane electrical patterns. They are not simply six additional angles on the frontal-plane hexaxial diagram. A lead close to the right anterior chest often sees the main ventricular activation moving away, whereas a left lateral chest lead commonly sees a larger positive R deflection. Normal R-wave progression is related to the changing orientation of recording positions relative to the combined ventricular activation pattern. It is not a direct measurement of the amount of muscle lying beneath each electrode.

The distinction between the frontal and horizontal views becomes evident if the heart's net vector has a large anterior or posterior component. Two frontal leads could indicate very similar projections for two vectors differing substantially in their depth direction. Chest leads offer additional spatial information. Even the full standard recording cannot uniquely reconstruct every microscopic current source because many different source distributions can produce similar boundary voltages. This is why physiological interpretation combines lead geometry with knowledge of cardiac anatomy and conduction.

7. Calibration and quantitative measurement

An electrocardiographic trace has two axes. The horizontal axis represents time, and the vertical axis represents voltage. A small grid square commonly measures one millimetre on each side, and a large square spans five small squares. At the standard recording speed of 25 mm/s, a horizontal millimetre represents 1/25 s = 0.04 s = 40 ms. Five millimetres represent 0.20 s = 200 ms. At the standard gain of 10 mm/mV, a vertical millimetre represents 0.1 mV and five millimetres represent 0.5 mV. Horizontal and vertical divisions have equal physical lengths but different units and meanings.

A calibration pulse of 1 mV produces a vertical displacement of 10 mm at the standard gain. The recording speed determines how much horizontal distance represents the pulse duration. The pulse does not originate in the myocardium and must not be identified as a pathological cardiac wave. Its role is to verify the relationship between input voltage and displayed height. In digital displays, apparent centimetres on a screen can change with resizing; a labelled grid, scale or time stamp takes precedence over measuring the physical dimensions of a phone screen.

The general conversion for time is t = d/s, where d is horizontal distance in millimetres and s is recording speed in mm/s. The units cancel to seconds. The general voltage conversion is V = h/g, where h is signed vertical displacement in millimetres and g is gain in mm/mV. The result is in millivolts. At 50 mm/s a 5 mm interval represents 0.10 s rather than 0.20 s. At 5 mm/mV a 5 mm deflection represents 1 mV rather than 0.5 mV. A change in either setting alters apparent geometry without necessarily altering physiology.

Consider two hypothetical traces of the same ventricular activation event. Trace A is displayed at 25 mm/s and the QRS spans 2.5 mm. Trace B is displayed at 50 mm/s and the same complex spans 5 mm. The calculated durations are 2.5/25 = 0.10 s and 5/50 = 0.10 s. Both describe the same electrical duration. In a separate example, a positive R wave of 8 mm at 10 mm/mV corresponds to +0.8 mV. If the gain changes to 20 mm/mV, that same voltage produces 16 mm of displayed displacement. One must not infer that ventricular electrical activity doubled.

Resolution introduces uncertainty. If a student can identify the onset of a wave only to within half a small square at 25 mm/s, the onset uncertainty is approximately ±20 ms. Both the onset and end contribute to uncertainty in an interval. Reporting a manually measured duration such as 0.16347 s from a coarse grid suggests precision that the measurement cannot support. Digital sampling can improve time resolution, but identification of waveform boundaries may still be difficult due to low amplitude, noise or gradual transitions. A reasonable answer states the calibration, measured distance and appropriate significant figures.

The use of a stable baseline is also important for voltage measurement. The TP segment between ventricular repolarisation and the next atrial activation often provides a useful reference when clearly visible. At rapid rates it may be shortened or absent, and a different appropriate reference may be needed. Baseline drift due to breathing, motion or electrode contact can alter apparent wave amplitude. The examiner may supply an ideal baseline; actual physiology experiments require careful consideration of recording quality.

8. Atrial activation, the P wave and atrioventricular delay

In a normal sinus beat, the sinoatrial node initiates excitation and the impulse spreads through atrial myocardium. The node itself contains relatively few specialised cells and its direct electrical signal is not normally resolved as a distinct surface ECG wave. The P wave principally represents the spread of atrial depolarisation. The right atrium begins activation near the sinoatrial node, and activation reaches the left atrium through interatrial conducting pathways and myocardium. Contributions from the two atria overlap. Therefore the P wave is a combined recording, not two completely separate peaks that must always be visibly identifiable.

Atrial depolarisation triggers calcium-dependent contraction after a short electromechanical delay. Atrial systole helps complete ventricular filling when the atrioventricular valves are open. The P wave marks excitation, not the onset or full duration of mechanical atrial shortening. If muscle contractility decreases without a major change in atrial conduction, the P wave may remain similar even as the mechanical contribution to ventricular filling changes. A simultaneous pressure or ultrasound recording provides the complementary mechanical information.

The PR interval extends from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex, whether the initial ventricular deflection is negative or positive. It includes atrial activation, transmission through the atrioventricular junction and conduction along the pathway up to the earliest ventricular myocardial activation. In a typical resting adult human ECG, values around 120–200 ms are commonly used as a reference. These bounds depend on age, rate and physiological context and are not universal thresholds for all animals.

The PR segment is measured from the end of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex. It differs from the PR interval, which includes the P wave itself. During much of this segment, atrial depolarisation is largely complete while the impulse continues through the atrioventricular junction and specialised pathways before widespread ventricular activation. The relatively small tissue mass of the conduction system and cancellation of electrical contributions produce little surface deflection. An apparently flat PR segment therefore does not imply that the impulse has stopped moving.

The atrioventricular node conducts relatively slowly compared with the His–Purkinje network. This delay helps coordinate atrial and ventricular contraction. Calcium-dependent depolarisation and the cellular organisation of nodal tissue contribute to slow conduction. If atrioventricular conduction slows but the atrial and ventricular activation patterns themselves remain unchanged, the PR interval lengthens while P-wave duration and QRS duration may remain similar. This prediction connects a change in an anatomical conduction region with a measurable timing feature. A long PR interval does not, by itself, locate every millisecond of delay specifically within the AV node because the interval includes other conducting structures.

Atrial repolarisation also produces extracellular electrical activity. It is often not distinguishable on a routine trace because it is small and overlaps the much larger ventricular depolarisation signal. The absence of an obvious atrial repolarisation wave is not evidence that atrial cells remain depolarised. In recordings with unusual atrioventricular timing or special measurement configurations, atrial recovery can sometimes become more apparent. The general interpretation rests on temporal overlap and signal magnitude rather than the idea that atrial repolarisation is biologically absent.

9. Ventricular activation and the QRS complex

The ventricular conducting network distributes excitation rapidly through the bundle branches and Purkinje fibres. This arrangement helps activate widely separated ventricular regions over a short interval, supporting coordinated force development. The QRS complex records ventricular depolarisation as a changing sum of electrical contributions. Its onset is the earliest clearly detectable ventricular activation and its end marks the approximate completion of the major ventricular activation process. It is not the duration of ventricular systole, which continues far longer.

The letters Q, R and S identify the direction and sequence of deflections relative to a baseline. A Q wave is an initial negative deflection preceding a positive R wave. An R wave is a positive deflection in the ventricular complex. An S wave is a negative deflection following an R wave. A wholly negative complex without an R wave may be called a QS complex. Not every lead displays all three deflections. The name QRS complex remains useful even when a particular waveform lacks a Q or an S. A small initial negative deflection can be physiological in some leads and should not be automatically equated with tissue death.

Early activation of the interventricular septum often has a left-to-right component. The much larger combined activation of the ventricular walls commonly produces a dominant leftward vector, influenced strongly by the larger left ventricular muscle mass. Late activation of basal regions can change the direction again. In a simplified lateral lead, these sequential vectors may generate a small initial negative deflection, a large positive deflection and a terminal negative deflection. An anterior right-sided lead may show a different pattern because its positive measurement direction is different.

The duration of the QRS reflects the time over which detectable ventricular depolarisation occurs. It is typically less than 120 ms in adults with ordinary rapid ventricular conduction; a representative narrow complex might measure 80–100 ms. If excitation originates in ventricular myocardium instead of entering through the usual specialised conduction network, propagation across ventricular tissue can be slower and differently oriented, frequently producing a broader complex. Interruption of a bundle branch can similarly delay activation of one ventricle. A broad complex is therefore evidence of altered activation timing, but does not uniquely establish its anatomical cause.

Suppose a control recording has a PR interval of 160 ms and a QRS duration of 80 ms. A selective intervention affecting ventricular conduction changes the QRS to 140 ms while the PR remains 160 ms. The first ventricular tissue is still activated at approximately the same measured time after atrial onset, but the rest of the ventricular myocardium takes longer to activate. In contrast, if the main change is an increase in the PR interval from 160 to 240 ms while QRS duration remains 80 ms, delayed transmission before ventricular activation is the more direct inference. The two intervals partition a continuous conduction sequence into experimentally useful measurements.

Amplitude must be considered separately from duration. An increase in ventricular mass, a change in electrical orientation, reduced cancellation, altered tissue conductivity or a gain change can all influence amplitude. Duration is influenced by activation timing, path length and propagation velocity. A taller R wave does not necessarily imply a faster conduction velocity or stronger contraction. A broader QRS does not necessarily imply that the action potential of each ventricular cell has become longer. The latter property relates more directly to recovery timing and is only indirectly reflected in the surface recording.

10. The ST segment, T wave and ventricular recovery

At the end of the QRS complex most ventricular myocardium has undergone depolarisation. The junction between the end of the QRS and the beginning of the ST segment is called the J point. The ST segment extends from this junction toward the beginning of the T wave. During much of this time many ventricular cells occupy the plateau portion of their action potentials. Their electrical states are relatively similar, so the net surface potential difference is often small. This segment is normally near the reference baseline in many leads, despite substantial ionic current and force development within the heart.

A spatial difference in membrane state or action-potential shape can disturb this near cancellation. In an idealised experiment, one region may remain in a different electrical state from neighbouring myocardium during the plateau or resting period. The resulting extracellular potential gradients can shift the relative position of the ST segment and baseline. This is a general electrophysiological explanation of why ST changes may carry physiological information. The pattern depends on source distribution, lead direction and reference; it cannot be translated into a unique diagnosis merely by seeing an upward or downward shift in one schematic trace.

Ventricular repolarisation produces the T wave. Repolarisation differs electrically from depolarisation because the previously activated region is returning toward its resting membrane potential. In a simple strand, a repolarisation front travelling in the same direction as a depolarisation front would give the opposite extracellular polarity under comparable conditions. However, in the normal intact ventricle, spatial differences in activation time and action-potential duration mean the overall recovery sequence can differ from the activation sequence. Two sign reversals, one associated with the electrical nature of repolarisation and one with the direction of the recovery sequence, explain why the T wave is commonly concordant with the dominant QRS deflection in many leads.

A simple calculation shows the importance of action-potential duration. Suppose an inner myocardial region activates at 0 ms and recovers 320 ms after activation. An outer region activates at 30 ms and recovers 250 ms after activation. The inner region recovers at 320 ms and the outer region at 280 ms. Although the outer region was activated later, it recovers earlier. If instead both regions have identical action-potential durations, their recovery order follows their activation order. The actual intact ventricular recovery pattern is more complex than a strict two-layer scheme; the teaching model demonstrates the timing principle without asserting a universally uniform direction for all regions.

The QT interval extends from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave. It includes ventricular activation and recovery and is an approximate measure of the overall time occupied by those electrical processes. The interval is rate-dependent: at faster heart rates it commonly shortens, although adaptation can lag behind a change in rate. The QT interval is not a direct measurement of the action-potential duration of one cell, nor is it identical to ventricular mechanical systole.

For rate-aware comparisons, one conventional correction is Bazett's formula, QTc = QT/√RR, with QT and RR expressed numerically in seconds and QTc reported conventionally in seconds. The formula is empirical and conventionally refers the value to a one-second RR interval; the dimensional shorthand should not be interpreted as a universal physical law. Another commonly used correction is Fridericia's cube-root formula, QTc = QT/RR^(1/3), with the same time convention. For a QT of 0.36 s at RR = 0.64 s, Bazett gives 0.36/0.8 = 0.45 s, while Fridericia gives approximately 0.418 s. The discrepancy demonstrates that correction depends on model choice, especially at rates far from ordinary resting values. No correction perfectly eliminates all rate and individual effects.

The end of the T wave may be difficult to identify when the slope is shallow, the following wave overlaps or baseline drift is present. A small U wave can sometimes follow the T wave; its physiological origin is not completely settled. An examiner may provide a marked T endpoint to avoid this measurement ambiguity. In a real recording, the method used to determine the endpoint and the quality of the signal affect the QT estimate. Correct arithmetic cannot rescue an incorrectly selected endpoint.

11. Heart rate and the distinction between rate and rhythm

A regular sequence of ventricular complexes permits calculation of ventricular rate from the time between corresponding points, often successive R-wave peaks. The rate in beats per minute is 60 divided by the RR interval in seconds. For a constant interval of 0.80 s, ventricular rate equals 75 beats per minute. This expression follows directly from the conversion between seconds and minutes. If intervals vary, a rate computed from one interval describes that interval rather than the mean over a longer period.

At 25 mm/s, one minute corresponds to 25 × 60 = 1500 mm of horizontal recording. Therefore, for a regular rhythm, rate equals 1500 divided by the number of small squares between R waves. Since a large square spans 5 mm, the equivalent expression is 300 divided by the number of large squares. For an RR distance of four large squares, the rate is 300/4 = 75 beats per minute. At 50 mm/s the corresponding constants are 3000 and 600, not 1500 and 300. These are consequences of calibration, not independent empirical formulas to memorise.

For an irregular rhythm, counting complexes over a known duration gives an estimate of average ventricular rate. Eight QRS complexes observed during a six-second window correspond to an estimated rate of 8 × 10 = 80 beats per minute. A longer window generally reduces the effect of whether a complex falls just inside or outside a window boundary. Another method uses the number of complete intervals divided by their summed duration and multiplies by 60. If six complete RR intervals total 4.8 s, the average is 60 × 6/4.8 = 75 beats per minute. The difference between counting complexes and counting intervals must be recognised because seven peaks delimit six complete intervals.

Average rate is not the same as regularity. Two hypothetical recordings may each contain five complete intervals in four seconds. In one, all intervals equal 0.80 s; in the other, the intervals are 0.50, 1.10, 0.60, 1.00 and 0.80 s. Both yield an average of 75 beats per minute over the specified intervals, but the second rhythm is much more variable. Computing the mean of instantaneous rates, 60/RR, gives a different quantity from dividing the number of cycles by elapsed time because reciprocal transformation is nonlinear. For physiological questions about beats per unit time, the latter method is usually intended.

Atrial rate may be calculated from P-to-P intervals when distinct atrial waves are visible. In ordinary one-to-one atrioventricular conduction, atrial and ventricular rates are equal over a sufficiently long observation period. With intermittent conduction failure they differ. If P waves recur every 0.60 s and alternate atrial impulses produce ventricular complexes, the atrial rate is 100 per minute and the ventricular rate is 50 per minute, assuming the pattern persists. The ventricular rate is not an estimate of how frequently the sinoatrial node is firing.

Rate describes how often events occur; rhythm describes their temporal organisation and origin. A sinus rhythm implies atrial activation originating from the sinoatrial region, with expected atrial morphology and appropriate temporal organisation. In a normal conducted sinus rhythm, each P wave is followed by a QRS and each QRS has a preceding P wave with an appropriate PR relation. Regularity alone does not establish a sinus origin because an escape rhythm or another pacemaker can generate regular ventricular complexes. The morphology and atrioventricular relationship provide independent evidence.

12. Mean frontal electrical axis

During ventricular depolarisation the instantaneous electrical vector changes continuously. The mean frontal QRS axis represents a summary direction of ventricular depolarisation projected onto the frontal plane. It is not the same as the anatomical long axis of the heart, the instantaneous R-wave vector or the direction of blood flow. The mean axis is usually estimated using the relative net QRS deflections across limb leads. Its interpretation relies on the conventional reference axes and assumptions about projection of the equivalent ventricular electrical activity.

Lead I has its conventional positive direction toward the left side of the subject and is assigned 0°. Lead aVF points inferiorly and is assigned +90°. In a simple quadrant approach, a positive net QRS in both I and aVF places the axis in the quadrant between 0° and +90°. Positive I with negative aVF places it in the superior leftward quadrant, where lead II helps distinguish a modest superior direction from a more leftward axis below approximately −30°. Negative I and positive aVF place the axis between +90° and +180°. If both are negative, the axis lies in the remaining superior rightward quadrant. Adult human reference ranges depend on convention, age and clinical context; for introductory reasoning −30° to +90° is a commonly used approximate range.

Net QRS deflection can be estimated by combining positive and negative contributions, not by using the R-wave height alone. In a simple height-based exercise, a lead with R = 7 mm, Q = −1 mm and S = −2 mm has a net deflection of +4 mm. A second lead with R = 4 mm and S = −6 mm has a net deflection of −2 mm. A more rigorous estimate can use signed waveform area to account for duration. Height-based estimates are useful when the question explicitly supplies comparable deflections but are not equivalent to every algorithm for mean axis calculation.

If lead I is positive and lead aVF is also positive, with equivalent orthogonal components of 0.6 and 0.6 mV in a simplified equally scaled model, the vector lies at +45°. If I equals 0.8 mV and the inferior component equals −0.8 mV, the direction is −45°. To find an angle from components, use the inverse tangent with quadrant information, or atan2 of inferior and leftward components. The raw augmented and bipolar lead signals do not have identical geometric scale factors in every idealised formulation. Therefore exact vector magnitude or angle should not be inferred by treating arbitrary measured leads as equal-scale orthogonal Cartesian components unless the problem explicitly adopts that approximation.

An isoelectric or nearly equiphasic lead suggests the mean vector is roughly perpendicular to that lead's positive direction. There are two perpendicular possibilities separated by 180°. Other leads identify which is more plausible. If lead III, whose conventional axis is +120°, is approximately isoelectric, the mean axis may be near +30° or −150°. A positive lead I favours +30°. A small net deflection may also arise from cancellation of multiple evolving vectors, so this is an approximation rather than an exact reconstruction of activation.

Changes in axis can reflect altered conduction sequence, changes in relative ventricular contributions, body position or electrode misplacement. A leftward shift does not prove an increase in left ventricular mass, and a rightward shift does not by itself prove a particular disorder. The same observed vector direction may arise from more than one mechanism. In examination questions, the provided intervention and the other recording features restrict the interpretation. In physiology, independent structural or functional measurements may be needed.

13. Sinus rate changes and atrioventricular conduction patterns

Sinus bradycardia describes a sinus rhythm with a relatively slow rate, and sinus tachycardia describes one with a relatively fast rate. In adults at rest, conventional teaching uses rates below 60 and above 100 beats per minute, respectively, but these numbers are descriptive reference points rather than universal biological cutoffs. A trained athlete, a sleeping adult and a exercising individual can have different appropriate rates. Other species have markedly different physiological resting rates. The source and conduction relationships are as important as the numerical classification.

Autonomic modulation changes sinoatrial pacemaker timing. Increased sympathetic influence generally accelerates diastolic depolarisation, while vagal influence can slow pacemaker activity and atrioventricular conduction. If a sinus rate increases while atrial activation and ventricular conduction remain coordinated, successive P–QRS–T sequences occur closer together. The P and QRS durations may not change proportionally to the shortening of the entire cycle. Consequently a faster rhythm often reduces the time available between beats and for ventricular filling. Detailed autonomic and pumping regulation belongs to later nodes.

Atrioventricular conduction disturbances are useful models for separating atrial excitation from ventricular excitation. In first-degree AV block, atrial impulses are conducted but the PR interval is prolonged; the usual adult reference is greater than 200 ms. For example, a repeated sequence with P waves every 800 ms, each followed by a narrow QRS after 260 ms, demonstrates delayed but maintained transmission. The ventricular and atrial rates are equal. Calling this pattern a complete interruption of atrioventricular conduction would be incorrect because every observed atrial impulse reaches the ventricles.

In second-degree AV block, some atrial impulses are not conducted to the ventricles. One representative pattern, often termed Mobitz I or Wenckebach, displays progressive PR lengthening before a nonconducted P wave, followed by resumption with a shorter PR interval. A simple sequence might contain PR intervals of 160, 200 and 240 ms followed by a P wave with no QRS. The key evidence is the changing timing of conducted impulses before the missed ventricular response. In a second representative pattern, Mobitz II, a nonconducted P wave occurs without the preceding progressive PR prolongation, with relatively constant PR intervals of conducted beats. A short strip with two-to-one conduction may not show enough information to confidently distinguish these patterns.

A complete atrioventricular block produces independent atrial and ventricular rhythms because atrial impulses fail to conduct to the ventricles. A subsidiary pacemaker can maintain ventricular excitation at a slower rate. P waves and QRS complexes may each be regular while their apparent PR relationships continually change. An occasional P wave can fall close to a QRS by coincidence. Therefore observing one apparently associated P and QRS is not proof of conduction. A sufficiently long recording is needed to establish whether a fixed relationship exists.

Atrioventricular dissociation is the observation that atrial and ventricular activation are independent. Complete block is one possible cause, but other circumstances involving competing pacemakers can also produce dissociation. It is scientifically safer to describe the observed relationship and use the experimental context to infer a mechanism. If a problem explicitly states that AV conduction has been abolished and a ventricular escape rhythm persists, independent P and QRS rhythms are expected. If only a brief ambiguous trace is supplied, the same inference may not be uniquely supported.

Escape rhythms illustrate the hierarchy of pacemaker function. A subsidiary pacemaker can generate excitation when the usual higher-rate driver fails to reach a region. If the escape focus is junctional and ventricular activation uses the normal His–Purkinje pathway, the QRS may be narrow. A ventricular focus often produces a wider complex because activation spreads differently. Thus the width of an escape complex helps reason about activation pathways but is not an infallible locator. The ECG indicates the timing and distribution of electrical excitation; it does not by itself indicate whether a given escape rate provides adequate tissue perfusion.

14. Connecting electrical events to pressure and movement

In the normal sequence, atrial depolarisation precedes atrial contraction, and ventricular depolarisation precedes the major rise in ventricular pressure. The QRS complex occurs around the start of ventricular mechanical systole, followed by closure of the atrioventricular valves as ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure. Ventricular ejection begins only after ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the relevant outflow artery. This delay explains why the QRS complex is not the exact instant at which blood leaves the heart.

Ventricular contraction depends on excitation–contraction coupling, including calcium entry, calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and interaction of calcium with the contractile regulatory system. These processes develop force after electrical activation has started. During the ST segment, many ventricular cells remain depolarised and ventricular pressure can remain high or ejection can continue. Ventricular repolarisation represented by the T wave occurs as the electrical activation state resolves. Mechanical relaxation follows changes in intracellular calcium and force, and valve events depend on pressure gradients rather than a wave label itself.

The first heart sound is associated mainly with vibrations during atrioventricular valve closure and early ventricular systole, whereas the second is associated mainly with semilunar valve closure at the end of ejection. Neither is an electrical waveform on the ECG. Simultaneous phonocardiography, pressure recording or echocardiography can be aligned with ECG timing to determine the relation between excitation and mechanical events. A pulse recorded at the wrist is delayed further because it reflects propagation of a pressure wave through arteries, not instantaneous ventricular electrical activation.

A hypothetical integrated recording illustrates the distinction. Set ventricular electrical onset to 0 ms. The ventricular pressure begins to rise at 25 ms, the atrioventricular valves close at 40 ms, arterial ejection begins at 90 ms, and a peripheral pulse wave is detected at 180 ms. These values are illustrative, not published normal measurements. The ECG provides a common time reference, but each subsequent event depends on distinct physiological conditions. A higher arterial pressure can delay the opening of an outflow valve without necessarily prolonging the QRS complex. A longer conduction time can change activation synchrony without a proportional change in arterial pulse transit time.

The absence of a palpable pulse does not always mean absence of cardiac electrical activity. Electrical excitation can persist without generating a useful mechanical output. Conversely, an apparently flat or low-amplitude signal in one lead may arise from the geometry of the cardiac vector or poor recording quality rather than absence of cardiac excitation. A reliable physiological conclusion requires checking the quantity actually measured. This principle applies throughout animal physiology: a signal related to a process must not be substituted for every other property of that process.

The following node develops the Wiggers diagram and pressure–volume loop, which integrate the pressure, volume and valve sequence quantitatively. The present chapter prepares that reasoning by identifying the electrical reference events and the limits of ECG-derived information. Later work on stroke volume and cardiac output will explain why equal electrical rates need not imply equal blood flow.

15. Experimental interpretation and measurement limitations

A useful physiology experiment compares an ECG with a second signal that reflects a different property of the same heartbeat. A researcher might synchronise the electrical recording with a ventricular pressure trace and ask whether a perturbation primarily changes impulse conduction, excitation–contraction coupling or ejection conditions. A baseline recording establishes the order and time separation of events. Following the perturbation, the same calibration and lead configuration should be used. Changes are evaluated relative to the baseline and appropriate experimental controls rather than interpreted from isolated waveform appearance.

Imagine a hypothetical preparation in which the PR interval increases from 150 to 230 ms while the QRS duration remains 90 ms and the delay from QRS onset to the initial pressure rise remains 30 ms. The pattern is most consistent with delayed transmission before ventricular activation while the measured ventricular activation duration and electromechanical delay are preserved. The recording cannot by itself prove the exact molecular target responsible. Additional local electrical recordings or information about the applied perturbation would be needed to identify whether the change is due to nodal conduction or another component of the interval.

In another hypothetical preparation the PR remains 150 ms, QRS remains 90 ms and the delay from ventricular electrical onset to a pressure rise increases from 30 to 75 ms. The most direct inference is a change in the relationship between excitation and mechanical response rather than a delay in atrial-to-ventricular electrical conduction. Calcium handling, force development or loading conditions could contribute. A ventricular pressure trace is essential to this distinction because the electrical trace alone might be nearly unchanged. The experimental question determines which combination of measurements is informative.

An ECG can also be contaminated by skeletal muscle activity, movement of electrodes, fluctuations in electrode–skin contact, respiratory baseline drift and electrical interference. A noise pattern may be mistaken for irregular cardiac activity if a reader counts every sharp deflection as a ventricular complex. Comparing simultaneous leads can help distinguish a consistent cardiac event from local electrode contamination. However, artefacts can affect multiple leads if they share an electrode, so agreement among leads does not guarantee that a signal is biological.

High-pass filtering may reduce baseline drift, and low-pass filtering may suppress high-frequency noise. Excessive or inappropriate filtering can distort the morphology of genuine cardiac signals, including the shape of rapid complexes or slow segments. Sampling too slowly can also fail to preserve rapidly changing signals. The general experimental principle is that a processed signal should remain adequate for the measurement task. A trace optimised for rate counting might not be suitable for precise analysis of small ST deviations or subtle waveform morphology.

Consider a simple reproducibility exercise: record the same stable rhythm in lead I before and after exchanging its input terminals. The polarity of the entire ideal difference reverses, while the time between events remains unchanged. A second manipulation changes recording gain, increasing deflection heights without altering correctly measured durations. A third changes paper speed, altering horizontal distances but not times after conversion. These are controls for interpreting lead orientation, gain and time base independently of biological changes.

Real physiological measurements rarely provide completely unambiguous explanations from one signal. The strongest conclusion is the one directly supported by the observed data and experimental constraints. A regular P-wave sequence with intermittent absent QRS complexes supports a failure of some atrial activations to produce ventricular activation in the given recording, but the anatomical site and cause may require further evidence. A change in net electrical axis supports a change in the projected direction of electrical activity, but does not by itself quantify muscle mass. Such reasoning is the foundation of analytical CSIR-NET questions.

16. Deriving the frontal vector model from two independent measurements

The ideal limb-lead geometry can be developed further without treating the body as an exact mathematical triangle. Choose the positive horizontal axis to point toward the subject's left and the positive vertical axis to point inferiorly. Let X and Y be the horizontal and inferior components of an equivalent cardiac source, expressed in voltage units for equally scaled ideal leads. Lead I has unit vector (1, 0), lead II has unit vector (1/2, √3/2), and lead III has unit vector (−1/2, √3/2). Taking scalar products gives I = X, II = X/2 + √3Y/2, and III = −X/2 + √3Y/2. These expressions immediately reproduce the relation II = I + III. This derivation helps connect the geometry with the algebra while keeping their logical origins distinct.

If the measured lead I is 0.8 mV and lead II is 0.7 mV under the assumptions of this model, X = 0.8 mV and Y = (2II − I)/√3 = (1.4 − 0.8)/1.732 ≈ 0.346 mV. The angle is arctan(0.346/0.8) ≈ 23.4° and the vector magnitude is √(0.8² + 0.346²) ≈ 0.872 mV. Lead III is −0.4 + 0.866 × 0.346 ≈ −0.1 mV. Its negative sign is expected because its axis points at 120°, more than 90° away from the inferred direction. A student can check the answer by substituting back into both independent equations instead of accepting the trigonometric output uncritically.

Now derive the augmented lead aVF from these expressions. Its algebraic definition in terms of bipolar leads is aVF = II − I/2. Substitution gives aVF = √3Y/2. Thus aVF and lead I lie along orthogonal directions in this model, but their projection coefficients are not numerically identical. If the raw aVF value is used directly as Y while raw lead I is used as X, the angle will generally differ slightly from the angle obtained by applying the explicit ideal geometry. Many introductory problems assume equally scaled orthogonal components or use a graphical approximation; when the assumptions are specified, follow them. The quadrant signs remain valid under positive scaling even when the exact angle differs.

The approximate geometry can also reveal why simultaneous signals in different leads contain related information. With X known from lead I and Y calculated from lead II, the ideal frontal projections of all other limb leads can be predicted. For example, aVL = I − II/2 = 0.8 − 0.35 = 0.45 mV, while aVR = −(I + II)/2 = −0.75 mV. The predicted aVF is 0.30 mV. These are signed instantaneous values. If each lead's largest positive height was measured at a different time, the calculated numbers would not describe a single instantaneous cardiac vector. An analysis of a time-varying source requires simultaneous observations or a clearly stated averaging method.

Consider the case X = 0. This is not a zero cardiac vector unless Y is also zero. Lead I is isoelectric in the ideal geometry, whereas leads II and III both give the same inferior projection. If both are positive, the vector is inferior; if both are negative, it is superior. Likewise, if II equals zero, the vector lies perpendicular to its axis of +60°, near −30° or +150°. The sign of I selects between these alternatives. The ability to make a directional inference from a nearly absent signal may seem surprising, but the absence is useful because the projection geometry is known. Without that geometry, a zero trace could reflect many possible mechanisms.

The algebra does not provide a unique microscopic source distribution. Imagine two different pairs of simultaneous wavefronts whose vector sums are identical. The equivalent X and Y values are identical, so every ideal frontal lead is identical, although local activation patterns differ. The full twelve-lead recording supplies additional spatial information, but it still cannot determine the exact electrical activity of every cell. The physiological model is therefore an inverse problem: electrical measurements constrain possible sources, but additional anatomical knowledge and experimental observations are necessary to identify a particular source. This is why the net electrical axis is useful without being a complete image of the heart.

17. Distinguishing conduction velocity, activation distance and electrical duration

Conduction time depends on the distance over which a wave travels and on its propagation velocity. For a single wavefront travelling uniformly along a known path, the simplest approximation is t = d/v. Here distance d may be expressed in centimetres and velocity v in centimetres per second; the ratio is in seconds. If a 4 cm path conducts at 50 cm/s, its traversal time is 0.08 s. If velocity decreases to 25 cm/s without changing the path, traversal time doubles to 0.16 s. This relation is an elementary physical model rather than a direct method for calculating whole-heart conduction velocity from the QRS interval.

The intact ventricles contain multiple pathways and regions that begin activation at different times. The His–Purkinje network rapidly delivers impulses to many sites, so excitation does not have to travel sequentially through the entire ventricular muscle from a single starting point. The QRS duration depends on the difference between the earliest and latest detectable ventricular activation times. If two regions activate in parallel, their individual propagation times need not add. If region A requires 50 ms and region B 70 ms from a common onset, completion occurs after approximately 70 ms, not 120 ms. This is an important distinction between a distributed network and a serial chain.

Consider a simplified model in which left and right ventricular activation each begin 10 ms after the initial detectable QRS onset. The left region completes activation after a further 65 ms and the right region after a further 55 ms. The overall completion time is 75 ms. Now suppose a disturbance delays the onset of right ventricular activation to 80 ms after QRS onset while its local propagation duration remains 55 ms. The overall completion time becomes 135 ms because the right side completes last. The increased QRS duration reflects altered activation sequence rather than necessarily slower propagation in every individual cell. This type of reasoning explains why a delay in a specialised pathway can produce a broad complex even when the working myocardium itself retains normal local excitability.

Changes in cell-to-cell coupling provide another useful conceptual perturbation. When current transfer between neighbouring cardiomyocytes is reduced, a depolarised cell may take longer to bring its neighbour to threshold. In a simplified homogeneous strand, propagation can therefore slow. In an intact preparation, the observed effect depends on the location and extent of the affected tissue, the availability of alternative paths and the balance between source current and electrical load. A selective atrial change might prolong the P wave without causing a major QRS alteration; a ventricular change might broaden the QRS. A uniform intervention affecting many regions could change several waveform features simultaneously.

Distinguish activation time from action-potential duration using an illustrative model. Suppose ventricular region A depolarises at 0 ms and region B at 80 ms. Their action potentials last 300 and 250 ms, respectively. Region A recovers at 300 ms and region B at 330 ms. If a conduction change delays region B activation to 120 ms, its recovery becomes 370 ms even if its action-potential duration is unchanged. The electrical recovery window may therefore shift as a consequence of altered activation timing alone. Conversely, prolonged action-potential duration can delay recovery even with unchanged QRS duration. A longer QT interval has several possible components; separating the activation portion from the recovery portion can clarify the physiological question.

For an illustrative pair of recordings, the QRS duration changes from 90 to 150 ms and QT changes from 390 to 450 ms at the same RR interval. The arithmetic difference QT minus QRS is 300 ms in both cases. This difference, often termed the JT interval, approximately represents the time from the end of ventricular activation to the end of the T wave. Here the increase in QT parallels the increased QRS duration rather than an increase in that remaining interval. The JT interval is not a perfect measurement of a single cellular recovery process, but it illustrates why a composite interval should be decomposed before assigning a mechanism. The chapter does not require clinical thresholds for this quantity; it is introduced as a logical extension of interval interpretation.

A different recording may have QRS = 90 ms at both observations while QT increases from 390 to 470 ms. The remaining interval changes from 300 to 380 ms. If the rate is unchanged, altered ventricular recovery timing is a reasonable hypothesis. It is not enough to identify a specific ion channel without additional evidence. The point of the comparison is to locate the portion of the measured sequence in which the timing change occurs. Experimental physiology proceeds by narrowing possible mechanisms with measurements whose meanings are understood.

18. Recovery dispersion and the interpretation of waveform shape

A surface wave is generated by the distribution of electrical states through time. Two hearts can have similar mean activation and recovery times yet show different waveform shapes because different regions contribute at different moments. The spread of regional recovery times is sometimes called dispersion of repolarisation. A region's recovery time equals its activation time plus its action-potential duration. Dispersion therefore depends on both the conduction sequence and local cellular recovery characteristics. It cannot be inferred from action-potential duration in a single sampled cell.

Imagine three regions with activation times of 0, 30 and 60 ms. In preparation A their action potentials last 300, 270 and 240 ms, so all three recover at 300 ms. In preparation B they all have durations of 300 ms and recover at 300, 330 and 360 ms. The activation sequence is identical in both preparations, but the recovery patterns differ. The maximum minus minimum recovery time is 0 ms in the highly simplified first example and 60 ms in the second. The examples are hypothetical; real hearts have continuous spatial distributions rather than three perfectly uniform regions. They demonstrate why a normal activation sequence does not guarantee an identical recovery signal.

If many regions change their electrical state almost together, contributions may overlap strongly. If recovery is spread across time, contributions may persist across a longer portion of the recording. But it is too simple to claim that the T-wave amplitude must always increase with more synchronised recovery or that T-wave duration always equals the maximum minus minimum local recovery time. Lead orientation, cancellation, signal thresholds and regional electrical strength all influence the surface waveform. The T wave is informative precisely because it integrates regional changes, and that integration is also the reason its interpretation has limits.

Suppose a hypothetical intervention increases action-potential duration in one region by 60 ms without affecting activation in that region or elsewhere. The altered region now remains electrically different from neighbours for an additional time. The T wave might change in width, amplitude, polarity or endpoint depending on how the region contributes to the observed lead. If the region is small or its source is nearly perpendicular to the lead, the visible change may be subtle. A recording from another orientation may reveal a larger effect. This prediction explains why comparing several leads is more informative than treating one surface trace as a complete report of the myocardium.

The direction of repolarisation also needs to be interpreted in the context of the electrical sign of the moving boundary. In the simple one-dimensional model, take a depolarisation wave moving from left to right and a lead positive on the right. During propagation the right side ahead of the wave is relatively positive, so the trace deflects upward. If recovery travels from left to right, the region behind the recovery front is restored toward the resting state while the region ahead remains activated. The extracellular polarity of the simplified boundary reverses compared with depolarisation, producing a negative signal. If recovery instead travels from right to left, that spatial reversal restores the original lead polarity. This is the logic behind concordant T and QRS deflections in many normal leads, not an assertion that every individual ventricular region recovers in a single uniform direction.

Wave duration and amplitude can also change when the baseline or recording filter changes. A slow component that is attenuated by an unsuitable filter may appear smaller or shifted relative to the displayed reference. If an experiment compares T-wave or ST morphology, the recording conditions must be kept consistent between observations. A large difference between two recordings made with different filter settings is not sufficient evidence that biological recovery changed. The scientific requirement is a suitable control for the instrument as well as the biological preparation.

19. Comparative physiology and the limits of the human reference tracing

The principles of extracellular potential measurement apply across animals that possess electrically active contractile tissue. However, the shapes conventionally labelled P, QRS and T in the human tracing reflect the organisation of the mammalian heart and its electrical pathways. In another animal, the relative size and arrangement of cardiac chambers, the timing of their activation, the position of the heart and the surrounding medium may differ. Consequently the relationship between surface waveforms and internal events must be established for that organism. Simply attaching a human label to an unfamiliar deflection does not verify its origin.

A small mammal with a high physiological heart rate may have much shorter intervals than an adult human. Temperature is particularly important in ectothermic animals because ionic channel kinetics, pacemaker timing and conduction can depend strongly on tissue temperature. A slowing of rate when temperature decreases need not indicate the same mechanism as an adult human sinus bradycardia pattern. For a comparative experiment, species, body size, temperature and recording context should be recorded. The meaningful comparison may be the relative change within a species or preparation rather than an absolute comparison with human reference intervals.

Consider a hypothetical comparison between two species recorded at identical calibrated speed. Species A has an RR interval of 0.8 s and a QRS duration of 90 ms; species B has an RR interval of 0.2 s and a QRS duration of 30 ms. The rates are 75 and 300 beats per minute, respectively. The absolute QRS duration is shorter in B, but the activation interval occupies 30/200 = 15% of its cycle, compared with 90/800 = 11.25% in A. These proportions describe electrical timing in relation to cycle length. They do not establish that species B has a less efficient heart or a particular conduction velocity. Path length, organisation and recording geometry remain relevant.

The same considerations apply when comparing the heart with different experimental preparations. An isolated perfused heart may be recorded in a conducting bath, while an intact animal has tissues surrounding its heart. The positions of the electrodes relative to the source, the electrical conductivity of the bath or body and the boundaries of the conducting medium influence the measured amplitude. A larger extracellular signal from the isolated preparation cannot automatically be interpreted as a larger cellular action potential. The comparison must address how the measurement geometry changed.

The history of cardiac electrophysiology provides examples of the importance of independent measurements. Observing a propagating electrical signal, a pressure wave or a mechanical contraction addresses different aspects of cardiac function. If an investigator records electrical activity and force simultaneously, their relative timing can reveal whether a perturbation affects excitation or the response to excitation. If the investigator records only force, a missed contraction could result from failure to excite the tissue or failure of activated muscle to generate force. If only ECG is recorded, preserved electrical activity might conceal a reduced mechanical response. A combination of methods is therefore more powerful than either alone for questions of electromechanical coupling.

In a neurogenic heart, rhythmic neural input may organise contraction differently from a vertebrate myogenic heart. Nevertheless, electrical recordings may contain contributions from neural and muscle activity depending on electrode placement and recording conditions. The presence of a rhythmic signal alone does not prove the location of the pacemaker. Evidence about whether rhythmic activity persists after removal of neural input, together with appropriate tissue recordings, helps address that question. This chapter uses such comparisons only to clarify the interpretation of electrical signals; detailed pacemaker classification belongs to the previous node.

20. Building and testing an experimental interpretation

When a question presents multiple recordings, a systematic procedure is useful. First identify the measurement setup and calibration. Next identify repeatable atrial and ventricular events, using several leads when available. Measure the relevant intervals in units of time and determine whether the comparison is between equal or different rates. Examine the relationship between the atrial and ventricular sequences before interpreting wave shape. Finally connect the observed changes to a physiological hypothesis and identify the limits of that inference. This procedure avoids choosing a mechanism merely because a familiar term appears in a question.

Consider the following illustrative dataset obtained with identical time calibration in a model cardiac preparation. At baseline the P-to-P interval is 800 ms, PR is 160 ms, QRS is 90 ms, and QT is 390 ms. Intervention A produces P-to-P = 800 ms, PR = 240 ms, QRS = 90 ms and QT = 390 ms. Intervention B produces P-to-P = 500 ms, PR = 150 ms, QRS = 90 ms and QT = 340 ms. Intervention C produces P-to-P = 800 ms, PR = 160 ms, QRS = 150 ms and QT = 450 ms. Intervention D produces P-to-P = 800 ms, PR = 160 ms, QRS = 90 ms and QT = 470 ms. Assume every P is followed by a QRS, the measurements are reliable and the same lead set is used.

The baseline atrial and ventricular rates are 75 beats per minute. Intervention A preserves that rate and selectively increases the measured atrial-to-ventricular interval by 80 ms. It is most consistent with delayed transmission before widespread ventricular activation. Intervention B increases the rate to 120 beats per minute. The shorter QT may reflect rate adaptation; it is not enough to infer an independent shortening of intrinsic action-potential duration at a fixed rate. Intervention C prolongs ventricular activation and increases QT by the same amount, while leaving the JT difference unchanged. Intervention D prolongs recovery-related timing without changing the measured rate or activation duration. No intervention can be assigned a unique molecular target from these observations alone.

Compare interventions A and C. Both prolong one measure of electrical timing by at least 60 ms, yet their likely effects on sequence differ. A delays the start of ventricular excitation relative to atrial excitation; C allows ventricular excitation to begin at a similar time but prolongs the time required to complete activation. If mechanical recordings were available, A might shift ventricular contraction later relative to atrial contraction, whereas C could produce a more dispersed pattern of regional ventricular force development. Those are hypotheses, not direct observations from the supplied ECG. A regional motion or pressure recording would help assess the mechanical effects.

A second illustrative dataset concerns electrode voltage. At one instant before a recording manipulation, R = −0.2 mV, L = 0.3 mV and F = 0.4 mV. After manipulation, R = 0.3 mV, L = −0.2 mV and F = 0.4 mV. The initial limb leads are I = +0.5 mV, II = +0.6 mV and III = +0.1 mV. After manipulation they become I = −0.5 mV, II = +0.1 mV and III = +0.6 mV. This pattern is exactly consistent with exchanging the arm electrode assignments. It satisfies Einthoven's relation in both conditions. A biological change could theoretically generate a similar voltage pattern at one instant; comparing the entire trace, checking the electrode arrangement and controlling the experimental configuration strengthen the inference.

A third experiment records an electrical sequence and an arterial pulse simultaneously. In a stable one-to-one rhythm, six consecutive ventricular complexes each precede a pulse. During a perturbation, six ventricular complexes remain visible but only five pulses exceed the detection threshold. The observation shows that the electrical rate and detected peripheral pulse rate differ during that window. A reduced pulse amplitude, altered mechanical performance, a detection problem or some combination could explain the missing detected pulse. It does not prove that one ventricular electrical activation failed to occur, because the electrical activation is visible. An independent measurement of ventricular pressure or ejection would address the mechanical alternatives.

Controls are especially important when the intervention has several effects. For instance, a change in temperature could alter pacemaker rate, conduction velocity and action-potential duration simultaneously. If the aim is to compare recovery duration independently of rate, the researcher needs either a design that controls rate or an analysis that accounts for its influence, recognising the limitations of empirical corrections. A before-and-after comparison alone cannot separate all variables that change together. The examiner may specify a controlled pacing rate or identical RR intervals to remove this ambiguity. Recognising such information is part of answering the scientific question, not merely reading numerical values.

A good explanatory answer states the observation before the mechanism. Instead of claiming that intervention A blocks a particular calcium channel, state that the PR interval increased without a change in QRS duration or atrial rate, suggesting delayed transmission before ventricular activation. Then add that local recordings or a known selective perturbation would be required to identify the exact site and molecular basis. This way of writing is useful across CSIR-NET Part C: distinguish what is measured, what can be calculated and what remains a mechanistic inference.

21. Rate correction, sampling uncertainty and quantitative consistency

Time calibration remains important when comparing several parameters derived from the same recording. Suppose a digital display provides a time scale of 200 ms per large grid division. A P onset at division 1.1 and a QRS onset at division 1.9 yield a PR interval of (1.9 − 1.1) × 200 = 160 ms. If the QRS ends at division 2.35, its duration is (2.35 − 1.9) × 200 = 90 ms. If the T wave ends at division 3.85, QT equals (3.85 − 1.9) × 200 = 390 ms. Measuring the absolute positions from a common origin helps avoid accidentally adding a time before QRS onset to the QT interval. If successive R peaks are four large divisions apart, RR = 800 ms and rate is 75 per minute.

The measurements are not infinitely precise. If the position of each endpoint is uncertain by 0.05 large divisions, this corresponds to approximately 10 ms at the stated scale. In a conservative worst-case estimate, two independently selected endpoints could produce up to about 20 ms interval uncertainty if their errors act in opposite directions. Errors might partly cancel if both endpoints share a consistent offset. The purpose of this example is not to impose one statistical error formula but to explain why modest differences near the resolution of the measurement require cautious interpretation. A reported change from 160 to 165 ms may not be distinguishable by a coarse manual method.

Sampling frequency is another dimension of time resolution. A signal sampled 250 times per second has a nominal interval between samples of 1/250 = 0.004 s, or 4 ms. At 500 samples per second, the nominal interval is 2 ms. Increasing the sampling frequency reduces the spacing between recorded time points but does not automatically establish the true onset of a small noisy waveform to that precision. The electrode signal, amplifier, filter and algorithm identifying the boundary also affect the measurement. A sampled recording is not simply a photograph of a continuously known waveform; it is a sequence of measurements made at discrete times.

Compare rate correction under two illustrative conditions. At baseline RR = 1.0 s and QT = 0.40 s. Bazett and Fridericia both produce a numerical corrected value of 0.40 s because the reference interval is one second. During a faster rhythm RR = 0.50 s and QT = 0.32 s. Bazett gives 0.32/√0.50 ≈ 0.453 s and Fridericia gives 0.32/(0.50)^(1/3) ≈ 0.403 s. The uncorrected QT is shorter in the faster rhythm, yet the two models disagree about how much of that shortening is expected. Fridericia produces nearly the same corrected value as baseline in this example, while Bazett produces a larger corrected value. This illustrates why the correction formula must be specified before interpreting a rate-corrected measurement.

An important limitation is that QT adaptation is not always instantaneous. When rate changes abruptly, the QT of the first few beats may reflect previous cycle lengths as well as the current interval. A single RR and QT pair may therefore be a poor description of steady-state rate dependence. In a conceptual experiment comparing an intervention at two rates, the timing of measurements relative to the rate change should be considered. If the question specifies a stable rhythm and adequate adaptation time, the simplified correction is more defensible than if the rate is changing beat by beat.

The relation between instantaneous and mean rate likewise illustrates the importance of using the proper denominator. Suppose two consecutive RR intervals are 0.5 and 1.0 s. Their instantaneous rates are 120 and 60 per minute, whose arithmetic mean is 90. But two complete intervals occupy 1.5 s, so the rate over the observed time is 2 × 60/1.5 = 80 beats per minute. Averaging the instantaneous rates weights the short and long intervals equally rather than weighting by elapsed time. When a question asks for the overall number of cycles per unit time, count cycles and divide by time. When it asks for the average of calculated instantaneous rates, use that definition and recognise that it answers a different question.

A final consistency check combines voltage and time. A recording at 50 mm/s and 5 mm/mV shows a QRS 6 mm wide and a dominant deflection 4 mm high. The duration is 6/50 = 0.12 s, and the amplitude is 4/5 = 0.8 mV. At the conventional speed and gain the same signal would appear 3 mm wide and 8 mm high. The changed display makes the complex visually taller and narrower but the biological values remain identical. This example brings together the two axes, the importance of units and the distinction between measurement representation and physiology.

22. Research evidence and the limits of model-based inference

A useful example of the difference between a mathematical correction and a physiological measurement comes from studies comparing alternative rate corrections on repeated electrocardiograms. A 2021 investigation published as Influence of heart rate correction formulas on QTc interval stability analysed repeated measurements from healthy volunteers and compared several correction methods. Its central question was whether the corrected value remained sufficiently stable as heart rate varied. The comparison found poorer stability with Bazett's correction than with several alternatives and emphasised that stable heart rate is important for reliable assessment. These observations support the earlier numerical example: a calculation can be internally correct yet produce an inappropriate comparison because the assumed relation between interval and rate does not describe the biological data adequately. The study does not establish that a single alternative formula can reconstruct each individual ventricular action potential or eliminate every influence on recovery timing.

An instructive extension is to imagine an experiment comparing two rate correction models on a hypothetical group of subjects. Each subject contributes a recording at a slower stable rate and another at a faster stable rate, without any deliberately applied recovery-altering intervention. One can calculate a corrected value by each formula, then compare the change within each person. If correction A produces a systematic increase at the faster rate whereas correction B produces changes distributed more closely around zero, correction B better reduces the observed rate dependence under those conditions. The within-person comparison helps account for differences in baseline intervals across individuals. The outcome could differ if the range of heart rates, the sample characteristics or the stability of the underlying physiological state changes. A reduction in average variation does not automatically imply that every participant has the same ideal correction.

What would count as a suitable control in such an experiment? Recording configuration, gain, time calibration and the method for determining the T-wave endpoint should be consistent. The two conditions should have sufficiently stable rates for the intended comparison. An investigator should also recognise that a change in autonomic state might affect ventricular recovery as well as pacemaker rate. If the study aims to isolate rate dependence, an appropriate controlled physiological preparation can help separate these influences; in intact subjects some covariation remains possible. A formula fitted to observations under one set of conditions is a model of those observations, not proof that recovery is controlled by heart rate alone.

The historical development of the ECG also illustrates how recording fidelity changes the scientific question that can be addressed. The capillary electrometer and later string galvanometer did not change how the heart activated; they changed how accurately electrical activity could be registered. Rapid transitions in voltage demand an instrument capable of following those transitions. If the measuring system responds slowly, the displayed signal may take longer to return toward its baseline, and neighbouring electrical events can appear to merge. Correct interpretation of the observed time course requires knowledge of the instrument's response. A known test input permits comparison of the recorded response with the actual imposed signal. This principle connects historical measurement advances to present-day digital calibration and filtering.

Imagine comparing two hypothetical amplifiers using a known input pulse. Amplifier A records a rapid rise and return close to the imposed waveform, whereas amplifier B displays a slow rise and a prolonged return. Both might give a similar steady amplitude during a long input, but they do not reproduce a short transient equally well. A ventricular activation complex contains rapidly changing components, so a slow instrument could distort its width or sharpness. A much slower baseline fluctuation also requires suitable recording response if its relationship to the ST segment is to be preserved. The quality of the data depends on whether the instrument is appropriate for the particular biological feature under study, rather than merely whether some oscillating signal is visible.

A strong examination response therefore connects the intended physiological inference with measurement validation. If the question concerns a change in ventricular conduction duration, it is necessary to check that the time scale and signal fidelity are comparable. If it concerns an electrical axis change, electrode placement and polarity need to be known. If it concerns recovery duration at different rates, rate correction and adaptation must be considered. If it concerns pumping, an electrical trace should be accompanied by a suitable mechanical measurement. These are distinct controls because the underlying quantities and possible errors differ. Their common principle is to avoid attributing a change to biology when a change in measurement conditions could explain the observation.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. ECG is a mechanical contraction trace.

    It measures surface voltage differences. The association between electrical and mechanical events tempts students to equate them. In a simultaneous recording, compare onset timing and never infer stroke volume from waveform height.

  2. P is the direct action potential of the SA node.

    P is predominantly atrial myocardial depolarisation. The node initiates the event but its small signal is not usually resolved. A question separating impulse initiation from atrial spread tests this distinction.

  3. Atrial repolarisation does not occur.

    It occurs but is commonly obscured by the QRS complex. Students confuse invisibility with absence; think about overlap and relative amplitude.

  4. Lead and electrode mean the same thing.

    A lead is a specified potential difference or combination; an electrode is a physical interface. Twelve traces do not require twelve physical electrodes.

  5. Einthoven's relation works only for a normal heart.

    It is an algebraic identity for consistently derived simultaneous voltages. An abnormal rhythm or swapped electrodes can still satisfy it; use the signed definitions.

  6. The tallest R peak indicates the strongest contraction.

    Amplitude depends on source geometry, cancellation, volume conduction and gain. Examiners may change gain without changing physiology; convert the units and distinguish voltage from force.

  7. A negative deflection means electrical failure.

    Negative polarity means the net projected voltage is negative relative to the lead reference. It may be entirely physiological. Reverse electrode order mentally and observe that the source need not change.

  8. PR interval and PR segment are identical.

    The interval includes the P wave; the segment begins at P end. A diagram with marked endpoints resolves the ambiguity.

  9. The ST segment is flat because ventricular cells are resting.

    Many ventricular cells remain depolarised in the plateau phase, but gradients are small. A pressure trace during ST can demonstrate ongoing contraction.

  10. An upright T wave is another ventricular depolarisation.

    T reflects ventricular recovery. Differences in recovery order and electrical sign can produce concordant QRS and T polarity; track both sign and direction.

  11. QT is the action-potential duration of a single ventricular cell.

    QT combines activation and recovery across distributed tissue. A wider QRS can lengthen QT without increasing QT minus QRS; decompose the interval.

  12. The 300 rule works at every paper speed.

    It follows specifically from 25 mm/s and 5 mm large squares. At 50 mm/s use 600 for regular rhythms; derive rather than memorise.

  13. A regular rhythm must originate from the sinus node.

    Subsidiary pacemakers can be regular. Examine P morphology and P–QRS relations rather than rate and regularity alone.

  14. All P waves must produce QRS complexes.

    Intermittent or complete AV block can separate atrial and ventricular activity. Calculate atrial and ventricular rates independently when necessary.

  15. An axis shift proves ventricular hypertrophy.

    Electrical orientation can change for several reasons, including altered conduction or electrode placement. Use experimental context and avoid assigning a unique structural cause.

  16. QT correction perfectly removes heart-rate effects.

    Correction is empirical and formula dependent. Compare like methods at stable rates and recognise that rate adaptation can lag.

  17. A human normal interval is a universal animal reference.

    Species, body size, conduction architecture and temperature influence timing. Keep human reference values distinct from general physiological mechanisms.

These are original practice questions written for the Academy, not previous-year examination questions.

Part B — Conceptual foundations

1. Which measurement is directly represented by a surface ECG?

  1. The difference in electric potential between defined recording references
  2. The transmembrane voltage of a single myocyte
  3. The instantaneous ventricular pressure
  4. The rate of aortic blood flow
Answer and explanation

A. The difference in electric potential between defined recording references. A surface lead measures an extracellular potential difference. A single-cell membrane voltage needs intracellular and extracellular reference points; pressure and flow require mechanical measurements.

2. What chiefly produces the P wave?

  1. SA nodal action potential alone
  2. Atrial depolarisation
  3. Atrial contraction
  4. Ventricular recovery
Answer and explanation

B. Atrial depolarisation. Atrial myocardial excitation produces the principal P-wave contribution. The SA node initiates excitation but its small mass does not ordinarily produce a distinct surface deflection.

3. What does the QRS complex principally record?

  1. Ventricular depolarisation
  2. Ventricular ejection
  3. Atrial repolarisation alone
  4. Ventricular repolarisation
Answer and explanation

A. Ventricular depolarisation. Rapid ventricular activation produces the dominant complex. Atrial recovery can overlap it but is not its main source, and ejection follows electrical activation.

4. The T wave primarily represents which event?

  1. Atrial filling
  2. Ventricular depolarisation
  3. Ventricular repolarisation
  4. AV nodal excitation alone
Answer and explanation

C. Ventricular repolarisation. The T wave reflects spatially distributed ventricular recovery. It is not a direct trace of pressure or contraction.

5. Which definition of lead I is correct, using R and L for right- and left-arm electrode potentials?

  1. R − L
  2. L − R
  3. L + R
  4. F − R
Answer and explanation

B. L − R. Lead I is positive at the left arm relative to the right arm; F − R is lead II.

6. Which expression is Einthoven's relation for simultaneous signed voltages?

  1. I = II + III
  2. III = I + II
  3. II = I + III
  4. II = I − III
Answer and explanation

C. II = I + III. The difference L − R plus F − L equals F − R, so the left-arm potential cancels.

7. A standard human 12-lead ECG conventionally requires how many physical electrodes?

  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 10
  4. 12
Answer and explanation

C. 10. Four limb and six chest electrodes provide the standard measurement configuration. Several leads are derived combinations, so electrode and lead counts differ.

8. Which lead is directed conventionally along the inferior frontal axis?

  1. I
  2. aVR
  3. aVL
  4. aVF
Answer and explanation

D. aVF. aVF views the inferior direction, conventionally +90°. I is leftward, whereas aVR and aVL are superiorly directed.

9. What is the Wilson central terminal in the ideal conventional formulation?

  1. Average of the three exploring limb potentials
  2. Voltage of the right leg alone
  3. Sum of chest electrode potentials
  4. Potential of the SA node
Answer and explanation

A. Average of the three exploring limb potentials. The central terminal combines right-arm, left-arm and left-leg potentials by averaging; it is a derived reference, not a single cardiac electrode.

10. At 25 mm/s, what time is represented by a 5 mm horizontal distance?

  1. 0.02 s
  2. 0.04 s
  3. 0.20 s
  4. 0.50 s
Answer and explanation

C. 0.20 s. Time = distance/speed = 5/25 s = 0.20 s. The 0.04 s value corresponds to 1 mm.

11. At 10 mm/mV, a negative 3 mm deflection corresponds to:

  1. +0.3 mV
  2. −0.3 mV
  3. −3 mV
  4. −30 mV
Answer and explanation

B. −0.3 mV. Voltage = signed height/gain = −3/10 mV. Neither the sign nor gain may be discarded.

12. The PR interval starts and ends at which landmarks?

  1. P end and T end
  2. P onset and QRS onset
  3. R peak and T onset
  4. P onset and P end
Answer and explanation

B. P onset and QRS onset. PR includes atrial activation and conduction up to ventricular activation. P end to QRS onset is the PR segment.

13. What is the most suitable explanation for the near-isoelectric ST segment in a normal simplified trace?

  1. All ventricular cells are at resting potential
  2. The heart stops contracting
  3. Ventricular tissue is relatively uniformly depolarised
  4. The electrodes cease to function
Answer and explanation

C. Ventricular tissue is relatively uniformly depolarised. Many cells are in their plateau phase, but the spatial net electrical gradient is small. Mechanical systole continues.

14. A Q wave is defined as:

  1. Any negative deflection after an R wave
  2. Any positive deflection
  3. An initial negative deflection before an R wave
  4. The end of the T wave
Answer and explanation

C. An initial negative deflection before an R wave. The naming rule concerns sequence and polarity. A negative deflection following R is S; a Q wave is not intrinsically pathological.

15. The QT interval includes:

  1. Only atrial recovery
  2. Only ventricular depolarisation
  3. Ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation
  4. Only mechanical ejection
Answer and explanation

C. Ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation. QT spans QRS onset through T end and is affected by activation and recovery timing.

16. Which property most directly distinguishes a rhythm from its rate?

  1. The paper width
  2. The temporal organisation and origin of beats
  3. The voltage gain alone
  4. The number of electrodes
Answer and explanation

B. The temporal organisation and origin of beats. Rate counts events per unit time. Rhythm considers their timing, sequence and source; equal rates can have different rhythms.

17. Which finding illustrates first-degree AV block in an adult teaching example?

  1. PR 260 ms with every P followed by QRS
  2. Complete absence of P waves
  3. Regular independent atrial and ventricular rhythms
  4. Progressively changing PR followed by a missed QRS
Answer and explanation

A. PR 260 ms with every P followed by QRS. First-degree block is delayed but preserved conduction. The final option describes a representative second-degree pattern.

18. Which feature best explains why atrial repolarisation is often not distinct?

  1. Atrial cells never repolarise
  2. It overlaps the larger ventricular activation signal
  3. The atria lack ion channels
  4. The atria recover only after the next beat
Answer and explanation

B. It overlaps the larger ventricular activation signal. Small atrial recovery contributions are commonly obscured by ventricular depolarisation.

19. What is the mean frontal QRS axis?

  1. An estimate of the net direction of ventricular electrical activation in the frontal plane
  2. The physical direction of aortic flow
  3. The mean arterial pressure
  4. The direction of atrial contraction
Answer and explanation

A. An estimate of the net direction of ventricular electrical activation in the frontal plane. It summarises electrical vector orientation, not flow, pressure or anatomical orientation alone.

20. Which signal is required to directly investigate ventricular pressure development alongside ECG?

  1. Another ECG gain setting
  2. An appropriate ventricular pressure recording
  3. A count of limb electrodes
  4. A different voltage reference alone
Answer and explanation

B. An appropriate ventricular pressure recording. Pressure recording supplies the mechanical variable missing from an electrical trace. Changes in electrode configuration do not turn voltage into pressure.

Part C — Analytical reasoning

21. At a simultaneous instant I = −0.4 mV and III = +0.9 mV. Find II.

  1. −1.3 mV
  2. −0.5 mV
  3. +0.5 mV
  4. +1.3 mV
Answer and explanation

C. +0.5 mV. II = I + III = −0.4 + 0.9 = +0.5 mV. Adding absolute magnitudes would incorrectly ignore polarity.

22. R = −0.2, L = +0.4 and F = +0.2 mV. Find aVL.

  1. +0.4 mV
  2. +0.2 mV
  3. −0.4 mV
  4. 0 mV
Answer and explanation

A. +0.4 mV. aVL = L − (R + F)/2 = 0.4 − (−0.2 + 0.2)/2 = 0.4 mV.

23. The same voltage pulse produces 6 mm at 10 mm/mV. If gain doubles, what happens?

  1. Height becomes 12 mm, voltage stays 0.6 mV
  2. Height stays 6 mm, voltage doubles
  3. Height becomes 3 mm, voltage halves
  4. Duration doubles
Answer and explanation

A. Height becomes 12 mm, voltage stays 0.6 mV. The original voltage is 6/10 = 0.6 mV. At 20 mm/mV the height is 12 mm. Gain is a vertical scale, not a time scale.

24. Using the calibrated graph above, find the regular ventricular rate.

  1. 37.5/min
  2. 75/min
  3. 150/min
  4. 300/min
Answer and explanation

B. 75/min. The R peaks are separated by eight major grid intervals, equivalent to 40 mm on standard paper. RR = 40/50 = 0.8 s, giving 60/0.8 = 75/min. The 300 shortcut is invalid at 50 mm/s.

25. Five complete RR intervals total 3.6 s. The average ventricular rate is approximately:

  1. 60/min
  2. 83.3/min
  3. 100/min
  4. 120/min
Answer and explanation

B. 83.3/min. Rate = 5 × 60/3.6 = 83.3/min. Five complete intervals are bounded by six R peaks.

26. Two RR intervals are 0.4 and 0.8 s. What is the overall rate over these intervals?

  1. 75/min
  2. 100/min
  3. 112.5/min
  4. 150/min
Answer and explanation

B. 100/min. Two cycles take 1.2 s; 120/1.2 = 100/min. Averaging instantaneous rates 150 and 75 gives 112.5, a different quantity.

27. A cardiac vector of aligned equivalent voltage 2 mV makes an angle of 120° to the positive lead axis. Predict the voltage.

  1. +2 mV
  2. +1 mV
  3. −1 mV
  4. 0 mV
Answer and explanation

C. −1 mV. The ideal projection is 2 cos 120° = −1 mV. The negative sign indicates an opposing component, not absence of activity.

28. Lead III is nearly isoelectric; lead I is positive. In the simple axis model, which angle fits best?

  1. −150°
  2. +30°
  3. +120°
  4. −60°
Answer and explanation

B. +30°. The perpendiculars to the +120° axis are +30° and −150°. Positive I favours +30°.

29. An ideal frontal recording gives I = 0 and II = +0.6 mV. Consider the following statements: (1) X = 0; (2) Y is positive; (3) the axis is approximately +90°; (4) the entire cardiac vector is zero. Which combination is correct?

  1. 1 and 4 only
  2. 2 and 4 only
  3. 1, 2 and 3 only
  4. 3 and 4 only
Answer and explanation

C. 1, 2 and 3 only. I = X = 0; II = √3Y/2 is positive, hence Y is positive and the vector is inferior. Statement 4 confuses a zero projection with a zero vector; the nonzero lead II excludes that conclusion.

30. PR increases from 150 to 230 ms while PP and QRS are unchanged. Which immediate conclusion is justified?

  1. Ventricular muscle force has doubled
  2. Pre-ventricular transmission time has increased
  3. The ventricular action-potential plateau has disappeared
  4. The SA node must have stopped
Answer and explanation

B. Pre-ventricular transmission time has increased. PR measures time from atrial onset to ventricular onset. Unchanged PP and QRS argue against a necessary change in rate or ventricular activation duration.

31. A selective ventricular pathway delay broadens QRS from 80 to 140 ms but leaves PR unchanged. Which prediction is best?

  1. Ventricular activation ends later relative to onset
  2. Atrial activation necessarily becomes slower
  3. Ventricular ejection must last exactly 140 ms
  4. Atrial recovery ceases
Answer and explanation

A. Ventricular activation ends later relative to onset. The broadened complex indicates extended detectable activation after onset. Neither mechanical duration nor atrial behaviour follows uniquely from QRS width.

32. Region A activates at 10 ms with AP duration 290 ms; B at 50 ms with AP duration 220 ms. Which recovers first?

  1. A at 290 ms
  2. B at 270 ms
  3. A at 300 ms
  4. Both at 300 ms
Answer and explanation

B. B at 270 ms. Recovery times are A = 10 + 290 = 300 ms and B = 50 + 220 = 270 ms. Later activation does not force later recovery.

33. At equal RR, QRS increases from 90 to 150 ms and QT from 400 to 460 ms. What happens to QT minus QRS?

  1. Increases by 60 ms
  2. Decreases by 60 ms
  3. Remains 310 ms
  4. Becomes 460 ms
Answer and explanation

C. Remains 310 ms. Both original and final differences are 310 ms. The increase in QT accompanies increased activation duration; a longer recovery interval is not demonstrated.

34. QT = 0.32 s and RR = 0.64 s. What is Bazett QTc?

  1. 0.256 s
  2. 0.32 s
  3. 0.40 s
  4. 0.50 s
Answer and explanation

C. 0.40 s. The square root of 0.64 is 0.8; QTc = 0.32/0.8 = 0.40 s using the conventional seconds formula.

35. P waves occur every 0.5 s; each alternate P is followed by a QRS. What are atrial and ventricular rates?

  1. 60 and 120/min
  2. 120 and 60/min
  3. 120 and 120/min
  4. 60 and 60/min
Answer and explanation

B. 120 and 60/min. Atrial PP = 0.5 s gives 120/min. Alternate conduction gives ventricular RR = 1 s and 60/min.

36. A recording has regular P waves at 90/min and regular QRS complexes at 35/min, with continuously changing PR relationships. What is directly observed?

  1. Atrioventricular dissociation
  2. One-to-one sinus conduction
  3. A fixed first-degree delay
  4. Absence of ventricular electrical activity
Answer and explanation

A. Atrioventricular dissociation. Independent rhythms and drifting relationships demonstrate dissociation. Complete block is one possible mechanism, but the observation alone need not uniquely establish its cause.

37. A stable recording shows PR intervals 160, 200, 240 ms followed by a P without QRS, then the pattern resets. Which pattern fits?

  1. First-degree AV block
  2. Representative Mobitz I conduction pattern
  3. Normal one-to-one sinus rhythm
  4. Isolated increased gain
Answer and explanation

B. Representative Mobitz I conduction pattern. Progressively longer conducted PR intervals before a nonconducted atrial impulse and reset are characteristic of the representative Wenckebach pattern.

38. Right and left arm electrode labels are exchanged, with left leg unchanged. Which transformed combination is correct?

  1. I unchanged, II reversed
  2. I reversed; II and III exchanged
  3. All leads become zero
  4. Only III reverses
Answer and explanation

B. I reversed; II and III exchanged. New I = R − L = −I. New II = F − L = III and new III = F − R = II. The underlying cardiac physiology can be unchanged.

39. ECG QRS count is unchanged, but a simultaneous arterial pulse detector records fewer pulses. What is the strongest conclusion?

  1. Some ventricular electrical events have no detected corresponding pulse
  2. The SA node no longer generates impulses
  3. Every QRS is a mechanical contraction of identical force
  4. There is proven complete AV block
Answer and explanation

A. Some ventricular electrical events have no detected corresponding pulse. The recordings disagree in electrical versus detected peripheral mechanical events. Pump performance and detection quality require independent investigation.

40. An intervention changes QRS-to-pressure-rise delay from 30 to 70 ms, with PR and QRS duration unchanged. Which is best supported?

  1. A longer measured electromechanical delay
  2. A 40 ms longer atrial conduction delay
  3. A reversed lead I axis
  4. A necessarily faster heart rate
Answer and explanation

A. A longer measured electromechanical delay. The changed interval lies between electrical onset and mechanical response. Calcium handling, loading or force development may contribute; the ECG alone cannot identify which.

  3. 100 ms
  4. 200 ms

45. At gain 5 mm/mV, an R wave is 7 mm high. Its amplitude is:

  1. 0.35 mV

  4. 35 mV
Answer and explanation

C. 1.4 mV. 7/5 = 1.4 mV. Dividing by the standard gain of 10 would be inappropriate because the specified gain is different.

46. Which paired recording best tests whether ventricular electrical onset precedes force development?

  1. ECG and a synchronised mechanical force or pressure signal
  2. Two unsynchronised ECG photographs
  3. Only an ECG at increased gain
  4. Only a pulse count after the experiment
Answer and explanation

A. ECG and a synchronised mechanical force or pressure signal. Synchronised electrical and mechanical measurements allow direct estimation of the temporal separation; unsynchronised or single-variable observations do not.

47. A ventricular recovery time is 350 ms. Activation occurred at 70 ms. The local action-potential duration in the model is:

  1. 70 ms
  2. 280 ms
  3. 350 ms
  4. 420 ms
Answer and explanation

B. 280 ms. Duration = recovery time − activation time = 350 − 70 = 280 ms.

48. What is the nominal sample interval at 500 samples/s?

  1. 0.2 ms
  2. 2 ms
  3. 5 ms
  4. 500 ms
Answer and explanation

B. 2 ms. 1/500 s = 0.002 s = 2 ms. The ability to identify an actual waveform boundary may be less precise.

49. A recording at 50 mm/s and 5 mm/mV displays an event 5 mm wide and 5 mm high. Find duration and voltage.

  1. 200 ms, 0.5 mV
  2. 100 ms, 1 mV
  3. 100 ms, 0.5 mV
  4. 200 ms, 1 mV
Answer and explanation

B. 100 ms, 1 mV. Time = 5/50 = 0.1 s; voltage = 5/5 = 1 mV. Convert each axis separately.

50. An animal's ECG is compared with human resting reference intervals without controlling species or temperature. Which critique is strongest?

  1. Human reference values are universal
  2. Temperature and species-specific organisation can alter timing
  3. Only voltage gain matters in animal comparisons
  4. All vertebrates must have the same RR interval
Answer and explanation

B. Temperature and species-specific organisation can alter timing. Shared electrical principles do not establish universal rates or intervals. Species and temperature are important physiological variables.

51. An almost isoelectric lead is observed while another lead shows a large QRS. Which explanation is plausible?

  1. The first lead may be nearly perpendicular to the net vector
  2. The heart necessarily stopped
  3. The second lead measures pressure rather than voltage
  4. All ventricular cells are simultaneously at rest throughout QRS
Answer and explanation

A. The first lead may be nearly perpendicular to the net vector. Different projection directions can produce different amplitudes of the same underlying source. A flat projection is not proof of global inactivity.

52. QT = 390 ms, QRS = 90 ms. Find JT in this teaching example.

  1. 90 ms
  2. 300 ms
  3. 390 ms
  4. 480 ms
Answer and explanation

B. 300 ms. JT = QT − QRS = 300 ms. The interval is a surface timing measure, not a direct single-cell action-potential duration.

Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

The ECG records extracellular potential differences arising from distributed cardiac excitation and recovery. Lead orientation, tissue geometry and recording calibration determine the appearance of the electrical record. Atrial activation creates P; ventricular activation creates QRS; ventricular recovery creates T. Intervals locate timing changes within the excitation sequence, while pressure and flow recordings establish mechanical consequences independently.
