1. Why oxidation must be coupled to ATP formation

A cell can dismantle glucose into carbon dioxide and still have a problem: most of the useful chemical energy is not yet in ATP. During glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle, enzymes transfer electrons from fuel molecules to oxidised coenzymes. The reduced forms, especially NADH and enzyme-bound FADH₂, contain electrons that can ultimately be transferred to oxygen. If the cell simply allowed that transfer to occur without an organised mechanism, much of the available free energy would be dissipated. The respiratory chain solves this problem by dividing the oxidation process into a series of reactions and using the released energy to move protons across a membrane.

Consider a muscle cell that has suddenly increased its ATP consumption. The citric acid cycle cannot keep supplying reducing equivalents indefinitely unless NAD⁺ and FAD are regenerated. Oxidative phosphorylation addresses two linked needs: it recycles electron acceptors for continued fuel oxidation and converts a substantial fraction of the energy released by electron transfer into ATP. These are distinct functions. An experimentally uncoupled mitochondrion can still oxidise NADH and consume oxygen even while its ATP production is very low. Understanding that separation will be essential when interpreting respiration experiments.

Recall three ideas from earlier chapters. Oxidation means loss of electrons; reduction means gain. A negative Gibbs free-energy change favours a process under the stated conditions, but does not specify its speed. Finally, a selectively permeable membrane can maintain different ion concentrations and electrical potentials on its two sides. Respiratory enzymes connect these three principles. Electron transfer provides free energy, proton translocation stores part of that energy as an electrochemical difference, and ATP synthase permits a controlled return of protons while catalysing phosphorylation of ADP.

The term oxidative phosphorylation therefore does not mean adding oxygen to ADP. Oxidation of a donor such as NADH is coupled indirectly to phosphorylation of ADP with inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ). Oxygen is the final electron acceptor in the conventional mammalian chain, not the source of the phosphate in ATP. In contrast, substrate-level phosphorylation transfers a phosphoryl group directly from a metabolic intermediate to ADP or GDP, as in the phosphoglycerate kinase or succinyl-CoA synthetase reactions. Neither of those reactions needs ATP synthase. The remainder of this chapter uses the mammalian mitochondrion as the reference system, with other organisms mentioned only to clarify common bioenergetic principles.

2. The mitochondrial compartments make coupling possible

The mitochondrial outer membrane contains porins that allow many small solutes to reach the intermembrane space. The inner membrane is different: its low permeability to ions, including protons, enables it to maintain an electrochemical difference. The matrix is enclosed by the inner membrane and contains pyruvate dehydrogenase and most citric acid cycle enzymes. The respiratory complexes and ATP synthase are associated with the inner membrane; its folds, called cristae, provide surface area for these proteins. A large membrane area permits more catalytic machinery, but area alone cannot sustain a gradient if the membrane is leaky.

For proton translocation it is useful to call the matrix the N side and the intermembrane-space side the P side. During forward respiration the N side is electrically negative and generally more alkaline relative to the P side. These are relative descriptions, not an assertion that the matrix is strongly alkaline in an absolute sense. Electrons enter the respiratory machinery from matrix NADH or other donors, while selected complexes move H⁺ from N to P. ATP synthase has its catalytic head on the matrix side and uses the reverse flow of H⁺. Thus the orientation of a protein within the membrane matters just as much as its catalytic identity.

Imagine rupturing the inner membrane while leaving the respiratory enzymes catalytically intact. Electron donors and oxygen might still react, but protons can no longer be kept apart. The free energy stored by charge and concentration differences disappears, preventing normal chemiosmotic ATP synthesis. This explains why an intact inner membrane is necessary for oxidative phosphorylation but is not required for every individual redox reaction. Experimentally, sealed vesicles made from membrane fragments can retain or rebuild coupling; their orientation must be known before deciding which way protons move.

3. The energetic direction of electron transfer

An electron acceptor with a more positive reduction potential tends to accept electrons from a donor with a more negative potential, provided the actual reaction conditions support that direction. Standard biochemical reduction potentials, E°′, normally use a buffered pH of 7. They describe reduction half-reactions, so the potential of a donor is not assigned a new sign before subtraction. For a reaction transferring n electrons, ΔE°′ = E°′(acceptor) − E°′(donor). The free energy is ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′, where F is the Faraday constant, about 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹ of electrons. One volt times one mole of electronic charge is an energy; the minus sign indicates that positive potential difference gives favourable electron transfer.

For NAD⁺/NADH, take E°′ ≈ −0.320 V; for O₂/H₂O take E°′ ≈ +0.815 V. NADH provides two electrons, so the net reaction is NADH + H⁺ + ½O₂ → NAD⁺ + H₂O. The potential difference is 0.815 − (−0.320) = 1.135 V. Therefore ΔG°′ = −2 × 96.485 × 1.135 ≈ −219 kJ mol⁻¹ NADH. The value is large compared with the free energy required to synthesise a mole of ATP. It sets an energetic ceiling, not a promise that all that energy will be converted into ATP.

In a cell the concentrations and redox states of the carriers affect their effective potentials. For a reduction written Ox + ne⁻ ⇌ Red, with other reactants held at the defined transformed conditions, the Nernst relation is E = E°′ + (RT/nF) ln(aOx/aRed). Accumulation of the reduced form lowers the reduction potential of that couple. Changes in oxygen availability, the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio, and the redox state of ubiquinone can alter the net driving force. A sequence of standard potentials is a useful guide to direction, not an absolute guarantee of the direction of each elementary transfer. In particular, some electron transfers within a complex are made possible by coupling to other favourable steps.

Why not pass electrons directly from NADH to oxygen? Besides losing opportunities for energy conservation, uncontrolled partial reduction of oxygen can generate reactive species. The chain provides specific redox sites that partition free-energy release and direct electrons toward a catalytic site where oxygen is normally fully reduced to water. Electron carriers that deliver one electron and those that deliver two must be connected coherently. This is why flavins, iron–sulfur centres, quinones, haems and copper centres appear in the same biochemical system.

4. The respiratory chain as a connected circuit

There are two principal entry routes in the textbook model. NADH donates electrons to Complex I. Succinate is oxidised by Complex II and transfers electrons through its enzyme-bound FAD and iron–sulfur centres. Both routes converge on the ubiquinone pool in the membrane. Ubiquinol delivers reducing equivalents to Complex III, which reduces cytochrome c. Cytochrome c then moves to Complex IV, where oxygen is reduced to water. Complexes I, III and IV contribute to the transmembrane proton gradient; Complex II does not.

Ubiquinone, also called coenzyme Q, has a hydrophobic tail that allows it to move through the membrane's lipid phase. Its oxidised form Q can accept two electrons and two protons to become QH₂; semiquinone is an intermediate with one electron added. In contrast, cytochrome c is a small haem-containing protein exposed to the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane. Its haem iron cycles between Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺, carrying one electron at a time. The different locations and electron capacities explain why Complex III must transform a two-electron input into two successive one-electron outputs.

A useful bookkeeping convention is to describe every complex per pair of electrons ultimately reaching half a molecule of oxygen. The number of protons translocated is approximately four at I, zero at II, four at III and two at IV. Do not add Complex II to the NADH path: I and II are parallel entry routes, not consecutive steps. The total of ten for NADH and six for succinate-derived electrons is the foundation of the usual ATP-yield calculation. Complex V is the ATP synthase; it uses the gradient rather than transferring electrons to oxygen.

5. Complex I: linking NADH oxidation to proton pumping

Complex I, NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase, is a large L-shaped enzyme whose water-facing arm reaches into the matrix and whose membrane arm participates in proton translocation. NADH cannot hand its electron pair directly to a single iron–sulfur centre because these centres typically change oxidation state by one electron. The flavin mononucleotide (FMN) near the NADH binding site accepts the reducing equivalents from NADH and then supplies electrons individually to a series of iron–sulfur centres. The electrons eventually reach ubiquinone. The FMN and iron–sulfur centres are attached to the enzyme and are recycled; they are not released as freely circulating respiratory intermediates.

Reduction of Q requires two electrons and two protons. On the matrix side, NADH oxidation can be written NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻, and Q reduction as Q + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → QH₂. Adding these half-reactions gives NADH + Q + H⁺ → NAD⁺ + QH₂. This chemistry alone does not show the transport of protons across the membrane. The separate energetic output of Complex I is approximately four matrix-to-P-side translocated H⁺ per electron pair. Combining the two balances gives NADH + Q + 5H⁺(N) → NAD⁺ + QH₂ + 4H⁺(P). The one extra matrix proton is net chemical consumption, not a fifth pumped proton.

Electron transfer near the quinone-binding region changes local charge, protonation and protein conformation. These changes are coupled to proton-transfer pathways within the membrane arm. The central examination principle is long-range coupling: a redox reaction at one region changes the ability of distant membrane regions to take up and release protons. The detailed atomic sequence of these events is not required to calculate stoichiometry, and describing the four pumped protons as if they were all chemically carried by QH₂ would be incorrect.

Complex I operates forward when NADH supplies electrons, Q is sufficiently oxidised and the electrochemical load does not exceed the energy available. Under some conditions, a strongly reduced Q pool and a large proton motive force can instead drive reverse electron transfer through Complex I, favouring NAD⁺ reduction. This exception reinforces the idea that the chain is thermodynamically coupled rather than a rigid one-way pipe. A block at the Q-reduction region, such as rotenone inhibition, prevents normal NADH-linked respiration but leaves the downstream chain potentially available to electrons supplied by a different entry route.

6. Complex II and electron entry without proton pumping

Succinate dehydrogenase is both an enzyme of the citric acid cycle and a component of the respiratory chain. It oxidises succinate to fumarate by removing two hydrogen equivalents. Its FAD prosthetic group accepts the reducing equivalents and transfers electrons through iron–sulfur centres to Q. Because the FAD belongs to the enzyme, there is not a freely released molecule of FADH₂ that diffuses from the cycle to Complex II. The compact textbook net reaction is succinate + Q ⇌ fumarate + QH₂.

Unlike NADH oxidation, the succinate/fumarate reaction supplies a smaller free-energy margin for reduction of Q. Complex II has no proton-pumping apparatus comparable with that of Complex I. Its useful contribution is to feed electrons into the membrane Q pool, allowing Complexes III and IV to continue to conserve energy. This is why succinate-linked respiration can still support ATP formation even though Complex II pumps no protons. When calculating yield, the six protons associated with the downstream path replace the ten of the NADH route.

The Q pool is also a point of entry for other metabolic reactions. For example, electron-transferring flavoprotein carries reducing equivalents from several acyl-CoA dehydrogenases to ETF:ubiquinone oxidoreductase, which reduces Q. The mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase also reduces Q. These inputs do not literally pass through Complex II. They share the energetic consequence of bypassing Complex I, under the conventional assumption that electrons proceed through III and IV. The distinction prevents an incorrect inference that all flavin-dependent dehydrogenases are blocked by a Complex II inhibitor.

Malonate resembles succinate and can competitively inhibit succinate dehydrogenase. In an isolated preparation supplied with succinate alone this will decrease electron supply to Q. A separate substrate that produces matrix NADH can support respiration through Complex I if the rest of the system remains functional. This logic allows localisation of a defect by changing the entry substrate rather than trying to interpret a fall in oxygen consumption as a unique molecular diagnosis.

7. Complex III: the Q cycle and one-electron outputs

Complex III, ubiquinol:cytochrome c oxidoreductase or the cytochrome bc₁ complex, contains a Rieske iron–sulfur protein, cytochrome b haems and cytochrome c₁. Its task is not simply to pass a pair of electrons to a single cytochrome c. A QH₂ molecule can donate two electrons, whereas each cytochrome c accepts only one. The Q cycle solves this mismatch while achieving vectorial proton transfer. There are two quinone reaction sites: Qₒ, close to the P side, and Qᵢ, close to the N side. Keep track of the sites separately rather than memorising four protons as an unexplained number.

During the first turnover, QH₂ binds at Qₒ. Its oxidation releases two protons into the P side. One electron goes through the Rieske centre and cytochrome c₁ to reduce one cytochrome c. The other electron passes through the two b haems toward Qᵢ, where it reduces Q to a semiquinone intermediate. Two electrons leave QH₂ but only one has yet left Complex III toward Complex IV. The second remains within the quinone-reduction branch. This bifurcation is the key to understanding why a complete cycle requires two turnovers.

During the second turnover, a second QH₂ is oxidised at Qₒ. Two more protons are released to the P side and a second cytochrome c is reduced through the high-potential branch. The other electron reaches the Qᵢ semiquinone; with two protons taken from the N side over the completed reduction, it produces QH₂. Thus two QH₂ molecules have been oxidised at Qₒ but one QH₂ has been regenerated at Qᵢ. On net, one QH₂ is consumed and two cytochrome c molecules are reduced. Four protons appear on the P side and two additional protons are taken from the N side at Qᵢ.

The net bookkeeping equation is QH₂ + 2 cyt c(Fe³⁺) + 2H⁺(N) → Q + 2 cyt c(Fe²⁺) + 4H⁺(P). The two H⁺ bound in the net consumed QH₂ are already part of the reduced carrier. When its formation on the matrix side is included, Complex III contributes an effective four translocated protons per electron pair. It is not four protons plus another two pumped protons: the two taken at Qᵢ are included in the overall mechanism. Unlike a simple channel that moves four free protons through one pore, the Q cycle accomplishes vectorial movement through quinone chemistry at spatially separated sites.

Antimycin A inhibits the Qᵢ branch and disrupts normal cycling. A Qₒ-site inhibitor such as myxothiazol blocks QH₂ oxidation through a different site. In both cases downstream electron flow is impaired, but the local redox states and propensity for electron leakage can differ. For NET problems the essential conclusion is that neither NADH entry nor succinate entry can bypass a complete block at Complex III by merely changing the substrate. Both depend on the same downstream segment.

8. Complex IV: completing oxygen reduction

Cytochrome c oxidase receives electrons from reduced cytochrome c on the P side. Electrons pass through copper and haem centres to the oxygen-reduction site formed by haem a₃ and CuB. Molecular oxygen binds at this binuclear centre. Complete reduction of oxygen to water requires four electrons and four chemical protons. Since cytochrome c brings only one electron per encounter, four reduced cytochrome c molecules are needed for the reduction of one O₂ molecule. The enzyme holds and processes oxygen-derived intermediates during turnover rather than releasing partly reduced oxygen as the intended product.

In addition to those chemical protons, Complex IV pumps protons from the matrix to the P side. For one full oxygen molecule, the conventional stoichiometry is four H⁺ pumped and four H⁺ consumed chemically. The net equation is 4 cyt c(red) + O₂ + 8H⁺(N) → 4 cyt c(ox) + 2H₂O + 4H⁺(P). Dividing by two gives the electron-pair convention used throughout this chapter: two protons are pumped and two are chemically consumed, with ½O₂ reduced to H₂O. The distinction matters in any problem asking for protons moved across the membrane rather than total matrix protons removed.

Complex IV provides the terminal sink for electrons. If oxygen is absent or the enzyme is inhibited, the earlier carriers progressively become reduced because their usual outlet is blocked. NADH oxidation slows and the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio tends to rise. Oxidative reactions requiring NAD⁺, including pyruvate dehydrogenase and several TCA reactions, become limited. ATP production from oxidative phosphorylation ceases as the existing gradient is consumed or leaks away. Cyanide, carbon monoxide under suitable binding conditions, and azide inhibit cytochrome c oxidase. Adding more succinate cannot overcome a terminal-chain block because its electrons still need Complex IV.

The observation that oxygen consumption decreases after a respiratory inhibitor is useful, but it does not directly establish which step was affected. An upstream donor shortage, an ATP synthase block that raises the gradient, or limited oxygen delivery can all reduce the rate of oxygen use. The respiratory chain must therefore be understood as part of a coupled system whose flux depends on both chemistry and energy demand.

9. Chemiosmosis: deriving the proton motive force

The difference in proton concentration is only one part of the stored energy. A proton has charge +1, so a proton moving toward a more negative compartment is also electrically favoured. To derive the energy of a transfer from P to N, consider the chemical potential μ = μ° + RT ln a + zFψ. Here a is the dimensionless activity of the ion, ψ is electrical potential in volts, z is charge number, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. Subtracting the starting potential from the final potential for H⁺ gives ΔG(P→N) = RT ln(aN/aP) + F(ψN − ψP).

Because pH = −log₁₀(aH⁺), ln(aN/aP) = −2.303(pHN − pHP). Define Δψ = ψN − ψP and ΔpH = pHN − pHP. Then ΔG(P→N) = F[Δψ − (2.303RT/F)ΔpH]. With these explicit signs the inward transfer is negative when the matrix is negative and more alkaline. Some books define the proton motive force as a positive magnitude for this favourable inward movement, while others use the signed quantity Δp = Δψ − (2.303RT/F)ΔpH. Both describe the same physical situation. Always identify the reference direction before substituting a membrane potential.

At 37 °C (310 K), 2.303RT/F = 2.303 × 8.314 × 310 / 96485 ≈ 0.0615 V per pH unit. Suppose the matrix potential relative to P is −150 mV and matrix pH is 7.8 compared with P-side pH 7.3. Then ΔpH = +0.5 and Δp = −150 − 61.5 × 0.5 = −180.75 mV. Convert this to volts: −0.18075 V. The free energy per mole of entering protons is 96.485 × (−0.18075) ≈ −17.44 kJ mol⁻¹. Moving the same number of protons outward against the same gradient costs +17.44 kJ mol⁻¹, provided conditions are held constant.

This example reveals why a pH gradient of only half a unit can support useful energy conservation. The electrical term often makes a large contribution in mitochondria; the chemical term adds to it. If a question gives a matrix potential of −150 mV but no pH difference, do not silently insert an assumed 0.5 pH unit. Either compute the electrical contribution alone or state the missing quantity. Similarly, a gradient with an opposite pH difference can partly oppose the electrical component. It is the sum of both contributions that determines whether proton entry is favourable.

Compare the standard NADH oxidation energy of about 219 kJ mol⁻¹ with the cost of moving ten protons against the illustrative 180.75 mV gradient: about 174.4 kJ mol⁻¹. There is an energy margin in this example, although exact cellular efficiency requires actual donor and acceptor potentials. As the gradient becomes larger the work per proton rises, so pumping becomes progressively harder. At sufficiently high backpressure, the forward respiratory rate falls. The relationship between respiration and ATP synthesis is therefore bidirectional: the chain energises ATP synthesis, and utilisation of the gradient influences the chain.

10. How experimental evidence established chemiosmotic coupling

Early explanations of oxidative phosphorylation sought a transferable, energy-rich chemical intermediate connecting oxidation and phosphorylation. Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 that the shared intermediate was instead a transmembrane electrochemical gradient. The model predicted that electron transport and ATP formation need not be physically joined by a phosphorylated molecule; both could interact through a membrane-maintained proton difference. The proposal was initially controversial because it replaced familiar soluble enzyme coupling with a spatial process.

A decisive type of test was to impose a proton gradient independently of electron flow. In the classical acid–base transition experiment of Jagendorf and Uribe, isolated chloroplast thylakoids were exposed to acidic conditions and transferred to an alkaline medium containing ADP and phosphate. A transient ATP synthesis occurred in darkness when the imposed gradient was present. This is a chloroplast experiment, but its relevance here is the general principle: a proton gradient can provide the immediate driving force even in the absence of ongoing light-driven electron flow. It does not imply that normal mitochondrial respiration needs light, nor that a pH gradient is the only component of the proton motive force.

Racker and Stoeckenius subsequently reconstituted membrane vesicles containing the light-driven proton pump bacteriorhodopsin and mitochondrial ATP-synthesising machinery. Illumination supplied the energy for proton transport and ATP formation even without the usual mitochondrial respiratory complexes. The pump and ATP synthase came from very different systems. That result makes sense if the transferable energetic intermediate is a proton electrochemical difference rather than a special high-energy molecule produced uniquely by the respiratory chain. The result does depend on sealed vesicles and appropriate enzyme orientation; a leaky membrane would destroy the gradient as it formed.

The model also predicts that an uncoupler that dissipates the proton motive force can separate oxygen consumption from ATP production. This prediction is complementary to the reconstitution experiments. Gradient formation can be sufficient under appropriate conditions, and gradient dissipation makes ordinary oxidative ATP formation fail despite electron flow. No single measurement of ATP concentration proves a coupling mechanism. Strong evidence combines manipulated gradients, controls lacking one component, independent measures of electron transfer and ATP production, and knowledge of membrane integrity.

11. ATP synthase: a reversible rotary enzyme

ATP synthase, often called F₀F₁-ATP synthase or Complex V, has a membrane-embedded F₀ sector and a catalytic F₁ sector projecting into the matrix. F₀ contains a ring of c subunits and an a subunit that provides two access pathways for protons from opposite sides of the membrane. The F₁ head contains three α and three β subunits arranged around a central γ shaft; the major catalytic sites lie on β subunits. Peripheral stalk components hold the catalytic head relatively stationary while the c ring and central shaft rotate. Without a stator, the head could simply turn with the rotor and relative conformational changes would not be transmitted effectively.

The two proton-access pathways in the a subunit do not form a freely open pore across the membrane. A proton approaching from the P side protonates a conserved acidic residue in a c subunit. Protonation neutralises the charge and favours movement of that site into the hydrophobic membrane environment as the ring rotates. When it reaches the N-side access pathway, deprotonation is favoured and H⁺ is released toward the matrix. Repeated proton uptake and release, coupled to the electrochemical bias, produce net rotation. The details differ among ATP synthases, but the exam-level principle is a conversion of transmembrane ion flow into rotational motion.

Paul Boyer's binding-change model explains how this motion is converted into chemistry. Three catalytic β sites adopt different conformations, conventionally called open (O), loose (L) and tight (T). The loose state can bind ADP and phosphate, the tight state strongly favours the bound ATP state, and the open state permits product release and renewed access. Rotation of the asymmetric γ shaft causes each β site to change conformation. During each approximate 120° step, one site proceeds through each transition; in a full 360° turn each of the three sites completes one catalytic cycle, producing three ATP molecules in the ideal tightly coupled model.

An important refinement is that the energy of proton flow is not simply used to form a phosphoanhydride bond at an otherwise unchanging active site. Binding interactions stabilise ATP inside the catalytic site, and conformational work is required to change ligand affinities, particularly to release tightly bound ATP and reset the catalytic sites. This resolves the apparent paradox that a chemical reaction whose standard free energy is unfavourable can proceed within a molecular machine. Rotation couples the entire cycle, including substrate binding and product release, to an energetically favourable process.

ATP synthase is reversible. If the proton motive force is too low but ATP is available, the enzyme can hydrolyse ATP and rotate in the opposite direction, pumping protons outward. In living mammalian mitochondria regulatory mechanisms, including inhibitory factor IF₁ under certain conditions, can limit wasteful reverse hydrolysis. The direction is determined by the energetic balance between ATP chemical potential and the proton electrochemical difference, not by the name of the protein. Isolated F₁ can hydrolyse ATP without F₀, but that observation alone does not demonstrate normal membrane-coupled ATP synthesis.

12. Making ATP available outside the matrix

Matrix ATP production is useful only if ADP and phosphate reach the catalytic head and ATP reaches the cytosol. The inner membrane that maintains the proton motive force also restricts passive transport of these charged metabolites. The adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT) exchanges matrix ATP for cytosolic ADP. In the conventional charge accounting, ATP⁴⁻ leaves the negative matrix while ADP³⁻ enters. The net movement is one negative charge outward, so a negative matrix potential favours the exchange. This exchange is electrogenic: it consumes part of the electrical component of the gradient even though it does not itself transport a proton.

The phosphate carrier supports entry of inorganic phosphate, commonly represented as H⁺/H₂PO₄⁻ symport or phosphate/OH⁻ exchange. This is an effectively electroneutral process that consumes the pH component of the gradient. In the simplified overall energy budget, supplying phosphate and exporting ATP together cost approximately the equivalent of one inward proton per ATP delivered to the cytosol. The two contributions use different parts of the same electrochemical difference. Counting one full proton for each carrier separately would double-count the usual simplified transport cost.

An ATP/ADP exchange inhibitor such as atractyloside therefore reduces ATP delivery and limits matrix ADP supply even when ATP synthase itself is intact. As matrix ADP is used up, proton return through the synthase slows and respiration becomes restricted by the growing gradient. An uncoupler can relieve that backpressure and stimulate electron transport, but it cannot repair ANT or restore ATP export. This reasoning distinguishes a membrane transport defect from a catalytic defect using the same coupled-system logic.

The mitochondrial carriers also explain why ATP yield in an intact cell is not necessarily identical to yield measured in a membrane vesicle exposed to freely accessible ADP and phosphate. A numerical problem must specify whether it counts ATP synthesised in the matrix, ATP available outside it, or a conventional effective P/O ratio that combines these costs and other losses.

13. P/O ratios and the limits of ATP accounting

The P/O ratio expresses the amount of ATP formed per oxygen atom reduced, equivalently per ½O₂ or per pair of electrons delivered to oxygen. Do not confuse this convention with ATP per O₂ molecule, which is twice as large for the same system. A textbook calculation uses roughly ten translocated protons per matrix NADH and six per succinate-derived electron pair. With an effective expenditure of about four proton equivalents per ATP delivered, the respective yields are 10/4 = 2.5 and 6/4 = 1.5 ATP. Fractional ATP values are average stoichiometric ratios over many turnovers, not the formation of half a molecule in a single chemical event.

The effective four-proton figure is a useful convention, not a universal structural constant. If a rotor contains c proton-binding subunits and each binding site transports one H⁺ per turn, a complete turn translocates c protons and produces three ATP at F₁. The intrinsic H⁺/ATP ratio is c/3. Mammalian mitochondrial ATP synthase has an eight-membered c ring in the commonly described structure. The ideal intrinsic value is 8/3 ≈ 2.67, and adding an approximate one-proton-equivalent transport cost gives 3.67. Idealised predictions are then 10/(11/3) = 30/11 ≈ 2.73 and 6/(11/3) = 18/11 ≈ 1.64. The conventional 2.5 and 1.5 values incorporate a round effective cost and are widely used for metabolic bookkeeping; they need not equal a perfectly coupled structural upper estimate. Other organisms can have different rotor sizes.

Measured yields are affected by proton leak, imperfect coupling, membrane transport and the experimental definition of ATP output. Older textbooks sometimes report three ATP per NADH and two per FAD-linked pair, reflecting earlier conventions about discrete phosphorylation sites. Do not switch between old and modern ratios midway through a calculation. If the question explicitly supplies 3 and 2, use the supplied assumptions and label the result accordingly. In the absence of a specified convention, modern approximate 2.5/1.5 yields are generally appropriate for mammalian metabolic calculations.

For a numerical example, suppose a preparation oxidises 12 μmol matrix NADH and 8 μmol succinate-derived electron pairs, with complete oxygen reduction. The oxygen requirement is (12 + 8)/2 = 10 μmol O₂. Proton translocation is 12 × 10 + 8 × 6 = 168 μmol H⁺. At four proton equivalents per ATP, the predicted output is 42 μmol ATP. The mixed P/O ratio is 42/(2 × 10) = 2.1; alternatively 42/20 gives the same result. Dividing by 10 would give ATP per O₂, not the conventional P/O. If an additional measured leak dissipates 24 μmol proton equivalents before ATP synthesis, the remaining 144 give 36 μmol ATP under the same effective-cost assumption. This simple model holds electron flux fixed; in real mitochondria the flux itself may change as the gradient changes.

At a proton motive force magnitude of 180.75 mV, 2.67 entering protons provide roughly 46.5 kJ mol⁻¹ ATP, before transport costs are considered. Actual phosphorylation free energy depends on the ATP/ADP/Pᵢ activities and can exceed the standard biochemical value of approximately +30.5 kJ mol⁻¹. A synthase cannot maintain an arbitrarily high ATP/ADP ratio at fixed proton motive force. At equilibrium the available free energy from the coupled proton transfers balances the phosphorylation requirement; productive net synthesis requires a favourable total free energy. This is why ATP yield and maximum phosphorylation potential are related but not interchangeable quantities.

14. How cytosolic NADH reaches the respiratory chain

Glycolysis produces NADH in the cytosol when glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate is oxidised. The inner mitochondrial membrane does not allow NADH to freely cross, so a cell cannot simply send all glycolytic NADH to matrix Complex I. Yet the glycolytic enzyme requires NAD⁺ to continue operating. Redox shuttles solve the problem by transferring reducing equivalents between compartments while recycling the cytosolic NAD⁺ pool. The actual NADH molecule on one side need not be the molecule that appears reduced on the other side.

The malate–aspartate shuttle uses a pair of malate dehydrogenases and a pair of aspartate aminotransferases. First, cytosolic oxaloacetate accepts reducing equivalents from NADH to form malate, regenerating NAD⁺. Malate enters the matrix in exchange for α-ketoglutarate. Matrix malate dehydrogenase oxidises malate to oxaloacetate while reducing matrix NAD⁺ to NADH. This matrix NADH can now supply Complex I. The problem is that oxaloacetate cannot simply diffuse back through the inner membrane. Matrix aspartate aminotransferase transfers an amino group from glutamate to oxaloacetate, forming aspartate and α-ketoglutarate.

Aspartate exits through the aspartate–glutamate carrier, coupled to glutamate and proton entry in the usual mammalian shuttle direction. Cytosolic aspartate aminotransferase then converts aspartate and α-ketoglutarate back to oxaloacetate and glutamate. The metabolite cycle is thus closed: malate carries reducing equivalents inward and the coupled amino-acid exchanges restore the starting carbon skeletons. A driving force for the transport and suitable metabolite ratios are needed; the shuttle is not simply a freely reversible NADH pore. Conventional ATP accounting assigns a cytosolic NADH transferred by this route the matrix NADH yield of approximately 2.5 ATP, with the understanding that exact transport energetics and tissue conditions can alter actual yield.

The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle instead uses dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) as the cytosolic electron acceptor. Cytosolic NAD-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reduces DHAP to glycerol-3-phosphate, regenerating NAD⁺. A different FAD-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase on the outer face of the inner mitochondrial membrane oxidises glycerol-3-phosphate back to DHAP. Its enzyme-bound flavin transfers electrons to ubiquinone. DHAP is available to the cytosolic enzyme again and no net glycerol-3-phosphate needs to accumulate. This route bypasses Complex I and has the conventional yield of about 1.5 ATP per cytosolic NADH.

The second shuttle sacrifices some energy-conservation potential because its electrons enter the Q pool rather than the NADH entry site. It may nevertheless support rapid cytosolic redox turnover where it is active. The relative importance of the two shuttles varies with cell type and metabolic state; classical examples associate the malate–aspartate shuttle with liver and heart and glycerol phosphate with certain muscle and neural contexts, but these are not exclusive tissue assignments. Both shuttles allow glycolysis to proceed aerobically without physically transporting NADH across the membrane. If respiratory electron acceptance is limited, reduction of pyruvate to lactate provides a different cytosolic route for NAD⁺ regeneration, but lactate formation itself supplies no oxidative phosphorylation yield.

15. Deriving the aerobic ATP yield from glucose

To connect the respiratory chain to the preceding chapters, start with one glucose molecule and explicitly count where each reducing equivalent arises. Glycolysis produces two net ATP and two cytosolic NADH, as well as two pyruvate molecules. Pyruvate dehydrogenase converts the two pyruvates into two acetyl-CoA molecules and generates two matrix NADH. Two turns of the TCA cycle then generate six matrix NADH, two succinate-derived electron pairs and two GTP or ATP equivalents by substrate-level phosphorylation. The total is eight matrix NADH, two cytosolic NADH, two Q-entry electron pairs from succinate and four substrate-level ATP equivalents.

Using the malate–aspartate shuttle convention, the two cytosolic NADH are counted as two additional matrix NADH equivalents. The oxidative yield is (8 + 2) × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 = 28 ATP; adding four substrate-level equivalents gives 32 ATP per glucose. With the glycerol phosphate shuttle, matrix NADH contributes 8 × 2.5 = 20, and four Q-entry pairs contribute (2 + 2) × 1.5 = 6. The oxidative yield is 26, and the total is 30. These estimates assume complete oxidation, the chosen P/O convention and no separately specified costs or losses. They are not exact yields in every living tissue.

There is an independent check using oxygen. Each glucose completely oxidised supplies twelve electron pairs: ten NADH-equivalent pairs plus two succinate-linked pairs. Each pair reduces half an oxygen molecule, giving six O₂ in the net complete oxidation of glucose. Both shuttles lead to the same oxygen requirement in this ideal full-oxidation calculation; the difference is the number of proton equivalents conserved, not the number of electrons originally extracted from the glucose. A problem in which oxygen consumption is unchanged but ATP yield differs can therefore be explained by a change in electron-entry route without invoking incomplete oxidation.

Why does glycolysis alone yield much less ATP? The substrate-level reactions use only some of the energy available from the glucose, and the carbon in pyruvate or lactate remains partially oxidised. Under oxygen limitation, lactate dehydrogenase reoxidises cytosolic NADH but the electron pair does not enter the conventional respiratory chain. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack mitochondria, so they depend on glycolytic ATP even when oxygen is plentiful. Oxygen availability is necessary for conventional mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation but not sufficient: respiratory machinery, membrane integrity and substrate access must all be present.

16. Respiratory control: why ADP changes oxygen consumption

Mitochondria adjust energy production to energy consumption. When ATP utilisation is low, ADP and phosphate availability may limit ATP synthase flux. Proton pumping initially builds a large gradient, but the rising energetic cost of pumping opposes further electron transfer. Oxygen consumption declines to the rate needed to balance proton leak and any remaining ion transport or ATP turnover. This is respiratory control, also called acceptor control when referring specifically to the dependence on phosphate acceptors.

If ADP is added to isolated mitochondria supplied with respiratory substrate, phosphate and oxygen, ATP synthase can work faster. Protons return to the matrix, the gradient drops somewhat, and the respiratory complexes face less backpressure. Electron flow and oxygen consumption increase. After the finite added ADP has been converted to ATP, the rate falls again. These phases are commonly called state 3 (ADP-stimulated respiration) and state 4 (resting respiration after ADP exhaustion, under the classical definition). State 4 respiration is not zero in normal preparations because the inner membrane has a finite proton leak. Some modern protocols define an oligomycin-induced leak state separately, so inspect the experimental definition before comparing values.

The respiratory control ratio, RCR, is the state 3 rate divided by the state 4 rate measured under comparable conditions. If oxygen consumption increases from 18 to 108 nmol O₂ min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ mitochondrial protein after ADP addition and then returns to 18 after ADP exhaustion, RCR = 108/18 = 6. A high value is generally consistent with good coupling and preserved membrane integrity. However, a low ratio alone is not a unique diagnosis: a damaged membrane may elevate the resting rate, a respiratory defect may depress the stimulated rate, and limited substrate or poor assay conditions may also affect the result. Absolute rates and experimental conditions matter.

Oxygen electrodes or optical oxygen sensors provide a record of oxygen concentration versus time. A steep downward slope indicates a high rate of consumption, not a high remaining oxygen concentration. For a closed chamber, if oxygen concentration drops by 40 μM in two minutes in a 0.5 mL assay, the consumption is 40 nmol mL⁻¹ × 0.5 mL / 2 min = 10 nmol O₂ min⁻¹. Divide by protein mass if a specific rate is requested. Measuring ATP formation, membrane potential and redox state alongside respiration helps distinguish the underlying causes of a change. This is more informative than treating oxygen consumption as a direct ATP meter.

17. Inhibitors, uncouplers and diagnostic reasoning

A respiratory-chain inhibitor interrupts electron transfer at a particular component. At a strong block, carriers upstream of the block tend to become reduced and carriers downstream tend to become oxidised, provided alternative electron inputs and oxygen sinks are absent. A Complex I inhibitor prevents NADH-derived electron flow but succinate can supply Q through Complex II. A Complex III inhibitor blocks both entry routes. A Complex IV inhibitor prevents the final reduction of oxygen, so all conventional upstream entry routes are affected. These predictions refer to steady behaviour after the perturbation; the existing pools and gradient may persist briefly.

Oligomycin inhibits proton translocation through F₀ of ATP synthase. ATP synthesis stops or falls markedly, and because proton return is restricted the gradient initially increases. The high proton motive force slows the respiratory chain even though oligomycin is not a direct inhibitor of Complex I, III or IV. If an uncoupler is then added, protons can bypass the blocked synthase. Backpressure decreases and oxygen consumption can resume or increase, but ATP synthesis remains inhibited. This is a particularly useful discriminating experiment: restoration of electron flow without restoration of ATP shows that the original suppression of respiration was secondary to gradient accumulation.

Protonophores such as 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) or FCCP dissipate the gradient by facilitating proton movement through the membrane. In a substrate- and oxygen-replete preparation, partial to effective uncoupling tends to stimulate electron transport because the pumps face less resistance. ATP produced per oxygen consumed decreases, and more energy is dissipated as heat. Very high uncoupler concentrations, depleted substrate or damage can instead lower respiratory rate. Therefore the statement that uncouplers always increase oxygen consumption is too absolute. A properly interpreted examination question usually specifies a functioning preparation, adequate substrates and a suitable uncoupler concentration.

Ionophores illustrate the distinction between the electrical and chemical parts of the proton motive force. Valinomycin facilitates K⁺ movement; in a medium allowing inward K⁺ movement it tends to dissipate the negative-inside potential. Nigericin exchanges K⁺ and H⁺ electroneutrally and can diminish the pH gradient under suitable conditions. Compensation between Δψ and ΔpH may occur, so eliminating one component is not necessarily equivalent to eliminating the total driving force. These compounds are experimental probes of membrane energetics; the exact result depends on ion concentrations and the available transport processes.

A physiological form of uncoupling occurs in brown adipose tissue, where uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) provides a regulated proton conductance. The resulting oxidation of fuel generates heat rather than maximising ATP yield. This is an adaptation, not a failure of all respiratory complexes. Normal mitochondrial proton leak also contributes to basal energy expenditure, whereas excessive leak may compromise ATP supply. The biological interpretation should always distinguish useful regulated thermogenesis from loss of membrane integrity.

18. Reading combined oxygen, ATP and redox measurements

Consider the illustrative experiment below. Each treatment is applied to a separate aliquot of coupled mitochondria with adequate oxygen, phosphate and substrates that generate matrix NADH. Rates are normalised to the same amount of protein. In the untreated ADP-stimulated sample, oxygen consumption is 40 nmol O₂ per minute and ATP formation is 200 nmol per minute. The P/O ratio is 200/(2 × 40) = 2.5. Adding oligomycin lowers respiration to 8 and ATP synthesis to zero while the potential rises. This is not the signature of a protonophore. The synthase is blocked and the respiratory pumps are opposed by a larger gradient.

An FCCP-treated aliquot consumes 64 nmol O₂ per minute and produces only 16 nmol ATP per minute, giving P/O = 16/(2 × 64) = 0.125. The high respiratory rate is not evidence of efficient ATP production; it is consistent with substantial uncoupling. A preparation exposed to both oligomycin and FCCP consumes oxygen rapidly but does not make detectable oxidative ATP. If oxygen consumption remains very low even after adding an uncoupler, the next question is whether electron transport is directly limited or whether the preparation lacks substrate, oxygen or intact catalytic machinery.

In the final aliquot, rotenone reduces the NADH-linked rate almost to zero. Supplying succinate restores a rate of 35 nmol O₂ per minute and ATP production of 105 nmol per minute, giving a P/O of 1.5. Succinate has bypassed Complex I, not reversed the binding of rotenone. The downstream segment remains functional. If antimycin A were also present, succinate would no longer restore the ordinary chain's oxygen consumption. This series of perturbations can distinguish a site-specific block from an inhibition caused indirectly by a high gradient.

One must also interpret the redox signal correctly. NADH absorbs strongly near 340 nm and is fluorescent under suitable optical conditions; NAD⁺ contributes much less to that signal. If a purified NADH-linked preparation shows increasing reduced NADH signal after a terminal-chain block, the usual interpretation is accumulation of NADH because oxidation slows, not necessarily stimulation of the dehydrogenases. In intact cells NADPH can contribute to autofluorescence and additional pathways can change the signal, so it is not an exclusive measure of mitochondrial NADH unless the assay provides that specificity.

19. Reactive oxygen, alternative pathways and biological limitations

The intended terminal product of aerobic electron transport is water. However, some electrons can be transferred prematurely to oxygen, forming superoxide (O₂•⁻). Complex I and the quinone chemistry of Complex III are important sites under many mitochondrial conditions. Superoxide dismutases convert superoxide to hydrogen peroxide, which is removed by antioxidant systems such as glutathione peroxidases and peroxiredoxins. At controlled levels reactive oxygen species participate in signalling; excessive production can damage proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. The mere presence of electron leakage does not mean the entire respiratory chain is defective.

The risk of leakage depends on the redox state of the carriers, oxygen availability and membrane energetics. A highly reduced Q pool and high gradient can favour reverse electron transfer at Complex I under appropriate conditions. Blocking a complex can change the local redox state and sometimes increase reactive oxygen formation even when overall oxygen consumption decreases. Consequently, oxygen flux, ATP output and reactive oxygen production are three different measurements. It is unsafe scientifically to infer one exactly from another without information about the experimental system.

Not all organisms use the precise mammalian four-complex architecture. Many bacteria operate respiratory chains in the plasma membrane and can employ alternative terminal acceptors under anaerobic conditions. ATP synthesis driven by ion gradients can therefore occur without oxygen in organisms possessing suitable respiratory pathways. Some microbes even couple ATP synthesis to sodium gradients. Plant mitochondria possess alternative electron routes including alternative oxidase, which changes proton conservation; their detailed physiological regulation is studied with plant respiration. These examples clarify the common principle of electrochemical coupling while preserving the mammalian model for the quantitative problems in this chapter.

Finally, ATP supply depends on the integrated metabolic network. A cell with normal Complex IV but defective pyruvate transport may have poor respiration on glucose-derived pyruvate, yet retain respiration on other fuels. A cell with a very leaky inner membrane may oxidise fuel rapidly but fail to maintain phosphorylation potential. A cell lacking oxygen can regenerate some cytosolic NAD⁺ by fermentation, but mitochondrial NADH recycling becomes constrained. Distinguishing these situations requires identifying the missing input or coupling step rather than assuming every energy failure is an ATP synthase defect. This integrated perspective prepares the study of glucose homeostasis and alternative carbohydrate pathways: when ATP or redox conditions change, the choice of metabolic pathway changes with them.

20. Part B: original conceptual and assertion–reason questions

1. Which complex links the TCA cycle directly to the Q pool?

A. I · B. II · C. III · D. IV

Answer and reasoning

II. Succinate dehydrogenase catalyses succinate oxidation and transfers electrons to Q. I uses NADH, III oxidises QH₂, and IV reduces oxygen.

2. Which carrier normally transfers one electron per encounter?

A. NADH · B. QH₂ · C. cytochrome c · D. ATP

Answer and reasoning

Cytochrome c. Its haem iron changes between Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺. NADH and fully reduced Q carry pairs; ATP is not a redox carrier.

3. Which process directly consumes oxygen in the conventional chain?

A. NADH binding to I · B. succinate oxidation · C. Q cycle · D. oxygen reduction at IV

Answer and reasoning

Complex IV. The upstream processes pass electrons, whereas oxygen is the terminal acceptor at the oxidase.

4. Which statement describes F₁?

A. Pumps protons by itself · B. catalytic head of ATP synthase · C. reduces Q · D. oxidises oxygen

Answer and reasoning

The catalytic head. F₀ provides membrane coupling; Q reduction and oxygen reduction are functions of respiratory enzymes.

5. The Q cycle consumes one QH₂ net per two cytochrome c reduced. Why?

A. One QH₂ forms ATP · B. one of two oxidised QH₂ is regenerated · C. cytochrome c carries two electrons · D. Q never accepts protons

Answer and reasoning

Two QH₂ oxidations at Qₒ are balanced by regeneration of one QH₂ at Qᵢ. Cytochrome c accepts one electron and Q does bind protons.

6. Which directly dissipates the proton gradient?

A. Rotenone · B. malonate · C. oligomycin · D. FCCP

Answer and reasoning

FCCP. Rotenone and malonate restrict electron entry; oligomycin restricts proton return and initially raises the gradient.

7. Assertion: succinate-linked ATP yield is below NADH-linked yield. Reason: Complex II bypasses the proton translocation of I.

A. Both true, reason explains · B. both true, reason unrelated · C. assertion only · D. reason only

Answer and reasoning

Both true and the reason explains. Six rather than ten effective protons are conserved per electron pair.

8. Assertion: after oligomycin, oxygen consumption can decline. Reason: oligomycin directly inhibits cytochrome c oxidase.

A. Both true, reason explains · B. both true, reason unrelated · C. assertion only · D. reason only

Answer and reasoning

Assertion true, reason false. The decline arises because F₀ blockade increases backpressure.

9. Assertion: cytosolic NADH does not need to cross the inner membrane as NADH. Reason: redox shuttles transfer reducing equivalents through metabolic reactions.

A. Both true, reason explains · B. both true, reason unrelated · C. assertion only · D. reason only

Answer and reasoning

Both true and explanatory. Malate or glycerol phosphate transfers the reducing equivalents; NAD pools remain compartmentalised.

10. Which ratio is the conventional P/O?

A. ATP per O₂ molecule · B. ATP per ½O₂ · C. H⁺ per ATP · D. oxygen per NADH

Answer and reasoning

ATP per oxygen atom reduced, i.e. per ½O₂. The first is twice this ratio; the others quantify different coupling properties.

21. Part C: original quantitative and analytical problems

1. A mitochondrion oxidises 6 μmol NADH and 4 μmol succinate. Calculate proton equivalents, oxygen use and ATP output at four H⁺ equivalents/ATP.

Answer and reasoning

60 + 24 = 84 μmol H⁺. Ten electron pairs reduce 5 μmol O₂. ATP = 84/4 = 21 μmol; mixed P/O = 21/10 = 2.1. Counting all ten pairs as NADH would incorrectly give 25 ATP.

2. At 310 K, Δψ = −120 mV and ΔpH = +0.8 (matrix minus P side). Find signed Δp and inward ΔG per mol H⁺.

Answer and reasoning

Δp = −120 − 61.5×0.8 = −169.2 mV. ΔG = 96.485×(−0.1692) = −16.33 kJ/mol. Both components favour inward flow.

3. A matrix is −160 mV but its pH is 0.3 units below the P side. Find Δp at 310 K.

Answer and reasoning

ΔpH = −0.3. Δp = −160 + 18.45 = −141.55 mV. The chemical gradient opposes the electrical one but inward flow is still favourable.

4. A rotor has 12 c subunits. Assume three ATP per turn and an effective transport cost of one H⁺ equivalent per ATP. Estimate P/O for NADH and Q entry.

Answer and reasoning

Intrinsic cost = 12/3 = 4; effective cost = 5. NADH: 10/5 = 2; Q entry: 6/5 = 1.2. Rotor size matters; 2.5 and 1.5 are not universal.

5. A sample consumes 15 nmol O₂/min and produces 45 nmol ATP/min. Is the yield consistent with conventional succinate-only coupling?

Answer and reasoning

P/O = 45/(2×15) = 1.5, consistent with the convention. It does not prove succinate is the only substrate: mixtures with losses may give the same ratio.

6. ADP-stimulated and resting rates are 90 and 15. After damage they are 90 and 45. Interpret both RCRs.

Answer and reasoning

Before: 6; after: 2. The capacity in state 3 is unchanged but the resting rate is higher, consistent with increased leak. Other resting ion cycles or ATP turnover must also be considered.

7. Rotenone blocks respiration on pyruvate/malate. Succinate restores it, then antimycin abolishes it. Which segment is rescued?

Answer and reasoning

Succinate supplies Q via II and bypasses I. III remains necessary and antimycin interrupts it. Neither treatment repairs the I block; a terminal IV block would not be bypassed by succinate.

8. In a preparation with oligomycin, addition of FCCP raises oxygen consumption but ATP remains near zero. Which proposed mechanism is supported?

Answer and reasoning

Backpressure caused the respiratory slowing: FCCP dissipates the gradient while F₀ remains blocked. Direct inhibition of IV would not be relieved by a protonophore.

9. Two QH₂ molecules react at Qₒ during a full Q cycle. Track electrons, net QH₂ and protons.

Answer and reasoning

Four electrons leave the two QH₂. Two reduce two cytochrome c; two reduce Q at Qᵢ. One QH₂ is regenerated with two N-side protons. Four P-side protons are released and one QH₂ is consumed net.

10. For three molecules of O₂ reduced at IV, how many electrons and pumped versus chemical protons are required?

Answer and reasoning

3×4 = 12 electrons. IV pumps 12 H⁺ and consumes 12 chemical H⁺ to form six H₂O. Total matrix H⁺ removed is 24, but only 12 are pumped.

11. One glucose is oxidised with one glycolytic NADH using each shuttle. Calculate conventional total ATP.

Answer and reasoning

Eight matrix NADH plus one via malate shuttle give 9×2.5 = 22.5. Three Q-entry pairs (two from TCA plus one glycerol shuttle) give 4.5. Add four substrate-level ATP: 31 total.

12. A preparation consumes 0.20 μmol O₂ while producing 0.8 μmol ATP. How does this compare with ideal NADH coupling?

Answer and reasoning

P/O = 0.8/(2×0.20) = 2.0. It is below 2.5. A mixture of entry routes, proton leak or other losses could explain it; the measurement alone cannot select one cause.

13. Vesicles contain only a proton pump and correctly oriented ATP synthase, plus ADP and phosphate. What controls establish gradient coupling?

Answer and reasoning

Compare active pump versus inactive pump, intact versus leaky membrane, and complete versus synthase-free vesicles. ATP synthesis should require a maintained gradient and synthase. The controls exclude direct chemical energy transfer by the pump.

14. If actual ATP synthesis requires +52 kJ/mol and inward proton ΔG is −16 kJ/mol, can exactly three H⁺ drive synthesis under those stated conditions?

Answer and reasoning

Three provide −48 kJ/mol; net is +4, so no forward net synthesis under those assumptions. Four provide −64; net −12. A real synthase has fixed coupling stoichiometry and cannot freely choose an integer each ATP.

15. A cell lacks matrix malate dehydrogenase activity but retains glycerol phosphate shuttle and normal ETC. Predict the likely effect on cytosolic NADH oxidation and yield.

Answer and reasoning

The malate–aspartate route is impaired; the alternative route can regenerate NAD⁺ if sufficient capacity exists. Electron entry shifts toward Q, lowering conventional ATP yield per transferred NADH. Complete cytosolic NADH accumulation is not inevitable.

22. Consolidation and the next metabolic question

Reconstruct the chapter as a causal chain rather than a list of complexes. Fuel oxidation generates NADH and other reducing equivalents. Their electrons pass to Q, cytochrome c and oxygen; three respiratory complexes conserve some of the energy as a transmembrane proton motive force. The gradient combines electrical and chemical potential differences. ATP synthase turns favourable proton entry into conformational cycles that synthesise and release ATP. Metabolite carriers exchange ATP, ADP and phosphate, while redox shuttles transfer cytosolic reducing equivalents without moving NADH itself across the inner membrane.

For examination problems, establish the electron-entry point, the unit of oxygen, the direction of the proton gradient, and the assumed energetic cost before calculating anything. Then ask whether the perturbation blocks electron flow, blocks ATP synthase, changes metabolite access or increases proton leak. Oxygen consumption and ATP production need not change in parallel. The subsequent chapter applies this energy and redox framework to gluconeogenesis, glycogen turnover and alternative carbohydrate pathways, where ATP, NADH and NADPH have distinct metabolic roles.
