Why molecular separation matters

A preparation of biological material is rarely a single chemical species. Even a sample that looks completely transparent may contain several thousand proteins, a range of nucleic acids and small dissolved molecules. The question facing a biologist is not simply whether biological material is present, but whether a particular class of molecules can be distinguished from others. A mass measurement alone cannot answer that question. The same total mass of nucleic acid could represent intact chromosomes, small fragments or a mixture of both. Electrophoresis gives access to a physical distribution rather than a single aggregate measurement. The distribution is valuable because the positions of its components retain information about molecular properties.

Imagine two plant extracts from leaves of the same species. An enzyme assay gives the same activity per millilitre in both extracts. This equality is consistent with identical enzymes at identical concentrations, but it is also consistent with a difference in enzyme abundance compensated by a difference in specific activity. It could even conceal contributions from two different isoenzymes. If the extracts are separated while the enzymes retain activity, several active zones may appear. A subsequent separation under denaturing conditions may reveal the sizes of the component polypeptides. The two experiments answer different questions and are therefore complementary rather than competing procedures.

The term electrophoresis describes the movement of charged particles relative to a medium under the influence of an electric field. The medium can be an aqueous solution, a supported buffer, a porous gel or a capillary filled with an appropriate separation matrix. The driving force depends on effective electrical charge. The opposing forces arise from interactions with the surrounding solvent and, when present, the supporting matrix. As a result, migration is governed by a combination of charge, hydrodynamic properties, size, shape and experimental conditions. The importance of each factor depends on the chosen method.

The comparison with chromatography is instructive. Both techniques separate a mixture into observable components, but chromatography normally relies on unequal distribution between mobile and stationary phases, whereas electrophoresis requires an electrical field and different net rates of movement. A chromatographic peak represents an elution event. An electrophoretic band represents a spatially concentrated population, while a capillary electropherogram records a time-dependent signal. In each case, an observable feature represents a measurement, not an automatic chemical identification. A standard gives context to a measurement; an independent recognition method can test the identity of a feature.

Historical progress in electrophoresis illustrates why method design determines the kinds of questions that become answerable. Early solution separations established that different proteins could migrate at different rates. A supporting medium improved control over diffusion and convection. Porous matrices introduced a size-dependent obstacle, making separation based on molecular dimensions more useful. Introducing denaturing detergents reduced the variability caused by protein conformation and native charge. Combining distinct separation principles in two directions subsequently allowed many more molecular components to be distinguished. These developments were not merely improvements in appearance: they altered which property could be inferred from the final pattern.

A useful separation has three parts: discrimination, detection and interpretation. Discrimination is the physical sorting of molecules. Detection converts their presence into a visible or electronic signal. Interpretation uses standards, control samples and assumptions to relate the signal to molecular properties. A failure in any of these parts changes what can be concluded. For example, an invisible target might have been successfully separated but poorly detected. Conversely, a bright band might contain several unresolved species. An excellent experimental answer identifies the specific step that a control addresses rather than treating any control as proof of the entire experiment.

In this chapter the central example is the analysis of a benign mixture containing linear DNA fragments, plant proteins and a native enzyme complex. These molecules illustrate different relationships between chemical composition and observed migration. The DNA has many phosphate groups that are negatively charged at ordinary electrophoretic pH. The native proteins have variable charges that depend on their amino acid composition and solution pH. The enzyme complex contains more than one chain and may dissociate when exposed to denaturing conditions. Their different behaviours explain why no single electrophoresis method is optimal for every analytical objective.

Electric field, charge and mobility

A potential difference is the work per unit charge required to move a positive test charge between two points. In an ideal arrangement of electrodes separated by a distance L, a potential difference of magnitude V produces a field magnitude E approximately equal to V/L when the field is uniform. A voltage alone is therefore not sufficient to compare two arrangements of different dimensions. A hundred volts across ten centimetres produces a different field from a hundred volts across twenty centimetres. In a practical instrument, reservoirs, gels and other resistive regions may divide the total potential drop, so the simple expression requires a suitable approximation of the effective separation region.

By convention, the electric field points in the direction a positive test charge would move. In an electrophoresis cell the anode is positive and the cathode is negative. The force on a charged particle is qE in vector notation. The sign of charge reverses the direction of force for a negative species. A polyanion such as DNA thus travels electrophoretically toward the anode, unless bulk movement of the fluid is sufficiently large to change its observed direction. A protein that has positive net charge moves electrophoretically toward the cathode. This distinction between intrinsic motion and bulk motion becomes important in capillary methods.

The molecular motion is opposed by frictional drag. Consider a simple free-solution model in which drag is proportional to velocity, with frictional coefficient f. At steady migration, the electrical driving force is balanced by the drag force. The equation qE = fv then gives a signed velocity v = qE/f in one dimension with the positive axis defined along the field. Electrophoretic mobility, defined as μ = v/E, is therefore q/f in this ideal model. Mobility has SI units of square metres per volt per second. The useful feature of the derivation is that it separates the origin of motion, electrical charge, from resistance to motion, friction.

For an isolated rigid spherical particle of hydrodynamic radius r in a dilute Newtonian liquid of viscosity η, Stokes drag gives f = 6πηr under low Reynolds number, no-slip conditions. Substitution into the ideal force balance predicts μ = q/(6πηr). Increasing radius at constant effective charge decreases mobility magnitude. Increasing effective charge at constant hydrodynamic radius increases mobility magnitude. Increasing viscosity slows migration. Proteins, nucleic acids and associated counterions are not isolated ideal spheres; the equation is a useful directional model, not a general method of calculating their exact gel migration. In particular, ionic shielding and electrohydrodynamic interactions make effective charge different from merely counting formal charges in a chemical formula.

Charge is the sum of positive and negative contributions, but proteins are not permanently cationic or anionic. The amino terminus, carboxyl terminus and ionisable side chains contribute to the overall charge. Protonation depends on pH and the relevant acid dissociation constants. Lowering pH tends to favour protonated groups; raising pH tends to favour deprotonation. The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which the molecule has zero average net charge under specified conditions. A protein can contain many charged groups at its pI. It is their net sum that is approximately zero, not the number of charges that disappears.

For a protein at pH below its pI, the net charge is generally positive. For pH above pI, net charge is generally negative. The rule predicts direction under the field but not the magnitude of movement, because the slope of the charge versus pH relation differs between proteins. An acidic protein of pI 5 and a basic protein of pI 9 placed in a buffer of pH 7 are expected to migrate toward opposite electrodes in free solution, provided other forces do not dominate. A protein with pI near 7 may travel very little. In native electrophoresis the direction of loading and the chosen buffer pH must therefore agree with the properties of the sample.

The phosphate backbone of DNA has a different relationship to pH. At the usual pH range of DNA separation, most backbone phosphates contribute negative charges. Longer linear double-stranded DNA contains more of these groups, so its total charge is greater in magnitude. Yet it also experiences greater drag and obstruction. A superficial argument based on charge alone would predict that the longest fragment moves fastest. The actual behaviour in a suitable gel is usually the reverse. Free-solution mobility of long DNA molecules can be much less dependent on chain length than gel mobility because electrical driving and friction both grow with length. A matrix introduces the necessary discrimination.

The distance travelled during an ideal constant-velocity migration is d = vt = μEt, where t is elapsed time. Suppose a molecule has a mobility magnitude of 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ in a particular medium, a field magnitude of 500 V m⁻¹ and a migration time of 600 s. The predicted displacement magnitude is 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ × 500 × 600 = 0.006 m, or 6 mm. This example assumes constant field, mobility and temperature and no bulk solvent flow. A real gel may depart from linear time dependence if sample heating, ionic gradients, matrix changes or interactions alter mobility. The numerical result is a teaching calculation, not a published measurement.

The voltage affects more than migration velocity. The current depends on the conductivity and geometry of the system. Dissipation of electrical energy produces heat, with total electrical power P = VI for a region carrying current I under voltage V. Locally, in an ohmic medium of conductivity σ, power density is σE². Doubling the field at constant conductivity increases local power density fourfold. Temperature changes can change viscosity, diffusion and matrix properties, causing uneven migration or distorted bands. Under constant voltage, a rise in conductivity can increase current and heat production. Under constant current, the voltage adjusts with resistance, giving a different response. Therefore faster electrical driving does not guarantee improved separation.

A buffer has two connected roles. Its ions carry current and its acid–base components resist pH changes. Electrochemical reactions at electrodes can change local pH; a sufficient buffer capacity helps maintain the conditions required for the molecules to migrate reproducibly. The buffer ionic strength influences screening, conductivity and heat production. An excessively conductive sample loaded into a differently conductive gel may create local field distortions. The same nominal voltage can yield different band patterns when conductivity differs. Matching samples and standards appropriately is therefore a matter of physical comparability as well as experimental convenience.

Diffusion acts even in the absence of an electric field. In one dimension, the variance of a freely diffusing narrow zone increases by approximately 2Dt, where D is its diffusion coefficient. For a starting variance σ₀², the variance becomes σ₀² + 2Dt under a simple diffusion-only model. Two species can have distinct mean positions yet remain difficult to distinguish when their widths are large. Increasing migration time can increase the difference between mean positions, but also gives diffusion more time. Initial sample width, overloading and nonuniform fields add further broadening. A good separation balances displacement differences against spreading rather than maximising one property without considering the other.

Agarose gel electrophoresis and DNA size separation

A gel is a hydrated network of polymer chains with spaces filled by liquid. It is not a solid sheet drilled with identical holes. The spaces vary in dimension and connectivity, and the moving macromolecule encounters a changing path through them. Agarose is a polysaccharide derived from agar, forming a network through noncovalent associations upon cooling from solution. Its relatively large effective pores make it particularly useful for separating many linear DNA fragments. A higher agarose concentration generally produces a tighter network. This reduces the mobility of a fragment of a given size and can improve discrimination between smaller fragments, within a useful range.

For a relatively small macromolecule in a porous network, the probability of accessing available spaces decreases with increasing effective dimensions. For sufficiently long DNA, the molecule behaves as a flexible polymer and can move through the network in a snake-like manner, often described by reptation-based models. Different regimes of size, field and pore structure produce different mobility relationships. There is no universally valid equation in which migration distance is simply inversely proportional to the number of base pairs. The empirical calibration of a specific gel is therefore central to interpreting a pattern.

Ordinary slab agarose electrophoresis compares the displacement of many DNA populations that start in separate wells. The field moves the negatively charged DNA towards the anode. The concentration of molecules that share similar migration properties appears as a band after detection. A sample containing two distinctly sized fragments can yield two bands if the difference in displacement is sufficiently large compared with their widths. A smear may indicate a broad size distribution, such as fragmented DNA, but the same visual effect can also result from sample overload, salt-related distortion or other technical problems. A smear is an observation, not a unique explanation.

The agarose concentration must be appropriate for the expected size range. In an overly open gel, small fragments may move too rapidly and with too little differentiation, or leave the useful separation region. In an overly restrictive gel, large fragments may move only a short distance or remain near the well. An investigator chooses the medium by considering the size interval that must be resolved, not solely by the maximum migration speed. Different types of agarose also vary in properties; a nominal polymer concentration does not guarantee the same separation behaviour between different formulations.

A DNA ladder is a reference mixture containing fragments with known lengths. It is useful because the mobility of the unknown can be compared against the mobility of chemically similar standards under the same gel conditions. Consider a linear DNA fragment that lies between the bands corresponding to 1,000 and 2,000 base pairs. It is tempting to estimate size by taking the simple midpoint, 1,500 base pairs. Within a suitable interval, however, a plot of logarithm of fragment length against migration distance is often closer to linear than a plot of length itself. A fragment halfway between those two positions would then be estimated at their geometric mean, approximately 1,414 base pairs. The correct interpolation depends on the calibration model and the quality of the fit.

To make the reasoning concrete, consider a hypothetical marker with bands at 20, 40 and 60 mm corresponding to 4,000, 2,000 and 1,000 bp respectively. Each additional 20 mm corresponds to a decrease by a factor of two. A band at 50 mm is halfway between the latter two standards. Its estimated logarithmic length is (log₁₀ 2000 + log₁₀ 1000)/2, and the antilogarithm is about 1,414 bp. A band at 90 mm would lie outside the calibrated interval; extrapolating the same slope to such a position can produce a misleading value. In the absence of additional standards, the correct report may be simply smaller than the smallest reliable marker.

Molecular length can be expressed in base pairs for double-stranded DNA or nucleotides for a single strand. Mass and length are related through base composition, with approximate average mass per base pair often taken as about 660 daltons for double-stranded DNA in textbook calculations. Thus a 1,000 bp duplex is roughly 660,000 Da, or 660 kDa. This approximate conversion is not necessary for reading a standard DNA ladder labelled in base pairs. Confusing DNA lengths in bp with protein molecular masses in kDa is a common error, especially when both separations use visually similar gels. The molecular class and calibration axis must be stated.

Staining provides a signal from the DNA population. A signal that increases with DNA mass within a validated range permits approximate relative quantification. But different stains have different response properties and sequence or conformation can affect their binding. An image detector may saturate at high intensity. Even with a linear signal, equal mass does not mean equal number of molecules when fragment lengths differ. For instance, two bands of a 500 bp and a 2,000 bp fragment with equal DNA mass correspond approximately to a fourfold difference in molecule number. The smaller fragments are more numerous because each has one-quarter of the mass.

In a hypothetical restriction analysis of a purified circular DNA of 5,000 bp, two complete cleavage products of 2,000 and 3,000 bp would together account for the entire molecule. Under a stain response proportional to DNA mass, the 3,000 bp band should contain approximately 1.5 times the signal of the 2,000 bp band if each fragment occurs once per original molecule. If the 3,000 bp signal is far weaker, possible explanations include unequal recovery, inaccurate calibration, imaging outside the linear range or an incorrect assumption about the fragment population. The sum of fragment sizes is a mass-balance test, but equal sums alone do not establish where on a circular molecule the cleavage sites lie.

The observable length of a DNA molecule must not be confused with its conformation. A covalently closed circular plasmid can adopt a supercoiled state. A single-strand nick relaxes the constraint and often produces an open circular molecule. Cutting both strands at one site produces a linear molecule. The three forms can contain the same nucleotide sequence and number of base pairs yet migrate differently. Frequently a compact supercoiled form moves faster than the corresponding linear form, and the relaxed open circle moves more slowly. This ordering can change with matrix conditions, molecular length, field and intercalating compounds. A linear ladder is appropriate for sizing a linear product, not for obtaining an exact length by interpreting a circular band as if it were linear.

The classic separation of very large DNA requires attention to how polymer orientation affects movement. When sufficiently large molecules are poorly resolved by a static field, switching the direction of the field can exploit differences in reorientation time. This is the conceptual basis of pulsed-field gel electrophoresis. Long molecules generally take longer to reorient than shorter molecules. It is not necessary for an examination student to memorise specialised switching programs. The important reasoning is that when ordinary length-dependent mobility becomes inadequate, a new size-dependent process can be incorporated into the measurement. Pulsed-field patterns are still migration evidence; an organism or chromosome cannot be identified merely by a band size without adequate context.

The modern use of agarose for DNA separation became established as improvements to gel materials and fragment preparation made reproducible sizing practical. Nancy Stellwagen's review of DNA electrophoresis discusses the physical relationship between free-solution and matrix-supported movement. Lee and colleagues' account of agarose gel electrophoresis emphasises the influence of fragment size, gel composition, conformation, field and buffer. The key inference from these sources is that size interpretation requires controlled conditions and a physically appropriate reference. These principles apply whether an investigator is checking DNA integrity, comparing marker alleles or assessing the products of an analytical reaction.

RNA, denaturing nucleic-acid gels and integrity

RNA presents a complication that is easy to miss when extending DNA gel logic. Many RNA molecules are single stranded, but a single strand is not necessarily an extended unstructured polymer. Complementary regions can pair within the same molecule to form stems, hairpins and other secondary structures. Two RNA molecules of equal nucleotide length can fold differently, producing distinct hydrodynamic and matrix interactions. Consequently, an ordinary native separation can distinguish conformational forms even when the primary chain lengths match.

Denaturing electrophoresis addresses this issue by reducing intramolecular base pairing so that chain length has a more predictable influence on mobility. Appropriate denaturing conditions must persist sufficiently throughout the separation; simply unfolding an RNA before placing it in a permissive environment can allow some secondary structures to reform. For small RNA populations, a denaturing polyacrylamide matrix offers fine size discrimination. For larger RNA, denaturing agarose formats may be more suitable. The student's task is to connect the choice of matrix with the size interval and the use of denaturing conditions with the need to reduce conformation-dependent migration.

RNA integrity measurements exploit the fact that cleavage creates a distribution of shorter molecules. A preparation of total RNA may show distinct prominent ribosomal RNA components against a background of other RNA species. For many eukaryotic total RNA samples, intact major large and small ribosomal RNAs give characteristic bands. However, absolute ratios and patterns vary with organism, sample type, extraction method and the analytical system. Plant samples may contain additional organellar ribosomal RNAs. Therefore a single visual rule about the relative brightness of two bands should not be treated as a universal diagnostic threshold.

Suppose two plant RNA preparations have equal absorbance-based concentrations. The first shows defined ribosomal bands and the second a broad distribution towards shorter sizes. The second pattern is consistent with greater degradation, but the observation does not alone prove when or how it occurred. If independent samples prepared at the same time show the same difference, a biological or shared handling factor becomes more plausible. If only one lane is distorted and the internal standard also migrates abnormally, a sample-specific analytical artefact deserves consideration. Comparing electrophoretic integrity with independent concentration measurement is useful because concentration and intact chain length are separate properties.

One-dimensional migration cannot generally identify a particular RNA transcript in total RNA. A transcript may be below the detection limit of a total nucleic-acid stain or comigrate with many other RNAs. Sequence-specific hybridisation provides a separate recognition step and can link the signal to a particular RNA population. A northern blot, for example, adds sequence recognition to size separation. A signal at the expected length supports a transcript-size hypothesis, but interpretation must still consider probe specificity and related transcripts. Detection of a transcript is not itself evidence that the corresponding protein is functional. Distinguishing these levels is a recurring requirement in molecular examination questions.

Two-dimensional nucleic-acid gel methods use a different second separation condition rather than simply repeating an identical run. If the first condition separates by an overall length-dependent property and the second changes the influence of conformation or branching, molecules with similar first-dimensional positions can separate in the second. Replication intermediates, for instance, have structural features different from simple linear molecules of comparable DNA mass. At NET/SET level the important lesson is that the classical two-dimensional protein strategy is not the only possible implementation of two-dimensional electrophoresis. The axes depend on the experimental design and must be read from the described method, not automatically assigned pI and protein mass.

A native RNA experiment, in contrast, may be designed precisely to preserve differences in folding or interaction. If a molecule changes conformation after ligand binding, an altered migration pattern can support formation of an altered physical state. The evidence is conditional: an unchanged band does not exclude binding if the complex dissociates during the run or the mobility difference is too small; a shifted band does not prove a specific interaction unless specificity and other explanations are evaluated. This distinction between revealing native state and suppressing native structure also governs the choice between native and denaturing protein separation.

An instructive form of two-dimensional DNA analysis concerns replication intermediates. A fully replicated or unreplicated linear reference population forms a size-dependent trajectory when separated under two defined conditions. Branched molecules of related DNA content can occupy different positions because their shape changes their retardation, especially in a second dimension chosen to make shape-dependent effects more prominent. A collection of related intermediates can appear as an arc rather than a single spot because the molecules represent a continuum of partially replicated states. The existence of an arc is therefore compatible with a distribution of replication structures rather than simply a collection of unrelated linear fragments. It is not an image of the replication fork itself. Sequence-specific recognition of a defined chromosomal region and comparison with expected patterns are necessary before attributing an observed trajectory to replication of that region. The molecular mechanism of replication belongs to Unit 3; the measurement principle here is the use of changes in discrimination between two separation dimensions. Published discussions of two-dimensional agarose analysis of replication intermediates illustrate this distinction.

An analogous conceptual strategy for RNA is to compare a native separation with a second condition that suppresses RNA secondary structure. A compact conformer and a less compact conformer of the same RNA length might differ in native mobility yet migrate similarly in a length-sensitive denaturing dimension. Two RNAs of different lengths that happen to have the same native mobility might instead diverge in the second dimension. Neither pattern alone proves sequence identity, and a changed pattern can also result from disruption of an intermolecular complex. The interpretation must explicitly state which structures are preserved and which are disrupted in each dimension. This is why the description of a two-dimensional nucleic-acid experiment must not be replaced by the pI-versus-mass axes appropriate to classical two-dimensional protein analysis.

Polyacrylamide gels and the molecular sieve

Polyacrylamide is formed through covalent polymerisation of acrylamide with an appropriate crosslinker, commonly bisacrylamide. The crosslinks connect polymer chains into a hydrated network that can provide finer pore control than many ordinary agarose formulations. Its small effective pores make it useful for resolving proteins and relatively short nucleic acids. Unpolymerised acrylamide is hazardous; the principles discussed here concern analytical interpretation rather than laboratory handling instructions. The important chemical distinction from agarose is the formation of a covalently crosslinked network rather than a gel formed primarily through reversible physical association.

Two composition variables used to describe a polyacrylamide gel are percentage total monomer, %T, and percentage crosslinker, %C. If a is the mass of acrylamide and b is the mass of bisacrylamide, in grams, and V is the solution volume in millilitres, %T = 100(a + b)/V. The percentage crosslinker is %C = 100b/(a + b). The first describes total monomer per hundred millilitres, whereas the second describes crosslinker as a percentage of total monomer mass. A confusion between these denominators gives incorrect predictions about the composition of the matrix.

As an illustrative composition calculation, a conceptual mixture with 9.5 g acrylamide and 0.5 g crosslinker in a total volume of 100 mL has %T = 10 and %C = 5. If both monomer masses are doubled at the same volume, %T increases to 20 while %C remains 5. Thus the total monomer concentration and the relative crosslinking proportion can change independently. This calculation is a mathematical illustration of the definitions, not a procedure for preparing a gel. The effects of changing crosslinker fraction on effective pore properties are not necessarily monotonic over all compositions, so a simple rule that every increase in %C always produces smaller pores is not defensible across all networks.

The practical relation between molecular size and gel mobility is often described by an empirical Ferguson relationship for suitable conditions. Written for the magnitude of mobility, log μ = log μ₀ − KᵣT, it describes a linear reduction of log mobility with gel concentration T over an applicable interval at fixed crosslinking proportion. The intercept corresponds to extrapolated free-solution mobility μ₀, and the slope involves a retardation coefficient Kᵣ that reflects the interaction of the migrating molecule with the gel matrix. A larger effective particle can show stronger retardation. The equation is useful for comparing several gel concentrations, but requires a defined logarithm convention and comparable experimental conditions.

Consider two hypothetical proteins A and B that have the same measured mobility magnitude in a 5% gel. At 10% gel, A travels less far than B in the same field and time. A has experienced stronger matrix-dependent retardation over this interval. If both follow a simple Ferguson relationship, their log-mobility versus gel-concentration lines have different slopes. They may also have different intercepts. It does not follow that the proteins have equal mass merely because their initial mobilities coincide. The two gel conditions reveal information that a single condition cannot resolve.

A gradient gel contains a systematic change in matrix concentration along the migration direction. In a typical protein size separation gradient, relatively large molecules can migrate in a less restrictive region while smaller molecules encounter increasingly restrictive material farther along the path. This can extend the useful mass interval in a single separation. It does not imply that every molecule reaches an absolute equilibrium position independent of time. Standards must be interpreted with the specific gradient and running conditions in mind. A gradient is a way to vary physical sieving, unlike an isoelectric focusing gradient whose defining feature is changing pH.

A slab matrix has a finite resolving capacity. Large sample amounts can broaden or distort bands, while very small amounts may fail to give detectable signal. Narrow wells and a sharp starting zone help convert mobility differences into spatial resolution. Two proteins that travel 40 and 43 mm are distinct in mean migration, yet their bands could be effectively inseparable if each is several millimetres wide. The interpretation of a band as a distinct molecular population therefore depends on the separation resolution as well as the chemistry of the mixture.

In a native polyacrylamide gel, a protein's effective charge is not replaced by an added uniform detergent charge. Consequently, separation reflects charge and friction in the solvent as well as size, shape and matrix effects. In a denaturing gel, specific chemical treatment can reduce differences in conformation or charge-to-mass ratio, changing the dominant discriminating property. The same type of polymer matrix can be used in either format; the gel material by itself does not tell the investigator whether the molecules retain their native conformation. PAGE describes the matrix, whereas native PAGE and SDS-PAGE describe important differences in the state of the migrating proteins.

Native PAGE and functional molecular complexes

A native protein is one whose relevant folded conformation and interactions are retained under the chosen conditions. The word native is not a guarantee that every cellular interaction remains intact: extraction, dilution, buffer composition and the separation process itself can perturb an equilibrium. Native PAGE aims to preserve more of the structural organisation than a conventional denaturing protein gel, making it useful when the question concerns activity, oligomeric assembly or a stable interaction. A native band is the outcome of a measurement in a particular environment, not a perfect photograph of the protein inside a living cell.

At a chosen pH, a native protein has a characteristic effective charge and hydrodynamic shape. Its mobility depends on both these properties and the matrix. A protein of 100 kDa with a high negative charge can migrate farther than a protein of 50 kDa with little negative charge. Conversely a compact molecule can migrate differently from an elongated molecule of the same mass and similar charge. This is why a molecular mass ladder calibrated for denaturing SDS-PAGE cannot be directly applied to ordinary native PAGE.

Suppose a plant enzyme occurs as a monomer of 40 kDa and a homodimer of 80 kDa. The homodimer contains twice the polypeptide mass, but its charge, friction and conformation need not be scaled in a simple way. Its mobility relative to the monomer cannot be predicted confidently from molecular mass alone. A native separation may nevertheless distinguish the two species. An independent activity stain can reveal which resolved species is enzymatically active under the detection conditions. If both positions show activity, either both forms can catalyse the reaction or one may have changed state during the detection process. Additional evidence is required to decide.

A change in native band pattern is commonly used as evidence for interactions. In an electrophoretic mobility shift experiment, binding of a protein to nucleic acid can create a species with altered migration. The name shift refers to the movement of a detectable population relative to a suitable control containing free nucleic acid. The physical cause may include changes in effective charge, size, shape or matrix interactions. The direction is often a retardation, but it is not a fundamental law that every possible complex always migrates more slowly. The comparison of free and bound species under matched conditions, rather than the sign of the shift alone, supplies the meaningful evidence.

An apparent binding band must be tested for specificity. A free-nucleic-acid control establishes the migration position in the absence of binding protein. A competing unlabelled molecule of the same recognition sequence can test whether it reduces the detected complex. A different sequence control tests whether this effect is selective rather than a nonspecific consequence of adding more polyanion. A band that remains at a fixed position while its intensity decreases after competition can be consistent with dissociation of a complex. These conceptual controls illustrate how physical separation becomes evidence for a biochemical interaction; a shift alone does not establish the exact binding site, stoichiometry or equilibrium dissociation constant.

Native separation is especially useful for distinguishing an isoenzyme pattern from a simple total protein measurement. Isoenzymes catalyse the same overall reaction but can differ in sequence, charge, kinetic properties, tissue distribution or assembly. When extracts are separated and the resolved molecules are detected through a compatible activity-dependent reaction, several active zones may appear even if all catalyse the same substrate conversion. The absence of an active zone cannot automatically be equated with absence of the protein, because the protein may have lost activity in the experiment. Conversely an activity zone establishes a catalytic phenotype under the conditions used, not its molecular sequence.

An important experimental distinction is between a native oligomer formed by noncovalent association and polypeptide chains linked by disulfide bonds. Both can appear as larger assemblies in a native measurement. In a denaturing detergent environment, many noncovalent interactions are disrupted. A covalent disulfide linkage can persist unless a suitable reducing treatment breaks it. Comparing native, nonreducing denaturing and reducing denaturing measurements therefore gives different types of evidence about molecular assembly. It is the pattern across these conditions that can distinguish competing structural hypotheses.

Blue native PAGE is a specialised native format in which dye association can help confer negative charge to proteins and complexes under compatible conditions. Its relevance here is conceptual: even a method called native may modify the migrating complex's effective charge to enable separation while preserving more structural interactions than SDS treatment. Such a method cannot be interpreted by assuming completely unmodified intrinsic charge. The core NET/SET comparison is between preservation of molecular organisation and deliberate denaturation for subunit mass analysis, with the appropriate qualifications for each method.

SDS-PAGE: converting protein chains into mass-dependent signals

Proteins are chemically diverse polymers. A chain rich in acidic amino acids can have a large negative charge at neutral pH, while another chain of similar molecular mass can contain more basic groups. Their folded dimensions may also differ because one chain is compact and the other extended. Ordinary native PAGE preserves many of these differences. That behaviour is valuable when studying native organisation, but makes it difficult to interpret a band position primarily in terms of polypeptide mass. The conceptual objective of SDS-PAGE is to reduce the influence of native properties so that matrix-dependent separation can become the dominant effect.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate, abbreviated SDS, is an anionic detergent. Its dodecyl group is hydrophobic, while the sulfate-containing head group carries negative charge under ordinary conditions. Interaction with proteins disrupts many hydrophobic and other noncovalent interactions that stabilise folded structure. Many proteins associate with detergent in approximate proportion to their polypeptide mass. This gives the resulting complexes a broadly similar negative charge per unit polypeptide mass. It also replaces much of the original variation in folded structure with a more uniform class of detergent-associated conformations.

The treatment does not remove every source of variation. Some proteins, particularly those with unusual composition, extensive hydrophobic regions, unusual modifications or resistant structural organisation, bind detergent atypically or migrate anomalously. The measured value is therefore apparent molecular mass estimated against suitable reference polypeptides. It is not the exact molecular mass that would be obtained by a sufficiently accurate mass spectrometric measurement. When an examination asks whether SDS-PAGE separates proteins by charge or mass, the best answer is that electrophoretic driving still depends on charge, but the treatment makes charge-to-mass variation relatively uniform for many proteins so that matrix-dependent size discrimination becomes dominant.

A useful way of thinking about the treatment is to separate three changes. First, detergent association supplies many negative charges. Second, disruption of native noncovalent interactions changes the size and shape of the migrating entities, often reducing an assembly to its individual chains. Third, a separate reducing treatment can break covalent disulfide bonds between or within chains. These changes are not identical and must not be collapsed into a single definition of denaturation. A detergent can unfold a protein without breaking the peptide bonds that connect its amino acids. A disulfide-reducing treatment changes the state of sulfur linkages but does not normally hydrolyse the polypeptide backbone.

Consider a native heterotetramer composed of two copies of a 25 kDa chain and two copies of a 50 kDa chain, held together solely by noncovalent interactions. Its total theoretical mass is 150 kDa. Under conditions that dissociate the assembly without cleaving peptide bonds, the expected SDS-PAGE pattern contains two apparent mass populations near 25 and 50 kDa rather than four distinct bands. Two copies of a chain that migrate identically occupy the same band. Under an ideal mass-proportional staining model, the mass in the 50 kDa population would be twice the mass in the 25 kDa population because both chain populations contain the same number of molecules but their molecular masses differ twofold. The result illustrates why the number of bands, the number of distinct chain types and the number of chains per native complex are three different quantities.

The number of chains cannot ordinarily be reconstructed from denaturing data alone. A band at 50 kDa could represent a single 50 kDa chain in a monomeric protein, each of four identical chains in a tetramer or several unrelated protein species that have similar apparent chain masses. Suppose independent size-exclusion measurements under native conditions suggest that the protein has an apparent assembly mass of 200 kDa, and reducing SDS-PAGE yields only a 50 kDa band. A homotetramer is a reasonable hypothesis, subject to the accuracy of native mass estimation and sample identity. A single SDS band without the native measurement would not establish that hypothesis.

Reducing and nonreducing electrophoresis distinguish another class of assemblies. A purified sample gives a band near 70 kDa under nonreducing SDS conditions and bands near 40 and 30 kDa under reducing SDS conditions. A plausible explanation is that the original material contains a disulfide-linked heterodimer. The approximate mass sum agrees with this explanation. If the original sample were instead a noncovalent complex of those chains that dissociated completely in both SDS conditions, two bands would be expected in both runs. The differential effect of reduction therefore supplies evidence about the type of linkage. The evidence remains conditional because incomplete denaturation, other modifications or heterogeneity can complicate the relationship.

Intrachain disulfide bonds can also influence apparent mobility. A chain with such a bond need not split into two fragments when the bond is reduced because the continuous peptide backbone remains intact. The chain may nevertheless change conformation or detergent association enough to alter its mobility. Thus a reduction-dependent band shift does not necessarily prove an interchain disulfide. A convincing structural interpretation considers whether new chain populations appear, whether the mass balance is plausible and whether independent measurements agree.

The gel matrix introduces size-dependent retardation. In a suitably chosen resolving gel, smaller detergent-associated polypeptides generally move farther than larger ones. The relationship is often approximately linear between log molecular mass and relative migration within a useful interval. Relative migration Rf may be defined as the distance from the origin to the protein band divided by the distance from the origin to a tracking dye front measured in the same run. The value is dimensionless. The tracking dye is a migration reference, not a molecular mass marker; a protein ladder provides the relationship between Rf and apparent mass.

Suppose a marker of 100 kDa has Rf = 0.30, a marker of 25 kDa has Rf = 0.70 and the unknown has Rf = 0.50. Under a local linear relationship between log₁₀ molecular mass and Rf, the unknown lies halfway between log₁₀ 100 and log₁₀ 25. The result is log₁₀ M = (2 + 1.39794)/2 = 1.69897, so M = 50 kDa. The arithmetic midpoint, 62.5 kDa, would be inappropriate for this model. The calculation is illustrative and assumes that the marker and unknown have comparable migration behaviour in the same gel.

A wider calibration set is preferable to two points because it permits detection of curvature or anomalous markers. If the very largest standards barely enter the gel, their positions may not lie on the same approximately linear calibration as intermediate proteins. If the smallest standards approach the dye front, their positions may be poorly discriminated. An unknown that lies near a poorly resolved boundary has greater sizing uncertainty. Reporting an apparent mass to many decimal places does not compensate for this limitation. An estimate such as approximately 50 kDa is more defensible than a claim of 50.000 kDa based on a typical visual separation.

The discontinuous buffer system used in many SDS-PAGE formats improves resolution through a separation of two functions: concentrating the starting sample and resolving the proteins. Without concentration, protein populations may begin in a relatively broad sample zone. Molecules with only modest mobility differences can overlap substantially if their starting positions are broad. Stacking reduces this initial dispersion before the resolving phase. A stacking region has a relatively open matrix and ionic conditions designed to collect the migrating complexes into a thin zone. The resolving region provides conditions that favour separation according to differences in matrix retardation.

The physical explanation involves differences in the mobilities of buffer ions. In a common discontinuous Tris–glycine system, chloride acts as a relatively fast leading ion. Glycine in the stacking environment has a comparatively low effective anionic mobility. Protein–SDS complexes are concentrated in the region between the leading and trailing ionic zones. The local electric fields adjust in a manner that promotes concentration of the intermediate mobility components. When the ionic environment changes in the resolving region, glycine's effective negative mobility increases, and the proteins no longer remain compressed in the same moving boundary. The resolving matrix then allows their different retardation behaviours to generate separate bands.

The stacking process is not merely a matter of placing a low-percentage gel above a higher-percentage gel. A less restrictive matrix helps allow the components to move while stacking, but discontinuities in ionic composition and pH are central to the classical mechanism. Likewise, the leading and trailing ions should not be confused with proteins serving as molecular standards. The ions help establish the electrical separation environment; the marker polypeptides provide known reference positions. A continuous system can separate proteins without the same stacking mechanism, but a broad initial sample distribution may compromise resolution.

Laemmli's 1970 paper provided an important example of how a high-resolution denaturing gel system could be used to study changes in structural protein composition during biological assembly. The primary contribution relevant to this chapter is that better separation of polypeptide populations enabled investigators to distinguish changes that would have been difficult to resolve in less discriminating measurements. A size difference between two protein bands can be consistent with processing or cleavage, but establishing a precursor-product relationship requires additional temporal, genetic or biochemical evidence. A denaturing gel distinguishes populations; it does not directly observe the reaction that created them.

This distinction is especially important in studies of post-translational processing. A plant protein sample from a control condition gives a major band at 60 kDa. A treated sample shows a weaker band at 60 kDa and a new band at 45 kDa. One hypothesis is proteolytic processing of the original chain. Another is that the abundance of two unrelated proteins has changed. If a protein-specific antibody recognises both bands, the processing hypothesis becomes more plausible, but the antibody could recognise a shared epitope on distinct species. Identification of the smaller form and appropriate controls are necessary before concluding that a particular cleavage occurred.

Post-translational modifications can also shift apparent mass or affect detergent binding. A glycoprotein may carry carbohydrate mass that alters its migration relative to ordinary polypeptide markers. Phosphorylation adds relatively little mass to a typical protein for each phosphate group, although it can have important effects on charge-sensitive separations. Some changes in electrophoretic position are disproportionately large compared with the true mass change because migration depends on several physical properties. A student should avoid treating every observed mass shift as a direct arithmetic measure of the chemical groups added.

Detection introduces a final limitation. General protein stains differ in sensitivity, selectivity and linear range. Two bands of equal intensity can represent different protein masses if the proteins bind stain differently. With validated linear response, equal intensity for the same protein across samples can support similar amounts of that protein, provided sample loading and recovery are comparable. However, a visual pattern alone is not sufficient for rigorous quantitative comparisons across unrelated proteins. In an overloaded band, intensity may saturate or the band may broaden, making both position and abundance estimates less reliable.

The most informative interpretation of SDS-PAGE states explicitly which protein state was analysed, whether reducing treatment was used, how the mass calibration was obtained, and whether identity was tested independently. For a purified enzyme, a chain mass estimate may be combined with an independent native size measurement and activity data. For a crude extract, a band at the expected size is only a candidate until a recognition method confirms its identity. This reasoning prepares the next technique, isoelectric focusing, in which the native charge property that SDS largely masks becomes the central variable.

Isoelectric focusing: separation by an equilibrium charge property

In ordinary zone electrophoresis, a charged molecule generally continues to migrate while the field is present and a suitable path remains available. If two molecules have different mobilities, their mean positions diverge with time. Isoelectric focusing, abbreviated IEF, uses a different principle. The separation region contains a spatial pH gradient, and the charge of an amphoteric molecule varies with the local pH. Each molecule migrates toward a position at which its average net electrophoretic charge is zero. The result is not simply a set of different migration speeds measured after an arbitrary period. Under suitable conditions, the positions correspond to isoelectric points.

The underlying acid–base chemistry can be developed from familiar amino acid behaviour. A simple amino acid without an ionisable side chain has an amino group that tends to be positively charged when protonated and a carboxyl group that tends to be negatively charged when deprotonated. At low pH, the carboxyl group is increasingly protonated and the average net charge tends toward a positive value. At high pH, the amino group loses its proton and the average charge tends toward a negative value. At an intermediate pH, the positive and negative contributions balance. The zwitterionic state still contains charged groups even though its overall charge is zero.

For a simple amino acid whose neutral zwitterion lies between two successive dissociation steps, the pI can be estimated as the arithmetic mean of the two relevant pKa values that bracket the zero net charge form. For instance, a hypothetical amino acid with pKa values 2.4 and 9.6 has an approximate pI of (2.4 + 9.6)/2 = 6.0. This familiar rule is not generally applied to proteins by averaging the pKa values of all their ionisable groups. A protein can contain many chemically distinct residues whose acid–base properties are influenced by the local structural environment. The protein's pI is instead the root of its total charge versus pH relationship under the specified conditions.

A useful elementary charge model represents the fraction of a basic group that is protonated as 1/(1 + 10^(pH − pKa)) and the fraction of an acidic group that is deprotonated as 1/(1 + 10^(pKa − pH)). Summing the positive contributions of basic groups and subtracting the negative contributions of acidic groups gives an approximate net charge curve. This independent-site description is a useful teaching model but can be inaccurate for a folded protein when the protonation of one site influences another or when the local environment shifts pKa values. A measured pI is consequently an experimental property that may differ from a simple prediction based only on primary sequence.

The spatial pH gradient is oriented with the acidic region toward the positive anode and the basic region toward the negative cathode. Consider a protein with pI 6 located at pH 4. Its net charge is generally positive, so it moves toward the cathode and enters progressively more basic regions. If a protein of the same species is initially at pH 8, it has a generally negative net charge and moves toward the anode, entering progressively more acidic regions. Both populations move toward the location where the local pH is near 6. Their initial locations differ, but their preferred focusing position is the same.

The restoring nature of the motion explains the term focusing. A molecule that diffuses slightly away from its isoelectric location into a more acidic region acquires positive net charge and experiences electrophoretic movement back toward the basic direction. If it diffuses into a more basic region, it acquires negative charge and moves in the acidic direction. Ordinary diffusion spreads the population, but the restoring electrophoretic motion opposes that spread. The resulting focused band has finite width rather than infinitely sharp boundaries because thermal diffusion and other sources of broadening remain present.

A simple near-focus model makes this balance quantitative. Let x increase from the acidic anode toward the basic cathode, and let the local pH increase at gradient g = dpH/dx. Near the focus x*, pH − pI is approximately g(x − x*). The effective charge generally decreases as pH increases, so write q ≈ −a g(x − x*), where a is the positive magnitude of the local slope of effective charge with pH. If the electric field points along positive x and the frictional coefficient f is approximately constant, the signed velocity becomes v ≈ −(a g E/f)(x − x*). The coefficient κ = a g E/f has units of inverse time in this simplified model. A positive displacement from the focus leads to a negative velocity, while a negative displacement leads to a positive velocity.

If diffusion has coefficient D and the field creates a linear restoring velocity, the steady zero-flux idealisation has a Gaussian-like concentration profile whose positional variance is D/κ. This follows by balancing advective flux vc against diffusive flux −D dc/dx, setting their sum to zero and integrating d ln c/dx = −κ(x − x*)/D. A larger restoring coefficient gives a narrower distribution, all else equal. The model is not a prediction of exact experimental band width, because gradients, friction, temperature and effective charge relationships vary. It clarifies why focusing can counteract diffusion and why separation conditions affect band sharpness even when the target equilibrium position remains nearly unchanged.

The pH gradient may be established using carrier ampholytes or immobilised buffering groups. Carrier ampholytes are amphoteric species that help establish a gradient under electrical conditions because of their different acid–base and migration properties. Immobilised pH gradients use buffering groups incorporated into the matrix to provide a spatially defined pH range. The important distinction is between the means of establishing the gradient and the reason the target protein focuses. A protein still focuses because the direction of its migration changes with local pH. A chemical component that creates a stable gradient does not serve as a direct molecular mass standard.

The width of the pH range determines how much space is available per unit difference in pI. In a hypothetical linear gradient extending from pH 3 to 10 over 140 mm, the gradient is 7/140 = 0.05 pH units per millimetre. Two proteins whose pI values differ by 0.10 would ideally focus 2 mm apart. If the same length instead covers pH 5 to 7, the gradient becomes 2/140 ≈ 0.0143 pH units per millimetre. A difference of 0.10 pH units corresponds to approximately 7 mm. A narrow gradient can therefore increase spatial separation between close pI values, provided both lie within the range and actual band widths permit resolution.

A narrow range is not universally better. If one protein has pI 4.5 and another 7.5, a gradient from pH 5 to 7 does not contain the equilibrium focusing locations of either species. The proteins may migrate toward an appropriate end of the gradient rather than form valid internal focused zones. A wide range is useful for surveying an unknown mixture, whereas a narrower range may be useful when analysing a known interval of closely related forms. The choice involves a trade-off between the span of accessible pI values and the spatial discrimination available per pH unit.

In a gradient that is not perfectly linear, pI cannot be calculated by using the nominal end points and proportional distance without checking the calibration. Standards of known pI or other information about the established gradient can provide a relationship between position and pH. If a focused band lies midway along a strip that is nominally pH 3 to 10, its pI is 6.5 only under the assumption of a linear gradient and correct position calibration. An examination problem often explicitly supplies this assumption. If it does not, proportional interpolation should be presented as conditional.

Solubility is another limitation. At the isoelectric point, electrostatic repulsion between some protein molecules is reduced, which can favour association or precipitation. This is not universal because protein solubility depends on many interactions, but the phenomenon can impair focusing and detection. Proteins may be analysed under conditions that disrupt native structure while retaining the charge-dependent focusing principle. Therefore IEF need not preserve native enzymatic activity or physiological conformation. The pI measured for a polypeptide under denaturing focusing conditions is not necessarily identical to an apparent pI of its native complex in another medium.

A conventional SDS-coated protein is not an ideal starting species for pI-dependent focusing because the detergent supplies strong negative charge and masks the native charge balance of the polypeptide. A protein might not reverse its migration appropriately over the desired pH range. In the classical two-dimensional approach the charge-based separation is therefore carried out before the SDS-based mass separation. Compatible solubilising conditions can help keep proteins available for focusing without imposing the same strong uniform anionic character as SDS. Students do not need to memorise specialised formulation recipes to understand why the order matters.

Biological changes can produce charge differences without large changes in molecular mass. A protein that gains a negatively charged modification can focus at a lower pH if the modification makes its total charge more negative across the relevant interval. The exact magnitude and sometimes the detailed interpretation depend on the chemical modification and protein environment. Phosphorylation often produces an acidic shift relative to an otherwise comparable form. Deamidation can also alter charge. Amino acid substitutions may change pI with little overall mass difference. IEF can reveal such differences, but a changed pI does not specify which chemical event occurred.

An illustrative comparison makes this ambiguity clear. Two purified protein forms have nearly indistinguishable apparent masses on reducing SDS-PAGE, but focus at pH 6.8 and 6.2 respectively. The lower-pI population is consistent with a more acidic form. If both are products of the same gene, a post-translational modification is a possible cause. However, unrelated proteins with similar mass can also have different pI values. To establish the modification hypothesis, independent identity evidence and an appropriate modification-sensitive comparison are needed. If a treatment known to remove a particular modification converts the two forms into one position, the hypothesis gains support, though treatment specificity and sample recovery must still be assessed.

The distinction between pI and pKa deserves particular attention. A pKa describes the equilibrium of a particular protonation step or an apparent transition. A pI describes the pH at which the overall net charge vanishes. A protein with pI 6 is not necessarily composed of residues with pKa 6. Nor is its catalytic optimum necessarily pH 6: enzyme activity depends on catalytic residues, folding, substrate chemistry and other factors. The coincidence of pI and an activity optimum would require evidence, not merely an argument from the names of the two properties.

Isoelectric focusing therefore supplies a physical description of an amphoteric molecular population. It can separate species whose chain sizes are nearly identical but charge relationships differ. It cannot by itself establish their sequence, function or cellular role. Its strongest use in molecular profiling arises when pI evidence is combined with a second, largely independent physical dimension.

One-dimensional and two-dimensional electrophoresis

A one-dimensional separation places molecules along a single coordinate. A mixture may contain two species that differ substantially in one property but share a similar value of the property measured by the chosen method. If they comigrate, the band has more chemical complexity than its appearance suggests. A second dimension becomes useful when it distinguishes properties that are not fully correlated with the first. The aim is to distribute molecules across a plane rather than compress every difference into a single line.

Classical two-dimensional protein electrophoresis first separates polypeptides by pI through isoelectric focusing. The resulting strip contains populations distributed along a charge-dependent coordinate. A second electrophoretic dimension then separates those populations according to apparent molecular mass under SDS-PAGE conditions. In the conventional representation, pI varies horizontally and the apparent chain mass varies vertically, with higher apparent masses nearer the top and lower masses migrating farther downward. Both axes require interpretation: the horizontal position reflects the calibrated pH gradient, while the vertical position reflects the mass calibration in the resolving gel.

The transition from the first to the second dimension must preserve the positional information gained during focusing while making the polypeptides compatible with the denaturing mass separation. Conceptually, the focused strip is aligned with the top of the second separation region. Protein populations leave their first-dimensional positions and move into the resolving matrix perpendicular to the original axis. The second dimension must not be confused with rotating the same unchanged experiment. The chemistry is different because SDS association suppresses much of the charge variation that created the first-dimensional separation.

If two proteins have the same pI but different chain masses, they occupy approximately the same horizontal coordinate and different vertical coordinates. If they have different pI values but equal apparent mass, they occupy distinct horizontal coordinates at similar vertical heights. If they have both indistinguishable pI and apparent mass, they may still comigrate as one apparent spot. This last case is not a failure of the concept of two-dimensional separation. It is a reminder that even two physical coordinates do not uniquely define a protein species. Sequence-level identity requires a separate analytical step.

The historical work of Patrick O'Farrell in 1975 demonstrated that coupling pI-based and size-based separations could produce high-resolution distributions of proteins in complex mixtures. The importance of the work is the use of two largely independent physical discriminators to increase the number of distinguishable species. If a first dimension could resolve ten position intervals and a second could resolve ten independent intervals, the theoretical two-dimensional grid would contain up to one hundred position combinations. This is an illustration of how separation capacity can increase, not a prediction that one hundred real proteins will necessarily be resolved. Real proteins are unevenly distributed across property space and spots have finite widths.

A two-dimensional gel may show a horizontal train of spots at approximately the same apparent mass but different pI values. Such a pattern is consistent with multiple charge forms of a protein. Successive phosphorylation events, for example, may alter charge sufficiently to shift pI while changing true mass by a small fraction of the chain mass. However, a horizontal train is not proof of phosphorylation. Unrelated species, other chemical modifications, or preparation-induced changes may produce a similar pattern. Evidence supporting common protein identity and a modification-specific intervention is necessary before assigning a biochemical explanation.

A vertical train at approximately the same pI but distinct apparent masses suggests a different kind of difference. Proteolytic processing, alternative chain forms or covalent modifications can change mass while retaining a similar overall pI. Again, none of these is uniquely established by the positions. A shift in both coordinates provides evidence that the measured pI and apparent mass differ, but it does not reveal whether the changes are caused by altered expression, chemical modification, degradation or a distinct molecular species.

Consider an illustrative 2D dataset in which spots A and B both lie near 55 kDa but have pI values 7.1 and 6.6 respectively. Spot C has pI 6.6 and an apparent mass of 35 kDa. A treatment reduces A and increases B while C remains unchanged. If A and B are independently confirmed as forms of the same protein, the pattern supports redistribution between charge forms rather than a simple change in total protein abundance. If the total signal from A and B remains comparable under an appropriate detection model, this interpretation becomes stronger. Without that identity information, reciprocal changes might instead reflect unrelated proteins responding in opposite ways.

A quantitative comparison of two 2D gels needs matching and normalization. The raw coordinates may differ slightly because gradients, migration distances, image geometry and matrix behaviour differ. A spot at a slightly different position is not necessarily a new molecular species. Reference features and suitable image alignment improve the reliability of spot matching. Signal intensity depends on staining and detector response; comparing raw spot brightness from two images captured at different exposures can create false abundance changes. If an experiment compares biological conditions, independent biological samples are necessary to estimate biological variability rather than only technical reproducibility.

For example, in a hypothetical pair of normalized spot measurements, a confirmed protein form gives 120 arbitrary units in control and 180 units in treatment. Its estimated fold change is 180/120 = 1.5. The increase is 50%, not 150%. Its base-two logarithmic fold change is approximately 0.585. This calculation describes the observed normalized ratio. It does not establish statistical significance or biological importance without estimates of variability, sample design and measurement uncertainty. A second protein form that changes from 2 to 3 arbitrary units has the same ratio, but the confidence may be much lower if those values lie near background noise.

Two-dimensional gels have limitations as a broad molecular profiling method. Highly hydrophobic proteins can be difficult to keep soluble under focusing conditions; proteins outside the selected pH or mass range may not be resolved. Highly abundant species can obscure lower-abundance species, and a large difference in detectability between proteins restricts quantitative comparison. The final pattern is therefore not a complete catalogue of every protein in the biological system. Absence of a spot means absence of a detectable resolved feature under the measurement conditions, not necessarily absence of the protein in the original tissue.

The benefits of two dimensions must be weighed against the analytical question. If the goal is to check whether a purified protein consists mainly of chains near an expected molecular mass, a well-controlled one-dimensional SDS gel may provide sufficient information. If the goal is to investigate charge variants of proteins with comparable masses, a pI-sensitive method or a 2D comparison becomes more useful. If the goal is exact molecular identification in a complex mixture, electrophoretic separation may be combined with another recognition or analytical method. Choosing a method by the question it can answer is more important than selecting the most complex available technique.

The same design principle applies to nucleic acids. Two-dimensional separation need not combine pI and molecular mass, which is the classical protein implementation. It may instead change the influence of DNA conformation or branching between dimensions. Therefore a diagram with two migration axes must be read in light of the conditions described. The axes of an unfamiliar method cannot be inferred simply from the fact that it is called two-dimensional electrophoresis.

Molecular markers: from fragment length to genetic evidence

The word marker has two uses that must be kept separate. A molecular size marker is a standard containing components of known size or apparent mass. A genetic molecular marker is a detectable molecular characteristic associated with a genomic locus or region. The first helps calibrate an instrument; the second provides information about biological variation. A DNA ladder does not become a marker of inheritance merely because its bands are visible, and an inherited marker allele cannot be assigned a physical length accurately without an appropriate analytical reference.

A genetic marker becomes useful when different samples can be assigned reproducible states. In a simple fragment-length polymorphism, two alleles at a locus may differ in length because of variation in the underlying DNA. If the selected analytical method distinguishes their products, a gel or electropherogram can record those different lengths. The observation belongs to the measured locus or fragment set. It does not automatically describe the rest of the genome. Two plants sharing the same allele at one locus may differ at many other loci.

A restriction fragment length polymorphism, or RFLP, is a difference in the lengths of restriction fragments associated with underlying sequence differences. A changed recognition site can alter the number and lengths of fragments produced; insertions or deletions can alter the distance between recognition sites. The result is an analytical fragment distribution, often interpreted using a defined locus-specific detection strategy. A band that has changed position might arise because a site is lost, a site is gained, or the interval has changed length. Distinguishing these possibilities requires additional information about the original fragment architecture and the completeness of cleavage.

Imagine an illustrative linear locus represented by a 900 bp fragment. One allele contains a site that divides this fragment into 500 and 400 bp products, whereas a second allele lacks that internal site. Under ideal complete cleavage and detection of all relevant fragments, the first homozygote shows 500 and 400 bp bands, the second homozygote a 900 bp band, and the heterozygote all three. The heterozygote pattern combines the contributions of both alleles. This simple example assumes that the fragments can be resolved, all are detectable, and there are no other related products. In a real locus-specific assay, probe placement may mean that only one of the cleavage fragments is detected. The pattern predicted by a question must therefore agree with its stated detection method.

Incomplete cleavage can produce a misleading pattern. If some copies of the first allele remain uncut, a 900 bp band can appear alongside the two shorter products even in the absence of the second allele. In the example above, this pattern superficially resembles heterozygosity. The crucial distinction is between inherited absence of a restriction site and a technical failure to cleave an existing site. Evidence from a known control of complete cleavage, a repeat measurement or independent locus characterization can discriminate these explanations. The observed number of bands is a starting point for interpretation, not a substitute for testing assumptions.

A simple sequence repeat (SSR), also called a microsatellite or short tandem repeat, is a locus containing successive copies of a short sequence motif. Different alleles may contain different numbers of repeat units. When the same flanking interval is measured, a difference of one repeat unit creates a predictable length difference. For a tetranucleotide repeat, two alleles differing by three repeat units have a length difference of 12 bp if the flanking sequences are otherwise identical. Accurate distinction of close sizes may require a high-resolution gel or a capillary separation; a coarse agarose pattern may not resolve the two alleles reliably.

Suppose a hypothetical marker has alleles of 144 and 156 bp. One diploid plant gives a single detectable band at 144 bp, a second gives both 144 and 156 bp, and a third gives a single band at 156 bp. If the locus is single-copy, both alleles are detected reliably and the products are resolved, the expected genotypes are AA, AB and BB. This is a codominant marker system because the two alleles can be distinguished in the heterozygote. A single band alone, however, is not conclusive proof of homozygosity. One allele may fail to be detected, or distinct alleles may have the same measured size. Technical allele dropout can conceal a heterozygous genotype.

A different class of markers is scored by presence or absence of a particular fragment. Random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) patterns and many amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) marker scores are commonly treated as dominant presence/absence data in diploids. The appearance of a band can reflect the presence of at least one copy of an amplifiable fragment state, but the pattern often does not distinguish whether that state occurs on one homolog or both. The examination distinction concerns information content: a dominant scored marker generally cannot identify heterozygosity as directly as a codominant length marker. The specific scoring scheme and method determine the inference, not merely the use of electrophoresis.

These marker approaches differ in reproducibility, resolution and the dependence of the observed signal on sample preparation and the upstream generation of fragments. RAPD fingerprints can be sensitive to analytical conditions. AFLP patterns can contain many resolved fragments, but multiple species of the same apparent size may comigrate. RFLP and SSR analysis can provide codominant locus information when the experimental design permits it. A molecular profile is best treated as a set of measurements subject to the limitations of the method rather than as a direct readout of every nucleotide difference.

Genetic linkage mapping uses the segregation of marker states in appropriate families or populations. A marker may be informative because it cosegregates with a trait or another locus more often than expected under independent assortment. The electrophoretic pattern provides an observable marker state, but it does not by itself establish a genetic map distance. Recombination data, informative crosses and an appropriate mapping model are needed. Similarly, a match between profiles at several loci is stronger evidence of similarity than a match at one, but its statistical meaning depends on allele frequencies and the relationship between loci. These inheritance and population concepts belong primarily to Unit 8; here the focus is the quality of the measured marker state that enters the analysis.

One recurring error in interpreting molecular profiles is to equate equal fragment length with equal sequence. Two distinct DNA molecules can have the same number of base pairs. An SSR allele can even have an equal total length despite differing in underlying motif composition or flanking sequence. Conversely, a difference in a band position may reflect different molecular length, conformation or analytical conditions rather than a specific sequence substitution. The validity of an allele call depends on a defined locus and a validated association between the measured feature and its intended interpretation.

For examination reasoning, consider two plant parents scored at a codominant marker. One has allele A only and the other B only. Under the assumptions of a diploid locus, homozygous parents and reliable detection, all F1 progeny should show both bands. Selfing the F1 gives expected genotype proportions of one-quarter AA, one-half AB and one-quarter BB in the absence of segregation distortion or selection. Among 80 F2 individuals the expected counts are 20, 40 and 20. The band pattern is the phenotypic observation used to assign each molecular genotype; the ratio follows from Mendelian segregation, not from the physical mechanism of gel migration. Failure to observe the expected ratio may involve biological causes, scoring errors or sampling variation.

Capillary electrophoresis and electropherogram interpretation

A slab gel records position after a separation, whereas a capillary instrument commonly records the passage of populations through a fixed detection window. The resulting plot of signal against time is called an electropherogram. Its peaks represent populations that reach the detector at similar times. Unlike a conventional gel image, in which migration distance is compared within a common time, a capillary electropherogram often compares migration times across a common effective path. The difference between time and distance is fundamental for interpreting which component has moved more rapidly.

A capillary can contain an electrolyte solution, and some formats also incorporate a sieving medium for size-dependent separations. In capillary zone electrophoresis the intrinsic electrophoretic mobility of a species is an important discriminator. In capillary gel or polymer-matrix separations, molecular sieving introduces further size dependence. These formats should not be treated as identical simply because the instrument has a narrow separation channel. A question may specify a separation matrix and a fragment-length standard; a different question may specify free-solution charge-dependent mobility. The appropriate model is determined by that information.

An additional phenomenon in many capillary instruments is electroosmotic flow, abbreviated EOF. At a suitable pH, an uncoated silica capillary wall can carry negatively charged surface groups. The adjacent liquid contains counterions that contribute to an electrical double layer. Applying a field causes a net motion of mobile charge in this region, and viscous coupling moves the bulk liquid. Under common normal-polarity conditions with a negatively charged wall, this bulk flow is directed towards the cathode. Its magnitude and even direction can vary with surface chemistry, buffer and instrument configuration.

The observed velocity of a molecular population combines its intrinsic electrophoretic velocity relative to the liquid and the bulk liquid velocity. With a positive coordinate defined along the field from anode to cathode, the simple signed relation is v_obs = (μ_ep + μ_EOF)E. A positively charged species may have positive μ_ep, while a negatively charged species may have negative μ_ep. If EOF is positive and larger in magnitude than the negative electrophoretic mobility, the anion may nonetheless move towards the cathodic detector. This apparent paradox disappears when motion relative to the liquid is distinguished from motion relative to the instrument.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose the field magnitude is 1,000 V m⁻¹ and the signed EOF mobility is +3.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. A neutral marker with zero intrinsic electrophoretic mobility has an observed velocity of +3.0 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹. A cation with intrinsic mobility +1.0 × 10⁻⁸ has an observed velocity of +4.0 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹. An anion with intrinsic mobility −2.0 × 10⁻⁸ has an observed velocity of +1.0 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹. All three move toward the cathode in this example, but the cation arrives first and the anion last when their starting positions and detector path lengths are equal. If the anion's negative mobility were greater in magnitude than the EOF mobility, it would instead move in the opposite direction.

The migration time for a population that moves uniformly toward a detector is t_m = L_d/v_obs, where L_d is the distance from the injection region to the detector. If the voltage drop V occurs over total capillary length L_t, the field is approximately V/L_t and the apparent mobility is μ_app = L_dL_t/(Vt_m). Both lengths matter: the effective length to the detector can be shorter than the total capillary length over which voltage is applied. Using the effective length for both terms without justification gives a biased estimate. The formula also assumes a reasonably uniform field and stable velocity.

For instance, a hypothetical capillary has total length 0.50 m, detection length 0.40 m, voltage magnitude 10,000 V and migration time 200 s for a cathode-directed component. The apparent mobility magnitude is (0.40 × 0.50)/(10,000 × 200) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. If a neutral EOF marker has a measured apparent mobility of +6.0 × 10⁻⁸ under identical conditions, the intrinsic electrophoretic mobility of that component is estimated as +4.0 × 10⁻⁸ with the stated sign convention. A neutral marker provides a measure of bulk flow only if it is not retained or otherwise affected by additional interactions.

A peak's horizontal position usually reports time or a derived calibrated property. Its height reports maximum detected signal. Its area is the integral of the signal above an appropriate baseline over time. Peak height and area are not interchangeable when peak widths vary. Two populations containing the same detected amount can yield different maximum heights if one zone is more dispersed. Conversely, equal heights can be associated with different areas. For quantitative comparisons, detection response, baseline treatment and, in some electrophoretic formats, migration-velocity corrections must be considered. A simple area ratio can be interpreted as an amount ratio only when the response model supports that conclusion.

Peak overlap limits interpretation in much the same way as band overlap on a gel. A common illustrative measure of chromatographic-style separation resolution is R_s = 2|t₂ − t₁|/(w₁ + w₂), where t values are peak centres and w values are baseline widths measured in the same time units. If two peaks occur at 5.0 and 5.4 minutes with baseline widths 0.20 and 0.30 minutes, R_s = 2(0.4)/(0.50) = 1.6. If the peak widths each double while the centres remain the same, the resolution falls to 0.8. This numerical index describes separation quality under the specified width convention; it does not establish identity or imply that real asymmetrical peaks have a universal baseline-resolution threshold.

In fluorescence-based fragment analysis, a size standard may be analysed with the sample so that migration time can be converted to an estimated fragment size. Small time differences do not automatically correspond to equal base-pair differences over the full range. Calibration functions and appropriately spaced standards are needed. A fluorescent dye attached to a fragment can also affect migration, so the standards and measurement model must be suitable for the analytical format. Peak position, assigned size and allele call are progressively interpreted quantities; none should be confused with the original measured signal.

An electropherogram can reveal technical artefacts. In a repeat-length marker analysis, subsidiary peaks may arise because of changes in repeat number during the upstream amplification process. Detector saturation can flatten large peaks or distort relative signal measurements. Baseline noise can produce small apparent features that should not be confidently assigned as alleles. A peak's position relative to a standard, its signal strength, reproducibility and expected marker-specific behaviour all inform the decision. A low peak does not automatically represent a true low-abundance biological component.

The choice between a slab and a capillary method depends on the biological question and required resolution. A visually inspected slab gel can be sufficient to check the approximate length of separated DNA fragments or the presence of major protein bands. A capillary system can provide electronically recorded migration times and automated calibration useful for closely spaced fragments. Neither automatically provides molecular identity. A precisely calibrated peak may still represent more than one unresolved species. The purpose of the separation is to reduce complexity and measure physical differences; the interpretation still requires independent knowledge about the molecules.

Experimental controls, molecular profiling and analytical decisions

A molecular profile is the complete set of observable features obtained from a particular analytical design. To compare two profiles scientifically, it is necessary to identify what was held constant and which variable was deliberately changed. A difference between two lanes could reflect biology, preparation, separation or detection. Without a design that distinguishes those possibilities, the safest conclusion is that the observed patterns differ. It is premature to attribute that difference to expression, mutation, enzyme activity or molecular degradation.

Begin with the question of sample comparability. If equal volumes of two extracts are loaded, but the concentration of one extract is twice that of the other, equal-volume loading is not equal-mass loading. Suppose a particular protein constitutes 10% of total protein mass in both samples. Loading 10 micrograms total protein from one sample and 20 micrograms from the other would be expected to yield approximately twice as much target protein in the second lane, even without a change in relative abundance. A stronger target band may thus reflect the loading difference rather than a biological regulation event. The required normalization depends on whether the question concerns abundance per sample volume, per cell, per total protein or another defined denominator.

A standard of known size calibrates position. A positive control tests whether a defined material can produce the expected analytical feature. A negative control can help assess whether a signal is generated in the absence of the target or intended recognition event. An internal reference can help account for technical variability, provided it is stable in the relevant experimental context. These control categories address distinct failure modes. For example, a successful DNA ladder confirms that reference fragments migrated and were detected, but it does not show that a particular sample was recovered successfully. A blank without signal provides evidence against some forms of contamination, but does not demonstrate that all sample lanes contain competent material.

Sample-specific effects are especially important when a standard is loaded in a separate lane. One lane may have an unusual ionic composition, detergent concentration or amount of insoluble material. The marker can look normal while that sample lane displays retardation, broadening or material remaining near the origin. An internal standard may reveal a sample-specific migration effect when the standard and sample experience comparable conditions. A replicate prepared independently helps distinguish a reproducible biological distribution from a one-off handling or measurement artefact. Repeating an image of the same gel only assesses some detection variability; it does not repeat the entire analytical process.

A curved or smiling pattern across lanes can be caused by nonuniform temperature or electrical conditions. If the same marker species migrates different distances in different lanes, a single global size estimate may be unreliable. A bowed band that extends over a wide sample width can also make its centre difficult to define. The correct analytical response is not to choose whichever part of the band produces the expected molecular mass. It is to identify the distortion, evaluate whether local calibration is defensible and determine whether an independent separation is needed. A visual correction alone cannot recover resolution lost through physical overlap.

Consider a purification study in which four successive fractions are analysed by SDS-PAGE and by an independent enzyme assay. The starting extract contains many visible bands and has a specific activity of 2 units per mg protein. A subsequent fraction has fewer bands and a specific activity of 10 units per mg. This is a fivefold increase in specific activity. If the total recovered activity has fallen from 1,000 units to 400 units, the activity yield is 40%. The two measurements describe enrichment and recovery respectively. A cleaner pattern does not establish that a large fraction of the original activity was retained, and a high activity yield does not ensure chemical purity. Electrophoresis contributes evidence about the molecular distribution within the fractions; the enzyme assay supplies functional evidence.

A fraction that shows one dominant SDS-PAGE band is not necessarily completely pure. An unrelated protein may comigrate, a contaminant may be below the detection limit, or a component may respond poorly to the stain. Likewise, a sample with more than one band need not be chemically unsuitable if the biological target is a defined multichain complex. In a denaturing separation of a purified heteromeric protein, the expected outcome may deliberately include multiple chain populations. The appropriate purity claim must specify what is meant by purity and the analytical method used to support it.

A simple integrated decision illustrates how molecular profiling complements the preceding chromatographic node. An enzyme-containing fraction has a single dominant apparent 45 kDa reducing SDS band, but native size-exclusion chromatography suggests an assembly of approximately 90 kDa. One interpretation is a noncovalent homodimer. If nonreducing SDS-PAGE also shows 45 kDa, that is consistent with dissociation without breaking an interchain disulfide. If nonreducing SDS-PAGE instead shows 90 kDa and reducing SDS-PAGE gives 45 kDa, a disulfide-linked pair is a stronger hypothesis. The native size estimate is influenced by hydrodynamic properties and standards, so orthogonal evidence is still useful. Combining measurements with different assumptions is more powerful than repeating the same apparent mass estimate in a different visual format.

Now suppose a treated leaf extract shows an increase in the intensity of a spot at pI 5.8 and 45 kDa. A similar spot at pI 6.2 and 45 kDa becomes weaker. Possible explanations include a shift between charge forms of the same protein, differential expression of separate proteins or unequal recovery of species. If an identity-sensitive assay confirms the same protein at both positions, the first interpretation becomes more plausible. A modification-sensitive test can then evaluate one particular chemical hypothesis. If both spots respond similarly in independent samples and the sum of their normalized signals remains stable, redistribution between measured forms is supported more strongly. The conclusion must nevertheless reflect the selectivity of the identity test and the quality of the quantitative measurements.

This reasoning suggests a general sequence for examination problems. Identify the physical basis of the given method before reading the pattern. Determine the state of the molecules during separation, including whether denaturation or reduction is specified. Determine whether the horizontal or vertical axis is a distance, time, pH, mass or a calibrated derived variable. Compare the unknown against appropriate standards, and check whether the measured position lies within the useful interval. Distinguish signal magnitude from molecular quantity, and determine the denominator of any stated ratio. Finally, decide whether the claim concerns size, charge, molecular identity, assembly, activity or genotype, and ask whether the supplied evidence actually measures that property.

Experimental reasoning also requires consideration of negative results. Suppose no band appears at an expected DNA length. The target sequence could be absent, but insufficient material, degradation, inhibition of upstream fragment generation, failed loading or inadequate detection may also account for the observation. A positive control shows that the general analytical approach can produce a visible feature; an appropriate sample-specific reference tests whether the particular preparation is suitable. When the expected size lies beyond the resolved interval, a better matched separation is necessary. An absence claim becomes progressively stronger as relevant alternative explanations are excluded.

One of the most valuable lessons of electrophoresis is that the experiment determines which molecular properties survive into the final signal. Native separation may retain some information about molecular interactions and conformation. SDS-PAGE deliberately suppresses much of that information to simplify apparent chain-mass interpretation. IEF changes the local protonation environment to report pI. A two-dimensional combination increases discrimination but still does not give a full molecular structure. Genetic marker profiling uses the relationship between defined molecular variation and observed features, whereas electropherogram analysis translates a time-resolved detector response into a calibrated interpretation. Each method transforms the biological sample into a different kind of evidence.

The next subunit develops spectroscopic and other biophysical approaches that measure molecular properties through interactions with light, electromagnetic fields or other physical processes. Those methods also require a distinction between the original physical observable and a derived biological interpretation. In electrophoresis, students have already learned to read distances, times and charge-dependent positions as conditional measurements. This same habit will be essential when interpreting absorbance spectra, fluorescence signals, structural data and molecular interaction measurements.

Original examination practice

These original NET and SET-style exercises assess the interpretation of electrophoretic measurements. Numerical and experimental datasets are illustrative, not previous-year questions or published measurements.

Part B — Conceptual foundations

1. Which property directly determines the sign of the electrical force on a molecule?

  1. Diffusion coefficient
  2. Molecular mass alone
  3. Hydrodynamic radius alone
  4. Effective net charge
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Effective net charge

The force is qE. Mass and radius may influence friction and therefore mobility, but neither supplies the force sign; diffusion is random motion.

2. In an ordinary agarose slab, negatively charged linear DNA migrates electrophoretically toward which electrode?

  1. Anode
  2. Cathode
  3. Both equally
  4. Neither because DNA is a polymer
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Anode

The positive anode attracts the negatively charged DNA. The negative cathode repels it; polymer status does not remove the phosphate charge.

3. Which feature generally permits agarose to discriminate linear DNA by size?

  1. A uniform positive charge on every fragment
  2. Direct base sequencing by the stain
  3. The gel acting as a porous matrix
  4. A fixed pH gradient
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. The gel acting as a porous matrix

The network retards fragments differently. DNA is negatively charged, staining is not sequencing, and a pH gradient is the defining feature of IEF rather than routine agarose separation.

4. What happens to the useful size range when agarose concentration is increased, all else suitably matched?

  1. DNA becomes positively charged
  2. It always resolves larger chromosomes better
  3. It generally shifts toward better resolution of smaller fragments
  4. Size dependence vanishes
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. It generally shifts toward better resolution of smaller fragments

A tighter matrix tends to favour discrimination of smaller fragments; very large DNA can be strongly retarded. Neither DNA polarity nor the basis of sieving reverses.

5. What is the main reason for using a denaturing RNA gel when estimating transcript length?

  1. To remove phosphate groups
  2. To reduce effects of secondary structure
  3. To detect only one chosen transcript
  4. To convert RNA to DNA
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. To reduce effects of secondary structure

Denaturation reduces folding-dependent migration variation. Specific recognition requires another detection step; the RNA backbone need not be converted or dephosphorylated.

6. Which component defines the covalently crosslinked network in ordinary PAGE?

  1. Agarose helices
  2. Cellulose fibres only
  3. Polyacrylamide and crosslinker
  4. SDS micelles alone
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Polyacrylamide and crosslinker

Polyacrylamide chains connected by crosslinker form the matrix. SDS modifies proteins but is not the gel's structural network; agarose is another matrix.

7. A protein has pI 8.5 and is placed at pH 6.5. What is its usual intrinsic migration direction?

  1. Toward the cathode
  2. Toward the anode
  3. No motion regardless of field
  4. Toward higher molecular mass
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Toward the cathode

Below its pI the protein generally has positive net charge, so the force is toward the negative cathode. Zero net drift is expected near pI, not two pH units below it.

8. What does SDS principally accomplish in conventional denaturing protein PAGE?

  1. Hydrolyses all peptide bonds
  2. Supplies negative charge and disrupts native noncovalent structure
  3. Creates an immobilised pH gradient
  4. Specifically labels only the target sequence
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. Supplies negative charge and disrupts native noncovalent structure

SDS association reduces native charge and conformation differences. It does not normally cleave peptide bonds, focus proteins by pI or provide sequence-specific recognition.

9. Which treatment is needed specifically to break reducible disulfide linkages between protein chains?

  1. A size ladder
  2. A suitable reducing treatment
  3. A tracking dye
  4. Increasing detection exposure
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. A suitable reducing treatment

Reduction changes the covalent sulfur linkage. Detergent alone may disrupt noncovalent assembly, whereas ladder, dye and exposure are analytical references or detection variables.

10. In classical discontinuous Tris–glycine SDS-PAGE, the stacking region primarily does what?

  1. Separates all chains to their final masses
  2. Determines sequence by hybridisation
  3. Concentrates proteins into a narrow migrating zone
  4. Removes all modifications
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Concentrates proteins into a narrow migrating zone

A moving ionic boundary compresses the sample zone. The resolving region supplies mass discrimination; neither stage determines sequence or universally removes covalent modifications.

11. What does a tracking dye provide in a conventional protein gel?

  1. A direct protein mass value
  2. A reference for migration progress
  3. Proof that protein was loaded
  4. A specific antibody signal
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. A reference for migration progress

The dye indicates migration progress and may define the denominator in Rf. A separate ladder gives a mass calibration; neither dye nor ladder alone verifies a particular sample's loading.

12. Which variable is plotted vertically in the conventional protein 2D gel orientation?

  1. Genomic locus
  2. Apparent polypeptide mass
  3. Native catalytic activity
  4. Chromosome number
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. Apparent polypeptide mass

The second dimension is typically SDS-PAGE, with larger apparent chains nearer the origin. The first horizontal coordinate is pI.

13. Which condition defines a protein's isoelectric point?

  1. All ionisable groups are uncharged
  2. Its average net charge is zero
  3. The protein has maximum catalytic rate
  4. Its mass equals its charge
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. Its average net charge is zero

Opposite charges may remain present while summing to zero. An activity optimum and molecular mass are distinct properties.

14. A conventional IEF gradient is acidic near which electrode?

  1. Cathode
  2. Neither; pH is independent of position
  3. Both electrodes at the same pH
  4. Anode
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Anode

The acidic end is at the positive anode and the basic end at the negative cathode. This orientation supports restoring migration of amphoteric proteins.

15. Why is conventional SDS treatment generally applied after the IEF dimension in classical 2D protein gels?

  1. SDS would otherwise obscure pI-dependent charge reversal
  2. SDS stops all protein movement permanently
  3. IEF needs DNA as a template
  4. The second dimension measures enzyme turnover
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. SDS would otherwise obscure pI-dependent charge reversal

Strong anionic detergent association masks the intrinsic charge balance needed for focusing. After IEF, SDS supports apparent mass separation.

16. Which statement about a DNA ladder is correct?

  1. It provides a size reference for suitable comparable fragments
  2. It establishes exact sequence identity
  3. It proves every unknown lane has equal DNA mass
  4. It distinguishes every genotype automatically
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. It provides a size reference for suitable comparable fragments

A ladder calibrates migration position. Sequence, mass loading and genetic identity require additional information or controls.

17. Which of the following best describes an SSR marker?

  1. A difference in number of short tandem repeat units that may alter fragment length
  2. A universal protein mass standard
  3. A method for counting all chromosomal genes
  4. A dye front
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. A difference in number of short tandem repeat units that may alter fragment length

Repeat copy variation can give a length polymorphism at a defined locus. The other choices refer to unrelated measurements or calibration.

18. What is the usual information limitation of a dominant presence/absence marker in a diploid?

  1. The heterozygote and presence homozygote often cannot be distinguished directly
  2. It cannot detect any DNA
  3. All bands must be identical
  4. It always identifies both alleles separately
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. The heterozygote and presence homozygote often cannot be distinguished directly

A presence band may occur with one or two copies of the detectable allele. A codominant scoring system can distinguish both allelic products under appropriate conditions.

19. In an electropherogram, what does migration time normally represent?

  1. The time required to translate a protein
  2. The interval before a population reaches the detector
  3. A universal molecular mass independent of calibration
  4. The volume of sample
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. The interval before a population reaches the detector

Time is the direct separation coordinate in this detector arrangement. Mass or size is an inference requiring the appropriate mobility model or calibration.

20. Which claim is justified by one sharp band in a general protein stain?

  1. A dominant resolved population exists within the method's detection limits
  2. Complete chemical purity is proven
  3. The protein is necessarily a monomer
  4. The gene sequence is established
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. A dominant resolved population exists within the method's detection limits

Comigration and limited sensitivity prevent a claim of absolute purity. Native assembly and sequence are distinct from the observed apparent mass population.

Part C — Analytical and experimental reasoning

21. A molecule with mobility magnitude 3 × 10⁻⁸ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ moves for 500 s in a uniform 400 V m⁻¹ field. What is the predicted displacement magnitude?

  1. 0.6 mm
  2. 6 mm
  3. 60 mm
  4. 600 mm
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. 6 mm

Use d = μEt = 3 × 10⁻⁸ × 400 × 500 = 0.006 m = 6 mm. The other values differ by factors of ten; constant mobility and no bulk flow are assumed.

22. A gel is run at twice its previous field magnitude at unchanged conductivity. What is the predicted ratio of local electrical power density?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. 8
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 4

Power density is σE², so doubling E quadruples heat generation per unit volume. A linear relationship would apply to velocity under the simplest constant-mobility model, not to power density.

23. A DNA ladder has 2,000 bp at 30 mm and 500 bp at 70 mm. Under local semi-log linearity, what size is expected at 50 mm?

  1. 1,000 bp
  2. 625 bp
  3. 1,250 bp
  4. 1,500 bp
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. 1,000 bp

Halfway in distance is halfway in log size, so the geometric mean is √(2000 × 500) = 1000 bp. The arithmetic mean is 1250 bp and is not the correct model.

24. A 6,000 bp circular DNA is completely cleaved into 1,000 and 5,000 bp fragments. If there is one copy of each product per molecule and signal is linear in DNA mass, what is the larger-to-smaller band signal ratio?

  1. 1:1
  2. 1:5
  3. 5:1
  4. 6:1
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 5:1

Each molecule produces equal numbers of both fragments, but the 5,000 bp fragment contains five times the mass of the 1,000 bp fragment. The 6:1 ratio would compare parent length with the smaller fragment.

25. A purified plasmid has two bands before linearisation and one band after complete single-site linearisation. What is the strongest conclusion?

  1. Two different sequences are proven
  2. The initial bands are consistent with different conformations of one plasmid length
  3. The smaller apparent band was certainly RNA
  4. The DNA concentration doubled
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. The initial bands are consistent with different conformations of one plasmid length

Conformation can alter migration of identical-length molecules; a single linear band supports the topology hypothesis. Co-sized distinct sequences cannot be excluded by size evidence alone.

26. Two RNA extracts have equal concentration. One shows a low-size smear while the other shows sharp major rRNA features. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The concentrations must be incorrect
  2. The sharp lane contains no mRNA
  3. Every transcript in the smear has been identified
  4. The smear is consistent with reduced integrity, subject to preparation and separation controls
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. The smear is consistent with reduced integrity, subject to preparation and separation controls

Concentration and integrity are distinct. A smear is compatible with fragmentation but may also arise from analytical distortion. Neither pattern determines all transcript identities.

27. Protein A and B have equal native mobility at 5% gel. At 10% gel, A moves half as far as B. Which conclusion follows most directly?

  1. They have identical molecular masses
  2. B has no net charge
  3. A is definitely a tetramer
  4. A has a stronger relative matrix retardation over the tested interval
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. A has a stronger relative matrix retardation over the tested interval

At equal field and time the displacement compares mobility. The different response to concentration suggests different retardation; neither exact mass, assembly nor absolute charge follows.

28. An apparent 100 kDa native enzyme yields a 25 kDa reducing SDS band. Which interpretation is best?

  1. SDS hydrolysed the polypeptide into quarters
  2. Four chains are proven by the SDS lane alone
  3. The enzyme must be a monomer
  4. A homotetramer is consistent with the data, but native size accuracy and identity matter
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. A homotetramer is consistent with the data, but native size accuracy and identity matter

A 100/25 ratio supports four similar chains if native mass is reliable. SDS generally separates assemblies without peptide cleavage; the denaturing lane alone cannot determine stoichiometry.

29. A nonreducing SDS lane gives 80 kDa and a reducing lane gives 50 and 30 kDa. What is the most economical structural hypothesis?

  1. Two chains connected by reducible disulfide linkage
  2. A noncovalent complex already dissociated in both lanes
  3. A single intact 80 kDa chain with no modifications
  4. The proteins are necessarily unrelated contaminants
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Two chains connected by reducible disulfide linkage

Reduction produces two populations whose apparent masses sum to 80 kDa. This supports an interchain reducible linkage, whereas a purely noncovalent assembly should generally dissociate in both SDS conditions.

30. A 60 kDa nonreducing SDS band shifts to 65 kDa after reduction without new bands. Which claim is justified?

  1. The protein must split into two chains
  2. Reduction added exactly 5 kDa of atoms
  3. Reduction may change intrachain structure and apparent mobility
  4. No disulfide could have existed
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Reduction may change intrachain structure and apparent mobility

Intrachain disulfide reduction can change migration without splitting the peptide backbone. The apparent mass shift does not quantify added material and does not prove interchain linkage.

31. A protein marker of 80 kDa has Rf 0.20 and one of 20 kDa has Rf 0.60. A sample has Rf 0.40. What is its estimated mass under local log-linear calibration?

  1. 30 kDa
  2. 40 kDa
  3. 50 kDa
  4. 60 kDa
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. 40 kDa

The midpoint in log mass gives √(80 × 20) = 40 kDa. The arithmetic midpoint 50 kDa is inappropriate. The answer is an apparent mass estimate.

32. A fraction has total activity 240 units in 12 mg protein; the initial extract has 600 units in 60 mg. What are purification fold and yield?

  1. 5-fold and 20%
  2. 2-fold and 40%
  3. 2-fold and 20%
  4. 0.5-fold and 40%
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. 2-fold and 40%

Initial specific activity = 600/60 = 10; final = 240/12 = 20 units/mg. Fold = 2 and yield = 240/600 × 100 = 40%. Protein recovery alone is not activity yield.

33. A linear 120 mm IEF strip covers pH 4–10. A protein focuses 50 mm from the acidic end. Estimate pI.

  1. 6.5
  2. 6.0
  3. 5.5
  4. 7.5
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. 6.5

The gradient is 6/120 = 0.05 pH/mm. pI = 4 + 50 × 0.05 = 6.5, assuming correct linear calibration.

34. Two proteins have pI 5.9 and 6.1. In a linear 100 mm gradient, compare separation in pH 3–10 and pH 5–7 strips.

  1. 0.2 mm in both
  2. 10 mm and 2.86 mm
  3. 2.86 mm and 10 mm
  4. 7 mm and 2 mm
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 2.86 mm and 10 mm

Broad gradient: 0.2/(7/100) ≈ 2.86 mm. Narrow gradient: 0.2/(2/100) = 10 mm. Wider pH range reduces spatial separation for a fixed length.

35. A focused protein with pI 7 diffuses to a more acidic location. What happens in an ideal IEF gradient?

  1. It becomes negative and moves further toward the anode
  2. It acquires positive charge and returns toward the cathode direction
  3. Its mass doubles
  4. It is permanently trapped away from the focus
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. It acquires positive charge and returns toward the cathode direction

Below pI the net charge becomes positive; intrinsic motion toward the basic cathode is restoring. This explains finite-width focusing in competition with diffusion.

36. A protein's pI is 9.2, but the strip covers pH 4–8. What is the expected behaviour under usual polarity?

  1. It focuses at pH 8 exactly
  2. It focuses at pH 4 because it is basic
  3. It remains neutral everywhere
  4. It tends toward the basic end without a valid internal pI focus
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. It tends toward the basic end without a valid internal pI focus

The entire gradient lies below pI, so the protein tends to remain positive. It moves toward the cathode; an end accumulation must not be interpreted as an interior focus.

37. Spots X and Y share 50 kDa apparent mass but have pI 5.4 and 6.2. Z has pI 5.4 and 25 kDa. Which pair is horizontally separated at the same height?

  1. Y and Z only
  2. X and Z
  3. X and Y
  4. None
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. X and Y

The horizontal axis represents pI and the vertical axis apparent mass. X and Y differ in pI alone. X and Z differ vertically, and Y and Z differ in both coordinates.

38. Two independently confirmed forms of one protein shift from normalized signals A=80, B=20 to A=30, B=70. Both have comparable detection response. What is supported?

  1. A redistribution between forms at approximately constant combined signal
  2. A 100% increase in total protein
  3. A fivefold increase in molecular mass
  4. Loss of all protein
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. A redistribution between forms at approximately constant combined signal

The total is 100 in both conditions. The changed fractions support redistribution; the chemical cause remains to be verified and statistical inference requires replicates.

39. A spot increases from 150 to 225 normalized units. What is the fold change and percentage increase?

  1. 0.67-fold, 50%
  2. 1.5-fold, 150%
  3. 1.5-fold, 50%
  4. 2.25-fold, 75%
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 1.5-fold, 50%

225/150 = 1.5, and (225−150)/150 × 100 = 50%. Percent increase is relative to the initial amount, not the final amount or the fold expressed as a percentage.

40. A restriction-site allele yields 400 and 600 bp fragments, and a site-loss allele yields 1,000 bp. A sample shows all three. Which additional result most directly weakens a heterozygote interpretation?

  1. The site-loss control gives 1,000 bp
  2. The ladder is resolved
  3. The sample DNA is negatively charged
  4. A known site-containing homozygote also shows a 1,000 bp band under the same assay
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. A known site-containing homozygote also shows a 1,000 bp band under the same assay

An uncut band in the known site-containing homozygote supports incomplete cleavage as an alternative. The other observations are expected and do not challenge the genotype interpretation.

41. Two SSR alleles differ by five copies of a trinucleotide repeat and otherwise share flanking sequence. What size difference is predicted?

  1. 5 bp
  2. 15 bp
  3. 8 bp
  4. 25 bp
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. 15 bp

Five repeat units × three bases per repeat = 15 bp. The inference assumes no other length differences.

42. At a codominant marker, AB × AB produces 100 offspring. What are the expected band-pattern counts under Mendelian segregation?

  1. 33 of each with one missing
  2. 50 A-only, 0 both, 50 B-only
  3. 75 both, 25 B-only
  4. 25 A-only, 50 both, 25 B-only
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. 25 A-only, 50 both, 25 B-only

The genotypes AA:AB:BB occur in a 1:2:1 ratio. With reliable detection they produce the three respective band patterns. Actual counts can deviate by sampling variation.

43. In a capillary, μ_EOF = +4 × 10⁻⁸ and μ_ep = −5 × 10⁻⁸ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. What is the observed signed mobility?

  1. +9 × 10⁻⁸
  2. −1 × 10⁻⁸
  3. +1 × 10⁻⁸
  4. −9 × 10⁻⁸
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. −1 × 10⁻⁸

The signed sum is 4−5 = −1 in the given units. The intrinsic anionic movement outweighs cathode-directed EOF; adding absolute values is incorrect.

44. A capillary has total length 0.60 m, detector distance 0.45 m, voltage 15,000 V and migration time 180 s. Find apparent mobility magnitude.

  1. 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹
  2. 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹
  3. 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹
  4. 3.0 × 10⁻⁸ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹

μ_app = L_dL_t/(Vt) = 0.27/(2.7 × 10⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷. Both lengths are required; using one length twice changes the result.

45. Two electropherogram peaks occur at 8.0 and 8.5 min with baseline widths 0.3 and 0.2 min. What is R_s using the specified baseline-width equation?

  1. 0.5
  2. 1.0
  3. 2.0
  4. 4.0
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. 2.0

R_s = 2(0.5)/(0.3+0.2) = 2.0. Widths and time difference must use the same units. This index describes resolution, not identity.

Integrated, assertion–reason and SET-style practice

46. Assertion: Two DNA fragments with the same electrophoretic mobility necessarily share the same sequence. Reason: Electrophoresis measures physical migration rather than nucleotide order.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Assertion false, reason true

The assertion overstates the method: unrelated fragments can comigrate. The reason correctly distinguishes physical separation from sequence determination.

47. Assertion: A focused band has a finite width. Reason: Diffusion competes with charge-dependent restoring migration in IEF.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Both true and reason explains

Diffusion spreads the molecules and restoring drift opposes it, so the population is not an infinitely thin plane. Both statements are true and causally linked.

48. Assertion: Reducing SDS-PAGE establishes native enzyme activity. Reason: SDS disrupts many noncovalent interactions and reduction changes disulfide linkages.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Assertion false, reason true

The conditions commonly disrupt the native state and may impair activity. The reason is correct; native activity needs a compatible functional measurement.

49. Which combination correctly pairs objective and method? (I) approximate linear DNA length—agarose with linear ladder; (II) protein pI—IEF; (III) exact protein sequence—ordinary SDS-PAGE; (IV) activity-containing native zones—native gel with compatible functional detection.

  1. I and III
  2. II and III
  3. I, II and IV
  4. All four
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. I, II and IV

I, II and IV use the corresponding physical or functional discriminator. III is false because SDS-PAGE estimates apparent mass, not sequence.

50. A student claims that a broadening peak at the same height necessarily contains the same amount as before. What is the best critique?

  1. Peak height alone does not determine area; response and width matter
  2. The claim is always true
  3. Peak height is always equal to mass in daltons
  4. Only the migration time determines the amount
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Peak height alone does not determine area; response and width matter

A wider peak can have a different integrated signal even at equal height. Quantification also depends on detection response and baseline treatment.

51. A DNA lane is blank, while the ladder and positive control are normal. Which test best addresses the possibility of sample-specific analytical failure?

  1. Calling the sample negative without qualification
  2. A second photograph of the ladder
  3. Changing the assigned marker lengths
  4. An appropriate internal sample control
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. An appropriate internal sample control

The internal control tests whether the specific sample can produce the expected control feature. External controls alone cannot rule out failure in that sample.

52. Two bands of 400 bp and 1,200 bp have equal mass-proportional signals. What is the approximate molecule-number ratio 400:1200?

  1. 1:3
  2. 1:1
  3. 9:1
  4. 3:1
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. 3:1

At equal mass, molecule number is inversely proportional to fragment length; 1200/400 = 3. Equal signal does not imply equal molecule count.

53. A gel has total monomer 8 g, of which 0.4 g is crosslinker, in 100 mL. Find %T and %C.

  1. 8 and 5
  2. 8 and 0.4
  3. 7.6 and 5
  4. 5 and 8
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. 8 and 5

%T = 100×8/100 = 8. %C = 100×0.4/8 = 5. The crosslinker fraction is calculated relative to monomer mass, not total solution volume.

54. Which experiment most directly strengthens a hypothesis that two equal-mass, different-pI spots are differently phosphorylated forms of one protein?

  1. Assign both spots as phosphorylated solely from their mass
  2. Increase image contrast only
  3. Measure the DNA ladder again
  4. Confirm common protein identity and compare with a suitable modification-specific intervention
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Confirm common protein identity and compare with a suitable modification-specific intervention

Common identity excludes some alternative species; a modification-sensitive intervention tests the specific chemical explanation. Contrast and DNA markers cannot establish it.

55. A student observes one strong band after purifying a heteromeric enzyme. Which conclusion is safest?

  1. The enzyme is definitely a homomer
  2. The enzyme cannot contain any contaminant
  3. The method resolved one dominant population; independent assembly and purity evidence is still needed
  4. There were no other proteins in the initial extract
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. The method resolved one dominant population; independent assembly and purity evidence is still needed

A dominant band may include comigrating chains or contaminants below detection. It cannot prove native assembly, sample purity, or the composition of an earlier fraction.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Longer DNA must move faster because it carries more charge.

Why the assumption is tempting: The familiar force relation qE is recalled without the opposing drag and matrix effects.

Correct concept: The gel adds length-dependent retardation, and charge and friction both change with length.

Exam reasoning: An exam may give comparable linear calibration standards and ask for the larger fragment. For comparable linear DNA in the useful range, use the slower band's position rather than total charge.

2. Agarose concentration improves all size separations equally.

Why the assumption is tempting: Students remember that higher concentration means more sieving but overlook the finite size range.

Correct concept: A tighter network changes the useful separation interval, generally favouring smaller fragments.

Exam reasoning: A question may ask why large DNA remains near wells. Consider pore restriction, not an automatic absence of DNA.

3. An uncut plasmid can be sized exactly from a linear ladder.

Why the assumption is tempting: Linear DNA ladders look visually similar to bands of circular DNA, concealing the different topology.

Correct concept: Topology changes migration even at equal length.

Exam reasoning: Do not label open circles or supercoils by a linear calibration without qualifications; compare appropriate linear products.

4. A band is a unique molecule.

Why the assumption is tempting: A single sharp optical feature looks like a single chemically homogeneous substance.

Correct concept: One resolved feature may contain comigrating species.

Exam reasoning: If the exam asks for identity rather than size, require an independent recognition or identification method.

5. Equal fluorescence means equal molecule count.

Why the assumption is tempting: Signal intensity is often taught as quantity without distinguishing mass from molar amount.

Correct concept: Under a mass-proportional response, equal mass of unequal-length DNA gives different molecule counts.

Exam reasoning: Divide the inferred mass by relative fragment mass before comparing the number of molecules.

6. An RNA smear proves a particular nuclease acted.

Why the assumption is tempting: Fragmentation is a common cause of smearing, so alternative measurement failures are overlooked.

Correct concept: A smear may reflect degradation, overload, salt or other artefacts.

Exam reasoning: A cause-specific claim requires controls and comparison of independently prepared material.

7. All PAGE is denaturing.

Why the assumption is tempting: PAGE and SDS-PAGE are frequently used interchangeably in informal language.

Correct concept: PAGE names the matrix; native and denaturing conditions differ.

Exam reasoning: Read the description of detergents and treatments before choosing the measured property.

8. SDS breaks peptide bonds and disulfides automatically.

Why the assumption is tempting: Denaturation, reduction and backbone cleavage all change molecular structure but are distinct processes.

Correct concept: SDS disrupts many noncovalent interactions; disulfide reduction is a separate chemical treatment.

Exam reasoning: Compare reducing and nonreducing lanes to distinguish interchain linkages; do not infer cleavage of the peptide backbone.

9. One SDS band proves a monomeric protein.

Why the assumption is tempting: Students confuse a resolved chain-mass class with the number of chains in an assembly.

Correct concept: Identical chains from an oligomer can occupy one apparent mass band.

Exam reasoning: Combine chain-mass evidence with an independent native assembly measurement.

10. SDS-PAGE measures exact true protein mass.

Why the assumption is tempting: Marker labels in kDa suggest a direct mass measurement rather than a calibrated estimate.

Correct concept: It estimates apparent chain mass based on a calibration and near-uniform detergent association.

Exam reasoning: Unexpected migration may reflect composition or modifications rather than incorrect sequence length.

11. The stacking gel separates proteins primarily by size.

Why the assumption is tempting: The difference in pore size is more visually obvious than the moving ionic boundary.

Correct concept: Stacking compresses the starting zone through a discontinuous ionic system; the resolving region develops mass discrimination.

Exam reasoning: A question about improved resolution should distinguish narrow starting width from the sieving mechanism.

12. At pI there are no charged groups.

Why the assumption is tempting: Net charge is mistaken for the absence of individual charges.

Correct concept: At pI the average net charge is zero; individual charges can remain.

Exam reasoning: Distinguish a zwitterion from an uncharged chemical structure.

13. A protein below its pI travels toward the anode.

Why the assumption is tempting: Students recall that acidic proteins migrate to the anode without comparing pH with pI.

Correct concept: Below pI it is generally positive and travels toward the cathode in intrinsic electrophoretic motion.

Exam reasoning: Draw the acidic anode and basic cathode before predicting restoring drift.

14. IEF necessarily preserves native protein activity.

Why the assumption is tempting: The word focusing is incorrectly associated with gentle native-state preservation.

Correct concept: IEF measures charge balance in the conditions used; some focusing conditions are denaturing.

Exam reasoning: Do not infer activity from pI or from the label isoelectric focusing.

15. A pH gradient endpoint is a reliable pI assignment for any accumulated protein.

Why the assumption is tempting: The gradient label is mistaken for proof that equilibrium occurred at its endpoint.

Correct concept: A protein outside the gradient range may accumulate at an end without achieving an internal focus.

Exam reasoning: Check that the proposed pI lies within the range before using a positional calibration.

16. A horizontal 2D spot train proves phosphorylation.

Why the assumption is tempting: Phosphorylation is a familiar example of charge modification, encouraging premature assignment.

Correct concept: Charge variants or unrelated species may have comparable mass and different pI.

Exam reasoning: Seek identity and modification-sensitive evidence before choosing the biochemical cause.

17. All anions move toward the anode in a capillary instrument.

Why the assumption is tempting: Ordinary slab-gel motion is applied to a capillary without considering the moving solvent.

Correct concept: Observed velocity includes both signed intrinsic mobility and EOF.

Exam reasoning: Calculate the signed sum before predicting the direction relative to the detector.

18. Electropherogram peak height is interchangeable with peak area.

Why the assumption is tempting: Peak size is casually used to mean height or area regardless of its precise definition.

Correct concept: Height and area depend differently on peak width and response.

Exam reasoning: For abundance questions, identify the quantitative measure, baseline and validated response model.

19. One SSR band invariably establishes homozygosity.

Why the assumption is tempting: The ideal codominant model is memorised without the detection assumptions.

Correct concept: Allele dropout or same-size alleles can conceal variation.

Exam reasoning: Check the locus assumptions, controls and resolving power before assigning the genotype.

20. An absent band proves the biological target is absent.

Why the assumption is tempting: Absence of a detected signal is confused with absence of the biological molecule.

Correct concept: Sample failure, insufficient signal and migration outside the useful range are alternative explanations.

Exam reasoning: Use positive and sample-specific controls, and qualify the negative conclusion.

Revision and active recall

Chapter synthesis

Electrophoresis converts charge-dependent movement into spatial or temporal distributions. Agarose and polyacrylamide offer different sieving properties. Native separation retains some molecular organisation; SDS-PAGE reduces charge and structural variation to estimate apparent chain mass, and reducing conditions can reveal disulfide-linked chains. IEF focuses amphoteric species at their pI; 2D protein gels combine pI and apparent mass. Molecular marker analysis uses validated fragment states rather than band identity alone. Capillary detection records migration time, with observed motion potentially including bulk flow. Standards, controls, response calibration and orthogonal evidence define the strength of each conclusion.

Essential terminology

Electrophoretic mobility
Signed drift velocity relative to the medium per unit electric field.
Molecular sieving
Differential retardation by a porous matrix.
Native PAGE
Polyacrylamide separation under conditions intended to retain some native interactions and conformation.
SDS-PAGE
Denaturing separation in which detergent-associated chains are resolved principally by apparent molecular mass.
Reducing conditions
Conditions that disrupt disulfide bonds, distinct from detergent denaturation alone.
Isoelectric point (pI)
pH at which average net charge is zero under specified conditions.
Isoelectric focusing (IEF)
Concentration of amphoteric species near their pI in a pH gradient.
Codominant marker
Marker system capable of distinguishing both allelic states in a heterozygote.
Electroosmotic flow (EOF)
Electric-field-driven bulk liquid motion at a charged interface.
Electropherogram
Detector response plotted against migration time or a calibrated separation coordinate.

Examination revision strategy

First identify the measured property, then determine the migration direction and matrix or pH conditions. Draw a lane or axis sketch before interpreting a pattern. Perform calculations with explicit units, calibration assumptions and the proper denominator. Finally separate the observation from the biological claim and specify the control that would discriminate alternative explanations.
