A seed is a coordinated developmental system

A seed is often introduced as a miniature plant enclosed in a protective covering. That description identifies the future plant but leaves out an essential part of the developmental problem. At the moment of fertilization, the embryo has neither an effective root system to acquire minerals nor a fully functioning shoot to acquire carbon by photosynthesis. Its cells must nevertheless obtain raw materials, maintain their internal chemical environment, divide, establish tissue identities and grow in an ordered manner. The ovule contains several tissues that can help meet these requirements, but these tissues must also change their activities as development proceeds. The formation of a viable seed therefore requires much more than the formation of a zygote.

Consider a dry pea and a maize grain. When a pea seed is opened, two large fleshy seed leaves, called cotyledons, occupy most of its internal volume. A small embryonic axis lies between them. The cotyledons belong to the embryo itself, so a large fraction of the dry matter of the seed is contained in the future plant. In a maize grain, however, a large storage region surrounds a much smaller embryo situated to one side. Much of that storage region belongs to a different developmental lineage called the endosperm. Both seeds contain substantial nutritional reserves, but the tissue responsible for storing them is different. The contrast immediately raises a question: are the reserves of a seed necessarily embryonic, or can another tissue produce and retain them on behalf of the embryo?

The distinction between the seed and the fruit is the first necessary anatomical distinction. In ordinary flowering plant reproduction the ovule develops into the seed, whereas the ovary contributes to the fruit. Additional floral tissues may participate in fruit formation in some species. In a pea pod, the pod wall and the coats surrounding individual peas therefore do not represent the same structure. The pod is the surrounding fruit, while each seed has its own protective coat. A cereal grain is a special case because the fruit wall lies closely against and is fused with the seed coat. The term grain is practical, but it can conceal the different origins of the layers that cover the embryo and its reserve tissue.

Before fertilization, an ovule is already a differentiated part of the maternal plant. Its integuments surround the nucellus, within which the female gametophyte develops. The egg and the central cell have distinct identities inside that gametophyte. At fertilization the egg gives rise to the embryo lineage, and the central cell gives rise to the endosperm lineage. Neither fusion produces the integuments, which were present before fertilization. Instead, the integuments change from ovular coverings into a specialized seed coat. The three components originate at different times and need not contain the same number of chromosome sets. The relationships between them, rather than the growth of any one tissue in isolation, determine whether a seed can reach a functional condition.

It is helpful to distinguish a developmental function from the tissue that performs it. Protection may require a mechanically resistant coat, a hydrated external mucilage or chemical compounds in the outer layers. Nutritional support may require transport, temporary accumulation, permanent storage or remobilization. A developing embryo needs each of these functions at appropriate stages, but the botanical structure responsible may differ among species. The endosperm of a pea may support early embryo development and then be largely consumed; the endosperm of maize continues to hold reserves at maturity. The disappearance of a tissue does not erase its earlier developmental contribution.

Three questions will organize the study of seed formation. First, where did each compartment come from? This is a question of lineage and genetic constitution. Second, how does each compartment change as development proceeds? This is a question of division, growth, differentiation and timing. Third, how do the compartments affect one another? This is a question of material transfer, signaling and physical constraint. These distinctions are especially useful in interpreting mutant seeds, because a visible defect in one compartment may have been caused by an earlier failure in another.

Double fertilization establishes two offspring lineages

The term double fertilization refers to two separate fusion events involving the two sperm cells delivered by a pollen tube. In the conventional Polygonum-type embryo sac, one sperm nucleus joins the egg nucleus and the other joins the central-cell nuclear contribution. The egg and central cell remain distinct female gametophyte cells. Their developmental programs are not interchangeable. After fertilization the egg becomes a zygote, while the fertilized central cell begins endosperm development. The central-cell event does not produce a second ordinary embryo.

To follow the genetics, begin with a diploid sporophyte carrying two homologous sets of chromosomes. Meiosis in the female reproductive lineage normally produces a reduced megaspore with one set. Three rounds of mitosis in the common monosporic embryo-sac pattern generate nuclei descended from that megaspore. In the mature embryo sac the egg contains one chromosome set, and the two polar nuclei in the central cell each contain one. Their union, if it has already occurred, gives a central-cell nucleus with two maternal sets. Meiosis in the male reproductive lineage similarly gives rise to reduced pollen nuclei, and subsequent mitotic divisions form the sperm. This is why the conventional diagram labels the egg and each sperm as n, but the central-cell nuclear contribution as 2n.

The zygote acquires one maternal and one paternal chromosome set. Its mitotic descendants form the embryo proper and supporting embryonic structures such as the suspensor. The primary endosperm nucleus acquires two maternal sets and one paternal set under the assumptions just described. Its initial genomic constitution is therefore triploid, with an unequal contribution of parental genomes. Mitotic descendants normally retain this parental origin even when a subsequent DNA replication cycle temporarily changes their DNA content. The seed coat, by contrast, inherits both chromosome sets of the maternal sporophyte and receives no nuclear genome from the particular pollen donor.

A subtle point is that the two maternal nuclear contributions to the central cell are not equivalent to two independently chosen eggs. In the monosporic Polygonum-type embryo sac they normally descend mitotically from the same megaspore. At a locus at which the female plant is heterozygous, an ordinary reduced egg may carry either maternal allele. The polar nuclei in its own gametophyte ordinarily carry the same allele as that egg. Consequently, a maternal plant heterozygous A/a crossed to a pollen donor homozygous a/a can produce an Aa embryo associated with an AAa endosperm, or an aa embryo associated with an aaa endosperm. The maternal seed coat remains A/a around both classes. This relationship follows from cell lineage, not simply from counting chromosomes.

The seed coat being maternal does not mean that every allele in it came from the grandmother of the embryo. The maternal plant itself inherited genes from its own two parents. Here the adjective maternal identifies the generation and the tissue lineage relative to the current fertilization. A seed coat cell possesses the sporophytic genome of the plant on which the seed develops, including whatever alleles that plant inherited in the preceding generation. A genetic experiment must specify which generation is under consideration before assigning a maternal effect.

Ploidy and DNA content must also be distinguished. Ploidy counts chromosome sets. DNA content measures the quantity of DNA relative to a specified reference. If a reduced unreplicated nucleus has DNA content C, a typical triploid endosperm nucleus before S phase has 3C. Following replication it contains 6C, yet it still has three sets of chromosomes as counted by centromeres until the mitotic division separates sister chromatids. Endoreduplication can produce nuclei of still greater DNA content without generating an extra set of parental fertilization events. A DNA histogram therefore cannot be interpreted without considering developmental cell-cycle state.

The familiar 2n embryo, 3n endosperm and 2n coat arrangement is a useful default only when the problem specifies or implies ordinary diploid parents and the common embryo-sac organization. The biological basis for triploidy is the contribution of two maternal chromosome sets to the central cell plus one paternal set. Other female gametophyte types can contribute different numbers of sets to the central cell. Polyploid parents also change the absolute counts. To reason safely, reconstruct the gametic contributions rather than memorizing a rule that every seed must contain a triploid tissue.

How cytological evidence revealed the origin of endosperm

The discovery that an ovule receives two male nuclear contributions altered the understanding of flowering plant reproduction. The first fertilization event alone could account for the formation of an embryo, but not for the unusual genetic constitution of the nutritive tissue. Cytologists examined successive stages of pollen-tube entry and the changes in nuclear position inside fixed ovules. Sergei Nawaschin reported double fertilization in 1898, and Léon Guignard independently investigated the phenomenon shortly afterward. These observations established a cellular basis for distinguishing the origin of the embryo from the origin of the endosperm.

A microscope image provides a limited form of evidence. Two nuclei close together do not establish that they have already fused. Conversely, finding one enlarged nucleus does not by itself identify which genomes it contains. The strength of the classical interpretation came from comparing developmental stages and relating the positions of sperm nuclei to the positions of the egg and central-cell nuclei. The sequence explained why one developing structure was associated with the egg position while another began within the central cell. Subsequent genetic and cytological work could then test the consequences of the fusion model.

A useful modern extension is to track a distinguishable paternal allele. Imagine a maternal plant carrying only an allele M at a marker locus and a paternal plant carrying only allele P. A tissue-specific assay can determine whether the paternal marker occurs in the embryo, endosperm and seed coat. The double-fertilization model predicts P in the embryo and endosperm, but not in an uncontaminated sample of the coat. If all three samples contain P, the first question is whether the samples were cleanly separated. A small amount of adhering endosperm could contaminate a coat sample. Finding paternal DNA in a tissue preparation is not identical to showing that the tissue's cells inherited paternal chromosomes.

The marker can be used to distinguish the presence of an allele from its expression. A pollen donor carrying a reporter gene may contribute that gene to both fertilization products. If the reporter is visible only in the endosperm, the result may reflect an endosperm-specific promoter rather than selective entry of the paternal genome into the central cell. Conversely, a paternal allele may be present but transcriptionally repressed. DNA assays and RNA or protein assays answer different questions. This distinction becomes particularly important when studying genomic imprinting, because imprinting is a difference in expression according to parental origin, not an absence of the silent allele.

The genetic constitution of endosperm also shows why comparison with the nutrient tissue of a gymnosperm requires care. In many gymnosperms the embryo is surrounded by a nutritive female gametophyte whose formation precedes fertilization. In flowering plants the conventional endosperm begins after central-cell fertilization and includes a paternal genomic contribution. The two tissues can both supply resources, but their origin and genetic relationships differ. Their similar function is not sufficient evidence that they are the same developmental structure.

Historical observation established the separate lineages, but it did not explain how the fertilized central cell communicates with the maternal integuments or why the embryo can arrest when its surrounding endosperm develops abnormally. These later questions require perturbations, such as changing the activity of a hormone pathway in one compartment while observing responses elsewhere. In this way the study of seeds progresses from describing tissue origin to testing the mechanisms that integrate several lineages into one functional reproductive structure.

Early endosperm development and the nuclear pattern

Following fertilization, the primary endosperm nucleus enters a developmental program that differs from the first divisions of the zygote. In the nuclear type of endosperm development, early divisions of the nucleus are not accompanied by partitioning of the entire cytoplasm into individual daughter cells. Repeated nuclear divisions can therefore produce a multinucleate compartment. This arrangement is commonly called a coenocyte or a syncytial endosperm. In this context the shared cytoplasm arose by repeated nuclear division without immediate cytokinesis, rather than by fusion of many previously independent cells.

The cellular organization must not be confused with an absence of a plasma membrane. The central cell is already bounded externally by its own membrane. During free nuclear development, what is initially missing is a complete set of internal partitions separating the newly produced nuclei. A large vacuole often occupies the central region, with the nuclei situated in the surrounding cytoplasmic layer. The cytoskeleton helps position nuclei and organize the cytoplasm associated with each one. Thus a drawing of scattered nuclei represents a structured multinucleate cell, not an unorganized collection of naked DNA inside the ovule.

The distinction between mitosis and cytokinesis explains how this state can arise. The genetic material is duplicated and segregated by the mitotic machinery, so two daughter nuclei can be produced. In a typical plant somatic division, cytokinesis additionally creates a new partition between the daughter cytoplasms. A cell plate develops through vesicle delivery and expansion within a cytoskeletal framework. Delaying the partitioning phase permits additional nuclear cycles in a shared cytoplasm. The nuclear endosperm therefore illustrates the fact that nuclear multiplication and the formation of cellular boundaries are related but separable events.

Under a simplified mathematical model, each nucleus undergoes one successful mitosis during every round and no nuclei die. Starting with one primary nucleus, the number after k complete rounds is 2 to the power k. After five rounds there would be thirty-two nuclei. If no internal cell walls have formed, this does not mean that there are thirty-two separate endosperm cells. If a tissue is fully cellularized after the same five rounds, with one nucleus enclosed in each new cell, then there may be thirty-two cells. The same nuclear count can consequently describe two very different tissue architectures.

Actual developing endosperm rarely behaves like a perfectly synchronized mathematical population throughout its entire growth. Nuclei near the embryo or the chalazal region may enter and leave division cycles at different times. Nuclear migration, regional growth and later cellularization introduce differences that a simple doubling equation cannot capture. The model is useful for a defined examination question with idealized assumptions, but the number observed in a real section does not automatically reveal an exact integer number of completed division rounds. A section may also pass through only part of a three-dimensional structure.

A common developmental advantage of nuclear multiplication without immediate partitioning is the possibility of increasing the number of active nuclei and establishing nuclear domains within an expanding compartment before constructing all internal walls. Shared cytoplasm can permit local movement of metabolites and signals, although a large multinucleate cell is not necessarily chemically uniform. Nuclear positions, vacuolar geometry and the organization of the cytoplasm create regional differences. The timing of cellularization matters because the later tissue must form differentiated cells that can regulate transport and interact with the embryo.

In Arabidopsis, early endosperm development produces regions associated with the micropylar end near the embryo, a peripheral region and a chalazal region toward the maternal nutrient entry side. These names describe positions relative to the ovule rather than independent fertilization products. The regions can exhibit different developmental schedules and gene-expression patterns. The micropylar endosperm is especially relevant to the immediate environment of the embryo, while the chalazal end is associated with maternal interactions. The entire endosperm, despite this regional organization, originates from the central-cell fertilization event.

Cellularization, cellular endosperm and helobial development

Cellularization is the process by which a multinucleate endosperm acquires boundaries that separate nuclei and associated cytoplasm into cells. It is not synonymous with the first nuclear division. During a free nuclear stage, many nuclei may already exist, but the internal geometry has not been divided into ordinary cells. Cellularization changes that geometry by providing interfaces across which the movement of substances can be controlled. It also provides a structural basis for different cell identities to be maintained in neighboring parts of the tissue.

In Arabidopsis and the cereals, nuclear endosperm commonly proceeds from a coenocytic phase to a cellularized phase. Walls form around nuclear domains and extend so as to delimit individual cells. The progression can be spatially uneven; one domain may have advanced farther toward cellular organization than another. Cytoskeletal arrays and membrane trafficking provide the physical machinery needed to organize developing walls. The timing and geometry of these events are controlled by the species-specific developmental program, not by an obligatory universal number of nuclei.

Some flowering plants exhibit cellular endosperm development instead. Here cytokinesis accompanies the first division of the primary endosperm nucleus and subsequent divisions. The endosperm therefore develops as a population of cells without the extended free nuclear phase characteristic of the nuclear type. The genetic origin is unchanged: the endosperm still arises from the fertilized central cell in the conventional sexual pathway. The term cellular describes the mode of division, not the ploidy or the source of the chromosomes.

Helobial endosperm represents another pattern. The first division is accompanied by a wall that separates the embryo-sac space into two chambers, commonly a larger micropylar chamber and a smaller chalazal chamber. Further divisions in at least one chamber proceed without immediate cytokinesis, and the behavior of the two chambers can differ. Some members of monocot groups show this pattern. It is often taught as an intermediate form, but that description must be interpreted developmentally: the first partition precedes later free nuclear divisions. It does not imply that one entire half always remains permanently cellular while the other remains permanently nuclear.

There are consequently two distinct questions that should be asked of any diagram of endosperm. What happened at the first division? What happened during subsequent divisions? A wall following the first division together with free nuclei in one of the resulting compartments is strong evidence for helobial development. Many nuclei dispersed within an initially unpartitioned cytoplasm are characteristic of a nuclear phase. Repeated walls accompanying successive divisions indicate cellular development. A diagram showing only the final cellular tissue cannot necessarily distinguish whether the cells were produced from the beginning or emerged after a coenocytic phase.

The coconut illustrates another reason to separate the developmental type from the mature physical appearance. The liquid inside a young coconut is associated with the liquid endosperm phase, whereas the solid white tissue develops as cellular endosperm. Both belong to the endosperm system. The hard outer shell of the coconut fruit is not the same as the seed coat. Its presence does not change the origin of the internal storage tissue. Examinations frequently test such organ-origin distinctions because names based on everyday food use do not always correspond to anatomical or developmental categories.

Cellularization is not simply a sign that development is running on schedule; it can be required for embryo survival. A normal embryo growing within a persistently abnormal coenocyte may encounter altered nutritional or osmotic conditions. Alternatively, premature formation of walls may restrict a phase of expansion that normally increases seed capacity. A useful developmental hypothesis must therefore predict the consequences of changing the timing in either direction. It is not sufficient to say that earlier cellularization is always beneficial or that more nuclear divisions always produce a healthier seed.

Embryo growth depends on the developing environment

The preceding chapter established the basic geometry of plant embryogenesis. A zygote develops polarity and, in the representative Arabidopsis model, divides asymmetrically to initiate apical and basal lineages. Subsequent divisions establish the embryo proper, the suspensor and the characteristic embryonic body plan. Those events explain where the future shoot, root and cotyledons arise. They do not on their own explain where the expanding embryo obtains the substances it must use. For this question, the relationship between embryo and endosperm is central.

At the beginning of seed formation the embryo is a relatively small sink. A sink is a tissue that imports or consumes resources rather than providing a net export of photosynthetic products. The endosperm can proliferate and expand around the embryo while the embryo is still small. The maternal tissue must supply water, sugars, amino acids and mineral nutrients into the developing seed. The endosperm is positioned to receive, process and redistribute these materials. It is therefore more informative to think of it as a dynamic developmental interface than simply a bag of stored food.

The embryo initially has a limited contact surface with the surrounding tissues. The suspensor, which is an embryonic structure rather than part of the endosperm, can help position the embryo and participate in nutrient or signal exchange in many species. Its exact contribution varies according to embryonic morphology. As the embryo grows, the extent and character of its contact with endosperm change. The early and later embryo may consequently depend on different routes of nutrient movement and respond to different environmental cues.

The endosperm may also influence development by modifying the physical environment. A plant cell expands when water entry produces sufficient turgor and when its wall is capable of yielding. Surrounding tissues constrain this expansion. An expanding embryo displaces endosperm, and the endosperm in turn responds to the changing geometry. The reduction of endosperm volume in a developing dicot seed is not explained merely by compression. Cellular breakdown, turnover of reserves and transfer of material can contribute. The balance between these processes determines the relative sizes of the embryo and nutritive compartment.

In many grain legumes, including pea and bean, the cotyledons become major storage organs. Their cells receive imported carbon and nitrogen, and convert these resources into reserves appropriate to the species. The maternal plant continues to supply resources during development, but the main site of accumulated reserve changes as the embryo expands. A mature seed dominated by cotyledons therefore need not have developed without endosperm. Its appearance records the endpoint of a process in which an initially distinct supporting compartment has largely disappeared.

The embryo can sometimes continue development when removed from a defective seed and supplied with a suitable artificial environment. This observation is conceptually important because it distinguishes a defect intrinsic to the embryo from a failure of its normal developmental surroundings. If an isolated embryo grows while its genetically similar counterpart within an abnormal seed arrests, the surrounding tissue becomes a strong candidate for the primary defect. Rescue is not guaranteed, and an unsuccessful rescue is not proof that the embryo's genotype was defective; the experimental environment itself may be inadequate. Controlled comparisons are required.

The correct way to interpret co-development is to follow both compartments through time. A photograph of a mature aborted seed cannot reveal which tissue failed first. A series of observations can establish that an endosperm defect appeared before embryonic growth arrest, but temporal order alone does not demonstrate a causal mechanism. Stronger evidence comes from a tissue-specific perturbation, a rescue of the altered pathway or both. The developmental system makes this type of reasoning particularly valuable because defects are transmitted across tissue boundaries.

From maternal photosynthesis to seed reserves

An embryo cannot acquire all the carbon required for its growth directly from its immediate surroundings. Carbon enters the developmental system chiefly because photosynthetically active tissues of the maternal plant fix carbon dioxide and export assimilates. The developing seed receives a share of these products. A plant physiologist refers to a net exporting region as a source and a net importing region as a sink. Mature leaves often function as sources, while growing seeds are strong sinks. These are functional descriptions, not permanent identities of organs: a young expanding leaf may initially import carbon and later become an exporter.

Sucrose is a major carbohydrate transported over long distances in the phloem of many flowering plants. Movement through the maternal vascular system brings sucrose and other solutes to the vicinity of developing ovules. The supply route does not simply continue as an uninterrupted vascular strand through the endosperm and into the embryonic vascular system. The maternal and filial compartments have distinct developmental origins and their tissue interfaces commonly interrupt direct cytoplasmic continuity. The embryo can differentiate its own vascular tissues without establishing continuous sieve tubes with the mother plant. This distinction creates the need for specialized unloading and uptake mechanisms.

Two useful terms describe the possible paths of short-distance transport. The symplast consists of connected living cytoplasms linked by plasmodesmata. A solute can move through such connections where the channels permit its passage. The apoplast includes cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membrane. Moving from a maternal cell into a genetically separate filial compartment often requires passage through an extracellular interface. A molecule released from one cell must then be taken up by another. Solute availability in the extracellular space and the capacity of the membranes on both sides influence the resulting transport rate.

A plasma membrane consists principally of a lipid bilayer with associated proteins. Polar sugars do not readily traverse the hydrophobic interior of that bilayer unaided. Transport proteins permit movement according to their specific mechanisms. Some transporters allow facilitated movement down a chemical potential gradient, whereas others use the gradient of an ion to drive uphill solute accumulation. Energy for ion gradients may ultimately come from ATP-dependent pumps. A seed's capacity to import carbon therefore depends on living cells with active metabolism, not only on the concentration of sucrose in maternal sap.

An incoming sucrose molecule can be transported intact or hydrolyzed into glucose and fructose, depending on the location and activity of enzymes and transporters. Invertase catalyzes sucrose hydrolysis using water. The products can be taken up by cells equipped with suitable sugar transport systems. Hydrolysis also changes the relative amounts of sucrose and hexoses in the interface, potentially influencing osmotic conditions and sugar-responsive developmental pathways. The reaction is not an additional source of carbon: its two hexose products contain the same total number of carbon atoms as the original sucrose molecule.

Once sugar enters a developing cell, its carbon has several possible fates. It can supply substrates for respiration, contribute building blocks to growing cellular structures or enter storage pathways. A seed cell must expend energy to maintain membranes, synthesize enzymes and produce the polymers required for growth. A high rate of carbon import does not guarantee an equally high increase in storage mass, because part of the imported carbon is oxidized or used for nonstorage structural materials. This becomes important when a mutation changes respiratory demand or the efficiency with which imported compounds are retained.

Nitrogen and mineral nutrients are also necessary. An embryo cannot construct proteins from carbohydrate alone because amino acids contain nitrogen. Phosphate is required for nucleotides and phosphorylated metabolites, and mineral ions participate in numerous cellular functions. The maternal supply and the capacity of the seed to import and assimilate nutrients must therefore be coordinated. The endosperm may temporarily hold these nutrients and later transfer some of them to the embryo; persistent storage tissues retain a share of the reserves until the next developmental phase.

A simplified carbon budget can illustrate why the location and timing of reserve accumulation matter. If a seed receives 100 units of carbon over an interval and loses 25 through respiration, then at most 75 units can be retained in new biomass if there are no other gains or losses. Suppose 45 retained units accumulate in endosperm and 30 in embryo. This does not mean that only 30 units ever passed through the endosperm, because some carbon may have entered the endosperm and subsequently moved to the embryo. A net change in the size of a compartment is different from the total throughput of a pathway. Measurements of final dry mass alone cannot reconstruct every transport step.

Transfer cells and the maize nutrient interface

The challenge of moving material across a maternal–filial interface can be understood by examining a developing maize kernel. Maternal tissues near the base of the kernel provide the route by which vascular resources arrive. A specialized part of the endosperm adjacent to this region differentiates into the basal endosperm transfer layer, often abbreviated BETL. Its location places it at an important exchange surface. The rest of the endosperm can then use imported material for growth and storage. If the basal interface has limited transport capacity, increasing the supply of sucrose elsewhere in the maternal plant may not remove the restriction.

A transfer cell often develops wall ingrowths projecting into the cell's interior. The plasma membrane follows the contours of these wall surfaces. Imagine a membrane spread over a smooth rectangular area and another membrane folded along many inward projections. The folded surface may provide substantially more area in a similar overall volume. If transport proteins are distributed over that membrane, the increased area creates additional capacity for exchange. Wall ingrowths are therefore a particularly clear example of a structure adapted to a physiological function.

A larger area is not by itself proof of a faster flux. Transport depends on protein abundance, activity, substrate concentration and driving forces as well. If a cell has many wall ingrowths but few working transporters, its effective exchange capacity may remain low. Similarly, a mutation that compromises energy metabolism could reduce the functioning of active uptake systems even if the structural area is normal. An experiment that measures only the dimensions of wall ingrowths must therefore be complemented by a functional assay before claiming that nutrient uptake changed by the same proportion.

The maize Miniature1 locus provides a classic genetic example. A product of this locus, a cell wall invertase known as INCW2, is associated with basal endosperm transfer cells. Loss of normal enzyme function is linked with reduced kernel growth and abnormalities of the transfer-cell interface. The protein catalyzes the conversion of sucrose into glucose and fructose. In the developing seed, changing this reaction can affect the distribution of available sugars at the exchange surface and the nutritional or signaling conditions experienced by neighboring cells. The resulting phenotype connects a local biochemical change with the development of an entire kernel.

The distinction between a structural lesion and a metabolic lesion is useful here. A mutant transfer layer could form with an initially normal geometry but exhibit abnormal sugar composition because the enzyme that normally modifies incoming solute is absent. Alternatively, failure of sugar-responsive development could alter the wall architecture, which might further reduce exchange. These mechanisms can reinforce each other. Observing both an abnormal wall and altered sugar concentrations in a mature mutant does not establish which change happened first. A developmental time series is required.

A more powerful genetic test compares a mutant with a line in which normal gene function is restored at the relevant interface. If transfer-cell morphology and kernel growth improve together, the connection between the gene and the phenotype is strengthened. To interpret rescue precisely, it is necessary to check that the restored gene is active in the intended tissue and at the appropriate developmental time. Expression everywhere in the plant might rescue kernel growth indirectly by changing the maternal source supply rather than by directly restoring endosperm function.

In an examination, a reduced kernel size is rarely sufficient to identify a particular gene or transport mechanism. Several defects can converge on that phenotype. The maternal vascular supply might be inadequate, the transfer layer might have reduced uptake capacity, the endosperm might fail to proliferate or the embryo might lack the ability to assimilate imported material. A question giving the localization of an enzyme defect provides the extra information required to distinguish these possibilities. A reasonable inference is that a lesion localized to the basal transfer layer is capable of impairing the whole seed through defective exchange; the exact fraction of the phenotype attributable to each downstream effect cannot be inferred from size alone.

Differentiation of persistent cereal endosperm

Although the early cereal endosperm starts as a multinucleate compartment in familiar crop examples, its mature architecture contains several distinct cell populations. Cellularization provides the physical basis for these populations. Cells at the periphery experience a different environment from those at the center or near the embryo. Their developmental fates become specialized for storage, exchange or later mobilization of reserves. A mature grain consequently cannot be described accurately as a uniform mass of identical triploid storage cells.

The starchy endosperm forms much of the volume of a maize or wheat grain. During development, its cells accumulate starch in amyloplasts and store proteins in specialized intracellular structures. Starch is a polymer of glucose whose storage form allows a large amount of carbohydrate to be retained with fewer independently dissolved particles than the same number of free glucose molecules. The synthesis and organization of starch, protein and other reserves require specific metabolic machinery. The physical properties of the reserve tissue reflect the distribution and packing of these materials as well as the architecture of the cells that contain them.

The outer aleurone layer is distinct from the internal starchy endosperm. Its cells are also endosperm-derived, not part of the maternal seed coat, even though they lie immediately beneath the external maternal layers. Aleurone cells often remain alive in the mature cereal grain, whereas much of the starchy endosperm loses viability during its normal differentiation. A living aleurone layer can respond during the subsequent germination program to signals and produce enzymes involved in reserve mobilization. This latter function belongs to the next chapter, but its developmental preparation explains why the peripheral layer retains characteristics different from those of the internal storage tissue.

The basal transfer layer described previously is another specialization of the maize endosperm. In addition, a region immediately surrounding the embryo develops properties related to its close interaction with the embryonic tissue. These named regions are useful because a spatially restricted mutation can influence particular stages of seed formation without abolishing the entire endosperm lineage. A defect in the aleurone might leave early storage accumulation relatively intact yet affect later reserve use. A defect at the basal interface can instead restrict import during seed filling. A defect near the embryo may alter embryo growth despite substantial reserve accumulation farther away.

Some cereal endosperm cells undergo endoreduplication during differentiation. In an endocycle, DNA replication recurs without an ordinary complete mitotic division producing two daughter cells. The nucleus becomes larger in DNA content, often in association with large storage cells. This differs from the original triploid constitution established at fertilization. A cell with a high DNA content may still have received its original parental contributions in the usual two-to-one ratio. The larger DNA measurement arises during subsequent development, not because additional sperm repeatedly entered the original central cell.

For a grain to accumulate large amounts of reserve, the cell population must first establish a capacity for storage. Proliferation increases the number of potential storage cells; later cell expansion and biosynthesis determine how much material each cell can retain. A simple analogy is that a storage tissue's total capacity is the product of the number of compartments and their average content. In biology these quantities are regulated and not independent. If cell division is reduced, compensatory expansion may increase the size of surviving cells. If expansion is limited by the seed's geometry, the reduction in cell number may be reflected more directly in final mass.

The sequence of proliferation, differentiation and filling is not identical across cereals. Wheat, for example, differs from maize in details of grain anatomy and organization of nutrient entry. Many familiar wheats have a higher basic genomic copy number than diploid maize. Bread wheat is an allohexaploid with a somatic chromosome number of forty-two; normal reduced gametes contain twenty-one chromosomes. Its ordinary endosperm contains sixty-three chromosomes following central-cell fertilization. The conventional two maternal gametic contributions plus one paternal contribution still applies even though the term triploid relative to the gametic chromosome number must be distinguished from the number of basic chromosome sets. This is one reason to state both the chromosome count and the reference used for ploidy.

Why pea and bean seeds store food in cotyledons

A pea or common bean seed displays a different final allocation of internal volume. Instead of retaining a large persistent endosperm, the mature seed has two prominent cotyledons. The embryonic axis is relatively small compared with the cotyledons but contains the regions that will establish the shoot and root systems. Both cotyledons and axis belong to the embryo lineage. The distinction is developmental as well as anatomical: if the embryo inherited a paternal marker, the cotyledons normally share that nuclear genotype.

The development of these seeds includes a period in which endosperm is present and serves as a supporting tissue. As the embryo grows and the cotyledons enlarge, the endosperm becomes reduced. Nutrients available within the seed can be transferred to the embryo, and maternal nutrients continue to arrive. This means that a large cotyledon at maturity cannot be interpreted simply as the result of converting every molecule of a fixed initial endosperm into embryonic material. Seed filling is a dynamic process involving continuing import, metabolism, allocation and loss.

In standard botanical terminology, a seed retaining a substantial endosperm at maturity is called endospermic or albuminous. A seed in which the endosperm has been largely absorbed or is absent as a major mature storage tissue is called nonendospermic or exalbuminous. These terms describe the mature seed. They should not be used to classify whether double fertilization happened. Peas and beans are common examples of nonendospermic mature seeds, while maize and wheat are familiar examples of persistent endosperm.

The number of cotyledons and the persistence of endosperm are separate traits. A monocot normally has one cotyledon, and a eudicot normally has two. Many cereal monocots retain endosperm, while many familiar legume eudicots do not retain a large amount. This association is useful for recognizing common textbook examples, but it is not a logical consequence of cotyledon number. Castor is a eudicot whose mature seed has substantial endosperm. The cotyledons in castor lie within the reserve tissue instead of replacing most of it. The presence of two cotyledons therefore does not exclude persistence of endosperm.

The word cotyledon describes an embryonic organ, not a particular biochemical class of food. One cotyledon may accumulate substantial starch or proteins, another may specialize in absorbing resources from surrounding reserve tissues during subsequent development. In grasses the specialized cotyledon is called the scutellum. Its position between the embryo axis and the large endosperm illustrates a different structural relationship from that of a fleshy pea cotyledon. The scutellum is not itself a part of the endosperm, although it interacts with it.

A comparison of seed sections should thus identify lineages before assigning functions. An outer covering may belong to the maternal coat or fruit wall. A reserve compartment adjacent to the embryo may belong to endosperm, or to cotyledons that are part of the embryo, or in some species to persistent maternal nucellar tissue called perisperm. These tissues can contain comparable classes of stored compounds but differ in genetic origin. An investigator examining a new species should not infer that every starchy region is endosperm merely because it occupies a central position in a section.

The evolutionary significance of these differences lies in the distribution of investment before and after dispersal. A seed can carry much of its stored material inside the embryo, or retain a distinct tissue that supplies the embryo later. Both arrangements can produce a viable seed. Their relative value depends on the development and ecology of the species, not on a universal ranking in which one anatomical arrangement is superior. The appropriate examination answer is therefore usually a mechanistic comparison of origin and function rather than a claim that one group is more advanced.

Maternal integuments and seed-coat differentiation

The integuments are established around the ovule before the fertilization products begin to grow. They are maternal sporophytic tissues, and their future differentiation produces the seed coat. A typical description names an outer and inner integument, although the number, thickness and developmental fates of integumentary layers vary. In a mature seed, some layers remain recognizable while others are compressed, degraded or modified. The terms testa and tegmen are traditionally used for coverings associated with the outer and inner integuments respectively, but the final anatomy is species dependent.

A protective seed coat has to satisfy several conditions that can appear contradictory. During seed growth it must accommodate the expansion of the endosperm and embryo, but at maturity it often becomes mechanically resistant. During filling, substances and water must reach the internal tissues, while later the coverings can restrict access of water or gases. The coat must protect living embryonic tissues without necessarily remaining metabolically active in every cell at maturity. These requirements are met by changes in the differentiation and material properties of particular layers rather than by a simple monotonic increase in thickness.

Cell walls contribute strongly to these properties. The deposition and modification of wall materials can increase resistance to deformation or influence permeability. In some species, selected layers become thick walled or impregnated with substances that change their mechanical and chemical characteristics. Other cells may develop compounds that influence color or resistance to damage. The dry outer appearance of a seed is consequently the endpoint of a developmental history in which maternal cells have followed distinct programs. A coat that is darker than another is not necessarily thicker, stronger or less permeable; the nature and location of deposited materials matter.

The surface of Arabidopsis seeds provides a useful example of specialized seed-coat function. Some epidermal cells produce mucilage, a hydrophilic material rich in polysaccharides that becomes conspicuous on hydration. The material is manufactured by maternal tissue during seed development and released or expanded when water is available. This example separates the time at which a structure is formed from the later time at which it performs a visible function. The detailed role of water uptake in germination belongs to the next node, but the production of the covering is part of seed formation.

The micropyle, an opening between the integuments of the ovule, is another developmental landmark. It provided a route for the pollen tube in many flowering plants and can remain identifiable in the mature seed region. The hilum marks the region of attachment of the seed to the maternal structure through the funiculus in familiar seeds. These landmarks should not be confused: the micropyle relates to the ovular opening, whereas the hilum relates to attachment. Their exact geometry varies and one may lie close to the other. Neither identifies the lineage of the embryo or endosperm.

In a maternal-effect experiment, reciprocal crosses can distinguish an integument genotype from an embryo genotype. If a homozygous mutant mother is crossed to a homozygous normal father, the embryo may be heterozygous and genetically identical at the tested locus to an embryo produced by the reciprocal cross. The seed coat, however, has the genotype of the mother in each case. If the coat phenotype follows the mother, that is consistent with direct action of the maternal tissue genotype. The endosperm also differs in parental allele dosage between reciprocal crosses, so the experiment alone does not prove that the causal action occurred exclusively in the coat.

A maternal sporophytic defect can affect the seed without requiring an embryo to inherit two mutant alleles. If the integuments cannot expand normally, they can physically restrict internal tissues. If their nutrient-transfer function is compromised, the developing filial tissues can receive insufficient resources. If their differentiation alters a signal, the response may occur in another compartment. Conversely, a genetically normal maternal coat may develop abnormally when a fertilization product does not provide the signals needed to initiate or coordinate its growth. The lineage and the site of visible symptoms must therefore be kept separate when explaining the cause of a seed phenotype.

Communication after fertilization: auxin and tissue responses

The maternal integuments are not fertilized, yet normal seed-coat development is coordinated with events inside the female gametophyte. This poses a causal problem. A change in the genotype of the fertilized central cell cannot directly replace the chromosomes of the integuments. Instead, information about fertilization must reach maternal cells through changes in chemical signals, resource exchange or physical conditions. A plant hormone can provide a link because its production in one region and perception in another need not require genetic continuity between the two regions.

Auxin is a plant signaling molecule whose activity influences many aspects of growth. Indole-3-acetic acid is a prominent naturally occurring auxin. A cell may produce auxin, transport it, modify its active concentration or respond to it through regulatory machinery. These processes are separable. Observing an auxin response in a seed-coat cell does not automatically show that the hormone was synthesized by that cell. The hormone might have been produced elsewhere and moved to the responding tissue. Similarly, a cell could contain auxin but fail to produce a normal response if its perception or downstream regulation is impaired.

Experiments reported by Figueiredo and colleagues in Arabidopsis in 2016 addressed the connection between central-cell fertilization and maternal seed-coat initiation. They altered auxin production in the central cell and developing endosperm and examined whether maternal coverings began seed-coat development. The findings supported a model in which the fertilization product generates an auxin-related signal that can promote differentiation in neighboring maternal tissue. The implication is that the developmental state of the endosperm can regulate a genetically distinct compartment without transferring its chromosomes to that compartment.

The investigators used tissue-specific expression of auxin biosynthesis genes as a way of separating the effect of hormone production from the normal sequence of fertilization. The principle of this manipulation is particularly useful for examination reasoning. If a developmental response normally occurs only after an event, one can ask whether introducing a candidate downstream signal in the absence of that event will induce the response. A positive result supports sufficiency of the introduced signal under those conditions. It does not by itself show that this is the only signal used in a normal seed, nor that every other developmental requirement has been satisfied.

A second type of test is to reduce the production or response to the proposed signal. If normal seed-coat development then fails, the evidence supports a requirement for that process. The effect should be examined in the relevant developmental window, because auxin acts at different times in different tissues. A whole-plant alteration in auxin biology might prevent normal ovule formation before the question of seed-coat initiation even arises. A tissue-specific or temporally controlled perturbation can distinguish early general requirements from the communication event being investigated.

Maternal tissue responsiveness is as important as the presence of the signal. A normal endosperm may produce the relevant hormonal cue, yet an integument mutant lacking suitable downstream regulatory activity may fail to respond. In the reciprocal situation, maternal tissues can remain competent but receive an insufficient cue from an abnormal endosperm. Both scenarios can lead to failure of seed-coat development. Measuring hormone response in both tissues, determining the site of the genetic defect and comparing appropriate rescue conditions helps discriminate the possibilities.

Hormonal coordination is not equivalent to a universal concentration threshold shared by every species. A signal that promotes early expansion may need to decline or change distribution during later differentiation. In Arabidopsis, the timing of auxin activity also has consequences for endosperm cellularization, so the same broad class of hormone can be associated with different processes according to tissue and stage. The relevant principle is developmental regulation: a particular molecular signal acts in a spatial and temporal context, and its result depends on the competence of the responding cells. The full molecular biology of auxin perception and transport belongs to the plant physiology unit.

Controlling the transition from nuclear to cellular endosperm

A developmental transition requires a mechanism that prevents the next stage from starting too soon and allows it to begin when the preceding stage has accomplished its purpose. In the nuclear endosperm this requirement is evident because cells must ultimately establish internal walls, yet the early program deliberately produces many nuclei without those walls. A mutation that releases the restraint prematurely can reduce the duration of nuclear proliferation. A mutation that maintains the restraint for too long can prevent the acquisition of cellular organization. Either change can compromise the final seed.

One useful Arabidopsis example is AGL62, a member of the MADS-domain transcriptional regulator family. A transcriptional regulator influences the expression of other genes, so its activity can alter a developmental program without functioning directly as a wall-building enzyme. Kang and colleagues reported in 2008 that endosperm lacking normal AGL62 function cellularizes prematurely. In ordinary development, AGL62 expression is associated with the nuclear phase and declines as the tissue approaches cellularization. The mutant phenotype supports the interpretation that AGL62 helps maintain the early nuclear state rather than being a structural component of the eventual cell wall.

This reasoning is an example of the distinction between positive and negative regulation. If a regulator normally restrains cellularization, loss of the regulator can cause cellularization to occur earlier. A student might initially assume that deleting any gene involved in cellularization must prevent walls from forming. The mutant result contradicts that assumption. The direction of the phenotype depends on the normal role of the gene. Loss of a promoter of a process and loss of an inhibitor of that process can produce opposite changes, even though both genes are associated with the same developmental transition.

Certain mutations affecting the FERTILIZATION-INDEPENDENT SEED group of Polycomb regulators also interfere with normal endosperm development. Polycomb complexes are chromatin-associated regulators that can maintain repression of particular developmental genes. Before fertilization, restraint of the central-cell division program helps coordinate reproduction with the arrival of the paternal contribution. Some defects in this regulatory system permit nuclear proliferation in the absence of normal fertilization. Autonomous proliferation is not equivalent to formation of an ordinary viable seed. A normal embryo may be missing, the maternal coat may not coordinate its development and the proliferating endosperm may fail to execute later transitions.

The AGL62 study also connected prolonged expression of the regulator with failure of cellularization in some FIS-class mutant endosperms. This supports a relationship between the epigenetic regulatory system and the transition out of the nuclear state. It does not require the claim that every affected gene is directly bound by the same complex or that the same regulatory wiring occurs in all flowering plants. The precise interaction is not necessary for solving most examination problems. It is more important to understand the logic: a repressive regulator can control the timing of another regulator, and the second regulator can maintain a temporary developmental state.

Auxin provides another layer of control over timing. Batista and colleagues reported in 2019 that excess auxin production in Arabidopsis endosperm can delay or prevent its cellularization. A high auxin-related signal that is useful in initiating development may be inappropriate if maintained into the period when cellular walls should form. Reducing auxin production or signaling in certain abnormal seeds allowed cellularization and improved viability. Such results illustrate that the timing of a hormone response can be as important as its direction. More of a growth-associated hormone does not necessarily produce a more successful developmental outcome.

The simplest teaching model is to imagine an early state in which proliferative activity is supported and cellularization is restrained. Over time the regulatory conditions change and the restraint is released. The tissue then establishes individual cells and differentiates further. This model describes the direction of the transition without assuming a single molecular switch in every species. When analyzing a perturbation, identify the stage at which a molecular signal is altered and predict the state that will persist or be lost. A persistent early state is not merely a slower version of the mature tissue; it can lack qualitative functions that depend on cellular organization.

Parent-of-origin dosage and reciprocal polyploid crosses

The endosperm presents a special problem in developmental genetics because it usually contains unequal contributions from the maternal and paternal genomes. Under the standard sexual pathway with diploid parents, the maternal-to-paternal dosage ratio is two to one. This does not mean that every maternal allele is expressed at twice the level of its paternal counterpart. The copies may be regulated differently, and the expression of a gene depends on more than the number of DNA templates. The dosage ratio describes the initial inherited genome contributions.

It is useful to use x for the number of chromosomes in one basic set when comparing plants of different ploidies. A diploid plant has 2x chromosomes, and its ordinarily reduced gametes have x chromosomes. A tetraploid plant has 4x chromosomes and, if its meiosis produces balanced reduced gametes, those gametes have 2x chromosomes. In the normal monosporic embryo-sac model, the egg and each polar nucleus have the same number of basic sets as the female gamete. This allows the genetic constitution of every seed compartment to be predicted from the parental cytotypes.

Consider a diploid mother crossed to a tetraploid father. The egg contributes x and the sperm contributes 2x, so the embryo has 3x chromosomes. The two maternal polar nuclei together contribute 2x, and the second sperm contributes 2x. The endosperm therefore has 4x chromosomes, with maternal and paternal genome contributions of 2x each. Its maternal-to-paternal ratio is one to one, representing a paternal excess relative to the ordinary two-to-one balance. The seed coat is still derived from the 2x maternal plant.

Now reverse the cross. The tetraploid mother contributes a 2x egg, and the diploid father contributes an x sperm. The embryo again has 3x chromosomes. The endosperm, however, receives 4x from the two polar nuclei and x from the sperm, for a total of 5x and a maternal-to-paternal ratio of four to one. The coat has 4x chromosomes. The two reciprocal embryos thus have the same total number of basic chromosome sets, but their endosperms and maternal coverings differ. The different developmental outcomes of reciprocal crosses cannot be explained solely by the total ploidy of the embryo.

A classic study by Scott and colleagues in 1998 compared the development of Arabidopsis seeds from reciprocal interploidy crosses. Excess maternal genomic contribution was associated with restricted endosperm development, whereas excess paternal contribution promoted prolonged growth and altered the timing of cellularization. The specific severity of abnormal seed development depends on the cross and genetic background. Some triploid embryos can survive, and in other cases abnormal endosperm is associated with seed abortion. A triploid embryo should therefore not be declared intrinsically nonviable from its chromosome number alone.

When solving dosage problems, distinguish numerical excess from absolute ploidy. The 4x endosperm of a diploid mother by tetraploid father is paternal-excess even though it contains two maternal basic sets. The 5x endosperm of the reciprocal cross is maternal-excess. If both parents are tetraploid and produce ordinary 2x gametes, the embryo has 4x and the endosperm 6x, but the endosperm maintains the ordinary ratio of four maternal sets to two paternal sets. High ploidy does not automatically imply unbalanced parental dosage.

A possible evolutionary interpretation of parent-of-origin effects concerns allocation of maternal resources among offspring. Under particular mating systems, alleles inherited through the mother and father may experience different selective pressures concerning the extent of resource acquisition by one offspring. This is often discussed as a parental conflict hypothesis. The hypothesis can help explain why some endosperm regulators are sensitive to parental origin, but it should not be used as a complete explanation for every imprinted gene or every developmental difference. Measured dosage effects, molecular imprinting and evolutionary interpretations are distinct levels of evidence.

Imprinting and genetic interpretation of seed phenotypes

Genomic imprinting describes expression that depends on whether an allele was inherited from the mother or the father. In flowering plants, endosperm is a prominent tissue in which such expression differences occur. The molecular foundations of imprinting were considered earlier in the developmental biology unit. Here the important issue is how parent-specific expression changes the interpretation of a seed phenotype. A triploid tissue may carry two maternal alleles and one paternal allele while expressing predominantly the paternal copy of a particular gene.

Suppose a locus encodes a product that is needed during endosperm growth, and suppose its maternal copies are functionally silent while its paternal copy is normally expressed. A recessive loss-of-function allele delivered by pollen can then produce an endosperm phenotype even if the two maternal genomic copies are normal. The normal DNA sequence is present but not expressed sufficiently to replace the missing product. This is different from the situation of an ordinary autosomal recessive mutation in a diploid embryo, where one functional allele often supplies enough product to prevent a recessive phenotype.

The reciprocal cross provides a useful test of the hypothesis. If the mutant allele is inherited maternally but remains silent under the normal imprinting program, a normal paternal copy could provide the required function. The two crosses can therefore differ in phenotype. Nevertheless, reciprocal differences alone do not establish imprinting. The maternal sporophyte differs between the crosses, the endosperm has different allele dosage and the egg cytoplasm has maternal origin. All of these differences must be considered before concluding that an allele is imprinted.

Allele-specific expression offers a more direct approach. In a cross between parents with distinguishable sequence variants at the gene, the RNA molecules made by the endosperm can be assigned to the maternal or paternal allele. A reciprocal cross helps control for an allele that is simply stronger or weaker regardless of its parental origin. If the same allele is expressed when paternal and repressed when maternal, that pattern supports parent-of-origin regulation. Appropriate controls are needed for contamination because seed-coat RNA is exclusively from maternal tissue and can make a sample appear to have excess maternal expression.

An ordinary biallelic expression pattern also requires a careful baseline. If the two maternal copies and the single paternal copy of a gene each contribute equally, maternal transcripts are expected to represent two thirds of the total and paternal transcripts one third. A maternal predominance in that proportion is not evidence for imprinting. Strong deviation from the dosage expectation, consistent across reciprocal comparisons and after controlling technical biases, provides more meaningful evidence. The interpretation becomes more complicated when the copies differ in promoter sequence or the gene is expressed in only a subset of endosperm cells.

The coat can generate a different type of parent-of-origin phenotype. Since it is derived from the mother plant, coat color or texture may follow maternal sporophytic genotype without any imprinting at all. The trait can show a generation delay compared with the genotype of the enclosed embryo. If a cross between differently colored maternal and paternal plants produces a seed coat with the maternal color, the observation is consistent with the anatomy of the coat. One must not invoke epigenetic silencing of paternal genes in the coat when paternal chromosomes were never contributed to that tissue in the first place.

The endosperm and the embryo can also carry the same paternal allele but respond differently because gene expression is tissue-specific. A mutation affecting a storage enzyme may change the endosperm but leave early embryo shape normal. A mutation affecting embryonic meristem organization may change the embryo even in a seed with normal reserve accumulation. These cases are not necessarily examples of imprinting. The general examination strategy is to identify the tissue, reconstruct the genotype, determine which genes are expressed there and only then infer the likely molecular defect.

When endosperm failure arrests an otherwise competent embryo

A seed can fail even after both fertilization events have occurred and a recognizable embryo has formed. The failure may arise because the endosperm does not develop the properties required to sustain the embryo. In some Arabidopsis paternal-excess crosses, a prolonged free nuclear state is associated with embryonic growth arrest. The abnormal seed may initially enlarge, creating the misleading impression that development is progressing well. Later it can collapse and fail to produce a viable offspring.

An embryo surrounded by abnormal endosperm faces changes in its entire developmental environment. The availability and distribution of nutrients may differ, the extracellular chemical conditions may be altered and the physical relationship between the tissues may become inappropriate. The absence of normal cellular partitions can affect the regulation of solute and water movement. A developing embryo requires a controlled water potential as well as a supply of carbon and nitrogen. Consequently, a failure of endosperm cellularization cannot always be reduced to a simple shortage of food.

Water potential describes the tendency of water to move in a system relative to a reference state. In a simplified cellular description it includes a solute contribution and a pressure contribution. Increasing the amount of osmotically active solute generally makes the solute component more negative. Water moves in response to differences in total water potential, not just the concentration of one sugar. An embryo that encounters an abnormal external osmotic environment may lose the conditions necessary for normal expansion even if some nutritional substrates are present.

Experimental studies of Arabidopsis have found that embryos exposed to persistently uncellularized endosperm can activate stress responses associated with water relations. Such observations extend the older idea of a nutritive support tissue by showing that the endosperm can also influence the physical and hormonal environment of the embryo. The presence of a stress response alone does not identify its cause. However, if interventions that restore endosperm cellularization also improve embryo growth, the evidence supports a causal contribution of the endosperm defect.

Embryo rescue provides an independent line of reasoning. If an embryo isolated from a failing seed can continue development on a supportive medium, its arrest in the original seed was not necessarily due to an irreversible defect in its own developmental program. In Arabidopsis, triploid embryos from some paternal-excess crosses have been shown to be capable of further development outside the defective seed environment. This finding explains why two questions that sound similar require different answers: whether a triploid embryo is capable of growth, and whether the seed produced by a particular interploidy cross can support that growth.

Consider an experiment with four groups: intact normal seeds, intact abnormal seeds, isolated normal embryos and isolated abnormal embryos placed under comparable supportive conditions. If the intact abnormal seeds arrest while the isolated abnormal embryos grow, and normal embryos also tolerate the isolation procedure, the result supports an environment-dependent defect. If both isolated groups fail, the isolation conditions may be unsuitable. If only the isolated abnormal embryos fail, an embryo-intrinsic defect becomes plausible, but prior irreversible damage during growth in the abnormal seed must also be considered.

The developmental time at which rescue is attempted matters. An embryo may be competent early but lose competence after prolonged exposure to an adverse environment. A late failure to rescue does not disprove an environmental cause at an earlier stage. In addition, artificial culture can substitute for some functions of endosperm but not necessarily reproduce every signal or physical interaction. The purpose of a conceptual rescue experiment is not to claim that every embryo can be grown outside a seed. It is to test whether a specific developmental failure depends on the original tissue environment.

An endosperm defect can therefore affect reproductive isolation between plants with different chromosome constitutions or genetic backgrounds. Fertilization may be successful, and an embryo with a potentially functional genome may be produced, yet the cross may still fail because the supporting tissues cannot develop normally. This type of postzygotic barrier illustrates that the production of a new sporophyte requires coordination of several genomic compartments. It also explains why studying seed development is useful for understanding plant evolution without needing to introduce specialist breeding procedures.

Seed size: maternal capacity, filial growth and developmental timing

Seed size is an integrated developmental phenotype. It reflects the number and size of cells, the volume of stored materials, water content and the geometric limits imposed by surrounding tissues. Two seeds of the same external dimensions may contain different dry masses or different proportions of embryo and endosperm. Similarly, a large developing seed at one stage need not produce a large mature seed. The final phenotype depends on how growth, differentiation and storage proceed over the entire period of development.

The maternal integuments can influence the potential space available to the fertilization products. Before fertilization, the size and organization of the ovule establish part of the starting geometry. After fertilization, the coat expands and differentiates. A mutation that alters the number of integument cells or their capacity to enlarge can change the volume available to the internal tissues. An expanding endosperm can in turn influence the shape and growth of the coat. These relationships are reciprocal rather than purely one directional.

The APETALA2 gene of Arabidopsis illustrates a maternal influence on seed size. Studies of AP2 mutants have reported changes in embryo, endosperm and seed-coat development, with maternally acting AP2 associated with limitation of final seed size. The important developmental principle is that a gene active in maternal tissues can change the growth of the filial compartments indirectly. It is unnecessary to assume that AP2 acts as a direct transcriptional regulator of every observed response inside the embryo and endosperm. Changes in geometry, signaling and resource allocation can propagate the effect.

It is helpful to separate potential size from realized filling. A seed with a relatively large internal cavity has the physical capacity to accumulate material, but it may remain poorly filled if the nutrient supply is inadequate. A smaller seed with efficient filling can contain a large amount of dry matter relative to its volume. To understand a mutant seed, a scientist may compare total volume, dry mass, water content and the size of each compartment. Measuring only external dimensions at one time point cannot distinguish among these alternatives.

A basic model expresses tissue volume as the product of its cell number and average cell volume when extracellular volume can be neglected. If a control endosperm contains ten thousand cells of mean volume 2,000 cubic micrometres, the combined volume represented by those cells is twenty million cubic micrometres. A mutant with eight thousand cells of mean volume 2,500 cubic micrometres gives the same total. Thus a decrease in proliferation can be fully compensated in this idealized example by an increase in individual cell size. The equal total does not establish that the cells are functionally identical or that tissue organization is normal.

Timing introduces an additional variable. Prolonged nuclear proliferation before cellularization can increase the early volume of some endosperms, but it can also delay the formation of specialized transport and storage structures. If cellularization fails completely, a large early seed can subsequently collapse. Conversely, precocious cellularization can limit the expansion phase and result in a smaller seed. These outcomes explain why the relationship between cellularization time and successful final mass is not simply monotonic. The optimum developmental schedule must be compatible with the requirements of both the embryo and the surrounding tissues.

A maternal plant also has to distribute a finite supply of resources among multiple sinks. Developing seeds on the same plant can differ in their access to nutrients because of position, fertilization time and competition with other growing structures. These environmental influences can modify a genetically determined potential size. A controlled genetic experiment therefore compares seeds at equivalent developmental positions and stages, uses multiple plants and distinguishes the genotype of the maternal plant from that of individual offspring. Comparing many seeds from a single fruit is not equivalent to sampling many independently grown maternal plants.

The biological importance of seed size is not that larger seeds are universally superior. Larger reserves can improve establishment under some conditions, but producing each seed requires maternal investment and may influence the number that a plant can produce. Small seeds can be viable and developmentally well coordinated. The developmental objective is a functional seed with an appropriately organized embryo, sufficient resources and protective coverings, rather than maximal mass in isolation.

Embryo surfaces and endosperm differentiation must remain coordinated

A developing embryo is in physical contact with the surrounding endosperm, but it must eventually become a distinct body with its own external surface. This presents a problem that is different from the supply of nutrients. Embryonic epidermal cells must develop a continuous surface barrier while adjacent endosperm cells undergo their own differentiation. An embryo that obtains sufficient resources may still be defective if its external boundary is incompletely formed or if it remains inappropriately attached to surrounding tissue.

The plant cuticle is a lipid-rich extracellular barrier associated with epidermal surfaces. In the embryo it develops progressively rather than appearing fully formed in one step. A continuous cuticle depends on coordinated deposition over the surface. Local gaps can compromise barrier function even if large regions of the embryo appear normal. For this reason, an assay that tests whether a dye or water can cross the surface may reveal a defect not evident from the overall size of the embryo.

Studies of Arabidopsis mutants provide evidence that the endosperm and embryonic epidermis communicate during this process. The protease ALE1 is associated with the embryo-surrounding endosperm, while the related receptors GSO1 and GSO2 are expressed in embryonic epidermal cells. Disruption of these components can result in defects in the integrity of the embryonic surface. The relevant examination concept is intertissue signaling: a product from one developmental compartment participates in a response that must occur in a different compartment.

A protease cuts particular peptide bonds in a protein or peptide substrate. A receptor kinase transduces information from an extracellular or membrane-associated signal into changes in intracellular activity. In the cuticle example, the enzyme and receptors function in different tissues. This arrangement provides a means for the developing surface to respond to information about its surrounding environment. It would be inaccurate to say that the endosperm simply supplies the entire embryonic cuticle. The embryo must execute its own surface-building program, while the neighboring tissue contributes to its regulation.

A conceptual prediction follows from this arrangement. Restoring a signal-generating activity in the endosperm should help only if the embryo retains the capacity to perceive and respond to the signal. If the required embryonic receptors are missing, providing more of the upstream cue may not restore the normal phenotype. Conversely, normal embryonic receptors may not be activated appropriately if the endosperm fails to supply or process the relevant cue. Such reasoning is closely related to the general principles of induction and competence taught earlier in developmental biology.

The surface can also act as a physical boundary that influences the exchange of molecules between tissues. As an embryonic barrier becomes more complete, its permeability changes. Thus the outcome of signaling can modify the conditions under which further signaling occurs. This is a useful example of feedback in development, although detailed peptide-processing networks are beyond the scope of the present node. A developmental product is not merely an endpoint; it can alter the environment in which the process is regulated.

This case reinforces the need to measure multiple properties of a developing seed. Normal dry mass does not establish normal embryonic surface integrity. A visible seed coat does not establish normal formation of the embryonic epidermis, because these surfaces have different origins. Likewise, a normal embryo shape does not prove that the boundary is functionally impermeable. The distinct genetic and anatomical compartments create opportunities for interpreting mutant phenotypes with more precision than an overall description of seed size or appearance.

Diverse nutritive tissues and exceptions to the common model

The nucellus is the maternal tissue within which the female gametophyte develops. It is distinct from the two fertilization products and from the integuments that surround it. During ordinary development in many flowering plants, much of the nucellus becomes reduced as the female gametophyte and later endosperm expand. In some species, however, a substantial portion persists and differentiates into a major reserve compartment. Persistent nucellar tissue of this kind is called perisperm. Quinoa and amaranth provide familiar examples of seeds in which a maternal nutritive tissue contributes substantially to the mature reserve arrangement.

A perisperm is not converted into endosperm merely because it accumulates starch. The two tissues differ in lineage: one is a continuation of maternal sporophytic nucellar tissue, the other ordinarily descends from the fertilized central cell. In a diploid plant, a pre-replication perisperm nucleus would generally contain two basic chromosome sets, whereas conventional endosperm would contain three. The distinction can be investigated by genetic markers as well as morphology. A paternal nuclear allele should not be present in the maternal nucellar lineage, even though it can be present in the endosperm and embryo. As with the coat, sample contamination must be excluded before making a genetic inference.

The reduction of some nucellar layers can facilitate growth of the fertilization products. When maternal cells are removed during normal development, their former space becomes available and some of their components may contribute to local nutrient pools. Programmed cellular elimination is a regulated part of development, not necessarily a pathological sign. In a seed that retains a perisperm, some nucellar cells follow a different developmental path and deposit reserves instead of disappearing at the corresponding early stage. A comparison between perispermic and endospermic seeds thus demonstrates how variation in maternal tissue fate can shift the location of storage.

A second class of exceptions concerns the female gametophyte itself. The conventional Polygonum-type organization involves two polar nuclei descended from a single megaspore, but flowering plants have diverse patterns of megagametogenesis. Some central cells have different nuclear contributions. An embryo sac in which a single haploid central-cell nucleus receives one haploid sperm nucleus could produce diploid endosperm even though both parents are ordinary diploids. Other patterns can generate higher genomic contributions. The definition of endosperm rests on developmental origin and role rather than a universally fixed ploidy. An examination problem explicitly specifying an unconventional central cell takes precedence over the familiar textbook default.

Seed development without ordinary egg fertilization offers a further qualification. Apomixis comprises pathways by which an embryo forms without the conventional sexual fusion of a reduced egg and sperm. In many apomictic flowering plants the endosperm still requires fertilization of a central cell, a situation called pseudogamy. Other systems can initiate endosperm without fertilization. This variation shows that initiation of the embryo and initiation of its nutritive partner can be developmentally separable. It does not follow that every plant capable of an apomictic embryo can make a viable seed without pollination.

The reason to include these exceptions is not to replace the standard developmental model with a collection of unusual species. The model remains a reliable starting point for a question specifying ordinary diploid parents, a monosporic embryo sac and sexual fertilization. Exceptions show which assumptions generate its predictions. The prediction of triploid endosperm depends on the genomic contribution of the central cell and sperm. The prediction of paternal alleles in the embryo depends on fertilization of the egg. The prediction that a nutritive tissue is filial depends on its lineage, not its reserve content. Once these assumptions are explicit, unfamiliar examples can be solved by applying the same reasoning rather than memorizing a separate rule for every species.

Reading developmental time courses and distinguishing mass from flux

Seed formation occurs over time, so a useful experiment often samples the developing system at several stages rather than measuring the mature endpoint alone. Imagine a comparison of a normal plant and a mutant in which endosperm growth appears excessive. A researcher records endosperm volume, number of nuclei, fraction of the endosperm that has cellularized, embryo stage and dry mass at several points after pollination. Each variable captures a different aspect of development. The volume reflects spatial occupation, nuclear count reflects part of the proliferative program, and the fraction of the tissue with cellular boundaries records a structural transition. Embryo stage and dry mass report other consequences of growth.

Timing from pollination is a convenient reference in many experiments but does not guarantee that fertilization took place at exactly the same time in every seed. A mutant affecting pollen-tube growth may delay the arrival of sperm. If its seeds are compared with normal seeds three days after pollination, they might appear developmentally delayed merely because they were fertilized later. A careful comparison therefore distinguishes calendar time after pollination from stage after fertilization. In addition to recording the day of pollination, investigators can check the appearance of relevant embryonic and endosperm markers to establish the developmental starting point.

Consider a hypothetical pair of seeds at two sampling times. At the first time the normal seed has an endosperm dry mass of 4 milligrams and an embryo dry mass of 2 milligrams. At the second time the corresponding masses are 3 and 7 milligrams. Total mass of these two compartments has increased from 6 to 10 milligrams. The endosperm has lost 1 milligram net while the embryo has gained 5 milligrams net. It would be incorrect to conclude that the embryo acquired exactly 1 milligram of its gain from the endosperm. The endosperm might have imported additional resources and transferred more than its net loss, while both tissues may have respired substrates. A compartment balance reveals net changes, not all underlying fluxes.

Suppose instead that a pulse of labeled carbon is supplied to the maternal plant and its movement is followed through the seed. Label initially accumulates in the maternal vascular region, later appears in endosperm and subsequently increases in the embryo. This ordering is consistent with a route passing through the endosperm. To establish how much of the embryonic label actually passed through that compartment, one needs additional measurements or interventions. A parallel direct route could deliver some of the same label, and carbon could be metabolized into different chemical forms during movement. A time course of label distribution provides stronger information about transport than a measurement of total tissue mass, but it still requires a carefully specified model.

A comparison of fresh and dry mass is especially important during seed development. Fresh mass includes water, while dry mass represents material remaining after a suitable drying procedure. A large increase in fresh mass may reflect water uptake and expansion rather than equivalent reserve accumulation. For example, a hypothetical seed of fresh mass 40 milligrams and dry mass 12 milligrams contains 28 milligrams of water. If another seed of the same fresh mass has dry mass 20 milligrams, it contains only 20 milligrams of water but substantially more dry matter. Their similar external masses conceal different states of filling. The percentage water content must be defined on a fresh-mass or dry-mass basis before the values can be compared.

The basic experimental principle is to match each measurement to the question it can answer. DNA content does not establish nuclear origin without further information; dry mass does not identify the transport pathway; external dimensions do not establish embryo quality; and elapsed time does not guarantee developmental equivalence. These limitations are not weaknesses of scientific measurement in general. They explain why complementary observations and controls are needed in developmental biology. A coherent model predicts several kinds of evidence at once, making it possible to distinguish mechanisms that would otherwise produce the same visible seed phenotype.

Worked mechanistic reasoning: transport capacity and resource partition

The movement of nutrients at the seed interface can be analyzed with a simple capacity model without assuming that every detail of membrane transport is known. Let j represent a mean flux density in micromoles per square centimetre per hour, and let A represent the effective exchange area in square centimetres. If the local flux is approximately uniform and constant over the considered surface, the total rate of transfer J is j multiplied by A, with units of micromoles per hour. This expression is a definition of the integrated flux in the uniform case. It does not describe the molecular mechanism that establishes j.

Imagine a hypothetical developing seed in which the effective surface of an uptake region is 0.20 square centimetres and its mean flux density is 5 micromoles per square centimetre per hour. The transfer rate is then 1 micromole per hour. If wall ingrowths increase the effective surface area by fifty percent to 0.30 square centimetres while leaving the flux density unchanged, the calculated transfer rate becomes 1.5 micromoles per hour. Over a ten-hour interval at a constant rate, the transferred amount would increase from 10 to 15 micromoles. The conclusion is conditional on the availability of solute and the assumed constancy of local transporter activity.

Now imagine that the same increase in area is accompanied by a reduction in mean transporter activity, so the flux density falls to 3 micromoles per square centimetre per hour. The total transfer rate becomes 0.90 micromoles per hour, which is ten percent below the original value of 1 micromole per hour. Thus a structural increase in membrane area can coexist with lower total transport. This simple calculation explains why a histological measure of transfer-cell wall ingrowths is not an adequate substitute for a physiological measure of exchange.

A second simple model considers carbon retained in seed tissues. Let I be the carbon imported from the maternal plant per interval, R the carbon lost by respiration, and L any other carbon loss. Let the changes in embryo, endosperm and coat carbon contents be denoted by their respective changes in carbon mass. Conservation implies that their sum equals I minus R minus L, provided the three compartments include all relevant seed carbon pools. The equation refers to carbon, not total dry matter, because dry matter contains other elements as well. If an experiment measures dry mass instead, the two quantities cannot be equated without knowing composition.

In a hypothetical ten-day interval, a seed imports 18 milligrams of carbon and loses 5 milligrams by respiration. Suppose other losses are negligible and seed-coat carbon increases by 1 milligram. The combined increase of embryo and endosperm must be 12 milligrams of carbon. If the embryo gains 8 milligrams, the endosperm gains 4 milligrams net. If in a second condition the embryo gains 14 milligrams while coat carbon still increases by 1 milligram and import and respiration are unchanged, the endosperm must lose 2 milligrams net. Such a loss is compatible with continued endosperm import and turnover; it does not require that maternal nutrient delivery to the endosperm has stopped.

The thermodynamic background is equally important. A living seed uses metabolic energy to synthesize macromolecules and maintain solute gradients. Polymer formation and cellular assembly do not occur simply because nutrients are present. Cells must activate substrates, couple energy-requiring processes to favorable reactions and maintain suitable internal conditions. The total energy content of imported material can be partitioned between respiration, new structures and reserves. Detailed ATP accounting depends on the metabolic pathway and is outside the purpose of this chapter. Assigning a single fixed ATP cost to an entire seed without specifying its biosynthetic reactions would be scientifically unjustified.

These quantitative models are deliberately restricted to the aspects that can be calculated from the given information. The first relates exchange area to an assumed mean flux; the second uses conservation to constrain carbon allocation. Neither establishes a universal growth rate or predicts final seed viability from one parameter. Both models show how a biological question can be converted into a precise calculation while preserving the distinction between a measured value, an assumption and a mechanistic inference.

Completing the developmental system and preparing the next generation

A seed does not become functional merely when its embryo reaches a recognizable shape. The embryo must establish the organs and meristems needed for further growth, acquire the reserves or access to reserves required for establishment, and form a suitable surface. The supporting compartments must be appropriately differentiated. The maternal coat must accommodate the internal tissues during growth and then provide a suitable protective interface for the later life of the seed. The developmental transitions are coordinated, although the exact sequence and duration differ among species.

A useful way to integrate these processes is to distinguish initiation, capacity establishment, filling and final differentiation. Initiation follows the relevant reproductive events and includes activation of endosperm and maternal responses. Capacity establishment includes cell proliferation, enlargement of the seed compartments and formation of transport interfaces. During filling, imported resources are incorporated into the embryo, persistent endosperm or other reserve tissues according to the species. Final differentiation prepares the structures for the mature condition. These are overlapping functional phases rather than universally discrete stages of equal length.

A cereal seed allocates much of its reserve to a persistent endosperm, so the endosperm must continue to possess the structural properties needed for reserve retention. A pea seed instead allocates much of its final reserve to expanding embryonic cotyledons. In both cases the development of the embryo depends on access to resources during its formation, but the eventual organization of the seed is different. The coat and other maternal tissues regulate the interface between internal development and the external environment. Different storage arrangements require different later strategies for making reserves available to the growing seedling.

There is a difference between a formed seed and a viable mature seed. A seed may have recognizable external coverings and contain an embryo but nevertheless fail to complete normal development. Conversely, a small mature seed may contain a completely competent embryo and sufficient reserve for its species. The outcome should be assessed by examining development and function rather than relying on appearance or size alone. This is why experiments on seed formation often relate a structural or molecular defect to the later ability to establish a viable plant, even though detailed germination physiology is a separate subject.

The three ordinary seed lineages illustrate a broad principle of developmental biology. Different tissues with different genomic constitutions can form a functionally integrated system through resource exchange, signaling and mechanical interaction. This organization cannot be understood by tracing only the embryo lineage. Equally, the endosperm cannot be understood solely as a storage tissue, and the coat cannot be understood solely as a passive wrapping. A change in one component can alter the timing or properties of the others without changing their lineage. That is the defining mechanistic significance of embryo–endosperm co-development.

The next stage of the story concerns the transition from the developed seed to an independently growing seedling. Reserve accumulation, the acquisition of tolerance to water loss where appropriate, dormancy and later reactivation require further changes in the embryo and its surrounding tissues. The developmental arrangements established during seed formation determine what material is available, which structures must respond to rehydration and how the seedling can obtain resources before it becomes an effective independent source of assimilates. Worked calculations and checks

Nuclear division count

N₀ = 1; Nₖ = N₀ × 2ᵏ. For k = 5, N₅ = 32 nuclei. Units: count of nuclei. No division failure, loss or asynchronous behavior is assumed. With no cytokinesis this is still a shared compartment, not 32 separate cells.

Balanced gamete chromosome arithmetic

A diploid species with 2n = 18 has n = 9. Embryo: 9 + 9 = 18. Endosperm: 9 + 9 + 9 = 27. Coat: 18. These are chromosome counts, not post-S-phase DNA contents.

Flux density and surface area

J = jA. Baseline = 5 µmol cm⁻² h⁻¹ × 0.20 cm² = 1.00 µmol h⁻¹. Altered area and density: 3 × 0.30 = 0.90 µmol h⁻¹. Relative change = (0.90 − 1.00)/1.00 = −10%. Uniform mean flux is assumed; area alone is insufficient.

Conservation of carbon

Net carbon accumulation = import − respiration − other losses. 18 − 5 − 0 = 13 mg C. After subtracting the 1 mg coat and 8 mg embryo gains, endosperm net gain = 4 mg C. This cannot recover gross internal transfers.

Water fraction

For fresh mass 40 mg and dry mass 12 mg, water = 28 mg. Fresh-mass basis = 28/40 × 100 = 70%. Dry-mass basis would instead be 28/12 × 100 ≈ 233%. Specify denominator before comparing values.

Compensatory volume

10,000 × 2,000 µm³ = 20,000,000 µm³. 8,000 × 2,500 µm³ = 20,000,000 µm³. Equal modeled tissue volume does not imply identical differentiation or number of cells.

Revision and mastery

Seed formation links three major compartments with distinct origins. The two fertilization products are the embryo and endosperm, while maternal integuments become the seed coat. In the common diploid monosporic model, the initial constitutions are 2n, 3n and 2n respectively. The endosperm may begin as a coenocyte before forming cells, or it may follow cellular or helobial development. Later specialization supports nutrient transfer, reserve accumulation and embryo development.

Maternal supply reaches filial tissues through specialized interfaces. The seed coat and endosperm influence each other through growth and signals, including the Arabidopsis auxin example. The embryo's development depends on the timing of endosperm cellularization and the quality of its environment. Parent-of-origin dosage changes can disrupt this timing. Mature seeds differ in whether the main reserve is retained in endosperm, embryonic cotyledons or maternal perisperm.
