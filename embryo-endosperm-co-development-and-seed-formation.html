Embryo–Endosperm Co-development and Seed Formation

How three genetically distinct compartments coordinate seed development.

A seed is a coordinated developmental system

A seed is often introduced as a miniature plant enclosed in a protective covering. That description identifies the future plant but leaves out an essential part of the developmental problem. At the moment of fertilization, the embryo has neither an effective root system to acquire minerals nor a fully functioning shoot to acquire carbon by photosynthesis. Its cells must nevertheless obtain raw materials, maintain their internal chemical environment, divide, establish tissue identities and grow in an ordered manner. The ovule contains several tissues that can help meet these requirements, but these tissues must also change their activities as development proceeds. The formation of a viable seed therefore requires much more than the formation of a zygote.

Consider a dry pea and a maize grain. When a pea seed is opened, two large fleshy seed leaves, called cotyledons, occupy most of its internal volume. A small embryonic axis lies between them. The cotyledons belong to the embryo itself, so a large fraction of the dry matter of the seed is contained in the future plant. In a maize grain, however, a large storage region surrounds a much smaller embryo situated to one side. Much of that storage region belongs to a different developmental lineage called the endosperm. Both seeds contain substantial nutritional reserves, but the tissue responsible for storing them is different. The contrast immediately raises a question: are the reserves of a seed necessarily embryonic, or can another tissue produce and retain them on behalf of the embryo?

The distinction between the seed and the fruit is the first necessary anatomical distinction. In ordinary flowering plant reproduction the ovule develops into the seed, whereas the ovary contributes to the fruit. Additional floral tissues may participate in fruit formation in some species. In a pea pod, the pod wall and the coats surrounding individual peas therefore do not represent the same structure. The pod is the surrounding fruit, while each seed has its own protective coat. A cereal grain is a special case because the fruit wall lies closely against and is fused with the seed coat. The term grain is practical, but it can conceal the different origins of the layers that cover the embryo and its reserve tissue.

Before fertilization, an ovule is already a differentiated part of the maternal plant. Its integuments surround the nucellus, within which the female gametophyte develops. The egg and the central cell have distinct identities inside that gametophyte. At fertilization the egg gives rise to the embryo lineage, and the central cell gives rise to the endosperm lineage. Neither fusion produces the integuments, which were present before fertilization. Instead, the integuments change from ovular coverings into a specialized seed coat. The three components originate at different times and need not contain the same number of chromosome sets. The relationships between them, rather than the growth of any one tissue in isolation, determine whether a seed can reach a functional condition.

It is helpful to distinguish a developmental function from the tissue that performs it. Protection may require a mechanically resistant coat, a hydrated external mucilage or chemical compounds in the outer layers. Nutritional support may require transport, temporary accumulation, permanent storage or remobilization. A developing embryo needs each of these functions at appropriate stages, but the botanical structure responsible may differ among species. The endosperm of a pea may support early embryo development and then be largely consumed; the endosperm of maize continues to hold reserves at maturity. The disappearance of a tissue does not erase its earlier developmental contribution.

Three questions will organize the study of seed formation. First, where did each compartment come from? This is a question of lineage and genetic constitution. Second, how does each compartment change as development proceeds? This is a question of division, growth, differentiation and timing. Third, how do the compartments affect one another? This is a question of material transfer, signaling and physical constraint. These distinctions are especially useful in interpreting mutant seeds, because a visible defect in one compartment may have been caused by an earlier failure in another.

Double fertilization establishes two offspring lineages

The term double fertilization refers to two separate fusion events involving the two sperm cells delivered by a pollen tube. In the conventional Polygonum-type embryo sac, one sperm nucleus joins the egg nucleus and the other joins the central-cell nuclear contribution. The egg and central cell remain distinct female gametophyte cells. Their developmental programs are not interchangeable. After fertilization the egg becomes a zygote, while the fertilized central cell begins endosperm development. The central-cell event does not produce a second ordinary embryo.

To follow the genetics, begin with a diploid sporophyte carrying two homologous sets of chromosomes. Meiosis in the female reproductive lineage normally produces a reduced megaspore with one set. Three rounds of mitosis in the common monosporic embryo-sac pattern generate nuclei descended from that megaspore. In the mature embryo sac the egg contains one chromosome set, and the two polar nuclei in the central cell each contain one. Their union, if it has already occurred, gives a central-cell nucleus with two maternal sets. Meiosis in the male reproductive lineage similarly gives rise to reduced pollen nuclei, and subsequent mitotic divisions form the sperm. This is why the conventional diagram labels the egg and each sperm as n, but the central-cell nuclear contribution as 2n.

The zygote acquires one maternal and one paternal chromosome set. Its mitotic descendants form the embryo proper and supporting embryonic structures such as the suspensor. The primary endosperm nucleus acquires two maternal sets and one paternal set under the assumptions just described. Its initial genomic constitution is therefore triploid, with an unequal contribution of parental genomes. Mitotic descendants normally retain this parental origin even when a subsequent DNA replication cycle temporarily changes their DNA content. The seed coat, by contrast, inherits both chromosome sets of the maternal sporophyte and receives no nuclear genome from the particular pollen donor.

A subtle point is that the two maternal nuclear contributions to the central cell are not equivalent to two independently chosen eggs. In the monosporic Polygonum-type embryo sac they normally descend mitotically from the same megaspore. At a locus at which the female plant is heterozygous, an ordinary reduced egg may carry either maternal allele. The polar nuclei in its own gametophyte ordinarily carry the same allele as that egg. Consequently, a maternal plant heterozygous A/a crossed to a pollen donor homozygous a/a can produce an Aa embryo associated with an AAa endosperm, or an aa embryo associated with an aaa endosperm. The maternal seed coat remains A/a around both classes. This relationship follows from cell lineage, not simply from counting chromosomes.

The seed coat being maternal does not mean that every allele in it came from the grandmother of the embryo. The maternal plant itself inherited genes from its own two parents. Here the adjective maternal identifies the generation and the tissue lineage relative to the current fertilization. A seed coat cell possesses the sporophytic genome of the plant on which the seed develops, including whatever alleles that plant inherited in the preceding generation. A genetic experiment must specify which generation is under consideration before assigning a maternal effect.

Ploidy and DNA content must also be distinguished. Ploidy counts chromosome sets. DNA content measures the quantity of DNA relative to a specified reference. If a reduced unreplicated nucleus has DNA content C, a typical triploid endosperm nucleus before S phase has 3C. Following replication it contains 6C, yet it still has three sets of chromosomes as counted by centromeres until the mitotic division separates sister chromatids. Endoreduplication can produce nuclei of still greater DNA content without generating an extra set of parental fertilization events. A DNA histogram therefore cannot be interpreted without considering developmental cell-cycle state.

The familiar 2n embryo, 3n endosperm and 2n coat arrangement is a useful default only when the problem specifies or implies ordinary diploid parents and the common embryo-sac organization. The biological basis for triploidy is the contribution of two maternal chromosome sets to the central cell plus one paternal set. Other female gametophyte types can contribute different numbers of sets to the central cell. Polyploid parents also change the absolute counts. To reason safely, reconstruct the gametic contributions rather than memorizing a rule that every seed must contain a triploid tissue.

How cytological evidence revealed the origin of endosperm

The discovery that an ovule receives two male nuclear contributions altered the understanding of flowering plant reproduction. The first fertilization event alone could account for the formation of an embryo, but not for the unusual genetic constitution of the nutritive tissue. Cytologists examined successive stages of pollen-tube entry and the changes in nuclear position inside fixed ovules. Sergei Nawaschin reported double fertilization in 1898, and Léon Guignard independently investigated the phenomenon shortly afterward. These observations established a cellular basis for distinguishing the origin of the embryo from the origin of the endosperm.

A microscope image provides a limited form of evidence. Two nuclei close together do not establish that they have already fused. Conversely, finding one enlarged nucleus does not by itself identify which genomes it contains. The strength of the classical interpretation came from comparing developmental stages and relating the positions of sperm nuclei to the positions of the egg and central-cell nuclei. The sequence explained why one developing structure was associated with the egg position while another began within the central cell. Subsequent genetic and cytological work could then test the consequences of the fusion model.

A useful modern extension is to track a distinguishable paternal allele. Imagine a maternal plant carrying only an allele M at a marker locus and a paternal plant carrying only allele P. A tissue-specific assay can determine whether the paternal marker occurs in the embryo, endosperm and seed coat. The double-fertilization model predicts P in the embryo and endosperm, but not in an uncontaminated sample of the coat. If all three samples contain P, the first question is whether the samples were cleanly separated. A small amount of adhering endosperm could contaminate a coat sample. Finding paternal DNA in a tissue preparation is not identical to showing that the tissue's cells inherited paternal chromosomes.

The marker can be used to distinguish the presence of an allele from its expression. A pollen donor carrying a reporter gene may contribute that gene to both fertilization products. If the reporter is visible only in the endosperm, the result may reflect an endosperm-specific promoter rather than selective entry of the paternal genome into the central cell. Conversely, a paternal allele may be present but transcriptionally repressed. DNA assays and RNA or protein assays answer different questions. This distinction becomes particularly important when studying genomic imprinting, because imprinting is a difference in expression according to parental origin, not an absence of the silent allele.

The genetic constitution of endosperm also shows why comparison with the nutrient tissue of a gymnosperm requires care. In many gymnosperms the embryo is surrounded by a nutritive female gametophyte whose formation precedes fertilization. In flowering plants the conventional endosperm begins after central-cell fertilization and includes a paternal genomic contribution. The two tissues can both supply resources, but their origin and genetic relationships differ. Their similar function is not sufficient evidence that they are the same developmental structure.

Historical observation established the separate lineages, but it did not explain how the fertilized central cell communicates with the maternal integuments or why the embryo can arrest when its surrounding endosperm develops abnormally. These later questions require perturbations, such as changing the activity of a hormone pathway in one compartment while observing responses elsewhere. In this way the study of seeds progresses from describing tissue origin to testing the mechanisms that integrate several lineages into one functional reproductive structure.

Early endosperm development and the nuclear pattern

Following fertilization, the primary endosperm nucleus enters a developmental program that differs from the first divisions of the zygote. In the nuclear type of endosperm development, early divisions of the nucleus are not accompanied by partitioning of the entire cytoplasm into individual daughter cells. Repeated nuclear divisions can therefore produce a multinucleate compartment. This arrangement is commonly called a coenocyte or a syncytial endosperm. In this context the shared cytoplasm arose by repeated nuclear division without immediate cytokinesis, rather than by fusion of many previously independent cells.

The cellular organization must not be confused with an absence of a plasma membrane. The central cell is already bounded externally by its own membrane. During free nuclear development, what is initially missing is a complete set of internal partitions separating the newly produced nuclei. A large vacuole often occupies the central region, with the nuclei situated in the surrounding cytoplasmic layer. The cytoskeleton helps position nuclei and organize the cytoplasm associated with each one. Thus a drawing of scattered nuclei represents a structured multinucleate cell, not an unorganized collection of naked DNA inside the ovule.

The distinction between mitosis and cytokinesis explains how this state can arise. The genetic material is duplicated and segregated by the mitotic machinery, so two daughter nuclei can be produced. In a typical plant somatic division, cytokinesis additionally creates a new partition between the daughter cytoplasms. A cell plate develops through vesicle delivery and expansion within a cytoskeletal framework. Delaying the partitioning phase permits additional nuclear cycles in a shared cytoplasm. The nuclear endosperm therefore illustrates the fact that nuclear multiplication and the formation of cellular boundaries are related but separable events.

Under a simplified mathematical model, each nucleus undergoes one successful mitosis during every round and no nuclei die. Starting with one primary nucleus, the number after k complete rounds is 2 to the power k. After five rounds there would be thirty-two nuclei. If no internal cell walls have formed, this does not mean that there are thirty-two separate endosperm cells. If a tissue is fully cellularized after the same five rounds, with one nucleus enclosed in each new cell, then there may be thirty-two cells. The same nuclear count can consequently describe two very different tissue architectures.

Actual developing endosperm rarely behaves like a perfectly synchronized mathematical population throughout its entire growth. Nuclei near the embryo or the chalazal region may enter and leave division cycles at different times. Nuclear migration, regional growth and later cellularization introduce differences that a simple doubling equation cannot capture. The model is useful for a defined examination question with idealized assumptions, but the number observed in a real section does not automatically reveal an exact integer number of completed division rounds. A section may also pass through only part of a three-dimensional structure.

A common developmental advantage of nuclear multiplication without immediate partitioning is the possibility of increasing the number of active nuclei and establishing nuclear domains within an expanding compartment before constructing all internal walls. Shared cytoplasm can permit local movement of metabolites and signals, although a large multinucleate cell is not necessarily chemically uniform. Nuclear positions, vacuolar geometry and the organization of the cytoplasm create regional differences. The timing of cellularization matters because the later tissue must form differentiated cells that can regulate transport and interact with the embryo.

In Arabidopsis, early endosperm development produces regions associated with the micropylar end near the embryo, a peripheral region and a chalazal region toward the maternal nutrient entry side. These names describe positions relative to the ovule rather than independent fertilization products. The regions can exhibit different developmental schedules and gene-expression patterns. The micropylar endosperm is especially relevant to the immediate environment of the embryo, while the chalazal end is associated with maternal interactions. The entire endosperm, despite this regional organization, originates from the central-cell fertilization event.

Cellularization, cellular endosperm and helobial development

Cellularization is the process by which a multinucleate endosperm acquires boundaries that separate nuclei and associated cytoplasm into cells. It is not synonymous with the first nuclear division. During a free nuclear stage, many nuclei may already exist, but the internal geometry has not been divided into ordinary cells. Cellularization changes that geometry by providing interfaces across which the movement of substances can be controlled. It also provides a structural basis for different cell identities to be maintained in neighboring parts of the tissue.

In Arabidopsis and the cereals, nuclear endosperm commonly proceeds from a coenocytic phase to a cellularized phase. Walls form around nuclear domains and extend so as to delimit individual cells. The progression can be spatially uneven; one domain may have advanced farther toward cellular organization than another. Cytoskeletal arrays and membrane trafficking provide the physical machinery needed to organize developing walls. The timing and geometry of these events are controlled by the species-specific developmental program, not by an obligatory universal number of nuclei.

Some flowering plants exhibit cellular endosperm development instead. Here cytokinesis accompanies the first division of the primary endosperm nucleus and subsequent divisions. The endosperm therefore develops as a population of cells without the extended free nuclear phase characteristic of the nuclear type. The genetic origin is unchanged: the endosperm still arises from the fertilized central cell in the conventional sexual pathway. The term cellular describes the mode of division, not the ploidy or the source of the chromosomes.

Helobial endosperm represents another pattern. The first division is accompanied by a wall that separates the embryo-sac space into two chambers, commonly a larger micropylar chamber and a smaller chalazal chamber. Further divisions in at least one chamber proceed without immediate cytokinesis, and the behavior of the two chambers can differ. Some members of monocot groups show this pattern. It is often taught as an intermediate form, but that description must be interpreted developmentally: the first partition precedes later free nuclear divisions. It does not imply that one entire half always remains permanently cellular while the other remains permanently nuclear.

There are consequently two distinct questions that should be asked of any diagram of endosperm. What happened at the first division? What happened during subsequent divisions? A wall following the first division together with free nuclei in one of the resulting compartments is strong evidence for helobial development. Many nuclei dispersed within an initially unpartitioned cytoplasm are characteristic of a nuclear phase. Repeated walls accompanying successive divisions indicate cellular development. A diagram showing only the final cellular tissue cannot necessarily distinguish whether the cells were produced from the beginning or emerged after a coenocytic phase.

The coconut illustrates another reason to separate the developmental type from the mature physical appearance. The liquid inside a young coconut is associated with the liquid endosperm phase, whereas the solid white tissue develops as cellular endosperm. Both belong to the endosperm system. The hard outer shell of the coconut fruit is not the same as the seed coat. Its presence does not change the origin of the internal storage tissue. Examinations frequently test such organ-origin distinctions because names based on everyday food use do not always correspond to anatomical or developmental categories.

Cellularization is not simply a sign that development is running on schedule; it can be required for embryo survival. A normal embryo growing within a persistently abnormal coenocyte may encounter altered nutritional or osmotic conditions. Alternatively, premature formation of walls may restrict a phase of expansion that normally increases seed capacity. A useful developmental hypothesis must therefore predict the consequences of changing the timing in either direction. It is not sufficient to say that earlier cellularization is always beneficial or that more nuclear divisions always produce a healthier seed.

Embryo growth depends on the developing environment

The preceding chapter established the basic geometry of plant embryogenesis. A zygote develops polarity and, in the representative Arabidopsis model, divides asymmetrically to initiate apical and basal lineages. Subsequent divisions establish the embryo proper, the suspensor and the characteristic embryonic body plan. Those events explain where the future shoot, root and cotyledons arise. They do not on their own explain where the expanding embryo obtains the substances it must use. For this question, the relationship between embryo and endosperm is central.

At the beginning of seed formation the embryo is a relatively small sink. A sink is a tissue that imports or consumes resources rather than providing a net export of photosynthetic products. The endosperm can proliferate and expand around the embryo while the embryo is still small. The maternal tissue must supply water, sugars, amino acids and mineral nutrients into the developing seed. The endosperm is positioned to receive, process and redistribute these materials. It is therefore more informative to think of it as a dynamic developmental interface than simply a bag of stored food.

The embryo initially has a limited contact surface with the surrounding tissues. The suspensor, which is an embryonic structure rather than part of the endosperm, can help position the embryo and participate in nutrient or signal exchange in many species. Its exact contribution varies according to embryonic morphology. As the embryo grows, the extent and character of its contact with endosperm change. The early and later embryo may consequently depend on different routes of nutrient movement and respond to different environmental cues.

The endosperm may also influence development by modifying the physical environment. A plant cell expands when water entry produces sufficient turgor and when its wall is capable of yielding. Surrounding tissues constrain this expansion. An expanding embryo displaces endosperm, and the endosperm in turn responds to the changing geometry. The reduction of endosperm volume in a developing dicot seed is not explained merely by compression. Cellular breakdown, turnover of reserves and transfer of material can contribute. The balance between these processes determines the relative sizes of the embryo and nutritive compartment.

In many grain legumes, including pea and bean, the cotyledons become major storage organs. Their cells receive imported carbon and nitrogen, and convert these resources into reserves appropriate to the species. The maternal plant continues to supply resources during development, but the main site of accumulated reserve changes as the embryo expands. A mature seed dominated by cotyledons therefore need not have developed without endosperm. Its appearance records the endpoint of a process in which an initially distinct supporting compartment has largely disappeared.

The embryo can sometimes continue development when removed from a defective seed and supplied with a suitable artificial environment. This observation is conceptually important because it distinguishes a defect intrinsic to the embryo from a failure of its normal developmental surroundings. If an isolated embryo grows while its genetically similar counterpart within an abnormal seed arrests, the surrounding tissue becomes a strong candidate for the primary defect. Rescue is not guaranteed, and an unsuccessful rescue is not proof that the embryo's genotype was defective; the experimental environment itself may be inadequate. Controlled comparisons are required.

The correct way to interpret co-development is to follow both compartments through time. A photograph of a mature aborted seed cannot reveal which tissue failed first. A series of observations can establish that an endosperm defect appeared before embryonic growth arrest, but temporal order alone does not demonstrate a causal mechanism. Stronger evidence comes from a tissue-specific perturbation, a rescue of the altered pathway or both. The developmental system makes this type of reasoning particularly valuable because defects are transmitted across tissue boundaries.
